Chapter 35 - What He Writes First

Vantic cleared his throat before he spoke in the long room, every time, which he had been doing since the nineteenth of October and which Emeric had stopped hearing somewhere around the middle of December.

It was not nerves. Emeric had watched him do it before saying good morning to a stable boy and before telling Ferra that four of her entries were wrong. It was a bell rung before a man got up to speak, and it meant the same thing every time, which was that what came next was going on a page somewhere.

On the ninth of January he cleared his throat twice.

"I wrote to my house on Tuesday," he said. "The letter went with the Wayford post that morning. I am telling you on Thursday because Tuesday was the day I was certain and I do not write when I am nearly certain."

Emeric put his pen down.

"Say what's in it."

"Between the second of November and the ninth of December this barony bought grain from eleven sellers in ninety-one loads. It paid by the bushel at the seller's own measure, struck and witnessed, and every one of those loads has a note against it in the seller's hand or his factor's. It took the grain in by weight at the gate, on your beam, with your man calling it, into Mistress Ferra's book." Vantic did not look at any paper while he said it, which Emeric had learned meant he had looked at a great deal of it beforehand. "What arrived weighs less than what was paid for. Not once. Ninety-one times. One and three quarters in the hundred, near enough, and never in Kaldo's favour."

"How much is that."

Ferra said the number out loud from the end of the table, because she had done the arithmetic while he was still talking, and Vantic did not soften it and did not add to it.

"And you wrote that to Ducatis before you said it in this room."

"Yes."

"A consistent shortfall against a security you hold, in the first winter of the loan, on the borrower's own weighbeam." Emeric heard his own voice doing something he did not much care for. "You've written to a lending house that the grain they've lent against goes missing between the cart and the book. You've written that we're thieves and you've used ninety-one loads to say it."

"I have written that there is an unexplained variance of one and three quarters in the hundred by weight against measure across ninety-one intakes, that I have not established the cause, and that I will report the cause when I have it." Vantic's face did nothing at all. "That is what is in the letter, and it is on the second page, and the first page is about your foundry and is favourable."

"You could have told me on Tuesday and written on Wednesday."

"I could have."

"Then why in God's name didn't you."

"Because then it would be a letter you had a night to shape," Vantic said. "Not because you would have lied to me. Because you would have explained it, and you explain things well, and I would have written the explanation down with the variance, and my house would have received a variance already wearing an answer. The whole of what I am worth to them is that the thing they read has not been past you first. If I spend that once it does not come back." He set his hands flat. "You will be angrier about this in an hour than you are now, and I would rather you had the reason in front of you while you get there."

Emeric sat with it.

The ugly part was that his first thought had not been about the grain. His first thought had been that Ferra was going to be accused in a house in the capital by a man she could not answer, and his second thought had been what it would look like in the file that already existed with his name on it, and neither of those thoughts had anything to do with a hundred and forty-odd bushels of wheat that had not arrived.

"Ferra," he said. "Is any of it going out the gate."

"No."

"You're certain."

"I am not certain of anything at one and three quarters in the hundred, which is the whole trouble with one and three quarters in the hundred." She had the book open already. "It is too small to be a man stealing and too steady to be an accident, and if you asked me which of those frightened me more I would say the second, and I have been saying it to myself since Master Vantic asked me for the seller's notes eleven days ago and I did not come to you either."

"Why not."

"Because I could not make it mean anything, and I have watched you build a case out of one letter that was probably only a busy man being brief." She said it flatly, and it was fair, and it sat there. "I did not want to hand you a shape."

They took it apart over the following two days, and the thing that was wrong turned out to have been sitting on a wall in the weighbeam shed since April in Emeric's own hand.

He had built the beam in the spring because he wanted intake in weight. Volume was a basket and a man's arm and how hard he was willing to strike it level, and weight was weight, and a barony that measured its own harvest in baskets was a barony arguing with itself every August. So the beam went up, and the grain came over it, and Ferra kept weight.

And the world sold in bushels.

So somebody had to turn one into the other, and in April, standing in the shed with wet boots and four other things to do, he had given her a number. Sixty pounds to the bushel. He had said it the way a man says the number of feet in a mile.

"I measured it," he said. "That's the part I want on the table. I didn't guess. I filled the basket eleven times off the drying floor and weighed it and averaged it and wrote it up."

"Off the drying floor," Ferra said.

"Off the drying floor. Kaldo wheat, through my drum, out of my shed, dried to one condition because I built a shed that dries to one condition and a machine that threshes to one cleanness." He put his hand flat on the wall where the number was. "It's a true number about the grain in this barony in April. It is not a fact about wheat."

Vantic went and looked at the eleven sellers' notes again with that in his head and came back within the hour.

"Two loads out of Milford are barley," he said. "Three are described as wheat and are wheat with a good deal of rye in them, which I would expect at that price in November, and eleven are described as clean and are not clean, and the rest were cut wet, which is why they were available at all." He put the notes down. "You have applied one weight to a bushel of nine different things."

"And barley's light."

"Barley is very light."

"So the seller struck an honest bushel, and the beam weighed an honest weight, and my number turned the honest bushel into a wheat bushel that never came, ninety-one times." Emeric looked at the wall for a while. "Nobody took anything. I wrote the shortfall myself in April and it took nine months to arrive."

"That is my conclusion, yes."

"Then say the rest of it, because you've been standing there for four minutes with the rest of it."

Vantic cleared his throat.

"The number is not the fault," he said. "The fault is that it has no name on it and no date. A figure a man measures on a Tuesday and chalks on a wall is a measurement. The same figure eight months later, with nobody's name against it and nothing saying what it was measured on, is a fact, and men do arithmetic on facts without checking them, and your Mistress Ferra is the most careful person in this building and she did arithmetic on it ninety-one times." He picked up his case. "Your barony has a bar bolted to a foundry wall with a rule for what happens when a copy drifts. It has a number chalked in a shed with nothing at all."

What they did about it took an afternoon and was not clever.

The beam kept weight and the book kept weight, and the seller's measure went in beside it in the seller's own words, with the kind of grain named and the seller named, and the two numbers sat next to each other on the page and were not reconciled, because there was nothing honest to reconcile them with. Ferra ruled a third column and left it empty on purpose and wrote at the head of it that it was empty on purpose.

The number on the wall got a board of its own, with the date it was taken, and the words Kaldo wheat, dried, threshed, off the shed floor, and Emeric's name under it, and a line saying it was good for nothing else. Bram cut the board that evening without being asked and made it too large, deliberately, so that a man would have to walk round it.

The correction went to Ducatis on the fourteenth. Vantic wrote it, and did the thing Emeric had not expected, which was to hand him the draft before it was sealed.

"You can object to it. I would rather you objected now."

Emeric read it twice. It was four paragraphs and every sentence in it was true, and it said that the variance was accounted for, that the cause was an error of conversion in the borrower's own method, that the method had been corrected within nine days of the query, and that the security was not and had never been short.

"It's worse than the first letter," he said.

"In one respect."

"In one respect. The first one says something is wrong and nobody knows why. This one says the borrower's own house ran nine months on a number nobody had checked, and it says the man who found it was yours." He handed it back. "Send it. It's accurate."

"It is also true that eleven days after I asked for the seller's notes, without being told what I was looking for, Mistress Ferra had already gone back through forty of them on her own account and had the barley loads marked. I have put that in the fourth paragraph and my house will read it, and it is the only sentence in the letter that will do you any good at all."

"You didn't have to put it in."

"No." Vantic sealed it. "It happened, though."

He stayed behind afterward, which he did perhaps once a month and never for anything in the report.

"May I ask you something that is not my business."

"You've asked me one of those before."

"I have, and you answered it, which is why I am asking a second." Vantic stood with his case in both hands, not sitting, the way a man stands when he intends to leave whatever the answer is. "When you were sent here. Did you know on the day what was expected of you, or did you work it out on the road."

Emeric thought about it properly, because the man had asked it properly.

"On the day," he said. "It wasn't hidden. Nobody sits you down and says it, but the size of the escort says it, and what they put on the carts says it, and who came to the yard and who didn't. I had four days on the road to change my mind about it and I spent all four confirming it instead."

"And did you know that they expected you to fail, or that they had already agreed you had."

"That's two different questions."

"It is. I would like both."

"The second one," Emeric said. "It wasn't a test. A test has somebody waiting at the end of it to see how you did." He watched the man's face and got nothing from it, which by January he had stopped expecting to. "Why do you want to know."

"I would rather not say."

"That's not a refusal, that's a locked door with a note on it."

"Yes," Vantic said, and went to the door, and stopped there with his hand on the frame in a way that was not like him at all. "It is not about you. I want that much said, because you will otherwise spend a week on it, and you have a granary to build."

Then he was gone down the stair, and Emeric sat in the long room for a while with the sound of the outside door, and had absolutely nothing to write down about any of it.

Ferra came in near the end of the evening to put the third column in front of him for his hand on it.

"He is the first person who has ever read my books," she said, signing under him. "I had been telling myself that what I minded was being read."

"And?"

"What I mind is that he was right and that I was not the one who found it, and I have decided that minding that is not a fault I intend to correct." She blotted it. "The empty column stays empty. If either of us ever fills it in, the other one may say something unforgivable."
