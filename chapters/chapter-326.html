Chapter 326 - Empty

At one o'clock there were still fourteen inside.

Emeric had them all, the way a man at a dark window counts the lamps still lit in a street: nine in the choir, in their stalls; three in the north wall, in the passage inside its thickness, moving; one in the room over the north porch, asleep; and one at the foot of the south-west stair, behind the door, where he had been on the twenty-first, faint, and not moving at all. Fourteen. He said it into the radio's mouthpiece on the hour and on the half hour, and each time Ferra's voice came back up the line from seven hundred miles away, small and flat in the brass cup by his ear, and said the number back to him, and then said, "Written."

The K Discovery hung over the close at four hundred feet with her bow to the west front and her shell up, and the whole hill under her.

He had never felt the summit so empty. The castle on the east shoulder had gone out room by room at first light, and was a long pale empty house now with its banners still up; the Circle's yard on the north-west slope was empty to its wall; the streets inside the second ring were shut, their doors left open behind people who had gone out of them in a hurry, and in a lane below the crown wall a dog was going from door to door. Outside the ring the city was a roar. Inside it there was the close, and the fourteen, and Osric's forty in the nave in their grey, and on the west steps, under the saints, the ones who had stayed: three hundred and some, kneeling close together in their coats and shawls, with Gregory among them in his red.

"Fourteen," said Saban, beside him at the rail. He read with his eyes shut, slowly, like a man adding a column he did not trust. "And the one at the stair's not getting any louder."

"He's not getting any quieter."

"No," said Saban. "That's something."

The king stood on Emeric's other side in his plain coat and said nothing. His voice had gone out of the brass horns at mid-morning over the whole city, the hour and the distance and the names of the gates, and the city had gone very quiet to hear it and then very loud; and when it was done he had handed the mouthpiece back to Fletcher's mate and come to the rail, and he had been there three hours. He did not ask the number. He heard it every half hour.

Noon had gone at the bells. Emeric had felt a hundred and twenty thousand notes outside the ring turn toward the hill at once, the way a crowd turns at a shout; and then nothing had fallen, and they were turned toward it still.

Somewhere west a tower crew tried again. Their dead stone came down a quarter of a mile short on the empty lanes of the second ring, and for a breath a patch of the hill went out of his sense like a hole cut in a map. Then it closed.

"They'll have that range in an hour," said Fletcher, from the helm, without turning round.

"Then we'll be somewhere else in an hour."

"We'll be somewhere else when I say," said Fletcher, "which is the same thing, only I'm the one says it." He looked at the dial by his knee, the one that had been red at mid-morning and was not red now. "She's cool. If they come again I'll take her over the castle. There's nobody in it to mind."

The shore team's set clicked on its hook. It was Osric, a little out of breath.

"Another one off the north wall. Fire. Same slit. Missed. I've got the lads behind the third pier." A breath. "Where are they now?"



The three in the wall had been firing on the shore team since a quarter to twelve.

Emeric had felt the first. The team had been coming down the north aisle in two files with their shields up, walking it, every chapel and every tomb; and in the wall the three notes he had marked on the twenty-first had stopped walking and gone to one place, and one of them had done something small and quick, like a man striking a match. And out of a slit he had not known was a slit, a hand high and a yard long in the moulding over the third arch, had come fire.

Not a mage's fire. There was no mage in the wall. A commoner's note, small and grey, with a thumb pressed into a cut in a stone, and the stone doing the rest: the glyph already written in it, the charge already in it. Precast. Saban had told him of the priory where the lay brothers drilled at dawn like a garrison.

It had caught the shore team's second man on the forearm over his shield's rim and burned through his sleeve before he got the shield up; and the team had gone behind the piers, every man, the way Osric had drilled them, and stayed there. Since then the three had fired eleven more times from three slits and hit nobody, and nobody had been able to take a step down that aisle. That was why it was one o'clock and not twelve.

"West end of the passage," said Emeric into the short set. "All three. Where it widens, by the corner of the north transept. One's sat down."

"Tired?"

"I think so."

There was a silence on the set, with boots on stone in it, and somebody coughing.

"How many have they fired?" said Osric. "Each. Can you tell?"

Emeric went in closer. He had been reading them all afternoon as a count, one, two, three, and not as men.

They were not well. Their notes were thin and grey at the edges; and the one sitting down was greyer than the others, the way a lamp goes grey when the wick is drawing on nothing. A precast stone wanted almost nothing to wake it. But it wanted something, and these men had had little to spare to start with.

"The one sitting down's done four, I think. The others three each. They're spending what they've got." He stopped. "I don't think they know they're spending it."

"Nobody told them," said Saban, with his eyes shut.

"How many more's in them?" said Osric.

"I can't tell you that. I can tell you when they're grey."

"Then tell me when they're grey." Osric's voice went away from the set and came back. "I'm not sending anybody down that aisle to get burned for three lads with matches. They can spend it. I'll wait." A pause. "Is that all right, up there? You're the one paying for the wait."

Emeric looked at the king. The king was looking at the spire.

"It's all right," said Emeric.

"And the door," said Osric. "There's a door in that wall or they'd not have got in. I've been round the north transept twice and there's nothing but a tomb with a knight on it and a lot of saints."

Emeric went down into the stone to look. It was slow; four yards of rubble and mortar, and his sense went into it sinking, like boots into mud. The passage was a thread of air in the middle of it, the height of a man and the width of his shoulders, running inside the wall like a vein inside an arm. He followed it east, past the three by the transept window, down eleven steps to a flat place; and the flat place was the back of something.

"The tomb," he said. "The knight. The back of it's a door."

The set was quiet.

"I've had my hand on his face," said Osric at last. "Twice." A pause. "Right. Tell me when they're grey."

It took forty minutes. The three came back to the slits twice more and fired twice, both times at a shield a shore man held out from behind the fourth pier on the end of his rifle at Osric's word; and both times Emeric felt the grey come up in them a shade, and the one who had sat down did not get up the second time. At ten to two the other two went to him, and one knelt.

"They're grey," said Emeric. "All three. One can't stand."

"Going," said Osric.

Emeric felt him go, a knight's note, Traced and dense and warm, with the fire in it that he had learned late and hard to bring out of his hand on purpose, and three notes behind it; along the transept to the tomb, and the stone knight's back swinging in on its hinge with a dragging that made every man in the nave jump, Osric said afterward. Then into the wall.

The lay brothers did not fire. One of them put his thumb in the cut of the stone in his fist, a flat grey thing the size of a biscuit, and pressed, and pressed, and nothing happened, because there was nothing left in him to wake it; and he looked down at it in the lamplight and began to cry. They were young, the oldest perhaps twenty, in brown habits torn at the hem, with a lamp and a basket of bread; and the one who could not stand had wet himself.

The shore team brought them out through the knight's back, two on their own feet and the third over a big Grayrock man's shoulder, and laid them on the flags by the north door and gave them water; and Osric put their stones in a bag and the bag in his coat.

"Eleven," said Emeric into Ferra's line.

"Eleven," said Ferra. "Three out. Lay brothers. Written." Her pen. "Is anybody hurt?"

"One of ours. A burn. Imani's coming down." He stopped. "And them. They've spent what they had, pressing on those stones."

"Spent," said Ferra. "I'll write that too."



The woman over the north porch would not wake up.

She had slept through Harrold's voice from the brass horns and through the bells, the big one and the three small, rung by Saban's people from the belfry at the spire's foot for a quarter of an hour, so loud at the rail that Emeric had felt them in his teeth.

Osric sent a man up the little stair from the porch. Emeric felt him knock, and knock again, and open the door; and the woman's note came up slowly, the way a fish comes up through deep water, and said something, and went down again.

"She's turned over," said the man on the set, sounding injured. "She told me to go away. She said it wasn't her day for the early office and the sacristan knew it." A pause. "She's got her face to the wall."

"Is she ill?"

"She's snoring."

"Wake her again."

The man woke her again, louder, and this time her note came up off the bottom all at once, like a lid off a pot, and stayed up; and through his open mouthpiece, faint, a woman's voice began to talk, high and clear and very fast, and did not stop.

Saban opened his eyes for the first time in an hour.

She came down the porch stair at twenty past two with a basket of linen on her hip, a short broad woman of sixty with her sleeves rolled and her cap crooked. She had been up two nights starching the altar linen for the feast of the dedication, because nobody else in the close could get the lace right, and had gone to bed at seven in the morning with her ears stopped with wax, as she had after every vigil for thirty-one years, because the bells were over her head and she liked her sleep. Nobody had told her about any leaflet. She had been told by a young man in grey with a rifle, in her own room, with her stockings on the chair, that the cathedral was coming down at noon and that it was half past two; and she wanted to know, all the way across the north court to the line, who was going to tell the sacristan the high altar's frontal was not finished, and whether the young man had ever in his life ironed a yard of lace, which he had not.

"Ten," said Emeric. "A woman from the vestry. She slept through it."

"Through the bells?"

"Wax in her ears."

There was a sound on Ferra's line that he had heard from her perhaps a dozen times in thirteen years, and never over a radio: a laugh, short, through the nose, put down at once.

"Written," said Ferra. "Slept through it." Then, in her ordinary voice: "Does she know what's going to happen to her lace?"

"She's taken it with her."

"Good," said Ferra. "That's sensible."



The canons came out of the choir after that.

They had been in their stalls since the bells, the nine of them, in white, saying the afternoon office over and over, and the shore team had left them alone, because Osric's orders were to walk the building and not to drag men out of it while they could still walk. But the three from the wall went past the choir screen on their way out, grey and crying, and Emeric felt the notes in the stalls change one after another as they went by, the way a congregation changes when a child starts crying in a sermon and does not stop.

Two came out together, the young one holding the old one's elbow. Then one alone, walking very fast with his book under his arm. Then three together. Then one more. Emeric read each of them across the north court and through the line. Eight. Seven. Four. Three.

The last two would not come.

One was an old man folded in his stall, asleep or near it; when two of the shore team said his name, which the vestry woman had given them, he woke and said quite clearly that he would go now, then, if it was time, and went, slowly, holding the backs of the stalls. The other was at the altar rail on his knees with his hands on it, and when Osric said from three yards off that it was time, he took hold of the rail with both hands.

He was big, and not old, and dense; not a knight's density, but more than a commoner's, a canon of the close fed what the close fed its own. Two of Osric's men took his arms and he held the rail, and it creaked. Osric looked at it for a while, and then bent and took the man's hands off it one finger at a time, the way a man takes a child's fist off a toy, and the canon fought him for every finger.

Four of them carried him out, one to each limb, down the empty nave, and he fought them all the way and said things Osric did not repeat; and on the flags of the north court they put him down, and he lay on his back and looked up at the K Discovery, and stopped fighting all at once, and put his arm over his eyes.

"One," said Emeric.

"One," said Ferra. "Canons out?"

"All nine. One carried."

"Carried how?"

"Fighting."

"Written," said Ferra.

One: the cell at the foot of the south-west stair, where Osric's men had already found two walls where Emeric had said there would be one. And the steps.



Gregory had come out of the west doors at the end of the bells with the last of the people from the nave, and gone no further. He had turned at the top of the steps under the great round window, with his gold Eye on its chain, and looked at the two thousand going away from him down the court and out through the close's gatehouse, and begun the office. He did not raise his voice. That voice did not need raising. And three hundred and some of them had stopped, and turned, and come back, and knelt; and Gregory had come down two steps to be among them.

They were not inside. But they were inside the six hundred yards, at the foot of a west front like a cliff with a spire behind it, and nothing would fall on them; the rule on the sheet was Emeric's, and the six hundred were his father's, and both held. Nobody said a word to them. No war on the faithful: it was on the front of forty thousand sheets.

A little before three a man was brought up the hill on a door.

His two sons carried it, by their notes, which were like his, and his wife walked beside it with her hand on it. Emeric could read him from four hundred feet. In the crush at the gate under the second ring when the bells began, a cart had put him against a gatepost and held him there: three ribs broken on the left side, and his scalp split from the crown to the ear, with the blood dried in it and the gate's mud with it. He had lain in a yard below the ring since ten, and his sons had heard there was a cardinal still on the steps, and carried him back up past the shore team's line, which let them by, because nobody had told it to stop people walking in.

They laid the door at Gregory's feet, and Gregory knelt.

Emeric pulled his sense back, and it made no difference. It went into him as it had in the nave, like a hand into his own chest. The old words. The man on the door said I do, with his eyes shut. The thread went out of Gregory's hand on his forehead, fine and very strong, in through the door the man had opened; and the man's mana moved inside him, and went to the scalp and the ribs, and spent itself there all at once, poured, like a bucket thrown on a fire.

Saban made a short sound in his throat. His knuckles had gone white on the rail.

The king looked at the two of them.

"What is it?"

"The cardinal," said Emeric. He did not take his eyes off the steps. "He's in that man's head."

The king looked down, four hundred feet, at a red figure kneeling by a door among a great many small dark kneeling ones, and back at his son.

"In his head."

"The way I was in yours. At Ferra's table." Emeric's hands were cold on the rail. "With your leave."

Below them the man on the door cried out, and the people round him said Amen, all together, and it came up to the rail on the wind, thin, like a sound from the bottom of a well.

"Yes," said the king. "With my leave." He looked at the steps a while longer. "He didn't ask that man's."

"He asked. The man said I do."

"That's not what I said," said Harrold.

Emeric made himself feel the scalp close: the split skin pulling together from crown to ear, new and thin and clean, over the dried blood and the gate's mud, shut in. And the ribs not mended at all. Three broken ends where they had been, grinding, and the man not able to feel them grind.

The man on the door sat up. His sons caught him. He put his hand to his scalp and felt the new skin, and laughed, and wept, and the people round him began to weep too.

Gregory looked up. Not at the man; past him, past the west front and the gable, at the K Discovery hanging over the close, at the three men at her rail. He could not have seen their faces from the steps. He looked at the ship for as long as it takes to say a short prayer, with his hand still on the man's head; and his face, as far as Emeric could read anything of it from four hundred feet, did nothing at all.

Then he stood, and said, in that voice, that the Light had heard him.

"Get Imani," said Emeric.



Imani came down the K Endeavor's long ladder onto the close's south lawn at a quarter past three with her bag and two orderlies, and did the burned arm first.

The shore team had the man on a tomb's step with his forearm in a bucket of water, as they had been taught. It was a bad burn, white in the middle where it had gone deep. She read it, slowly, hollow, from inside, and it was as bad as it looked and no worse. "I'm going to clean it with Hester's broth," she said. "It'll sting like nothing you've had. Then I'll dress it and leave it alone, and you'll go up the ladder and lie down. Is that all right?" He was a Grayrock man, a lump, and he looked at her as if she had asked whether he would like his supper. "Yes, Mistress." He said a great many things while she cleaned it, and she let him.

Then she looked round the corner of the south-west turret at the steps, and the first thing she thought was that it looked like a feast day, and she did not like it. The people kneeling in rows with their faces up; the close's beggars on the flags below with their bowls and their crutches; the cardinal in the middle, saying the old words.

The man on the door was talking too much and too fast, with his wife holding one hand; and every few words he stopped and took a breath high and short in the top of his chest, and did not know he was doing it.

She went to him along the edge of the court, the way she went down a full ward, a woman in an apron with a bag. Some of the kneeling people looked at the apron and the brown face and looked away, and some did not. The cardinal did not look at her at all.

"I'm a doctor," she said to the wife, crouching. "From the hospital at Kaldo. Can I look at him?"

The wife looked at the cardinal, two steps up. "He's been healed. The Light's healed him."

"I can see it has," said Imani. "Can I look?"

"Let her look, love, she's come all the way up," said the man on the door, and laughed, and stopped laughing with a hitch; and his wife moved her hand.

Imani read him. The scalp first, because it was nearest: the new skin over the long split, pink and clean, pulled across like a sheet; and under it, all along the split, mud and dried blood and a splinter of the gatepost's wood a finger long, sealed in against the bone. Then the ribs. Three on the left, broken through, the ends riding past each other with every breath; and behind them the lung, with a small dark wrongness in it where one end had gone in this morning and come out again. It was leaking. It had been leaking since ten and had not been able to hurt him for five minutes, and so he had sat up and laughed and breathed deep for the first time since the gate; and it was leaking faster.

She sat back on her heels.

"Your ribs are broken," she said to him, to his face, as she would have said it on the ward. "Three. You can't feel it because of what was done. One of them's been into your lung and it's worse since you sat up. You need to lie back down and not talk, and come with me to the ship and let me look after it properly. If you don't, I think you'll be dead by morning."

"The Light healed me," he said.

"It turned the pain off. That's all it did. The pain was telling you not to move."

"Woman," said a voice above her, "this man has been made whole."

It was not the cardinal. It was the old canon from the choir, who had come round from the north court to kneel on the step above with his book. The cardinal's voice went on over all of them, low and warm, as if nobody had spoken.

Imani did not look up. "Lie down," she said to the man.

He lay down, because his breath had begun to go short and he had noticed it and was frightened; and his wife saw him notice it, and her face changed. Imani had her orderlies there with the stretcher before the canon on the step had found his next sentence, the man on his right side and his wife beside him and his sons carrying, away round the turret toward the ladder.



She saw the pilgrim because the pilgrim was looking at her.

A thin woman of forty or so in a brown travelling cloak gone pale at the shoulders, a pilgrim's lead token pinned to it, kneeling apart at the end of the third step with her left arm held across her body in her right hand, the way you hold a thing that does not belong to you. She had watched the whole of it. She had the face Imani knew from the back of the queue at the morning clinic, where the ones who had been sent away somewhere else first always stood.

Imani crouched on the step below her. "Your arm."

The pilgrim looked at the cardinal. Then she pushed back her sleeve and held it out.

It was clean. On the outside of the forearm, where a long cut had been, there was smooth pink skin, a little shiny, healed without a scar, the way nothing in the world heals except by a miracle. And round it the arm was swollen tight from wrist to elbow, and hot. Imani could feel the heat from where she crouched.

"When?"

"Three weeks Sunday. The feast. I'd cut it on the cart's iron at a ford coming down the north road, and walked five days on it, and it was going bad." The pilgrim's voice was low and flat and very tired. "At first light the canons came along the steps and chose me. He healed it in front of everybody. It stopped hurting. I wept. Everybody wept."

"And then?"

"A week on, on the road home, it started again. The heat. Under the skin, where there's nothing to clean. Worse every day." She said it without any change in her voice at all. "I came back. I thought, he'll heal it again if I ask. If my faith's strong enough this time. They say it comes back when your faith's not strong."

Imani read the arm, all of it, slowly; because a man's leg had gone past her at a pillar on the twenty-first and she had not been allowed to put her hand on him, and he would be buried by now. Under the clean skin the cut was still open. It had never closed inside at all, only on top. And in it and round it, going up the arm between the muscles toward the elbow, the rot. Three weeks of it, shut in, with nowhere to drain.

She came out of it and did not say anything for a while. She looked along the flags instead, at the beggars the canons had walked past at first light on every feast day for two hundred years without choosing. A man with a crutch and a foot wrapped in sacking. A woman with a running eye. A boy of twelve with a withered arm he had been born with. Old, most of them, and dirty, and sick with the slow things people on church steps are sick with; and alive.

"How many were chosen that morning?" she said. "With you?"

"Three. It's always three."

"Where are the other two?"

The pilgrim did not answer.

Imani turned on the step and raised her voice, not much; the voice she used on a full bay to find out who had eaten breakfast.

"Has anybody here been chosen? Ever? On a feast day, and brought in to the rail?"

Nobody answered for a long time. Then the man with the crutch put his hand up a little way and put it down.

"Not me. Not ever. Twenty years on these steps." He had a deep cracked voice. "You don't get chosen if they know your face."

"Has anybody here known somebody who was chosen?"

A good many hands went up, half way, the way people put their hands up when they are not sure they are allowed.

"Are they alive?" said Imani.

The hands came down. One at a time along the flags: the woman with the running eye first, then two old men together, then the rest. The boy with the withered arm kept his up longest, because he had not understood the question; then a woman beside him reached up and took his wrist and brought it down, and kept hold of it.

"They go home," said the man with the crutch. "That's what you hear. They're healed and they go home." He looked at his wrapped foot. "They don't come back to the steps, any road."

Imani crouched with the pilgrim's hot arm in front of her and did a sum.

She had been doing it, she understood afterward, since the plague year, without knowing it was a sum. Since the first woman who had come to the ward at Kaldo from the capital with a thing on her that had been healed, clean skin, rotten under, and died of it with Imani's knife an hour too late. A dozen of them since. More, if she went back through her books, which she would. She had thought it was the capital's doctors, then the Light. But she was looking at a court full of people the Light had never once been laid on, cold, on stone, in December, some of them for twenty years; and at a woman chosen three weeks ago who was going to lose her arm or her life, and two others chosen with her who were nowhere.

The ones the close chose to heal died. The ones it left on the steps lived.

Every healer in the realm closed wounds too fast, with poultices and mana herbs, and the skin came over dirt; she had spent years undoing it and telling them not to. She had never once thought of it as a count. Not the wound killing the patient; the mending killing the patient, more of them the more it was done. It was not the Light that was wrong with it, or the faith. It was the shutting. Anybody who shut a wound over what was in it, by a miracle or a poultice or her own hand, did what had been done to this woman's arm; and it was better to be left open and dirty on a church step for twenty years.

She was going to have to count her own. Every closing she had ever done, every one her nurses had done.

Her hands were not quite steady. She put them on her knees.

"I want to open your arm," she said to the pilgrim. "Here. Now. With a knife, along where the cut was, through the new skin, down to where it's bad. It'll hurt a great deal; I've a draught that takes some of that away and not all. Then I'll clean it out and leave it open to drain, and do that every day on the ship until it's clean, and it'll hurt every day. When it's clean I'll help it close. Properly, from the bottom." She stopped. "If it's too far gone I'll tell you, and what I'd do then, and you can say no to that too. I don't think it is." She looked at the shiny skin. "I'll be cutting what he did. You should know that. You can say no."

The pilgrim did not look at the cardinal this time. She looked at her arm.

"You'll not ask me to open anything," she said at last. "In myself. A door."

"No. Never."

"And it'll hurt."

"Yes. It's meant to."

The pilgrim put the arm out a little further, over the step, toward Imani's knee. "Then do it."

Imani did it there, on the third step, with the cardinal's voice going on above her, because the arm had waited three weeks and she did not see why it should wait for a ladder. An orderly held it on a folded cloth and held the woman's other hand, and Imani gave the draught and waited for it, and opened the clean pink skin along its length with her smallest knife. What was under it came out. She cleaned it the whole length with Hester's broth, reading as she went, following the bad up between the muscles and getting it out, while the woman bit on a fold of her cloak and did not scream; and when it was as clean as it would be today she laid a dressing on it, loose, and did not close it, and put nothing of her own into it at all.

The cardinal finished the office as she finished the dressing. He did not look at her.

There was an old man two steps down with his right hand wrapped in a clean cloth, healed at the same feast, the pilgrim said, who had watched the knife with his mouth open. Imani crouched by him and told him in plain words what she thought was under the cloth, and what she would want to do, and what it would cost him. He listened all the way through, and then put the hand inside his coat.

"No, Mistress. I'll go with His Eminence."

"It'll get worse."

"Then it'll get worse," said the old man. "I'll go with him."

She wanted very much to take hold of his wrist. He was old and slow and she was neither, and she knew exactly what was under the cloth.

"All right," she said.

She asked his name and where he lived, and wrote both in her book, and under them, no, and the day. Then she went back and sat on the cold stone beside the pilgrim while the orderly fetched the stretcher, and turned to a clean page at the back, and wrote on her knee, in her capitals.

WARD RULE.

Before anything is done to a body, say what you will do to it, in plain words, your own. Name it: a cut, a draught, a bone set, a mending. Say what it will cost and what it cannot do. Then ask.

No is no. Write it down, with the day. Don't ask twice in the same hour, and never ask in a way that makes it hard to say.

No set words. Not ever. If you find you are saying it the same way every time, stop, and say it again in your own.

And under it, smaller: Don't shut a thing over what's in it. By any road. Count.

Her initial, and the day. She closed the book.



Gregory led them out at a quarter to four.

Before he did, his note went still, the way a man's goes still when he is listening to something in another room. Emeric did not know what he was listening to. The hands going down along the flags, perhaps; the old man with his hand in his coat. Perhaps nothing. Then he closed his book and stood, and the people stood with him, stiff from the stone, helping each other up.

He walked. He did not take the closed carriage that had stood in the close's stable yard all day with its horses out. He went down the west court on his own feet, with the old canon at one elbow and a young woman with a baby at the other and three hundred and some behind him, the old man among them; through the gatehouse under its two towers, where the shore team's line stepped back for him; down through the empty second ring at an old woman's pace, because there were old women with him; and past the six hundred yards. Emeric followed his note out into the roar beyond the ring, and on, out of the city by the north road, alone now, with two canons, toward the falls, and a house above them where for a month Emeric had kept his sense from going.

He kept it from going now. He let the note go up the falls road until it was small.

"He's out," said Saban, with his eyes open. "The steps are empty."

"Not all of them," said Emeric.



The old man's door had been plastered over.

That was why the shore team had found a wall in the morning at the foot of the south-west turret's stair, plain dressed stone with a saint in a niche; and Emeric, reading it, had said no, the door's behind it. They had tapped, and found the niche was plaster painted like stone, and behind the plaster brick, and behind the brick an oak door with no handle on its outside and a hatch in it at waist height the size of a book. The hatch's bolt was on the stair's side, and bright with use. On the step under it stood a wooden bowl with the dregs of something in it, and a cup. Nobody had brought it today; Emeric had felt every soul on the hill all day. Somebody had been bringing it for a long time.

Osric opened the door himself, with a bar and two men and the better part of half an hour, because the hinges had rusted and the foot of it had swollen into the sill; and all the while Emeric read the note on the other side, and it did not change, not at the bar, not when the door shrieked and moved a hand's breadth and stuck.

Then it went in, and Osric went through with a lamp, and was quiet on the set for a while.

"He's here," he said at last.

"Is he hurt?"

"I don't know." Osric's voice was odd. "I don't know what he is. He's in the corner on straw. There's a bucket. It's a room the size of a cart, with a slit at the top for air; you can see the sky through it if you lie on the floor. That's all there is." A pause. "He's looking at me. He's not frightened. He's just looking."

"Can he walk?"

"No. I don't think he's walked for years." And then, not into the set, gently, in the voice Emeric had heard him use to Tess's boy on the Forge's stair: "All right, father. I've got you. Nobody's going to hurt you. We're going out in the air." And for the first time since the twenty-first the old man's note moved; it came up off the floor in Osric's arms and went up the turret stair one step at a time, and along the gallery, and out onto the west steps into the last of the daylight.

Osric laid him on the top step on his own coat, under the great round window. Imani was there by then, kneeling on his other side, and Emeric read him with her from the rail.

He weighed nothing. A little heap of bones in a shirt that had been white, with a long white beard and long white hair, skin like the paper of an old book, and eyes gone pale with the dark. His note was a single thread, so faint that Emeric had to listen for it, and steady. He had been fed enough to live and no more for a very long time. His hands lay open on Osric's coat, long, the joints swollen; and in the creases of the first two fingers of the right hand, ground in so far it had gone under the skin, there was ink. The kind of stain a clerk carries to his grave.

"His room's nearly empty," said Imani, very low. "There's not a thing in him to spare. If I tried to mend anything I'd kill him." She did not touch him. "Broth, a spoon at a time. Warm, not hot. Blankets, out of the wind, and nobody being clever. He'll live, if we don't do anything to him."

The old man's eyes went from her face to Osric's, and then up past them to the sky over the west front, grey going yellow, with the spire going up into it and the K Discovery hanging beside the spire; and he looked at the sky for a long time.

Then he spoke, in a voice like paper, the shape of words with very little behind them, like a page turned in another room.

"Did Jerome ever come back?"

Osric looked at Imani. Imani looked at Osric.

"Who's Jerome, father?" said Osric.

The old man did not seem to hear. He looked at the sky, and after a while his eyes closed, and he was asleep on the west steps with his inked hands open.

At the rail, Emeric said the question into Ferra's line, word for word.

"Jerome," said Ferra. "Does it mean anything to you?"

It was a priest's name, an old one; there were two parishes of it in the lower city. It meant nothing.

"No."

"Then I'll write it beside him," said Ferra, "and we'll find out." The pen. "One. An old man, from the cell at the foot of the south-west stair. Carried out by Osric. Alive. Imani says he'll live. Asked whether Jerome ever came back. Nobody knew the name. Written."

"Nought," said Emeric.

The line was quiet.

"Say it again," said Ferra.

"Nought. Nobody inside." He shut his eyes and read the whole of it, stone and stone and stone: the nave, the aisles, the side chapels and the galleries; the choir and the vestries and the crossing and the transepts; the passage in the north wall with its basket and its lamp, the room over the north porch with its stockings on the chair, the cell with its straw and its bucket and its slit of sky; the turrets; the spire, up through the belfry with its bells hanging still, to the gold at its tip. And the crypt under all of it, with the dead of two hundred years in their places, small and cold and quiet, and not one note among them. "Nought."

"Saban."

"Nought," said Saban beside him, with his eyes shut.

"Ward."

The other line clicked. Ward was on the K Amara's hatch floor, a mile up and a mile east, with his eye to Ivo's tube, and had been reading the building between his sightings since three.

"Nought," said Ward.

"Written," said Ferra. "Nought inside, at four. Three counts."

The king turned his head and looked at his son.

"Every one?"

"Every one."



He went down to the west steps before the first rod. He did not explain it, and when he went to the hatch the king came with him.

Fletcher held her a yard off the flags of the west court and they stepped down onto the cathedral's stones, the two of them, in their plain coats. Osric's men were carrying the old man toward the ladder on a stretcher with Imani beside him, and the last of the forty were going out through the gatehouse in two files; and the steps where three hundred had knelt all afternoon were empty, with a crutch left on the bottom one, and a bowl, and a pilgrim's lead token come off somebody's cloak.

The three doors stood open, as the shore team had opened them in the morning to let the people out faster. Emeric went up to the middle one and stopped on the threshold, and the king stopped beside him.

The nave was empty, the length of a street and the height of the sky, the smell of candles and old incense coming out on the cold air. The light came in low through the south aisle's windows in long yellow bars across the floor and up the piers, and lost itself in the vault. At the far end, past the screen, over the high altar, the great Eye hung on its gold chains out of the dark, a yard across, catching the last of the light; and it looked down the whole empty length of the nave at the two of them in the door.

He had looked at it all his life. He had been named under it. He had stood at its rail as a boy on feast days, bored, counting the links in its chains. An almond of gold, and in the middle of it, upright, the pupil: long, narrow, pointed at both ends.

The shape of the stone in his band. And a dragon's eye.

He did not say anything.

His father looked at his face for a long moment, and then at the Eye, and then at his face again; and said nothing either. After a while the king turned and went back down the steps toward the ship. Emeric stood in the door a little longer. Then he went after him.



The first rod fell at twenty past four.

The K Discovery was a mile up and well west by then, over the empty second ring, and the cathedral was small again below him, a grey cross on the summit with its gold point standing up into the yellow light; and beyond the ring the whole city on its walls and roofs, turned toward the hill.

"Rod one," said Emeric into the line. "The north aisle. The fourth bay from the west, over the passage in the wall. Named."

"Heard," said Ward, from the K Amara's hatch.

"Written," said Ferra.

Ward said now, to somebody beside him, and the rod went.

It was faster than anything he had felt fall that was not lightning, Fennick's tooth of tungsten in front and Sorrel's four white fins behind and the stone in its tail pushing the whole way. It went into the north aisle's roof a stride from where he had named it, and through the vault, and through the floor, and down into the crypt among the dead; and then Fennick's fuse. The sound came up late, the deep underground thud he knew from the quarry and then a great flat crack that went out over the city in a ring, and a hundred and twenty thousand notes went up at once like birds off a field. Where the fourth bay had been, dust rose in a slow grey column, and the roof on either side sagged toward the hole, and held.

"Rod two. The north aisle. The fifth bay. Named."

"Heard."

"Written."

Thirty-one, eleven of them Fennick's hollow kind and the rest plain. The list was in his coat in Ward's hand, in the order they had argued out on the sand table, so that nothing would fall outward over the close wall that did not have to. He named every one. Ward said heard, and Ferra said written, thirty-one times; and it was slower than he had thought it would be, because each one waited for Ward to lay the cross and the ship to come round, and between them Emeric read the building through the drifting dust before he named the next, though there was nobody in it. Ward had said every one, every time.

The north aisle went, and the south. The nave's vault came down between them in two long falls, slowly and then all at once, and the sound of that went on for a long time. The choir, the vestries, the room over the north porch and the porch. The transepts. The west front stood longest, the gable and the round window and the saints in their rows with nothing behind them, like the front of a house on a stage; then rod twenty-six went into the floor behind it, and it leaned inward and went down into its own dust with the saints still standing in their rows, and the two short stair turrets at its corners went with it.

Then there was only the crossing: four great piers standing up out of the dust alone on the summit, the belfry at their top with its louvres open, and the spire going up off it very thin into the last of the light, with the Gilded Eye at its tip catching the sun as it went down behind the western hills, the only thing in the city still lit.

The king had stood at the rail for the whole of it. He did not say anything now.

"Rod thirty-one," said Emeric. "The crossing. Through the belfry, into the floor between the four piers." He read it once more, the piers and the bells and the thin stone going up and the gold at the top. Nobody. "The spire. Named."

"Heard," said Ward.

"Written," said Ferra.

It went in through the belfry's roof, and he felt it touch the big bell on its way through the frame, and the big bell rang: once, very deep, the note that had told the city its hours for two hundred years, and it came up to the rail clean through all the dust. Then the fuse. The four piers went outward a little, all together, like a man's knees going, and the spire came down straight into the place where the crossing had been, belfry and bells and the long thin stone, telescoping into itself, and the gold Eye came down last of all through the dust, still lit, and went under.

The dust lay over the summit, grey, and spread, and the sun went down.

The castle stood on its shoulder, long and pale and empty, with its banners. The Circle's white tower stood on the north-west slope. And between them, where Emeric had been named, and his father crowned, and every one of his father's children named, there was a heap of stone in a ring of grey dust, and nobody under it.

The city was very quiet. Then from the walls and the roofs below the ring the noise began, and he could not tell, reading it, what kind of noise it was. He did not think the city could have told him either.

"Done," said Ward.

"Written," said Ferra. And after a little: "Come home when you can."

The king took his hands off the rail and put them in his pockets, and looked down at the hill in the dusk.

"He'll have heard that, up at the falls," said Saban, behind them. He was looking north, where the falls road went up into the dark. "He can stay in that house. Or he can be the Church in this city." He turned his hat in his hands. "He can't do both now."

Nobody answered him. Fletcher brought the K Discovery round, slowly, and took her up.
