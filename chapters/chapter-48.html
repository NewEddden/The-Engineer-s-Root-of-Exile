Chapter 48 - Terms

A lending house does not lend against a promise.

It does not lend against a title, and it does not lend against a prince, and Emeric had known that for most of his life without once having to know it about himself. A house like Ducatis lent against history. A borrower's history was the only thing a lender could read that the borrower had not written that morning, and Kaldo had none. It had three years of accounts, kept by a woman who held no licence, in a barony that had been a plague town, a treason case and a battlefield inside four years. That was Kaldo on paper, all of it.

He expected to be refused. He wrote the letter expecting it, and Ferra drafted the second plan beside it, and the second plan was worse than the first by every measure either of them had.

Ducatis did not refuse.

That surprised him for about a day. Then he understood it, and understanding it did not make him feel any better. A borrower with no record is not refused. He is priced.

The terms came back in the last month of winter, by the house's own rider, in a sealed case, and Ferra read them aloud in the small room with the door shut and Saban against the wall.

They were not long. That was the first thing. A house that lends to half the nobility of Randillia has had a long time to work out how few words it needs.

The interest was steep. Ferra read the figure and did not comment on it, which from Ferra was a comment.

One line was steeper than the rest. Ducatis had read the accounts she had sent, and had found the posted wage and the injury pay, and had priced them in writing, explicitly, as an elevated operating cost carried without security. It charged for the carrying. It said so in a sentence that had clearly been written by somebody who had seen a great many lords try to keep a promise with somebody else's money.

Emeric had known that sentence was coming since the night he told Ferra no. He paid for it, and said nothing about it, then or afterward.

The harvest was pledged. The foundry was pledged. All stores of grain were pledged.

He stopped her there.

"Read that again."

"All stores of grain held by the barony, of whatever kind, as security."

"Of whatever kind," he said. "That's the seed."

"Yes."

He took the draft from her and struck the line through, carefully, with a ruler, and wrote beneath it in his own hand that Kaldo pledged the harvest, the foundry, and every grain in its cellars that was not seed, and that the seed store stood outside the security, in writing, or there was no loan. A barony that has pledged its seed has pledged next year's harvest twice, and a lender who cannot see that is not a lender he wanted.

Ferra rewrote it cleanly. Saban sent it north the same day.

Ducatis accepted the exclusion and nothing else. It was the one thing Emeric fought for, and the one thing he won, and he knew perfectly well that the house had let him have it because it cost them nothing and cost him less than he would have paid to keep it.

The rest was the covenant, and the covenant was the part that mattered, because it governed everything after it.

He could repair, maintain and operate anything that had existed before the loan. He could not start anything new.

The farm would be rebuilt. The school would stay open. The foundry would run. The main would be relaid. Every one of those was a thing that already existed.

The port would not be touched. The yard at the lower water would not be started. The road up the hill would stay a bad road. The glassworks he had promised himself on a condition would not be built, whatever the condition did. The hospital Imani had told him the barony needed would stay a sentence Imani had said. Every line in his notebook that was not already standing would stop exactly where it was.

The term was twelve years. The covenant held for as long as any part of the principal was outstanding. Ferra's projection, at any surplus she was prepared to call plausible, was that the principal could be cleared in seven.

Those were the only two numbers anybody said out loud in that room, and nobody said them twice.

Emeric read the whole thing through two times, slowly, with his finger on the lines. Then he signed it.

Ferra opened the ledger to the back page, where she kept his rules, and wrote a date beneath them. He did not ask what it was. He knew what it was. It was the date on which she expected the covenant to become intolerable, and she had written it down now, while it was still a guess, so that when it arrived nobody could say they had not been warned.

The money came in a closed carriage with the house's mark on the door and two armed men riding behind it, on a grey afternoon, over the Left Bridge and through the Western Gate.

She came with it.

Not after it. Not ahead of it. Not announced separately by a letter and a date. She came up the road on the carriage with the strongbox bolted to its floor, because she was a clause in the contract, and a clause travels with the thing it governs.

She got down in the yard without help, a woman in a plain dark travelling coat and gloves, with a leather case of books under one arm. She looked at the keep. She looked at the gate she had come through. She looked at the ledger in her own hands. Then she asked, of nobody in particular, for the person who kept the books.

Ferra came out.

"Your name," Ferra said. "For the register."

"Vantic."

Ferra waited, with the pen over the page.

"The house's factors work under house names," the woman said. "Vantic will do for the register."

Ferra wrote it down. Nobody asked about it again. It was a dull answer and a complete one, and a dull complete answer is the one that nobody ever asks twice.

The strongbox was carried down to the strongroom under the keep, and Ferra and the factor counted it together on the long table, twice, each in her own column, while the two armed men stood at the door and Emeric stood by the wall and did not help. The columns agreed on the first count. Ferra said afterward that it had never once happened to her with a stranger, and said it in the tone of a woman reporting an irregularity.

What she was, was set down in the contract, and the contract was plain.

She was Ducatis's resident factor in Kaldo. She held book rights and ledger rights. She held a right of inspection over anything the security touched, which was nearly everything. She held a right to attend any decision to spend above a threshold the contract named. She would live in the keep, and she would hold all of it for the life of the debt.

She was stoic, precise, and entirely uninterested in being liked, and within a day nobody in the keep was confused about any of those three things.

She had also, it became clear over the first week, done her reading.

A house that lends to nobles has to predict which of them will be its debtors next year, and to predict that it studies them. She had come south having read everything that Randillia knew about Emeric Stelan Randillia. Which was that he drank. That he was a waste. That he was a third son of no consequence who had disgraced his father at a banquet in front of two hundred people and been sent to a dying barony in the south to be out of the way.

She could not reconcile that with what she found, and she did not pretend to.

She walked everything. She walked it on foot, with a notebook, alone, and asked questions of whoever was standing nearest, and wrote down the answers without comment. Within a week she had seen a school with glazed windows in it, bright rings of light moving across the floor from the bullseyes, and children reading under them. She had seen a foundry pouring bar to a pattern, and had asked Bram how often a casting was rejected, and had written down the answer, and had asked him a second question that made Bram look at her properly for the first time. She had stood for some while in front of the board in the market square where the posted rate was written, and read it, and read it again. And she had spent two full days with a set of accounts kept to a standard she had not seen outside a lending house.

None of it matched the file. She said nothing about the file.

Emeric had been read about his whole life, and he knew the look of a person comparing a man to a document. On the fifth day, crossing the yard, he stopped beside her.

"Your file says I drink," he said.

"My file says a number of things."

"Is it accurate?"

She considered the question the way she considered a line in a ledger, all the way through, before she answered it.

"It is accurately copied," she said, and went on to the foundry.

After that she simply stopped referring to it.

Her first real conversation in Kaldo was not with Emeric. It was with Ferra, and it was about carry lines.

It happened in Ferra's room on the fourth evening, and Emeric heard only the end of it, from the corridor, and stood there longer than he had meant to.

Vantic had found the injury pay in the books and had found how Ferra carried it. Not as a cost closed each week, but as a running line, carried forward quarter on quarter with its own reckoning of how long each man was likely to be on it, so that the book showed at any moment what the barony already owed its wounded for the rest of the year, and not merely what it had paid them so far. Vantic wanted to know how Ferra estimated the likely length. Ferra showed her. Vantic asked why the estimates were revised monthly and not quarterly. Ferra told her. Vantic asked a third question, and Ferra answered it, and asked one back, about how the house carried a pledged harvest against a loan before the harvest existed, and Vantic answered that.

It went on for two hours, and neither of them raised her voice or lowered it, and by the end of it there were four sheets on the table covered in two different hands.

"Who taught you to keep a book like this?" Ferra said.

Vantic looked at her for a moment.

"Who taught you?" she said.

Neither of them answered. They went back to the sheets.

The first friction between the factor and the prince was procedural, and it was correct, and nobody raised a voice.

She came to him in the small room at the end of the first week with the ledger open at a page.

"There's a weekly line against the security with nothing behind it," she said.

"The injury pay."

"I've read what it is. It isn't an expenditure a lender can recognise."

"I know," said Emeric. "Your house priced it."

"It did." She turned the ledger toward herself. "I'm recording it as an exception."

"Record it."

She did, there and then, standing, in a small precise hand, and closed the book and went. She recorded everything as something. That was the second thing about her that nobody in the keep was confused about by the end of the month.

Emeric treated her, from the first day, as exactly what she was, which was the bank's instrument.

And because she was the bank's instrument, he treated her as a constraint. A condition of the ground, like frost, or the planting window, or the covenant itself. A thing to be read and worked around and not argued with. That half of it was wrong, and he did not see that it was wrong, and not seeing it was the most comfortable thing about having her in the house.

What Ferra saw took her until the summer, and nobody else saw it at all.

It began with the letters. Vantic wrote to her house every week, and in some weeks twice, and what she sent was immaculate, and constant, and well past anything the contract asked of a resident factor. Ferra knew what the contract asked. She had read the contract more times than anybody in the barony. And Ferra had kept books long enough to know that nobody over-reports out of diligence. Diligence reports what is required and does it well. You over-report when you have a thin file and somebody is going to use it against you.

The second thing took longer to see. On small matters Vantic ruled at once, without hesitation, a hinge order, a line of lamp oil, a wage paid a day early, and was never wrong. On anything genuinely borderline, anything where a reasonable factor might have gone either way, she did not rule. She wrote. She sent it up to the house and waited for the house to answer, and it took weeks, and she let it take weeks.

Ferra put the two together in the way she put anything together, slowly and then all at once. Somebody at the far end of that correspondence was waiting for this woman to make a decision she could be faulted for. The woman knew it. And she was refusing, week after week, with great care, to hand them one.

It was not local. Nobody in Kaldo frightened her, and Ferra had watched her walk through a square that did not want her there as though the square were weather. It was not the barony. It was not the debt. It was something with reach, somewhere else, that could touch her from a long way off.

Ferra stopped there. She did not guess past it. She had no data past it, and she did not guess without data, and she did not intend to start with this.

She told Emeric once, in four sentences, as an operational fact.

"Our factor won't make a discretionary ruling. Anything borderline goes up to her house and waits. Don't build anything that depends on her deciding. Bring her things that are already decided."

She did not speculate in front of him. She never raised it with Vantic, then or ever.

And then she was kind about it, in the only register she had, which was work. She stopped asking Vantic to rule on anything. She began bringing her decisions already made, with the reasoning attached and the figures under the reasoning, so that what went into the factor's file each week was a record of Kaldo's judgment and not a record of Vantic's.

Vantic noticed inside a week. Neither of them ever said one word about it.

Emeric took the four sentences, understood them as a constraint on how the barony should route its paperwork, and filed them. It was exactly wrong, and it was exactly him.

The town took less time than Ferra did, and got to a simpler answer.

Kaldo decided within a fortnight that the woman from the money was not to be liked.

Nobody was rude to her. The town had learned something about rudeness from its prince, which was that it was expensive and gained nothing. But talk stopped when she came into the square and started again when she left it. Two stalls found that they had nothing that day that she might want. A carter who had been asked a question about the price of hay said he did not know the price of hay.

Sela charged her full price.

She did it at the soap counter, on a market morning, with a line of women behind the factor, and it was done in plain view. Vantic asked for two bars. Sela named the price. It was the posted price. Vantic paid it. Sela counted out the change into her glove, coin by coin, and said "Next," and looked past her.

It was the closest thing to a welcome anybody in Kaldo gave her that year, and several people who saw it understood that, and Vantic, who had spent her whole life reading rooms, understood it as well.

Late in the winter, on the night the strongbox was emptied into the barony's accounts, Emeric sat in the small room with his notebook.

He had kept it since the first summer. It was the list. Every thing he meant to build, in the order he meant to build it, with the reasoning next to each.

There was a line through most of it now.

The port. The yard at the lower water. The road up the hill to Grayrock. The glassworks, with its condition written under it. The hospital. A dozen more, in a dozen different inks, some of them three years old. And beside each one, in his own hand, the number of the covenant clause that stopped it.

He looked at the page for a long time, and the thing that bothered him was not the debt.

The debt was a figure. He had lived with figures his whole life, in both lives, and a figure with a date on it was only ever a problem to be worked.

What bothered him was simpler. For nearly four years he had been the only person in this barony who could read what Kaldo actually was. Not what it said it was. Not what the rolls said, or the tithe map, or the town's own idea of itself. What it was. That had been the one thing he had that nobody else had, and he had built everything on it.

There was now a second one. She was living in his house. She was reading his books. And she worked for somebody else.

He had come south with nothing and had spent four years making this place worth taking, and the first thing to take it had been a contract.

He closed the notebook. Down the corridor there was still a line of lamplight under the factor's door.

She was reading.
