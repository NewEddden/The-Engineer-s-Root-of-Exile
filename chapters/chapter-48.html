Chapter 48 - Nothing Goes Back Up

The woman from the lower ward came back about her roof in the third week of November, on a morning of steady rain, and stood in the petition line with a bucket.

She did not need the bucket. She had brought it so that he would see it, and he saw it. She put it down on the table in front of him, empty, and stood behind it with her hands folded.

"You said a year," she said.

He had. He remembered the ninety minutes, and the landlord and the parish's man on either side of her, and the timber and the men and the beer, and an arrangement that had held on nothing but three people not wanting to refuse a prince in front of each other. He remembered sitting there afterward and giving it a year, and seeing exactly where it would come apart.

It had come apart exactly there. The landlord had run short of timber in the spring and patched the last of it with old boards off a shed. The parish had decided in the summer that its men had been lent and not given, and taken them back for the churchyard wall. The beer had been drunk in the first week. And the November rain had found the old boards on the second night, and come through into the back room, and been coming through since.

"It's been fifteen months," the woman said. "So you were wrong."

"I was wrong by three months."

"You were wrong by a roof," she said, and the petition line behind her, which had been listening, laughed, and she did not.

He did it differently this time. He did not send for the landlord or the parish. He sent the carpenter and two of the gate crew to her house that afternoon with oak out of the baron's woods under another of Hale's waivers, and they had the roof sound by dark, and the next morning he had Hale write the cost of it against the landlord's rent, to be paid to the barony a little at a time out of what the woman paid him, until it was paid. The landlord came up the hill to complain about it and was told by Hale that a roof belonged to the house it was on.

Ferra entered the roof in her own book as a debt, with the landlord's name against it, and the date, and a ring round the date.

"Fifteen months," she said, when she had blotted it. "You should have done that the first time."

"The first time I didn't have a carpenter."

"The first time you wanted them to like you," Ferra said, and turned the page.



He went to the mines that week, for the whole of it, because the lower working was going to flood by the end of the month whatever the treadle pump did, and the crew wanted one more push before it did, and nobody on the hill would put a fire to the face unless he was holding it.

It was a good week, at the hill. Cold and dry. They drove the face a full yard in three days with fire and water and Emeric's hand on the flame, and the treadle pump kept the floor wet and not drowned, and the old miner said, on the last evening, over the turf fire, that it was the best week the hill had had since his father's time, and that he did not know what the hill would do without the prince on it in the winter, and that he did not like not knowing.

Emeric heard that, and heard what was under it, and was too tired to do anything with either.

He came home on the seventh day, late, in the dark, and stood at his own shut gate while the gate crew asked the keep down the wire whether they might open it, as Ward had told them to. Then he went straight to the dyehouse, because it was on the way and because there was a lamp lit in it.

Tess was there. She was sitting on her stool by the bench with her bench book shut on her knee and her hands folded on top of it, and on the bench in front of her, laid out in a row, were five short ends of fine copper wire, each one about as long as her hand, each one with a ragged end where it had broken.

She looked up when he came in. She did not get up.

"I tried the wheel again," she said. "Harl let me have the night hours. It broke at the fourth hole, same as in the summer." She touched the first of the broken ends. "Then by hand, with Bram's tongs, slow. The third." She touched the next. "Slower. The fourth." The next. "Annealing it twice as often. The fourth." The next. "Bram pulling and me feeding, because he's steadier than I am." The last. "The fifth. Once."

She put her hands back on the book.

"It doesn't come through for anybody but you," she said. "So nothing came through all week."

He looked at the five broken ends. At the coil on the nail, which was the same length it had been when he left, to the inch, because she would have measured it. At the zinc rods in their box, of which there were exactly as many as there had been, because nobody else could hold the retort's fire. At the jars on the bench, running down a little, as jars did, with nobody to make more.

A week. Nobody had planned for a week. He had not planned for it. He had built the whole of this room, every jar and rod and coil in it, around a pair of hands, and then walked off up the hill for a week and taken the hands with him.

"I should have told you," he said. "Before I went. That it would stop."

"I knew it would stop," Tess said. "I wanted to see if it had to."

Bram had come to the dyehouse door, looking for his apprentice. He stood in the doorway with his arms folded and looked at the five broken ends on the bench, and at his apprentice, and at the prince, and did not come in.

"A barony," he said, "with one pair of hands in it is a barony with one pair of hands in it."

He went back to the forge, and left it in the dyehouse with them.



Hollis's strip came up in the last week of November, all but the bottom of it.

Emeric went out to see it on the first dry morning after he got back, along the edge of the west side with the frost still white in the furrows, and found Hollis already there, standing at the hedge end of his own strip with his hands behind his back, looking at the ground.

The top of the strip was green. The winter wheat was up, thin and fine, in rows so straight you could have laid the reeve's stick along them, a haze of green over the brown earth. It came down the strip toward the hedge, row after row.

And then it stopped.

The last part of the strip, the part by the hedge, the part Hollis had sown by hand out of the brown sack in the first week of the month, was bare: brown earth with the frost on it and the marks of the harrow, and not a single blade in it, from one side of the strip to the other, as though somebody had drawn a line across the field with a ruler and told everything below it not to grow.

Hollis did not look round when Emeric came up beside him.

"You said," he said.

"I said."

"I heard you." Hollis looked at the bare ground. "I'm the seed man. Fifty years. The west side buys its seed off me when its own seed's thin, and bought it off my mother before me, because our seed comes up. Everybody in the west side's walked past this strip this week." He did not raise his voice. "I sowed it. Nobody made me."

"I was the one who didn't come back for the stone."

"You were," Hollis agreed. "And it was my sack. And my arm." He bent down, with a little difficulty, and picked up a clod from the bare ground and broke it in his fingers and looked at what was in it, and found a grain of the brown seed, whole, unsprouted, dead in the earth as a pebble, and held it out on his palm for Emeric to see. "Seed's seed, I said. It isn't. Not when it's been cooked. You knew that, and I didn't, and now I do."

"The barony will make it good. The seed. Whatever the strip would have been."

Hollis looked at him then, for the first time.

"The barony can make the grain good," he said. "It can't make the west side forget whose strip that is." He let the dead grain fall back onto the bare ground. "Leave it. I'll sow it again in the spring, barley. Let them walk past that."

He turned and went back along the hedge toward his house, and Emeric stayed at the edge of the bare ground in the frost, looking at the line across the strip where the green stopped, until the cold came up through his boots.



On the last school morning of November the exchange had every trade in it at once, because Sela had decided that the households should see, once, what they were not paying for, and had told them to come.

He had not been given a bench, and he had not asked for one. He stood in the doorway and watched.

Sela had the letters first, as always, the whole room at once, a hundred and thirty voices saying the same sound off the same sheet. Then the room broke up. Ferra took the oldest dozen to the back benches with slates and made them add a column of the barony's real numbers, the granary's, and they got it wrong, and she showed them where, and they got it right. Abloh had eight at a bench by the door with a crock of lye and an egg. Imani had six round a bucket of water, scrubbing each other's hands with soap and a brush and then holding them up to her, palms out, while she looked at them. Bram had refused to come, on the grounds that he did not stand up in front of rooms, and had sent Tess, and Tess had ten in the corner with a lump of iron and a file and was showing them how to feel whether an edge was true with the side of a thumb. Armand had the four smallest on the floor round his chair, and a stone, and was telling them where it came from. Farris was in the far corner with a bucket of soil, answering a great many questions about worms.

When the sand ran out in Sela's glass she clapped once, and every group got up and moved one place round the room, Ferra's dozen to Abloh's crock and Abloh's eight to Imani's bucket, and it took half the next glass to get them sitting again.

Ward had the yard outside, and the sound of children falling down in it came in through the high windows every few minutes, followed each time by laughing.

It was loud. It was the loudest room in Kaldo, with the mothers along the back wall talking over all of it. Emeric stood in the doorway with his shoulder on the frame and listened, and all morning he did not think about copper or wire or the hill.



He found the column that evening.

He had not been looking for it. He was at Ferra's table with the month's figures, and she had gone out to the forge with a question for Bram about the price of charcoal, and left her book on the table in front of her chair, open at the back.

The back of the book was not the barony's.

A column had been ruled down the page, with dates in it, a line for each day. It started at the end of October, the week Grayrock said no. Beside each date, in her small plain hand, there were a few words. Not many. She never used many.

Slept four, he says.

Hand shaking on the pen. Hid it.

Slept four.

Headache to noon. Zinc.

Short with Abloh. Abloh shorter.

Slept five, he says. Lamp in the dyehouse at two.

It went on down the page and over onto the next, three weeks of it, every day of them. Tess's jar was there, and the hazel for the hoops, and the clover, and the night he had done the season's column three times and got three answers, and every headache, and every hour of sleep he had admitted to and some he had not. The last thing written was on the day he had gone to the mines for the week, and it said only Mines, a week. Under it the dates went on, one to a line, every day since, down to today's, and there was nothing beside any of them.

She had gone on writing the dates.

He read it all. Then he put his hands flat on the table and did not touch the book.

The door opened. Ferra came in from the lane with the cold on her, her face pinched pale by it so the freckles stood out, and charcoal dust on one sleeve, and stopped, and saw where he was looking.

She did not say anything. She came to the table and sat down and pulled the book across to her side of it, and her fingers were cold against his, and she left it open. She did not explain it, and she did not apologize for it.

She picked up her pen. She found today's date, at the bottom of the column, and wrote beside it, and he read it upside down, as he had read her books since the first summer.

Home. Found it.

Then she turned back to the front of the book, to the barony's columns, and went on with the price of charcoal.
