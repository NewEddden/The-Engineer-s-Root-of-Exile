Chapter 116 - Fitting Out

Fletcher's letter came up the river while the forge was on Rowena's breastplate. It was one side of a sheet, as all his letters were, and it said that the cabins were done, and that his six were standing about with planes in their hands, and what else did his highness want in her before she went wherever she was going.

Emeric wrote back that he would come down himself. Before he went, there were two things to do in Kaldo.

The first was the cook.

Her name was Maren, and she had come off Ferra's census the way Bram's apprentices had once come off it, a page read twice and nothing on it that anybody could take hold of. She ran the barony's kitchen, the one that fed every crew in Kaldo out of the same pots, the drainage men and the foundry hands and the garrison. She was a broad, quick woman of forty with burn scars on both forearms, and she came up to the long room in her apron because she had not been told to take it off.

She was sworn in the long room, with Ward to hear it and Ferra to write it down, and she said the words back without stumbling. Then Emeric told her what the ship did.

She listened to all of it. When he had finished she thought about it for a moment, the way a woman thinks about an order for two hundred instead of a hundred and fifty.

"Does an oven work up there?"

"It will when there is one," he said. "That's why you're coming down to the lower water."

He gave her a sheaf of recipes before she went. He had written them out over three evenings in his own hand: bread that would rise in a ship's galley, a stew that held for three days, a pudding that could be steamed on the back of a range, a dozen more, with the quantities for eleven and the timings beside each step. Maren read the first two standing at the table. Then she looked up at him over the sheets.

"In my experience," she said, "the nobility don't touch this sort of thing. It's peasant work." She said it drily, and not unkindly, the way she would have told a garrison boy his hands were dirty. "I didn't know his highness had ever been nearer a kitchen than the far end of a table." She turned a sheet. "But somebody's stood over a pot with these. Nobody writes rest it a quarter of an hour who hasn't watched it fall flat for want of one."

He did not say anything. There was nothing he could say about where he had learned it that was true and that he could say, and so he said neither, and let it sit.

Maren looked at him a moment longer. Then she folded the recipes in half, and in half again, and put them in the pocket of her apron, and went back to her kitchen, and he could see that she had filed it.

The second thing was Ferra's.

"What do you mean to wear," she said, "in a queen's hall?"

He opened his mouth and found he had no answer. He had come south in travelling clothes and the heavy coat Varek gave him at the banquet, and in ten years in Kaldo he had not once owned a thing a court would recognise. He wore what the foundry wore and what the yard wore and, on the days he went to Wayford, what a baron's steward wore. None of it would stand in Emberfell's hall.

So a tailor on the quay street took his measure the next morning, in his shop, among bolts of cloth that had never before been cut for anybody higher than a Milford merchant's widow. Saban went looking for better cloth than the shop had. The tailor looked at the drawing Emeric had made of what he wanted, which was a court coat of the kind he remembered from the capital, cut long and closed high, and said that a coat like that was weeks of hand work if it was going to be done right, and that he would want a fortnight.

"Then you've got a fortnight," Emeric said.

Fletcher asked the question on the beach before he would show them anything.

He stood at the foot of her ladder with his arms folded, the way he had stood on the stocks, and Emeric knew what it was going to be before he said it, because it was the first thing he had asked on the stocks too.

"What does it weigh?" Fletcher said. "Whatever you're going to put in her."

"Less than what she was built to carry."

"Show me."

It was Ferra who showed him. She had come down the river with them, with her book and a set of scales the size of a cart's bed that Bram's men had carried up to the top of the beach and set level on planks, and she gave it to Fletcher the way she gave a figure to a creditor.

"You built her to sleep seventeen," she said. "Sixteen berths and a bed. Every station she has was sized for her full: her tanks, her bunkers, and a man in every berth. We are carrying eleven." She turned her book so that he could see it. "Six empty berths is six men who are not aboard, with their gear, and ten days of their water. Call it four hundred and fifty pounds a man, if he eats well. Two thousand seven hundred pounds. A ton and a third." She closed the book. "Everything we put in her goes over that scale first. When it comes to a ton and a third, we stop."

Fletcher looked at the scale on its planks at the top of the beach, and at her, and nodded slowly.

"And if she ever sails seventeen?"

"Then something comes out."

"Pine," said Fletcher, to himself, already turning toward her. "Felt. Tin sheet. No oak. No lead. No iron that doesn't have to be iron." He stopped with one hand on the ladder. "And no bath."

Emeric had asked him for a bath in the letter. He had asked for a tub a man could lie down in, because he had not lain down in hot water since he left the capital.

"A tub a man can lie down in," said Fletcher, "holds two men's weight of water. You'd fill it for everybody aboard once a day, and her tanks would be dry by the second morning." He went up the ladder. "Water's the heaviest thing she carries. It always is, in any ship. Wash in the sea like the rest of us."

That was the end of the bath.

They were down four days, Emeric and Ferra and Ward and Bram, and then they went home again, because the six would take two weeks at the carpentry and there was nothing in two weeks of sawing that needed a prince standing over it. Fletcher had the drawings. The six had their orders, which were boxes and pipes and doors and housings in the places marked, and slots in some of the housings, and nothing in the slots. They looked at the slots and asked nothing about them, because Fletcher had told them once in the summer to mind their planes, and they had.

Tobe kept the forge fed while Bram was gone. It finished Rowena's suit in the second week and started Rylan's, and nobody touched it but to put a billet in the cup at dusk.

When the six had gone up the beach for the last time and the yard was quiet, the four of them went down again, with Orrin, and the stones in a crate between Emeric and Bram, and seated them, and Fletcher walked them round her.

He did it quickly, because he had walked them round her once already, in the autumn, room by room, and what he wanted to show was what was new.

The room under the roof had been a room since Fletcher put the roof on. Now it was a room people could live in, and his six had started calling it the day room, and the name had stuck.

It ran from her stem back to the stack, under the curve of the shell, with the ports along both sides and the helm and the levers forward in the middle of it, and Fletcher's six had lined it. They had put felt over the bare iron wherever anybody would sit or lean or sleep, because iron under a roof in the band sweats and then freezes, and a man who puts his cheek to it at the wrong moment leaves some of the cheek. There were benches along both sides under the ports, with lockers beneath them. There was a chart table beside the helm with a lamp over it in a cage. And on the forward bulkhead, where the shell came down toward the bow, there was a shelf with a rail along its front, and on it a dozen books off the printer: primers for Orrin's school, mostly, and the eleven-line well standard, and the barony's measures, bound in boards at the finishing shop. They were the first printed books ever to leave Kaldo, and they were going to Emberfell as a present.

Ward went along the ports one at a time.

He did not say what he was doing. He stood at each port for as long as it took, and looked out of it, forward and back, and down, and then went to the next. Emeric watched him do every one of them, and understood that Ward was working out what a man could see from each of them and what he could not, and where a man standing at one could be seen from outside, and he did not ask why. Ward was the Secretary of War. He did not have to say why.

At the helm Fletcher stopped.

"I'll not steer a hull I can't see the front of," he said.

It had been in the letter, among other things, and Emeric had not understood it until now, standing at the wheel. Under the iron shell, with nothing but the side ports, a man at the wheel could see to either side of her and could not see ahead at all. At sea that was how a ship ran down a fishing boat in the dark and never knew she had. On the way down onto a harbour it was worse.

Fletcher's answer was above the wheel. His six had cut a hole in the shell, round, the width of a man's shoulders, and over the hole Fletcher had built a low dome of the glassworks' glass in iron frames, riveted and stayed, with shutters on the inside that closed down over the panes like a set of iron eyelids. A man at the wheel could look up and forward through it and see her bow, and past the bow, the sea.

"It's the first thing that's stood out of her since the roof went on," Fletcher said. He said it before anybody could say it to him. "I built you a shell with nothing standing out of it for the wind to get hold of. Now there's that." He looked up at it. "It stands a hand's width. The wind can have that much."

Aft of the stack her deck was open, as it always had been: the companion down to the saloon, the skylight over the saloon, the hatch over the hold, and between the stack and the hatch her boat, still turned over on its chocks. Fletcher had put two davits at her rail beside the boat, so that she could go over the side in the dark without a crowd on the falls. He did not show them off. He only put his hand on one, and said, as he had said in the autumn, that every hull he built carried her own boat.

The open deck was the trouble. Nobody could stand on it at speed. So getting from the room under the roof to the saloon aft had meant going out onto it, and in the band that was not a thing anybody would want to do twice.

So the six had cut doors.

They had cut one through each of the iron walls below her deck, low down, where the passage ran from the galley aft past the boiler and the engine and the hold to the saloon. Each door was an oval of iron plate on heavy hinges, with a raised sill a man had to step over, and six dogs round its edge on the inside that screwed down and pulled it tight against a gasket.

Fletcher had not wanted to cut them. He had built four walls and five rooms so that the sea could have one room and no more, and he said so again, standing at the first of the doors with his hand on its frame. Then he swung it shut and put the dogs down one by one, and pulled on it, and it did not move.

"Shut and dogged," he said, "the wall's whole. Open, it's a door." He looked at Emeric. "They're shut at sea. All of them. Every time. I'll want that in your book, not mine."

Ferra wrote it in hers.

In the air the aft companion and the skylight and the hatch over the hold would be dogged shut too, and the saloon and the cabins would be sealed rooms. The veins' thicker air would come aft to them in ducts behind the panelling, from the twig over the helm that had fed the room under the roof since the autumn.

The ducts carried the warmth as well.

Off the twig the air divided three ways: forward into the day room, aft to the saloon and its four cabins, and aft again to the stern cabin. At the head of each run a fire stone sat in a housing with an air stone to push, and the ducts ran behind the panelling. In the day room and the saloon and the stern cabin they ran under the floorboards first, and came up through a grating at the far end, so that the floor was warm before the air ever reached anybody. It was the oldest trick Emeric knew for warming a room, air under the floor, only it was done with a stone instead of a furnace, and it weighed less than water in pipes would have.

On the wall of each of those three rooms was a dial.

It was a brass face the size of a hand, with a pointer and a scale scratched on it, and behind it a house cell and a small reading stone watching a strip of brass and iron riveted together. Brass and iron do not swell alike in the heat. As the room warmed, the strip bent. The reading stone watched it bend, and the house's deciding stone, sitting in its housing forward, fed heat or cold down the duct to that room until the strip came to the mark on the dial. One way was warmer. The other way was cooler. Off was in the middle.

"And when the stone goes?" said Fletcher. He had asked it of everything.

"Then it's a knob." Emeric turned it. "More heat or less, by hand, and the man in the room decides. It's worse. It still works."

"And when all of it goes?"

"There are blankets in the lockers under every bench," Emeric said. "They go last."

The four cabins took the saloon's air and the saloon's dial. The galley and the crew's berths forward had never needed one. The range kept them warm, as Fletcher had built them to be kept, and as he had pointed out in the autumn, the men who worked her ought to be warm.

The range was still there in the galley, iron and black, with its rail round the top and its copper at the back.

"It stays," Emeric said, before Fletcher could ask. "It cooks with every stone in her dead."

Beside it, on an iron shelf of its own, there was a hob and a small oven, and neither had a fire in it. Under the hob two fire stones sat in their housings. Under the oven there was a third. Beside each was a splinter of crystal, and one small grey reading stone watched all three, and the house's deciding stone was told one thing about them, which was to keep each splinter at the scratch its brass pointer was set to. The pointers moved along brass plates by hand. It was the rig that had taken Orrin off his stool at the glass furnace in the summer, made small. The cook set the pointer where she wanted the heat, and the stone held it there.

It would be hot in the time it took to say so. It would hold a heat an oven could bake to. And it would cook in a foreign harbour with no fire in her and no smoke from her stack.

Everything in the galley and the dials and the ducts ran on one rack of cells, forward, in its own locker. Two dozen of them, apart from the rack in the hold that held her up. Orrin would fill them.

Emeric had written a rule over the page in the notebook where he drew the house, and he read it to Fletcher, because Fletcher would be the man at her wheel if it was ever broken.

"Nothing that cooks her dinner touches what holds her up."

Fletcher thought about it.

"Good," he said.

He had changed his mind about the boiler, and he told Fletcher so at the firebox door, because Fletcher had heard the other thing from him first.

Beside the grate, set into the firebox wall, were four fire stones in iron housings, with a reading stone looking at the steam gauge and a deciding stone told to keep steam at a mark. It was the hob's rig made large. It had its own bank of cells, off in its own locker, with their necks in the vein like the rack in the hold, so that it drank in the band with everything else.

"You said coal was cheap and cells weren't," Fletcher said. "On this beach. In the autumn."

"I did. It was true then." Emeric shut the firebox door. "Since the band, every time she flies, every cell in her fills. Ten minutes of steaming on stones before a lift costs less than she takes back in the first half hour up there. The cells aren't dear any more. They're full."

Fletcher looked at the housings for some time, and tried to find the hole in it, and could not. Emeric watched him try.

"And the coal?"

"Stays in her. It's never burned, so it's always there. When every stone in her's dead, these go dark with the rest, and the grate's what moves her." He put his hand on the grate door. "The same as you built her to."

"She'll steam with no smoke," Fletcher said slowly.

"Yes."

Fletcher did not like that. Emeric could see him not liking it. He had built hulls for forty years and come to steam late, and loved it the way a man loves a thing he came to late, and he had watched a thing he built go up into the sky over the sea, and it was a ship making steam with nothing coming out of her chimney that he found hardest to look at.

The rest of the house was plain, and he meant it to be.

Hot water came from a copper tank on a fire stone of its own, piped to the galley sink and both heads, and when the stone went, the copper at the back of the range was still there. Cold water came up the pipes pushed by a water stone, and when the stone went, a header tank on the deck beams fed the pipes by its own weight, weaker but still coming. There were proper showers in both heads now, a brass rose on a pipe over a grating. Fletcher's rule for them was every sailor's: wash in the sea, rinse in the fresh, and the tanks would be filled again at Emberfell. The heads flushed with the hand pumps from the autumn into the tank under them, as they always had, and there was nothing in them to fail.

The berths had horsehair mattresses on the boards now, and a curtain on a rail across every one, and a cover that closed over every lamp cage, so that a cabin could be made dark in the middle of a bright day two miles up.

Imani had sent her list down the river in her own hand.

A chest for her things, lashed to the saloon bulkhead so that it could not move in a sea. A table that folded down from the bulkhead of one of the cabins on two chains and would take a man lying flat, which Fletcher's six had built and which Fletcher had tried himself, lying on it on his back with his boots on while one of the six worked the cabin door against him to see what room there was. And at the top of the list, above the other two, underlined: *cork jackets for everyone aboard who cannot fly. Eight.*

Three people aboard would be wearing a cape and a chute. Eight would not. She had done the count. Under it, smaller, in the same hand, she had written a note to herself from the day in the steamboat: *plate sinks.* And under that, in Saban's hand, where Imani had sent the list to him first, was the name of a chandler in Milford who had cork.

The cold room was Maren's asking, and then Imani's.

Maren could feed eleven people for a week on salt meat and biscuit. She had said so, standing in the store beside her new galley with her arms folded. And by the third day they would be sick of her, and she would be sick of them, and it would be a long week. Imani's note had been shorter. She wanted cold for more than meat. She wanted it for a fever that would not come down, and for a wound that was swelling.

"She has a cold locker," said Fletcher.

She did. It was low down against her plating in the store, below where the waterline came, and it was cold to the hand on any day of the year.

"The sea keeps it cool," Emeric said. "Not cold. And where she's going, the water comes down off a mountain that smokes."

Fletcher looked at him. He had asked in the autumn where she was going, and been told he would know when there was something true to tell.

"Emberfell," Emeric said. "Along the coast, as far as the coasting boats go. We fly at midwinter."

Fletcher took it the way he took a price, and said nothing, and went on looking at the locker.

So the six had built a box inside the store, round Fletcher's sea locker and not in place of it, because Fletcher would not have his locker taken out of her. It had double walls of pine, a hand apart, packed between with sawdust and charcoal, and it was lined inside with tinned sheet. Its door hung on a felt seal and shut with a lever latch that pulled it tight. Inside it was two rooms, one over the other: a bunker at the top, and the food below it, because cold falls. The shelves had a rail along each. A pipe ran from the floor of the bunker down to her bilge.

There were three kinds of cold in it, and Emeric showed Fletcher each in the order they would fail.

The first was ice. The bunker at the top would hold a load of it, packed in sawdust, and with the door kept shut a full bunker would hold for most of a week, even in a warm port. There was no stone in it at all. When every stone in her was dead it would still be a box of ice.

The second was the band. A second twig came off the starboard vein, and a narrow iron channel ran from it through the wall of the bunker and out again. Up in the band the air in her veins was cold enough to stop a hand. Pans of water set in the bunker would freeze.

"She'll make her own ice," Fletcher said, when he had seen where the channel went. "Every time she flies."

"Every time she flies."

The third was for the week in port, in the heat, down below the band, where there would be no band to make ice and the ice she had would only melt. Orrin was watching when Emeric came to it, and Orrin asked what any mage in the barony would have asked.

"Why not just a stone that makes cold?"

"Because I've never seen one," Emeric said, "and Saban's never seen one for sale. As far as I know, you can't make cold. You can only carry heat out of one place and put it down in another, and you pay for the carrying." He put his hand on the thing he had built, which was an iron bottle the size of a small cask, and a coil of iron pipe, and a cylinder no bigger than a man's forearm. "This carries it."

He explained it to Bram, because Bram was the one who would have to mend it.

"Work a pump barrel hard," he said, "and it gets hot."

"It does."

"Because squeezed air is hot air. So." He touched the bottle. "An air stone off the house bank squeezes air into this, hard. It gets hot. The coil's down against her plating below the waterline, where the sea locker is, so the heat of the squeezing goes out through her side into the sea. Now the air in the bottle's squeezed, and it's no warmer than the sea." He moved his hand to the cylinder. "Then you let it out through here, pushing a piston, the way steam pushes the engine. Air that's done work comes out colder than it went in. A good deal colder. And it goes into the bunker."

Bram looked at the bottle and the coil and the cylinder for a long time.

"It's the engine," he said. "Run backwards."

"Near enough."

Bram looked a while longer, and then put his finger on the rod that came out of the cylinder and ran to a small pump on the bulkhead.

"And that works the water for the coil," he said. "So it pays for some of itself."

Emeric had been going to show him that. He did not need to.

It was governed by a small dish of water on a shelf in the bunker, with a reading stone looking at it, and a deciding stone told one rule: when the dish is not ice, run. The dish was the thermometer. Nothing else was needed.

It had a cost, and he told it to Orrin, because it was Orrin's. Below the band nothing filled the house bank but a man's hands, and in port that would be Orrin, every evening, all week. Orrin had known that was coming since the day he was asked aboard, and he said now that it would be less than the glass furnace and more than a lamp, and that he would want the cushion off the saloon bench to sit on while he did it.

There were two of the cold engines. One served the bunker. The other served the ducts to the rooms, and was the reason the dials had a cooler side at all. Either one could hold the cold room if the other failed, so that of everything in her the food would be the last thing to warm. At Emberfell, in the heat, it would be the second one that the whole ship leaned on.

Ferra had the count of stones by the evening, and she read it out as a figure, sitting at the saloon table with her book.

About twenty in the house, and one deciding stone over all of them, and two dozen cells in the house bank to feed them. And apart from all of those, on a bank of its own, the boiler's four fire stones and its reading stone and its deciding stone.

Then she read out what each of them fell back on when it failed, because Emeric had given her that too, and it was a longer list than the first. The boiler on the grate and the coal. The hob and oven on the range. The hot water on the range's copper. The pressure on the header tank. The cold room on the band, and then the ice, and the two engines on each other. The dials on their own knobs. The warmth on the blankets.

"And what doesn't have one?" she said.

"A second heater," Emeric said. "It'd want a coal stove aft, and a flue up through the deck, and a fire under a sealed room. That's a refit, not a fitting. And a second water tank, because it wants new frames under it." He did not pretend otherwise. "If the heat goes aft, it's the blankets. If the water goes, it's what's in the one tank."

Ferra wrote that down as well.

Maren came down the river on the last day, in the boat that brought the stores for the night, with her apron in a bundle and his recipes in her pocket.

They lit the range, and shut the galley door on it, so that the galley was the warmest room in her, and they put a dish of water on a shelf in the cold room, with nothing in the bunker, and shut its door on it. It was a mild night, wet, with the wind in the south off the sea, and well above any frost.

Maren cooked the first meal ever made aboard her, on the new hob, from the first of his recipes.

She burned the first thing she put on it. It was onions, in the heavy pan, and the stone under it was hot before she had finished cutting the second one, because it was hot the moment the pointer moved, and she was a woman who had spent six years cooking on a fire that took a quarter of an hour to come up. She said a word Emeric had not heard from anybody in the barony but Declan, and scraped the pan, and started again, and after that she did not burn anything. Then she baked, in the small oven, because the oven held a heat she could bake to, and the bread came out right.

They ate at the saloon table, the one he had drawn the size of the long room's table because it was the only size of table he had ever needed. Fletcher sat at it with them, at the end, with his hands on either side of his plate. It was the first meal anybody had ever eaten at it.

In the morning the dish on the shelf in the cold room was ice.

The range was still warm through the wall.

The bill came up the river after them on a sheet of its own, in Fletcher's hand: pine and felt, tinned sheet, horsehair, curtain cloth, copper pipe, the cork from Milford, the glass and the iron for the dome, the doors and their dogs, the iron for the cold engines, and six men's fortnight. Seven Gold Rand and one Silver. The stones were on the etching floor's count and not on it.

Ferra paid it. In the autumn her margin beside Fletcher's bill had said *Rooms*. This time it said *Dinner*.

On the last morning, before they went up the river, Fletcher had stood in the store in front of the cold room with its door open and his arms folded, looking at the dish of ice on its shelf.

"A mountain that smokes," he said. He looked at the ice. "And you're taking it ice."

He meant it as a joke. Emeric did not laugh, because it was not one.
