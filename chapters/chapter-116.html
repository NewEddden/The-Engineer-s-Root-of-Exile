Chapter 116 - A Matter

Ferra packed the boxes herself, on the night of the eighth of May, in her room on the second floor of the keep, and would not let anyone help, and then let Ward carry them down the stair because they were too heavy for her, and walked behind him all the way down with one hand out as if he might drop one.

There were three. Plain deal boxes with rope handles and lids that Bram had fitted with hasps, and in the hasps, padlocks, and Ferra had both keys to all three on a string round her neck under her coat, and Emeric had the seconds, because that was the rule now.

In the first box, the index. The long narrow book of the twin year, in her hand from its first line to its last. Fifty-one lodgment receipts, each a slip of the crown post's own paper in the gate clerk's hand, dated and timed, in the order they had been issued, tied in bundles of ten with tape. Fifty-one of the coast house's receipts for the twins, on the House's paper, and the House's capital office's dates for each, copied fair and sealed by the House's clerk there, which had come down in April by Vantic's line, the last of them a week ago, and which Vantic had handed across Ferra's trestle without a word and watched Ferra check against the index line by line, and then gone away.

In the second box, Bram's die. The barony's seal, cut new in the second year, with the nick at the shoulder of the tower that no forger casting off a good impression would think to copy, and beside it, in a smaller box of its own with a label in Bram's square hand, every die the barony had used since Wexler, dated, so that any letter of Kaldo's that ever turned up could be shown to be sealed with this one or that one, and when. Hale's dispatch book: every letter that had left the barony for the crown since Emeric came, with its date and its road. The November report, the barony's own copy, the one that had two pages about Milford in it.

And in the third box, on top, by itself, Paxton's four lines.

Ferra had held that one in her hand for a while before she put it in. Emeric had seen her do it, from the door. Then she laid it flat on top of everything else, the right way up, and shut the lid on it.



They went in the carriage.

It was the carriage he had come south in, four years ago, in the first April, with a poisoned cup behind him and a forest in front of him and somebody's plan for the ninth night waiting in it. It had sat in the barony's coach house since, under a sheet, except for the winter it had stood in the coach house without its springs while its stones worked the hill's beam and failed to keep a drowned mine dry. It had its springs back. Four gravity stones, one at each corner, where on any other carriage in the kingdom there would have been leaf springs of steel, and on any other carriage there would have been a jolt at every rut in the south road for twelve days.

The boxes went inside, under the seat. Ferra went inside with them. Ward rode on the right of it and Emeric on the left, and Declan behind with two of the garrison in Bram's new black plate under their cloaks, crestless, looking exactly like what they were meant to look like, which was three hired men guarding a broke lord's coach. A garrison man drove.

They went out of the South Gate on the morning of the ninth, at a walk, and turned north.

By the end of the first day Ferra had worked out the springs.

She had not worked out what they were. She had worked out that something was wrong. Emeric saw her do it, through the carriage window, at the ford below the woods, where the road went down a bank of loose stones into the water and up the other side, and the wheels went down and up and through, and the carriage on its stones went over it like a boat over a swell. Ferra, inside, with the barony's book open on her knee and the pen in her hand, did not lift the pen from the page. She looked at the pen. She looked out of the window at the ford, going away behind them. Then she looked at Emeric, riding beside the window.

"Wexler's carriage bounced," she said.

"Wexler's carriage had steel springs."

"And this one has?"

"Good ones," said Emeric.

Ferra looked at him for a little longer through the window. Then she looked down at the page, at the line she had been writing, which had gone straight across a ford without a wobble in it.

"They're very good," she said, and went on writing, and he saw the corner of her mouth go and stop, and did not know whether she had guessed something or only decided not to ask, and suspected that with Ferra those had always been the same thing.



On the sixth day they came to Wayford, and went through it, and did not stop.

Ward had wanted to go round. There was a road round, a day's ride to the east on bad tracks through the hills, that came back to the south road above the town. He had laid it out on the table in the hall a week before they left, with Saban's map beside it, Saban's map from the room drawn out on paper by Saban himself in a hand nobody could read but Saban, and shown them where Sethe's three companies were sitting in their three halls, a day's ride from his own, and where the south road went past each.

"He knows we are coming," Ward had said. "He reads our post."

"Not this," Emeric had said. "Nothing about this has gone by the post. It's gone by Fletcher and by the House's courier and by Saban's mouth. Sethe knows Kaldo writes to the crown every week. He doesn't know Kaldo's going to the crown." He had looked at the map. "And if we go round, and he hears we went round, he'll know we were frightened of something, and wonder what. If we go through at a walk on market day like a broke lord going to beg at court, he'll see a broke lord going to beg at court."

Ward had not liked it. He had not argued. He had put Declan and the two men in their black plate under their cloaks, and done nothing else, and Emeric had found out on the road that the two men had crossbows under the cloaks as well, and bolts, and that Ward had changed his own sword for the one Bram made him in the winter, and said nothing about either.

Wayford was a town on a river with a great house on a hill above it, and on the market day it was full of carts. They came down the hill road into it at noon at a walk and went along its main street at a walk, between the stalls, with the carriage swaying on its stones, and nobody looked at them more than people look at any coach with an escort, which is once, to see if there is anybody worth looking at in it, and then not again.

The road went down to the river at the bottom of the town, where the ferry was.

And at the head of the slope down to the ferry, a square stone house, older than the inns on either side of it, with the crown's arms worn nearly smooth over the door, and a back door, and a window.

Ferra was at the carriage window. He saw her see it. She had come through Wayford once before, four years ago, on the road to Keller, and gone past this house without a glance, because then it had been nothing. She had seen it since in the room, in glass, in Saban's hand, small, a long way off on the floor of the room above Kaldo, and she knew it. She looked at it as they went past at a walk, the square stone house with its door standing open and mailbags going in at it and a man in the doorway in his shirtsleeves, middle-aged, heavy, with a ledger under his arm, talking to a carter. Looking up at the coach as it went by, the way anyone looks up at a coach. And down again.

The window was at the end of the house, beside the back door. There was a lamp in it, unlit, in the daylight.

Ferra did not move her head as they went by. She did not look away either. She looked at the man in the doorway with the ledger under his arm until the carriage was past it and the house was behind them, and then she sat back from the window into the dark of the carriage, and Emeric, riding beside her, saw that her hand had closed on the pen in her lap so hard that the knuckles had gone white.

He did not say anything. Past the town, where the road climbed out of the river valley and the town fell away behind, she opened the window again and put her head out into the air, and breathed, and pulled it in again.

"Twenty-one items," she said. To nobody. To the road. "In two years. And fifty-one since." She shut the window. "He looked at us."

"He looks at every coach."

"I know," said Ferra. "That's what I mean."



The forest past Wayford was the one where somebody had meant to kill him, on the ninth night, four years ago.

He had not been back through it since the August of the first year. He rode into it on the seventh day with his hand near his sword and Ward closer to him on the other side of the carriage than Ward usually rode, and the trees came down on both sides of the road, old and tall and dark, beech and oak with the new leaves on them so that the road went along a green tunnel, and nothing happened. Birds. A woodcutter's cart going the other way, with a boy on it who stared at Declan's black plate where his cloak had blown back, and went on staring until the cart was round the bend.

In the middle of the forest the near carriage horse went lame.

It did it all at once, as horses do, between one stride and the next: a hitch in its walk, and then a nod of its head at every step on the off fore, and the garrison man on the box swore and pulled up. Ward was off his horse with his sword out before the carriage had stopped and standing at its door with his back to it, looking into the trees. Declan's two men had their cloaks back and their crossbows up. For the length of a long breath nobody did anything in the green road but listen.

Nothing. Birds.

"It is a horse," said Ward, after a while. He did not put his sword up.

"It's a horse," said Emeric.

He got down. He went to the near horse's head and spoke to it, which he had learned from Rylan on the road the first time, and ran his hand down its off fore from the shoulder to the hoof, slowly, the way Rylan had shown him, feeling for heat. There was none in the leg. He picked up the foot. The horse leaned on him, as horses do, all of it, and he leaned back, and looked.

A stone. A flint the size of a thumbnail, wedged in hard between the shoe and the frog, so that the horse had been walking on it every step since the last ford. He took his knife and worked it out, carefully, with the horse's weight on his shoulder and its breath on the back of his neck. It came out with a click and he threw it in the ditch and put the foot down.

The horse stood on it. It put its head down and blew, and stood square.

He straightened up, with his knife in his hand and his shoulder aching and horse all down the front of his coat, and found Ferra looking at him out of the carriage window.

She was leaning on the sill with her chin on her folded arms. He did not know how long she had been watching. He had been bent double under a horse with his back to her.

"You did that very well," she said.

"It's a stone in a hoof."

"I know what it is. My father shoes horses." She did not move her chin off her arms. "I've watched princes in this forest before. Through that window. In a coach. When I was nine, the old king's sister came south to see the sea, and she came through Kaldo, and her horse threw a shoe in the square, and four men stood round it for an hour arguing about whose business it was." She considered him. "You've got horse on your coat."

"I know."

"It's a good coat."

"It was," said Emeric.

Ferra looked at him a little longer. Then she pulled her head in, and he heard her open the book again, and after a moment the scratch of the pen.

Ward had put his sword up. He was standing by his own horse looking at Emeric with no expression whatever.

"What?" said Emeric.

"Nothing," said Ward. "Mount up. Highness."



Keller had moved. He was not in the chamber on the second floor of the court building any more, the small cold one with the window on the courtyard where Emeric had stood in his first August and again in the winter after the rite. He was a floor higher, in a room with two windows and a fire and a long table with nothing on it, and a clerk at a smaller table in the corner, and he was greyer than he had been at the turn of the year, and he did not stand.

He looked at the three boxes as Ward and Declan set them down on the long table, one after another. He looked at Ferra, who came in behind them and took the keys off the string round her neck and opened the three padlocks without a word and stood back.

"In what capacity," said Keller, "does this lady attend?"

"Chancellor of the barony of Kaldo," said Ferra.

Keller looked at her. He did not ask her name. He turned to his clerk.

"Present," he said. "The holder of Kaldo. The barony's Chancellor. A knight of the holder's household." He did not look at Ward. "Write it so." The clerk wrote it so. Keller turned back to the boxes. "You have brought me a year."

"Fifty-one weeks," said Emeric. "A year, near enough, from the first lodgment to the last."

"Dated, paired, attested."

"Dated, paired, attested."

"I said nothing of the kind," said Keller. "I struck it." He put out his hand. "Begin."

Ferra began.

She did not explain anything. She had told Emeric on the road, on the third night, at an inn in the hills, with the boxes under the bed and Ward asleep across the door: that Keller did not want to be told what a thing meant, he wanted the thing, in order, and he would work out what it meant himself and resent anybody who did it for him. So she gave him the things in order. The index first, open at the first page, with the line for the first week. Then the first bundle of lodgment slips, untied, the first slip on top. Then the coast house's receipt for the first twin. Then the House's capital office's date for it, under the House's seal. Then she stood back.

Keller read them.

He read all of them. He read the first week, and then the second, and the third, slip by slip, receipt by receipt, every one, with his finger going across from the index to the slip to the coast house to the House and back to the index, and nobody in the room said anything, and the fire burned down and the clerk got up without being told and built it up again.

He asked his first question at the ninth week.

"The gate clerk's slip," he said. "For this one. It is timed at a quarter before ten. The others are timed at the hour, or the half." He did not look up. "Why?"

"The steward was late," said Ferra. "It had rained all night and he fell in the mud outside the post house and went home to change his stockings before he lodged it. He told me. I wrote it in the margin of the index." She put her finger on the margin. "There. The gate clerk will remember, if you ask him. He laughed."

Keller looked at the margin. He went on.

He asked a dozen questions in three hours. Nine of them were about the receipts. Why this slip was on a different paper, and Ferra showed him that the post house had run out of its own for a week in July and used the town clerk's, and that the town clerk had written to say so, and here was the letter. Why the coast house had receipted one twin two days late, and Ferra showed him the storm. Why the House's capital office had dated two twins on the same day, and Ferra showed him the courier's bag, which had been held at a flooded ford and come in with both. He asked one about the die, and Emeric answered it, and showed him the nick under the clerk's lamp, and Keller looked at it for a long while through a glass and said nothing. He asked one about the dispatch book, the old one, Hale's, and why there was a gap in the second year, and Ferra said that was the war, and Keller said, yes, and did not ask any more about it.

He asked one about the line on the fifteenth of November with nothing in its right-hand column.

Ferra told him. The report. The two pages about Milford. The twin in the middle of a bale of Paxton's fleece in Dunnel's cart. And then she took Paxton's four lines out of the third box, flat, and laid them on the table in front of him, and stood back.

Keller read them.

He read them twice. Then he put one finger on the first line, the twenty-second of November, and looked back at the index, at the fifteenth, and Emeric watched him count. He did not count on his fingers. He did it behind his face, the way Ward did roads, and Emeric saw him get to the end of it and stop.

"Seven days," said Keller.

"Seven days from our lodgment," said Ferra. "We don't know when it reached the crossing. Three days at post pace, the post rider says. If it was read the day it came, four days from the crossing." She did not add anything. She did not say what it meant.

Keller did not say what it meant either.

He took his finger off Paxton's sheet. He looked at it for a while longer. Then he put it back on the table, square, the way Ferra had laid it, and turned to his clerk.

"The register," he said.

The clerk brought it. It was a great book, bound in black, nearly as long as the table was wide, and he set it down in front of Keller and opened it at a page near the end, where the last line written was in Keller's own small hand, and laid a pen beside it.

Keller dipped the pen.

"I am opening a matter," he said.

He wrote in the register. Emeric could not read it from where he stood, and did not try. It was one line. A number, and a date, and a few words after the number, short. Keller blotted it. Then the clerk brought him a folder, a plain one of grey card, tied with tape, and Keller wrote the same number on the outside of it, and the date, and laid it on the table beside the register.

It was empty.

He did not put anything in it. He did not put the index in it, or the slips, or Paxton's four lines. He left it lying there, empty, with the number on it, and put the pen down, and looked up at Emeric for the first time since Ferra had opened the first box.

"That is all it is," Keller said. "A number in a book and an empty folder. It does nothing, yet. It means that when the next thing comes, it has somewhere to go." He looked at the three boxes. "Take your papers home. Keep them as you have kept them. If I want them I will send for them, by a road of my choosing, not yours." He looked at Ferra. "Chancellor."

"Magistrate."

"You kept this book yourself."

"Yes."

"Every line."

"Every line."

Keller nodded, once, and turned back to his register, and picked up his pen, which was the end of the interview, and held it without dipping it.



Ferra did not say anything on the stair, or in the courtyard, or in the street. She walked beside the carriage with her hand on the door while Ward and Declan put the boxes back under the seat, and when they were in she did not get in after them. She stood in the street outside the court with her hand on the carriage door and her face turned up to the sky, which was blue, and the swifts going over it screaming, and she breathed.

"An empty folder," she said.

"A number in a book."

"Four years." She did not look down. "Four years of letters. Twenty-one in the first two and fifty-one since. Dunnel. Ninety-one head. A barn." She was quiet. "And it's an empty folder."

"It's an empty folder with a number on it," said Emeric. "That's the most that's ever happened to Kaldo in this city. Keller said it himself. When the next thing comes, it has somewhere to go."

Ferra looked at him then.

"You're not disappointed," she said.

"No."

"You thought it'd be a folder."

"I thought it'd be less," said Emeric. "I thought he'd read for three hours and tell us to come back in a year with another year."

Ferra looked at him a little longer. Then she let go of the carriage door, and took the pen out of her coat, and the barony's book out from under her arm, and opened it on the carriage's step, there in the street outside the court, with the swifts going over and a carter swearing at them to move the coach, and wrote.

He read it upside down. She let him.

A date. The twenty-first of May. And in the middle, in her small upright hand: Matter opened, the crown's court, by the magistrate. And the number, which Keller had not said aloud and which she must have read off the folder upside down across the table, as he read her books, and had kept in her head ever since. And in the right-hand column, nothing.

She did not rule it this time. She left it open.

"There'll be a figure in it," she said, and closed the book. "One day."

She got into the carriage. Ward mounted. The garrison man on the box gathered his reins and looked round at Emeric for the word, and Emeric looked down the street toward the south gate of the city and the south road and the forest and Wayford and the stone house at the ferry with its lamp in the window, and Kaldo at the end of all of it, twelve days away, on its river, with a hull on three legs in a shed under the north wall and a tower full of water on the top of the town.

"Home," he said.
