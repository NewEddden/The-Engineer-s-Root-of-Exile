Chapter 381 - Gills

There were six of them on the bench in the Works' test shed by the end of January, in a row, in the order they'd been made, like the skulls of some animal that had been changing its mind about what it wanted to be.

The first was the box. A tin box the size of a bread bin with forty plates of pressed air-stone dust folded inside it and a hand pump on the side, the one they'd put in the Challenger's nose drum last February with two bags of stale air and Tess's new oxygen meter, and watched the needle climb to Imani's line and stop. Ugly, Ilenne had said of it, through Zale. But right.

The second was the same box on a man's back on canvas straps, with a hose to a mouthpiece, which had worked in the tank for eleven minutes and then rubbed a hole in Finn's shoulder through his shirt. The third had a shell, bucket steel, shaped to the back, and the gill plates in two cartridges either side like saddlebags, and it had worked for an hour and floated Finn upside down. The fourth had a sealed helm, the round flight-helm glass with the demand valve inside, and the shell lower and heavier to right him, and it had worked for three hours, and the helm had fogged. The fifth had everything in one piece and a pump-jet at the bottom of the shell, the cave dive's jet, so that swimming pulled the water through the plates and the faster you went the more you could breathe, and it had worked for six hours in the tank and come out of it, Imani said, very nearly a suit.

The sixth was the suit.

It hung on a frame at the end of the bench, on the second of February, a Monday, in the cold light from the shed's high windows, and Emeric stood in front of it with his hands in his coat pockets the way he'd stood in front of the decoy in the Well's hangar four days before, and thought that it looked very like the cave suit, and that this was the point.

The close black skin, rubber over the wool. The plate cut down to what a swimmer could carry, at the chest and the belly and the shoulders and the forearms, the knees and the shins. The round glass helm. A stone lit at the breastbone, faint blue, the air stone's colour, never the gravity violet. And on the back, close and flat, the pack: the shell, the two bags of the loop inside it, the lime canister, the little oxygen bottle, the two gill cartridges low on either side, the pump-jet's mouth under them. Nothing stuck out. The fifth had had a cartridge that stuck out, and Finn had caught it on the tank's ladder and torn its seal, and they'd learned.

Under the chin inside the helm there was a small square mirror, angled, and in the mirror three dials on the collar ring, read backwards, the way a pilot read his sight. Oxygen in the loop. The bottle. The stone's charge.

And on the back, over the pack, a broad stripe of white enamel paint, because a black suit in dark water was a man nobody could find.

"It's the same suit," said Bram, who'd come in behind him to look. "As the cave."

"It's the same suit. Grown up."

"You could've made it look like something."

"It does look like something," said Emeric. "It looks like the one that worked."

Bram grunted, which was approval, and went round the back of the frame to look at the pump-jet's mouth, where he'd fitted a guard of fine bucket-steel mesh that afternoon so that nothing in the sea could be drawn into the impeller that shouldn't be, fish or weed or fingers.

The protection had been Imani's list, mostly. Under the rubber at the forearms, the thighs and the neck, a layer of chain, fine as cloth, Abloh's, so that teeth and coral would have to work at it. A bladder under the pack that a diver could puff up from the bottle with a valve at the left hip, to hang still in the water without kicking. A weight on the belt on a quick release with a ring the size of a fist, so that a diver who lost his senses could be made to float, by himself or a friend. A knife on the right calf. A reel of line on the left hip. A slate on the left forearm with a pencil on a string. And a tapper on the right wrist, a little brass hammer on a spring over a plate, because radio didn't go through the sea and it never would, and two divers in a murk had nothing to say to each other but taps.

Eight hours on the bottle, at rest. Seventy-two on the gill, if you kept swimming and the cartridges held. The cartridges were the scrubber's cartridges, the spent-stone kind, so the same suit was the personal scrubber for the thick water past the wall, and a knight who'd started to bleed on deck could put it on and breathe Kaldo air in a world that had none.

"Tank," said Emeric.

The tank was the big one, the Challenger's test tank, eighteen feet deep and thirty across, under the long shed's roof with the winter light coming down through the water in bars. Finn went in at nine. He'd been in every one of the five before it. He climbed down the ladder in the suit as if it were his own skin, and stood on the bottom in the green light with his feet apart, and looked up, and gave the sign with his thumb.

Imani was at the rail with her watch and her board. Noll was beside her, because Noll was the Challenger's stone-keeper and the cartridges were stones and he'd sealed every one of them himself.

That had been the long argument of the autumn, the seal. Not because it was hard. Because it mattered. A cartridge was a little cylinder of pressed plates with an intake at one end and a valve at the other, and if water got into it before it was meant to, or something else did, the plates spoiled; and a diver at ninety feet with a spoiled cartridge had his bottle and nothing else. So each one was sealed at the Works when it was charged, in a tin sleeve, with a disc of red wax over the valve end, and Noll's own stamp pressed into the wax, a small fish with its mouth open. And written in the book: the cartridge's number, the charge, the day, Noll's name. And nobody fitted a cartridge to a suit without breaking the wax himself and looking at the stamp first.

"Why would anybody tamper with a cartridge?" Ward had said in November.

"I don't know," Emeric had said. "That's why there's wax."

Finn sat on the bottom of the tank for an hour on the bottle with his hands on his knees. Then Imani tapped the rail twice with a ring and he got up and swam, slowly, round the tank's wall, round and round in the green, with the pump-jet humming at his back and the needle in the chin mirror, he told them afterward, going down a hair as the bottle shut and then steadying as the gill came on, the way the needle on Tess's bench had steadied in the nose drum a year before.

"On Imani's line," said Noll, reading the rail's own dial on its hose.

"Stopping there?" said Imani.

"Stopping there."

"Good."

The air stone held it. That had been the thing Emeric had said first in the spring, before anybody had built anything. That you could put too much air into a man. That a gill which pulled every scrap of oxygen it could out of the sea and pumped it into a diver at thirty feet would kill him, quietly, a fit and a drowning; and that the clever part wasn't getting the air in, it was stopping. So the regulator sat behind its little glass window in the pack and metered what came off the plates against what the diver breathed, and capped it at the line Imani had drawn for it on Tess's meter, and no higher. However hard he swam.

They drilled the rest of it all morning. A cartridge closed on purpose: Finn on the bottle, fitting the spare from his belt by feel, in the dark of the bladder's shadow, with Imani counting. The helm flooded on purpose, a little, through a test cock: Finn tilting his head back to clear it the way he'd been taught. The weight dropped: Finn coming up slowly, the right speed, his hand on the line Bram held, breathing out the whole way the way you had to or your chest burst. The tapper: two taps, I'm all right; three, look at me; one long, come here now.

At noon he came up the ladder and pulled the helm off and sat on the rail with water running out of his hair.

"It's good," he said. "It's the best one." He looked at the suit on himself, the plate, the black. "I want to go deep in it. Properly."

"South Sentinel," said Emeric. "Wednesday. If Imani says."

"I say," said Imani, writing.

Zale was waiting for them at the top of the slip at South Sentinel on the Wednesday, the fourth, with no stick.

He had been on his feet without it for nine days. He'd woken in the dome on the sixteenth of January, eleven days after he'd swallowed the stone, at about two in the afternoon, with Ilenne asleep on the gravel at the foot of his board for the first time in eleven days and Imani asleep in her chair beside Fletcher's board with her book open on her chest; and he'd lain there, he said afterward, for about a quarter of an hour, looking up at the tree's branches, trying to work out what was different about his legs, before he'd understood that it was that they didn't hurt.

He'd sat up. Fletcher, on the next board, had woken the same morning and was already gone, back to the yard, complaining. Zale had put his feet on the gravel, carefully, and stood, without holding anything. Then he'd walked round the tree. It had taken him a long time and he'd had to stop twice, but he'd done it, all the way round, and at the end of it Ilenne had been sitting up on the gravel watching him with her hood fallen back and her colour doing something Imani had written down afterward as no card line for it.

The stone had done what it did. It had mended what the bleeding had broken, the small vessels in the backs of his legs and his joints that had filled and burst and filled again since the wall water, and it had rebuilt round them, the way it had rebuilt Tess, the way it had rebuilt Armand's old heart; faster than Tess, because his was a younger damage and not years of wasting. He was thinner. He'd lost half a stone in the dome and Maren had been putting it back with what Ferra called intent. But he walked like a man who'd never had a stick, and he'd been in the bay pool every morning since the twentieth, at first with Imani watching and then without.

And on the third of February Imani had sat him on the end of a bed in the ward and listened to his chest and pressed the backs of his knees and looked at his eyes with her sight for a long minute, and written on his card, under a great many other things, Cleared for water. All of it. I. 3 Feb.

He'd read it upside down. He hadn't said anything. He'd gone out of the ward and down to the harbour wall and taken his boots off and his coat, in February, and gone off the end of the wall into the sea in his shirt, and swum out to the first mooring buoy and back, and climbed out up the steps blue with cold, laughing.

"You're happy," said Emeric, at the top of the slip.

"I'm going in the water," said Zale, "with my wife-to-be, who's going to teach me how to swim, which I have been doing since I was four." He looked out at the sea past the island's point, grey and quiet with the winter light flat on it. "I've been on a stick for a year. I'm allowed to be happy."

"Is she your wife-to-be?"

Zale looked at him.

"Nobody's asked anybody anything," he said, "so don't you start," and went down the slip.

Ilenne taught them on the Wednesday afternoon in the island's lee, in the shallow water off the pier, on her legs.

It was thin water, Kaldo water, and her tail didn't come in it; she'd said so in the autumn, the way you'd say you didn't wear a coat indoors. So she stood in it up to her chest in a plain dark shift Abloh had made her for swimming, with her hood off and her hair tied back and her gill slits shut tight along her neck, and looked at the seven of them in front of her with an expression Emeric had last seen on Armand facing a new class.

Finn and Emeric. Zale. Mira and Bren, because the relay team would be divers too. Noll, who'd begged. And one of the Isle's divers, the boy of fifteen who'd kicked Finn in the face in the cove in January, who was called Tamo and had come down on the K Endeavor's last run because Waylon had told him that the mermaid would be there and he hadn't believed it.

He believed it now. He was standing very close to Finn and not taking his eyes off her.

"You swim," said Ilenne. "Go. There." She pointed at a buoy fifty yards out. "Back."

They swam. Without the suits, in the plain wool they wore under them, because she'd said she wanted to see their bodies and not the suits' bodies first. Finn was fastest, and Tamo second, and then Mira, and then Zale and Emeric together, and Bren and Noll a long way behind. They came back blowing.

Ilenne watched them come. When they were all standing in the shallows again she shook her head slowly.

"You fight it," she said.

"Fight what?" said Mira.

"The water." Ilenne put her hand flat on the surface in front of her and pushed it, hard, so it splashed. "Like this. You push. It pushes. You push more. It is tired. You are tired." She shook her head again. "The water is not tired. Never. You lose."

"So how do you do it?"

Ilenne thought about that for a long time. It was, Emeric realised, a thing she'd never had to explain to anybody, because nobody she'd ever known had needed it explained; it would be like asking him how he lifted his foot to walk.

"Watch," she said, and went.

She didn't dive. She lay forward into the water as if it were a bed, and was gone, and was ten yards away before he'd seen her move, her legs together, her arms at her sides, and nothing at all happening at the surface. No splash. No wake, hardly, a faint line on the water like a finger drawn over a pond. She went out to the buoy and touched it and turned and came back, under the surface the whole way, and stood up in front of them with the water running off her shoulders, not out of breath at all.

"Again," said Emeric.

She did it again. He watched. He went into his own head, a little, the way he did with a drawing, and let the outside go slow, and watched her go away from him through the green.

And there. Just ahead of her, a hand's breadth in front of her head and her shoulders, the water was wrong. It moved before she reached it. It wasn't much. If he hadn't been looking for it he'd never have seen it; nobody would. The surface over her didn't break, but it softened, the way the air over a road softens on a hot day. As if the water in front of her had got thinner. As if it had decided, a moment before she arrived, to get out of the way.

She came back and stood up.

"You saw," she said. She was looking at him.

"I saw something."

"What?"

"It goes soft. In front of you. Before you get there." He held up his hand, flat, and moved it forward slowly through the air. "Like you're... making it thin. Ahead. So you go into thin water instead of thick."

He'd seen the high divers on Earth, in the films, going off the platforms at the swimming baths with a hiss of bubbles boiling up under them in the pool, so the water they hit was half air. He'd read about ships that blew air along their own bottoms so the hull slid on a skin of bubbles and burned less coal. It was something like that. It wasn't that. It was a first guess, and he knew it, and he kept the rest of it inside his head where it belonged.

Ilenne looked at his hand for a while.

"Not thin," she said. "I tell it. Small." She frowned. She didn't have the word, and it bothered her. "Like..." She put her own hand out, flat, beside his, and turned it a little, as if she were stroking a cat the right way. "Like this. I ask. It says yes."

"You ask the water."

"Everybody asks," said Ilenne, impatient, as though he'd said something stupid. "At home. A baby asks. You do not know how to ask. So it says no." She looked at the seven of them standing in the shallows. "You cannot learn. Not in one day. Maybe not in one life. But you can stop fighting." She pointed at Finn. "You. Arms. You pull and you pull. Stop. Put them here." She pressed his arms against his sides. "Kick from here." She put her hand flat on the small of his back. "Not the knee. Here. Slow."

And then she taught them, for three hours, in the cold, in the lee of the island, and nobody complained of the cold.

It was a good lesson. She was a better teacher than Emeric had expected and a far worse one than Armand, because she had no idea what it was like not to know the thing she was teaching, and when one of them got it wrong for the fourth time she looked at them with a mixture of pity and outrage that made Mira laugh so hard she swallowed half the bay. But she didn't stop. She went from one to the next, in the water, and put her hands on their backs and their shoulders and their ankles, and moved them, and said here and no and slow and, once, to Bren, a word in her own tongue that Zale wouldn't translate and that made Bren go red.

Tamo got it first. He'd been swimming since before he could walk, in the Isle's warm water off the east shore, and he swam like a seal already, and after an hour of her hands he swam like a seal that had been told a secret. Finn got it second, a little, enough that he came back from the buoy with a startled face and said, "That was faster. That was faster, wasn't it?" and Ilenne said, "Yes. A little. Like a fish that is sick," and Finn looked as pleased as if she'd knighted him.

Zale was the worst.

He'd swum since he was four, in the river at the palace and the sea off every port he'd ever put into, a strong plain sailor's stroke, all arms; and he couldn't stop. Every time she pressed his arms to his sides he swam six yards with them there and then they came out again of their own accord and started to pull, and he'd look down at them with an expression of baffled betrayal, and Ilenne would swim after him and put them back.

"No," she said.

"I know."

"You do not know. You know with the head." She tapped his forehead with a wet finger. "Not with the arms. The arms are stupid."

"They've kept me alive for forty years."

"They have kept you slow for forty years," said Ilenne, and put them back again.

He was, Emeric thought, watching him come in from the buoy for the ninth time with his arms going like a windmill and his face split in a grin he couldn't seem to get off it, the happiest man in the water. Off the stick. In the sea. Being told he was doing it wrong by a woman who went faintly blue at the throat every time she put her hands on him and pretended it was the cold.

"It's the cold," said Zale, when Bren pointed it out.

"It is the cold," said Ilenne.

Nobody believed either of them.

He drew it that night in the pier hall, by the stove, on the back of one of Ilenne's fish sheets, because it was the paper that was there.

The Challenger already did it. That was what kept bringing him back to it. He'd built her slip plating in the autumn out of Bram's dust in the steel, water-stone dust laid in the outer skin so that on command the sea gave way along the hull instead of dragging at it, and he'd done it because he'd known on Earth that drag was the whole fight for a boat and because he'd had the dust and it had seemed the obvious use. He hadn't known anybody did it for free. He'd built, out of stones and steel and a year of Bram's patience, a clumsy copy of something a mermaid baby did on its first day.

So a suit could do it too. The same dust in panels on the rubber, at the shoulders and the chest and the front of the helm, where Ilenne's water had gone soft ahead of her. Not asking. Telling, the way a stone told; badly, because a stone didn't know the water. And with the dust dead it was just a smooth suit, slower, like any other.

And for long dives, the deep ones in the cold, a thin warm mesh in the wool. He'd been arguing that one with himself all week. A good suit kept a man warm for an hour. It didn't keep him warm for six, because water took heat out of a body many times faster than air, and at Aumere they'd be down for six.

And boots. That was the other thing, the thing he hadn't said to anybody yet. If a stone in a suit could tell the water to get out of the way, a stone in a boot could tell it to hold still. To be ground.

He wrote Mark II at the top of the sheet, and slip skin, and warm layer, off unless, and Imani's cap; and then, at the bottom, smaller, boots, and a question mark, and put the pencil down. Then he looked at the other side of the paper, where the long flat fish with the comb fin lay on its side with GOOD and LEAVES beside it in her careful capitals, and turned it face down so it wouldn't get ink on it, and went to bed.

The deep trial was the Thursday, the fifth, on a grey still morning off the island's seaward side, where the bottom went down in shelves to a hundred and forty feet a quarter mile out.

Imani had the K Endeavor's tender anchored over it with a shot line down to a weight on the bottom, marked every ten feet with a coloured rag, and a second line for the stage weights, and Bram on a stool in the stern with the winch for the recovery line, and Noll with the book of cartridges and the stamp, and Ilenne sitting on the gunwale with her bare feet in the sea.

Three divers. Finn first, because Finn was always first. Then Mira. Then Emeric, last, because Imani had said she wasn't going to have the king go first at anything that might kill him and he hadn't argued.

Noll broke the wax on each cartridge in front of the diver who'd wear it. That was the rule. He held up the tin sleeve and showed the red disc and the little fish stamped in it, whole, and the diver looked at it and nodded; and then Noll cracked it off with his thumb and slid the cartridge out and read its number to Imani, and Imani found the number in the book and read back the charge and the day and Noll's name, and only then did it go into the pack.

"It takes a minute," said Mira, while hers went in.

"It takes a minute," said Noll. "And it'll take a minute every single time. And one day it'll be the minute that matters." He didn't look up from the pack. He'd been the stone-keeper of a boat at the bottom of the sea for long enough to have stopped believing in luck. "Seal's good. Number's good. In."

They went down by stages. Twenty feet: stop. Imani's tapper on the shot line, a long stone-headed rod she could strike, the sound going down the line and through the water to the divers' wrists; and each diver tapping back, two, all right, and writing on their slates the three dials in the mirror and holding them up to each other. Forty feet: the same. Sixty. Eighty.

Emeric hung on the line at eighty feet and looked at his slate and at Mira's and at Finn's below him, a dark shape with a white stripe going down into the green, and at the dials in his chin mirror, backward, the oxygen needle sitting on Imani's line exactly where it had sat on the bench in the shed and in the tank. Holding there. Not climbing.

It was very quiet. He could hear the loop breathing in its bags at his back, and the hum of the pump-jet when he kicked, and his own heart. The light came down in long grey bars from the surface and was swallowed below him. A shoal of something small and silver turned all at once at the edge of seeing and was gone.

A hundred feet.

Finn was there first, on the bottom shelf, on his knees on the sand by the shot weight, looking up. He'd puffed his bladder and come off the bottom a foot, so that he hung there over the sand without touching it, very still, like a man sitting in the air. As they came down to him he raised his slate and Emeric read it in the gloom: 100. ON THE LINE. I'M A FISH.

And then, in smaller letters, A SICK ONE.

They did the checks at the bottom. The dials, all three, all of them, written and shown. The seal on every cartridge, by touch, round the valve end, for the seep of water that would mean it had gone. A spare cartridge fitted, by Mira, on Finn, by feel, eyes shut, with Imani counting on the line above in long taps: she did it in forty seconds. The weights: Finn's first, on the count, and he went up the line slowly, slowly, breathing out, with Bram taking in the slack above him, all the way to the twenty-foot stop, where he hung for his three minutes and came the rest of the way.

Then Mira's. Then his own.

He dropped it at a hundred feet on Imani's tap, and the bladder took him, and he rose. Slowly. The rags on the shot line going past his helm one after another, red, yellow, white. Breathing out, the whole way, long and steady, because the air in the loop and in his chest got bigger as the sea above him got less, and if you held it in it broke you. The light got paler. The green went grey went white.

At twenty feet he stopped and hung on the line and looked up at the surface, a bright moving ceiling with the dark shape of the tender's hull in it and a pale pair of feet beside the hull, and the feet were kicking slowly, idly, as if their owner were sitting on a wall in the sun.

He came up beside them. Ilenne leaned down from the gunwale and put her hand flat on top of his helm, on the glass, and left it there a moment, the way she'd put her hand on Zale's chest on South Sentinel.

"Better," she said. "You kick from here now." She pressed his back through the water, at the right place, low, though he couldn't feel it through the pack. "A little."

"Like a sick fish?"

"Like a fish that is getting better," said Ilenne, and took her hand away.

Imani wrote it up in the tender's stern going home with the board on her knees.

5 Feb. Open water off South Sentinel, seaward shelf. Mark I (sixth). Three divers: F., M., E. To 100 ft by stages, 20 ft each. Oxygen on the line at every stage, all three, both cartridges, no climb. Seals checked at depth by hand: dry. Spare fitted blind at 100 ft, 40 s. Weights dropped on the tap: ascents at the right rate, stops kept. Taps and slates clear. Bladders: good. Cold: F. shivering at 100 ft after 40 min; E. and M. not (stones). Recommend the warm layer for long dives as E. proposes. Cartridges: wax and stamp checked before each fitting, numbers read and matched. All eighteen used today accounted for, broken seals kept. N. to file them.

And at the bottom, after a while, in a different ink, because she'd had to borrow Noll's pen:

Z. cleared for water 3 Feb. In it today, not in a suit, alongside, all afternoon, worst in the lesson by a long way. Happiest.

She looked at it. Then she put a small dot after it, the kind she put on a Band card, and the dot meant nothing at all on this card, and she put it there anyway.
