Chapter 321 - Two Fronts

Saban had the name on the twenty-fifth of November and kept it for two days, because he wanted to bring it up the hill with the other thing, and the other thing had not come yet.

He brought them both on the twenty-seventh, at eight in the morning, to the study, in his hat. Harrold was in the window seat with his tea. Ward was at the end of the desk with his arms folded, and Ferra was in the other chair with her book on her knee and her pencil already out; and Saban sat down and put the hat on his knee.

"Dace Callis," he said.

Ferra wrote it.

"Forester to Lord Lisle. Thirty-four. Lives in the keeper's house at the north end of Lisle's woods, a mile off the Wayford road, with a wife and a dog. He's ridden the Lieutenant's dispatches on that road since June, Wayford to the capital and back, twice a week, because the chancery pays a shilling a ride and Lisle doesn't pay his foresters anything they'd notice." Saban took the first slip out of the hat. "The chancery gives its riders their cord. Out of the stores, by the yard, signed for. My man copies the stores book too, because nobody else wants to. Seven riders on the Wayford road signed for cord in October. Six of them were in the capital on the ninth of November, or in Wayford with a dozen men who'll swear to it. Callis was neither."

"That's not a name," said Ward. "That's a man who wasn't somewhere."

"It's the start of one." Saban did not look round. "The horse that went north from the culvert had a bar shoe on the near hind. There's one smith on Lisle's side of the line who still puts a bar shoe on for a split heel, because the young ones call it an old man's shoe; and he put one on a brown cob for a forester on the eighth. He remembered, because the forester paid in capital silver and the smith had to go next door to change it." He laid down a second slip. "On the tenth Callis rode his dispatch a day late. He told the post house at Wayford the cob had gone lame."

"A forester doesn't have a forcing stone," said Emeric.

"Somebody gave him one. Somebody told him when the train was due. I don't know who. I know who he rides for, and I know he sat in his keeper's house for a week after and didn't go to the tavern, which he does most nights, and his wife told the baker's girl he'd been ill." Saban turned the hat once. "I've had a man on him since the twenty-fifth. A charcoal burner. He'll be on him till you tell me to stop."

"Don't take him," said Emeric.

"A man you're watching tells you what he's for," said Ward. It was the first thing he had said that sounded pleased. "A man in a cell tells you what he thinks you want."

"If he goes anywhere with more than his dog behind him," said Emeric, "I want Ferra to know before I do."

Ferra wrote that too. Then she looked at the hat, which still had something in it.

"And the other thing," she said.

"Kesrath's envoy left the capital yesterday." Saban took out the third slip and held it. "North road, first light, an escort of the Lieutenant's horse as far as the march. He'd been nine days waiting for a boat back across the strait, and it came on the twenty-fourth, and on the twenty-fifth he went up to the chancery with what was in it." He put the slip down on the others. "The king's seal. Kesrath's king has put his wax to the Lieutenant's governorship, and the Lieutenant his to Kesrath's right to the Claim. My man copied the fair copy for the rolls that evening, with the ribbon drawn in the margin."

In the window seat Harrold put his cup down on its saucer. It made a very small sound.

"And from the other end." Saban laid a fourth slip, on the back of a chandler's bill, in the cramped quick hand of his man in Kesrath's port. "New boats in the harbour since the middle of the month. Thirty, flat-bottomed, broad in the beam, with a ramp at the bow that lets down. My man's never seen boats like them; nobody in Kesrath has. Built in Kesrath's own yards in a hurry, and paid for in letters of credit on a house nobody in the port can name. The yards took the paper and didn't ask. My man asked a clerk at the harbourmaster's whose it was, and the clerk said it was good paper, and that was all he'd say."

"Landing boats," said Ward.

"They'll not use them while your ships are at home," said Saban. "They'll use them when every hull you have is hanging over one city five hundred miles from the strait."

It was what Ward had said at the long table on the sixth. He did not say so. He looked at the desk, at the place in front of him where there was nothing, and Emeric felt his note go steady and cold, and stay.

"Then it's two," said Emeric. "As we said."

"As you said," said Ward.

Harrold had picked up his cup again. He was looking out at the yard.

"His great-grandfather and mine signed the strait treaty in the old hall at Kesrath," he said, to the window. "I was shown the table as a boy. It had a burn on it where somebody had put a candle down." He drank. "He'll have sealed it in the same room, I expect. They don't have another."



The letter came for Armand on the noon train from Wayford, in the inner post, in a cover of the tower's grey paper; and he knew the seal before the boy had it out of his bag.

He took it up to the round room at the top of his tower on the dome's west side, where the four would come at two, and shut the door, and sat under the window with it on his knee, and looked at the seal for some time without breaking it. Grey wax. The tower's old mark, the star inside the circle, and under the star a small V, cut into the matrix for a woman who had been made an elder the year the old king died and had sat at the elders' table ever since, three places down from Armand's, on the window side, out of the draught.

He had written to them on the nineteenth. That was the law, and he had kept it as he had kept every law of the tower for sixty years whether he thought it sensible or not: a master who would put into print any matter touching the tower's own books laid it first before the elders under his seal, so that they might know of it. Not ask. Know. He had never once had cause to keep it, because he had never once printed anything. So on the nineteenth, with Hobb's forme still in its chase, he had copied out his own column fair: the back of the sheet, his half, the rite and the counts and the yard and the Proving and the king, and WE COUNTED. THEY DID NOT. Not the front. Not the hour, nor the map, nor the six hundred yards, nor a word of Doran's. His column, with his name at its head. He had sealed it with his own seal and put it in the post and gone down to supper.

He had not asked the table. It had not occurred to him to ask. It was his name.

He broke the seal.

One sheet, small and very upright, in a brown ink he remembered and a hand he remembered better; Hesper Vosse had kept the minutes of the elders' table in it for eleven years before they made her an elder, and he had read every page. She acknowledged receipt of Master Armand's matter, laid before the elders under his seal on the nineteenth day of November according to the tower's law. She noted that it was intended for print.

She did not dispute a figure.

He read it to the end and went back to the top looking for that: the place where she would say his count of the floor's dead in his own rotation was wrong, or taken from the wrong book. Hesper Vosse knew how to quarrel with a figure; she had quarrelled with his across the oak more often than anybody living. She went past every one of these as a clerk goes past a column she has already checked and found correct. The nine in ten. The rotation counts. The sixty-one and the twenty-two.

What she said was that the rotation books were not his. They were the tower's and the Church's, held in common under the old agreement and kept by the tower for both; and a master who had sat over them had sat by the tower's leave, and had no more right to print them than a steward had to print his lord's accounts because he had once added them up. She reminded him, in a sentence that was nearly kind, that he had been a very good steward.

And alone at the foot, above the V:

A master who prints the floor's dead will print the ring next.

Armand sat under the window and looked at that line for a long time.

He had not written the word anywhere. He had said it once, on the twenty-eighth of October, in this room, with the lamp unlit, to Emeric and nobody else on earth; and he had drilled the four in it every afternoon since behind this door, and they had not said it outside the door because he had told them not to and they were frightened of him. There was nothing in his column about glyphs, or dimming, or the faint coin-sized ring that came up in the air when a man went past the ceiling and the glyph emptied and the fire came anyway. There was nothing in it but the floor.

And she had read his column about the floor and written back that he would print the ring next. Not a ring. The ring. As a woman at a table names a thing the table has always had a name for.

He took his spectacles off, the plain glass ones he still wore out of habit, and held them.

He had sat with those people for forty years. He had argued crystal theory with her across the oak until the candles were down, and gone to her husband's funeral, and been, he had thought, as much among the elders as any man who was not one of the two or three at the head who had been there longest. There were things they knew that they did not say even to a High mage; he had told Emeric so in this room, and thought, when he said it, that he was guessing.

He was not guessing now. Some of them, at that table, had known that the glyph was a picture and the ceiling a fence painted on the air, and had taught the boys to draw anyway, every winter of his life; and had sat at the high end and watched him, the best crystal theorist the tower had, spend sixty years dimming against it, and let him. Because a tower of men slow by the length of a drawing needed its elders to tell it what it could do.

He found that his hands were not quite steady, and that this interested him.

The four came up the stair at two, arguing, and stopped at the door to stop arguing before they knocked. He locked the letter in the drawer and let them in, and drilled them on the empty ring for two hours, a little harder than usual, until the youngest got a light out of nothing, the size of a pea, that went out as soon as she looked at it. He told her it was very good, which it was, and sent them to their supper.

Then he took the letter down to the long room, where the six would be at six, and waited for them at the head of the table with his knees up.

He read it to them himself. He thought he owed them that. First what he had done and when, plainly, as a man reads out a fault in a casting; then Hesper Vosse's sheet, every line, in a voice that did not change; and laid it on the table and took his hand off it.

Ferra had written the date and the word elders and stopped. He watched her read the last line upside down, twice, and understand it on the second reading, and look at him.

Saban had gone very still. He was a man whose stillness had a temperature, and it had gone down.

"Twelve days," he said. "Twelve days before the sheet came down, you've sent the back of it to the tower's elders. Who sit across a yard from the close and dine with the canons on feast days, and have done for two hundred years." He put his hat down on the table, which Armand had never seen him do. "I've spent three years making sure nobody in that city knew a thing about this house that I hadn't put there for them to know. I've a man in the chancery and a man on the river and a carter and a charcoal burner, and every one of them hangs if the wrong word gets to the wrong door. And you sent it to them in the post."

"Not the front," said Ferra, flatly, as a fact and not a defence. "Not the hour. Only the column."

"They'll know there's a sheet."

"They'll know there's a sheet with Armand's name on it about the floor. They won't know there's a city on the other side of it." She looked at the letter. "The tower doesn't care about the cathedral. It cares about its books. That's what she wrote about."

Saban looked at her for a breath. Then he picked his hat up again, and Armand understood that the worst of it had been said and would not be said twice.

Ward was at his end of the table in Abloh's new coat, which he had already undone at the collar.

"It was his name," said Ward. "His column. His law. He's a master of that tower and the only one at this table. If the tower's law says a master lays his own matter before them, it was his to send. Not ours." He took his hands off the table. "That's all."

It was not said to make anybody feel better. It was only true, which was worse, and better.

"It was mine to send," said Armand. "And I should have asked. That's the fault. Not the law. I kept their law for sixty years without asking anybody, and when I came here I brought the habit in my luggage and never once took it out to look at it." He looked at Saban. "I'm sorry. To you most."

Saban turned his hat on his knee.

"Nothing's changed on the day," he said at last. "I'll want to know before you send them anything else."

"You'll know before I write it."

Ferra wrote. Armand could read it upside down: 19 Nov. A. to the tower's elders, the column, under his seal. Answered 27 Nov, H. Vosse. No figure disputed. And under it, smaller: The ring. They know.

Emeric had said nothing. Armand had felt his sense on the letter the whole time it was read, lightly, not leaning; and when Armand looked at him at last, Emeric only looked back, steadily, the way he had looked up from the prints on the round room floor a month ago. He had known, or near enough, since then.

It was a strange thing, at his age, in his new body, to be told by a boy he had taught that he had been right.

"They'll fight it," said Armand. "When it's shown. Not the Church. Us. I always thought the Church was the only house in the kingdom with something to hide about magic." He put the letter back in its cover. "I'll answer her after. When there's something to answer with."

"Is she one of them?" said Imani. "Or just the one who held the pen?"

"A month ago I'd have said I knew her better than anyone at that table," said Armand, and put the letter in his coat. In the passage the first of the captains were coming up the stair.



They had never seen it, and Emeric watched them come into the Works Office's map room at eight and see it, one after another, in the doorway, and stop.

The masters of the ten hulls: Fletcher, who had seen it, and came in talking; Cade from the K Endeavor, who stopped with his mouth open and left it open; Ned Tallis from the Hod; the escorts' masters and hulls two and four's. Ruan, the K Amara's gun captain, who stood against the wall and looked at the table without any expression at all for the whole of the evening. Rylan and Declan and Osric, and the regulars' captains, eleven of them, with their caps under their arms. Linus with his weather book. Harrold came in last and sat on the bench under the window, where a chair had been put for him and he had moved it back to the wall. Waylon came in behind him in his riding coat, because nobody had told him not to, and sat at the bench's end with his elbows on his knees.

The six were in suits. Abloh had cut them for the councils out of a dark Grayrock wool, against every principle he had: a short coat that buttoned in front and lay flat without a belt or a sash, and trousers of a piece with it. Lounge suits, Emeric had called them from the drawing, and nobody had asked him who in the kingdom had ever lounged. Bram looked like a bear that had been fitted for something. Saban looked born in his. Ward had worn his for three hours and hated it with a steady silent hatred Emeric could feel across the room.

Tess was in the head chair, straight from the Forge, with dust on her sleeves and her stick hooked over the arm, and she brought the table up for them from the bottom without being asked.

The river first, dark. The ground, the walls in faint gold, the streets thrown over it like a net. The houses, the long slope, the castle on the hill's east shoulder; and on the summit, charcoal and darker than anything round it, the cathedral, rising through the plates to its one needle spire, with a point of gold at its tip so small that Cade bent to look. The close's wall. The gatehouse with its two towers. The drill yard on the east side of the lower city, bare. The dotted ring at six hundred yards, in blue.

Then Tess moved her fingers, and the capital slid along the glass to the left and made room; and at the right end, in the same plates, the Isle came up.

A long black spine of rock in a dark sea. The west cliffs lit sharp and white from four years of runs; the Claim's dunes soft and pale, the marsh behind them in the dull green the table used for wet ground; the pitch lake, a flat stain darker than the sea; the gully a mile inside the Claim where Kaldo's well head stood with its little derrick and the three squat drums of the tank farm; the Claim's boundary stones along the dune ridge in a line of small white points. And past the last stone, eastward, the whole of Kesrath's end drawn in nothing at all: fog, a soft grey haze to the island's tip, with one bright point standing up out of it where the table knew there was a tower because Kaldo had seen its lamp lit on a September night, and nothing else.

"That's Kesrath's," said Emeric, before anybody asked. "The table won't draw what it hasn't seen, and we're not going to show it. That's the line. The wells and the beach and the tanks, as far as the charter runs, and not one foot past that last stone. Whatever's in their boats."

A grey square captain from the Grayrock company looked at the fog a long time.

"If they come at us out of that," he said, "we'll not know where from."

"No," said Emeric. "You'll know when."

The capital at one end and the Isle at the other, four hundred miles folded into a forearm of dark plate, with the table's own fog round the close too, where it had always been. Two fronts on one stack, in the same light. He had wanted them to see that before anybody said a number. He saw that they had.

Then Ward said the numbers, standing, with his hands on the rim, once, in a voice for the back of a yard.

"Knights. Three hundred. Every one of them flies. Two hundred go up: a hundred on the K Amara's aprons, twenty each on escorts one, two and three, and hulls two and four. Forty go to the Isle. Sixty stay home, for the wall and the island." He gave them exactly as long as it took to hear it. "Regulars. Three thousand. Fifteen hundred go up as shore teams for the capital, on the K Endeavor and hulls two and four. Five hundred to the Isle. A thousand home." His hand moved along the rim toward the fog. "The Isle goes in escorts three and four. Rylan and Declan have it. Three and four put them down and stay on the strait."

Emeric felt him decide to say the next thing.

"It's not enough," said Ward. "Forty knights and five hundred men against thirty landing boats and forty sail, on sand, with a high mage on a tower who's sat over those fields thirty years." He looked at Emeric across the glass. "Give me a hundred knights for the Isle, and the Hod to carry them."

"No," said Emeric.

The room was very quiet.

"The capital needs every hand we have low," said Emeric. "If it goes the way we've drawn it, it's a count, and a count is men on foot going room to room in a building with walls four yards thick. If it doesn't go the way we've drawn it, it's worse, and it's in lanes. And the Hod charges the escorts with hull two. Without her they run dry over the city."

Ward looked at him a moment longer.

"Then that's that," he said, and took his hand off the fog.

"Rylan," said Emeric.

"Yes," said Rylan.

Emeric had not asked him anything. He had opened his mouth to, and Rylan had said it into the space before the question, plainly, as if it were the answer to something asked a long time ago. Beside him Declan looked at the white cliffs in the glass and nodded once, to the table, the way he nodded to a horse.

"The wells," said Emeric. "And the beach and the tanks. Not past the stones."

"Not past the stones. I heard you the first time." Rylan looked at the fog. "So did they, I expect, wherever they are."

"Divers?" said Ferra.

"Thirteen, on Ottoline's bags," said Declan. "Fenn's swimmers, sixty that can do a mile in the dark in a winter sea, and Fenn. The rest on boats with no lamps. Hester's broth in every kit. A jar a man."

"Two," said Imani. "A jar's nothing."

"Two," said Declan, and Ferra wrote it.

Then Rylan turned to the other end of the glass and bent over the capital, over the close and the lanes round it, close enough that his breath went faintly on the case; the streets he had been going to walk on the day with a shore team behind him, cellar to belfry, before the Isle came and took him off them.

"That's yours now," he said to Osric, without looking up. "The count."

Osric came round the table and looked down beside him.

"Mine," he said.

"The three places. He'll give you them." Rylan put a finger on the glass over the nave, where Tess had lit three small red rings at the places Emeric had marked from inside: the passage in the north wall, the room over the north porch, the cell at the foot of the south-west stair. "And the crypt. Don't let them hurry you in the crypt."

"I'll not be hurried."

"You will. Everybody will be hurrying you. That's why I'm saying it." Rylan straightened. He looked at the lanes again, the little lit net of them round the close and down the hill, the market and the court behind the gatehouse and the long arcade down the east side of the cloth market, all drawn so sure and clean. "How old are the lanes?"

"Spring," said Emeric.

Rylan nodded.

"They'll not have moved the cathedral," said Osric, and two of the captains laughed, and Rylan grinned at the glass and went back to the Isle's end; and Ward was already talking about the boats.

Nobody followed it up.



The rest took three hours, and most of it was Ferra's: who went on which hull by name out of Ottoline's rolls, and what each carried and in what order it went in and would come off. Shells and powder, rounds by the cartridge, flasks in their plated sleeves, salt for the seeder, bread, chutes and the spares for the chutes, Kaldo plate in bales, stretchers. Linus on the weather, in a voice like a man reading a will. Fletcher on the arrowhead. Ward asked Ruan whether his guns were ready; Ruan said they were, and nothing else, and Ward seemed satisfied.

Then Ward stood up again, which made the captains look at him, because Ward did not stand up twice in an evening for anything.

"One more," he said. He did not look at Emeric. He looked at the masters round the rim, and at Ruan, and at the knights' leads.

"Nothing falls that hasn't been named. Out loud. By him." He tipped his head at Emeric without taking his eyes off them. "A tower, a gate, a wall, a ship, a stone, a bell. If he wants a thing down, he names it on the set, so every hull hears it and somebody writes it. If he hasn't named it, it stands. I don't care how easy it is. I don't care if it's shooting at you." He put one hand flat on the rim. "That's the fleet's rule. Not his. Yours. Every gun captain. Every knight with a stone. Every man with a break-stone in his kit." He sat down. "That's all."

Ward had said it on the wall walk in August, in the dark, to him alone: choose every one, out loud, so it's written. He had said it once there and once here, and it was not the same thing. On the wall it had been a condition. Here it was an order, and every man in the room had heard whose.

"Written," said Ferra. "The clerk on the K Amara's set takes each one down as it's said and reads it back. My clerks have it here off the line room. If there's a thing on the ground after that's not on the list, I'll want to know who put it there."

Ruan, by the wall, said, "Aye." It was the second word he had said all evening.

"And Kaldo," said Bram heavily. "Who's in it, while you're all up there. I know where I am. I'm at the works, because something'll break, it always does. Tess and Ambrose are on the line room's set because they built it. But who holds it. If somebody comes."

"Armand," said Emeric. "By the seventh clause."

Armand inclined his head.

"And Ferra."

Ferra did not look up. They had had it out in the map room in September and in her bed in October and once on the wall walk, and she had won it every time on the same ground: a war was a ledger, and somebody had to keep it where the money was. She wrote her own name at the head of a column, very straight.

"And Waylon," said Ward.

Waylon sat up on his bench.

"At the South Gate," said Ward. "The gate's men want somebody they'll do what they're told by at four in the morning without arguing. Who's not needed anywhere else, and won't go up on the wall to see what's happening when he's told to stay at the gate."

"I can't fight," said Waylon.

"The wall will fight. You'll stand at the gate."

Waylon looked at Harrold beside him on the bench, as if to see what Harrold thought of his son being given a gate; and Harrold did not look back, and did not need to.

"All right," said Waylon.

"And the king," said Saban quietly, from the foot of the table, "stays with the town."

It had been assumed, so nobody had said it, and Saban had said it to have it said. Harrold put his hands on his knees.

"No," said the king. "I'll go up."

Every head turned. He did not use the old voice, the one for the back of a hall; he said it the way he asked the price of mutton at Maren's, dry and plain.

"I asked once in Ferra's book, to be aboard when it's lit. I'm asking again out loud, since that seems to be the rule tonight." He looked at Emeric, not at Saban. "The K Discovery. In a plain coat. I've stood at the back of more rooms than anybody in this one."

"Father."

"I was crowned under that roof. I'll see it come down from where I can see it, and not hear it on a set in a castle in the south and be told about it after by a clerk." He took his hands off his knees. "And the decoy flies my standard, so they'll think I'm here. Which I'll admit I find funny."

Ward, at the end of the table, said nothing at all, and Emeric understood from that that Ward had known, and agreed, and let the king say it himself.

"The K Discovery," said Emeric.

"Thank you," said the king.

The table had begun to cloud, grey from the busiest layers outward, the close and the lanes round it first, so that the cathedral was the first thing in the glass to fade. Tess took her fingers off the stone and let the whole of it go dark at once, both ends, and sat back with her eyes shut.

"Trays," she said. "Into the racks tonight." She opened her eyes. "I've got to go back to the Forge."



Maren was talking about somebody's wife when Ferra came down to the kitchen on the twenty-eighth for the bread count, and did not stop because Ferra came in.

Emeric came in behind her with the K Amara's galley list, which Maren had sent back up the hill twice with remarks. The castle kitchen at noon was the hottest room in the four baronies: both ranges going, the big table under the hatch white with flour from end to end, the girls shaping the fleet's loaves in rows on the boards, and Maren at the far end with her sleeves up past the burn scars and her arms in the dough trough to the elbow. Nobody looked up. You did not, in Maren's kitchen at noon.

"Sixty-one," one of the girls was saying to the next. "She said so herself, at the fish stall. And not a grey hair on her, and her neck like a girl's."

"She's lying."

"Her own daughter's forty."

"Then her daughter's lying."

"Her husband's one of the Circle men that came south. The tall one with the cough that does the weather glass in ward two."

"Well, there you are," said Maren, from the trough, without taking her arms out. "A mage's wife stays young. Everybody knows that. My mother knew it." She turned the dough over in one heave and slapped it down. "It's the money. A mage's money buys a soft life, and a soft life keeps a face. She's never carried water, I'll lay. Never stood at a range." She punched the dough. "And the rest is vanity, and a pot of something off the apothecary she rubs on at night and doesn't say what it cost."

"My mam says it's the magic," said the second girl. "Rubs off on them."

"Your mam," said Maren, "believes the river talks to her on a Thursday." She took her arms out of the trough and looked down the boards. "That one's too slack. Do it again. And you, that's not a loaf, that's a cushion."

Ferra had gone to the bread book on its shelf under the salt box and was running her pencil down the K Endeavor's count. Emeric watched the pencil stop, halfway down the page, on a line of rye, for about the time it took to read one figure twice; and then it went on to the foot, and she wrote the total and initialled it, and turned to the K Amara's page. She did not look round at Maren. She did not look at him.

"And you can tell him," said Maren to the dough, "that if he sends that list down here a third time with salt fish on it, I'll put him in the trough."

"He heard," said Ferra.

"Good," said Maren.



Tess cut the escorts' pin stones in three nights, at the Forge, alone.

Four of them, from the two runs to the west cliffs: shard stone, clear as water, each the size of a fist, asleep in their padded boxes in the strongroom since the summer with Ferra's line against each in the stone book's second column, cut, not written. Emeric had meant to write them himself since August, and every night had been full until there were none left; and on the twentieth, when Fletcher asked for the fourth time, Tess had said she would do them before Emeric had finished saying that he would.

"You've the K Amara's shell to read before you go. And sleep," she had said. "It's one facet. Anchor, on the stern. I wrote the Well's. I can write four of those with my eyes shut."

She did not write them with her eyes shut. Emeric went down to the cellar at one in the morning on the twenty-seventh, after the map room, and found her on her high stool under the one lamp with escort one's stone in its cradle and both hands round it and her eyes wide on nothing, the way a delver's eyes went a long way inside. Ottoline's nephew was asleep on a sack against the far wall. Emeric stood at the foot of the stair with his sense out, not leaning, and felt the facet going in, one line at a time, slow and square and certain, the stern's grip on the still mana of the sky; and went back up without waking either of them.

Escort one's was done by four. Two's and three's on the twenty-eighth; she was still at the bench when the works' six o'clock whistle went. Fletcher's men seated the three in their sterns on the morning of the twenty-ninth, each in its housing behind the keel spine's last frame, and Fletcher had escort one pinned over the lake at five hundred feet, once, to hear the thump, and came down with his hat off.

The fourth was escort four's, and it had a feather in it: a fine white flaw near the thick end, the length of a fingernail, where the stone had cracked and healed in the rock. It did not matter for most things. It mattered for a pin, because a pin was the whole of a stone's day spent through one facet at once, and a flaw near it was a place where all of that could go somewhere it had not been told to. There was no other stone. The seam had given four. So on the third night she wrote round it.



The Isle detachment went aboard on the afternoon of the twenty-ninth, at the yard's quay, under a white sky, in a wind off the river that smelled of snow.

Escorts three and four lay below the sheds with their gangways down, black, side by side, a pair, as Fletcher built everything; and the five hundred and forty went up in two files, a company at a time, the regulars in Kaldo plate with their packs, the knights with their suits in canvas bags and their helmets hooked to the bags, past the boats lashed dark on the two decks with their oars muffled in sacking. It took two hours. Ward stood at the foot of escort four's gangway with Ottoline's rolls and ticked every name as it passed him, and said nothing to anybody.

The town had come down without being told. It stood behind the rope in its coats and shawls and joggers, wives and mothers and children and men off the shift, and the women from ward four with a basket of hot pies that Maren had not sent and would say afterward she had. It did not cheer. A quay did not cheer a ship going somewhere it could not be told about. It called names, and the names on the gangways turned round and waved.

Ivo had the motion camera on its legs at the quay's edge below escort four's gangway, cranking, two turns a second, with his hat pushed back; and Merrin beside him had the sound camera on its own legs with its horn up on a pole, because she said a quay was the best noise in the world and she wanted all of it, the gulls and the boots and the names.

Fenn was counting his swimmers up the gangway by their heads. Sergeant Ade Fenn, a barrel-shaped man with a voice like a gull, who had stood to his chest in the island's bay all October in his drawers shouting sixty knights and regulars into swimming a mile in the dark, and was very proud of it and would not say so; he touched each of them on the top of the head as they went past him, one, two, three, the way Wicket had counted them out of the water.

Behind the swimmers came the divers with their bags, thirteen; and the last of them was Robin Sennet, the youngest, who had to be told twice by Ward at the gangway's foot to take his hands out of his pockets, and took them out, and put them back in at the top.

Then two of the knights stopped by Ivo's camera, because one of them had seen it.

"Is that it? Is that the picture box?"

He was a big fair boy of twenty or so in a knitted grey scarf wound twice round his neck and tucked into his collar; the girl with him was small and dark, with her bag on her back and a face already trying not to laugh. Wilf Abery and Cady Lorne, by Ottoline's rolls: two of the forty, off the island's first hundred, both flown since September.

"Keep moving," said Ivo, without taking his hand off the crank.

Wilf had seen Merrin's horn. He went to it, while Cady said his name in a voice of warning, and bent and put his face close to its mouth, the way a man puts his face to a well.

"Is it on?" he said into it, very loud. Then louder, as if it went all the way to Wayford: "Mam. I've got my scarf."

Cady took him by the scarf and pulled him off it backwards, laughing so hard she nearly dropped her bag; and the women along the rope laughed, and the men off the shift, and Merrin with her hand on the pole; and Wilf went red to the hair and let himself be towed up the gangway by the neck, waving at the camera over his shoulder. Ivo, who did not laugh at anything while the crank was going, cranked, and said under his breath, "That's going in."

Ward ticked them both.

Declan went up last but one. His wife was at the rope's end, a small calm woman in a brown shawl who did not wave; he said something to her nobody else heard, and she put her hand flat on his chest for a breath, the way you would feel whether a stove was lit, and he went up.

Rylan went up last. Nessa had walked down from the lower ward on Emeric's arm, because Ward had asked him to bring her and he had not asked why; and she stood at the rope and did not move until her husband was in front of her.

"You'll write," she said.

"I'll be on the set."

"That's not writing. That's Ferra reading numbers." She took his coat by both lapels and looked at the buttons. "Your second button's going."

"It's been going for a year."

"Then it'll go on the Isle, and you'll lose it in the sand, and you'll come home with your coat open like a carter." She let go. "Come home with your coat open, then."

"I will," said Rylan.

She did not kiss him. She turned and went back along the rope with her head down, and the women let her through and closed behind her.

Rylan looked after her a while. Then at Emeric.

"I'll tell you when they come. On the set. Every line of it." He hitched his bag up. "You'll be busy."

"I'll listen."

"I know you will," said Rylan. "That's what I'm worried about," and grinned, the old grin from the carriage box on the south road thirteen years ago; and went up, and at the top turned and looked down once at the rope and the town behind it, and went in.

Ward ticked his name and closed the roll.

"Five hundred and forty," he said, to nobody. "Aboard."

They would sleep aboard at the quay, as Ward wanted, so that the last night home was a night in a ship and not a bed. The gangways came up at dusk. Ivo carried his camera off on his shoulder and Merrin wound her cable, and the women from ward four left the pie basket on the quay under a cloth; and somebody came down a rope from escort four's deck for it, and went up again with it, to a noise that could be heard at the sheds.



He went down to the Forge at eleven that night, before he went home.

Osric was there, on the sack against the wall, with his elbows on his knees and his coat off, because his coat was over Tess; and Tess was asleep on the bench. She had gone to sleep where she sat, her head on her arms and her stick fallen off the stool's edge onto the floor; and in front of her face, a hand's breadth from her nose, in its cradle, with her two hands still loosely round it as if she had forgotten to let go, was escort four's stone.

"She said wake her at nine," said Osric, low. "I didn't."

"No."

"She finished at a quarter to. I felt her come out. I can't feel much, but I can feel that." He rubbed his face with both hands. "She said, that's four, and put her head down. I've got the count, and I'm sitting in a cellar."

"You're sitting in a cellar," Emeric agreed.

"I'll go up in a bit," said Osric, and did not move.

Emeric did not touch the stone, because her hands were on it. He put his sense into it instead, lightly, the way he would have walked into a room where somebody was asleep: the clear cold weight of it, and the one facet on its stern face, Anchor, square and certain, line on line in Tess's hand and nobody else's; and near the thick end, the thing she had done about the feather. She had not written over it, or through it. The facet's lines went out wide on either side of the white flaw and met again past it, like a river round a rock, so that when the whole of the stone's day went out through that face it would part at the feather and close behind it and never once touch it.

He had not thought of that. He would have wanted another stone, and there had not been one, and she had known there would not be.

"Fletcher's men are at the yard at six," he said.

"I'll carry it," said Osric. "When she lets go of it."

Emeric picked her stick up off the floor and leaned it against the bench where her hand would find it, and went up the cellar stair into the cold, and across the inner ward toward the one window in the keep that was still lit.
