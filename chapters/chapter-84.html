Chapter 84 - Messages

Rylan put Osric on the ground in the garrison yard a little after dawn, in front of thirty-two men and a row of lime trees just coming into leaf, and then let Osric put him on the ground instead, which was the part he had come to show them.

He lay on his back in the dirt. Osric stood over him with a practice blade and leaned down, the way a man leans down to finish another man who is already beaten, and Rylan's legs came up off the ground faster than anyone in the yard had seen him move all spring and went round Osric's neck and one of his arms together, and locked at the ankle, and pulled. Osric's face went red, then a darker red. He put his free hand on Rylan's knee and patted it twice, quickly, and Rylan let go, and Osric sat down in the dirt beside him and breathed and said several things about Rylan's mother.

"On the port," Rylan said, getting up, to the yard, "when we were boys, the big ones always won standing up. So you learned to lose on your back." He dusted his hands. "Then I went for a soldier, and it turned out it's the same in the mud. A man who's down is a man everyone stops watching. The one who's beaten him leans over. Everybody leans over. Nobody can help it."

Emeric had seen it before. Not here. On a screen, on his other world, two men on a mat in a lit hall with a crowd round them, and one of them on his back, losing, and then not. He had not thought about it in three years. He found that his legs remembered the shape of it before he did.

Ward put them in pairs. Emeric drew Declan, who leaned over him with the patient face of a man who has been told to lose, and Emeric got his legs up and round on the third try and locked them wrong, and Declan pointed out mildly which ankle went where, and on the fifth try Declan patted his knee.

"Again," said Ward, from the well.

They did it again until the sun was over the wall. At the end of it Sela's youngest, who had come in at the gate to watch as he did every training morning and was not supposed to be there, was lying on his back in the corner by the lime trees with his legs round the neck of a boy a head taller than he was, and the bigger boy was patting his knee, and Arthur was looking at Rylan to see whether Rylan had seen.

Rylan had.



Armand's students came at noon, three of them, on a carrier's cart off the north road, sitting on their bags among sacks of somebody else's flour.

They were not what the town had expected. The town had heard mages and expected the Circle: the coat, the ring worn outside the glove, the way of looking over a gate crew's heads at something more interesting behind them. These three got down off the cart stiffly, like anyone who has sat on a flour sack for three days, and paid the carrier, and argued with him a little about the third day, and one of them shook the flour out of her skirts against the wheel and looked round the gate yard with the face of a farm wife pricing a market.

Paskin was the eldest, fifty or near it, square and grey, with the hands of a man who has shifted a great deal of earth by both methods. He had spent twenty years holding up a quarry face for a lord in the west who had never once come to see it. Dorren was young, from somewhere on the south coast that Emeric did not know, with a coast accent and a grin he could not keep off his face, and he had come, he told the gate crew before anyone asked, because Master Armand's letter was the first letter he had ever had with his name on it. The woman was Linnet Shaw. She was forty or past it, brown and weathered and dry, and her affinity was air, and for twenty years she had kept a lord's granaries in the north, drying the grain in a wet harvest and winnowing it in a still one, and been paid less than his steward and more than his dogs, which was, she said, about where she had expected to be.

Armand received them in his room off the hall like a man pretending not to be pleased.

He did it from the chair, with the blanket over his knees and the writing board across it, because he could not now do it standing, and they saw that the moment they came through the door. Emeric saw them see it. Paskin's face did not change at all, which was a thing a face had to work at. Dorren's changed and he looked at the floor. Linnet Shaw stood in front of the chair with her bag still in her hand and looked at her old teacher for some time.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I look old." Armand smiled. "It's different. You'd know if you'd ever listened in class."

"I listened. You said it was the same."

"Then I was wrong." He turned the smile on the other two. "Sit down. Not there. Those are Milford."

They were housed by the evening in two of the tithe houses on the lane behind the north well, which Ferra had had swept, and they were given work by the next morning, because Armand did not see the point of a mage who was not doing something. The neighbors' stones came first. Saban had been asking up and down the coast since the winter who would pay to have spent stones charged in Kaldo, and the answer had been more people than Ferra expected: two of the larger farms on the west side, a coaster out of the salt runs, and a dyer in the coast town who had heard about the soap and wanted to know what else Kaldo did. The fees went into Ferra's book on their own line under running work. Three more mages filling stones in Armand's room, where Armand could watch every one of them, was three more lines.

Ward counted them. He did it at the gate, as they got down off the cart, without moving his lips.

"Three," he said to Emeric afterward, in the gate passage.

"There'll be more."

"I know." Ward looked out at the gate yard, where Dorren was showing the gate crew something with a handful of gravel that made it stand up off his palm in a little pillar. "Three strangers who can cast, inside the walls. Sleeping on the lane behind the north well." He did not say anything more about it. He did not need to.

Ferra said it at her trestle, with figures. The three wages started from the day they came through the gate. Their fees would not cover their wages until the summer, and that was if the neighbors went on sending stones, and if the coaster paid. Until then the barony was paying for them out of the soap and the spirit, a little more each week, and she wrote what it would come to by midsummer at the foot of the page and turned the page round so that he could read it.

"Armand said ugly," he said.

"Armand was being kind." She turned the page back. "It's this."

Out on the barony farm that week, Farris had the seed drill in the wheat ground for the third spring under the rotation, and nobody came in from the west side to argue about it.



The grading was Armand's idea, and Linnet Shaw did it.

Anyone who was going to handle the barony's charged stones had to be read first. That was the rule in every tower and every lord's house that kept stones, she said, and Kaldo would keep it too, because a charged fire stone in a hand that could not hold it burned the hand, and a man who had been burned by a stone once never trusted one again, and that was a waste of a man. So on the second afternoon she set a box of drained fire stones on the edge of the well in the garrison yard, each one dark and grey and no bigger than a walnut, and every man who stood a turn on the wall or carried for the still crew came and held one in his bare hand for a count of a hundred, and gave it back, and she held it up to the light and closed her eyes and told Dorren a number, and Dorren wrote it on a slate.

The garrison read low. Everybody read low. A stone held for a count of a hundred by a man with nothing in him came back very nearly as grey as it had gone out, with a breath of warmth in it that Linnet could feel and nobody else could. She did not seem to think it was a bad thing. Most of the world, she said, was a breath.

Then the four knights.

Osric held his stone for his hundred and gave it back, and Linnet held it up and opened her eyes and looked at him. Declan's next. Then Rylan's. Then Ward's, which Ward handed her without a word and walked away from before she had read it. Dorren wrote the four numbers at the bottom of the slate, under all the others, and then looked at the slate, and then took it to Emeric without being told to and held it out.

The four numbers at the bottom were not on the same slate as the others. They were not in the same country.

Emeric looked at the slate a while. Then he looked across the yard at the wall by the guardhouse door, where Ward's own sheets had been nailed up since the summer before last, one for every man, a line a week. Sacks to the shoulder. The wall walk run against the sand-glass. A man carried thirty paces and then forty. Every line on every sheet went up. Two years of Ward's yard had put a hand's breadth on every man in it.

And on Linnet's slate, the oldest hands in the garrison, men who had stood in Ward's yard every morning for two years, read exactly what the boy who had been signed on the week before read. A breath.

Ward's yard had made every man stronger. It had not moved one man's capacity by so much as the warmth in a stone. And in two years nobody had asked why, because nobody had known there was anything to ask.



The fire in the garrison yard was lit most evenings in April, because the nights were still cold and the guardhouse fire smoked, and the men who were not on the wall sat round it until they were sent to bed.

That night there were five. The garrison had gone in. The four knights sat on the upturned buckets and the log by the well, and Emeric sat on the ground with his back against the well's stone, and the lime trees moved a little over them in the dark, and the fire made the circle of light that a fire makes, and outside it the yard was black.

"What makes a knight?" Emeric said.

Nobody answered at once. Osric looked at the fire. Declan looked at Osric. Rylan, on the log, had his elbows on his knees and was turning a stick round in his fingers.

"A sergeant," said Osric, at last. "A sergeant with a paper."

He told it well. He always told things well, and this one he had told before, Emeric could see, many times, and polished, the way men polish the stories they want to be known by. A recruiting sergeant in the yard of an inn, with a barrel of beer and a paper. Osric the third son of a man with two sons too many, sixteen and ready to be anywhere else. The paper said two years. He signed. He served the two years, and at the end of them he went to the sergeant's captain with the paper, and the captain read it to him, slowly, because by then Osric could read a little and the captain wanted him to follow along, and the paper said six.

"Six," said Osric, to the fire. "It said six. I'd been looking at a six for two years, thinking it was a two. That's what makes a knight. A sergeant who writes his sixes like twos." He laughed. They all laughed, because it was funny, because he had made it funny. Declan laughed last and least, and looked at Osric afterward, at the side of his face, in a way that said he had heard this story told before in a different voice, at a different hour, and knew what it cost, and was not going to say so.

"Declan," Emeric said.

"House guard at sixteen." Declan said it lightly, the way he said most painful things. "At nineteen I was noticed."

"For what?"

"For checking things. Doors. Who was on which door. Whether the man on the roster was the man in the corridor." He shrugged. "Somebody in the capital wanted to know whether a door was locked, and I was the one who always knew. So they made me something that could stand in front of the door as well." He smiled at the fire. "It is not a noble occupation. It is a useful one."

Emeric looked at Ward.

Ward sat on his bucket with his hands between his knees. He had been listening the way he listened to everything, with no sign of it on his face. He did not look up.

"A knight is a man who stays where he is put," Ward said. "Whatever comes. That is all it is. The rest is armour."

It was a good answer. It was an answer to a different question. Emeric heard it go past him and land somewhere else, and he realized, sitting against the cold stone of the well with the fire in his face, that he had known Ward for fifteen years and Ward had never once told him a single fact about himself. Not where he was born. Not who had put him forward. Not what had been done to him in what room. Nothing. Every thing Emeric knew about Ward, he knew because he had watched it.

He did not ask. Ward would not have answered, and Emeric would have known he had asked.

The fire settled. A log broke and went down into the red.

"I'll tell it," Rylan said.



He told it in order, from the beginning, plainly, the way a man gives evidence. He did not look at any of them while he told it. He looked at the stick in his hands.

"I grew up on the port," he said. "Here. When it was a port. A boy on the port had two roads out. The river or the army. I went for the army at nineteen." The stick turned. "We were a thin unit. They kept it thin on purpose. They didn't fill it up when men died, they waited till there was nothing left of it and filled it all at once. A year and a half of that. Then the campaign ended, and the captain sat down with his list and crossed off the dead, and there were eleven of us left on it, and he put four forward."

"For what?" said Emeric, though he knew.

"For the rite." Rylan turned the stick. "There was no test. Nobody asked us. Nobody tested anything. The captain had a list and four names on it and mine was one of them." He was quiet. "I was proud. For about a week. I thought it meant I was something."

Osric had stopped looking at the fire. He was looking at the ground between his boots.

"The night before," Rylan said, "we did what the unit always did. Each man tells the man beside him what to say, and who to, if he doesn't come out. A message. I couldn't read or write, so I held them in my head. I took three, one from each of the others. I gave mine to all three of them. So that whoever came out would have it."

"Then the room. Stone, no windows. A drain in the floor." He said the drain the way he said everything else, as a fact about a room. "Nineteen of us from all over, other units, other houses, stripped to our shirts and laid out on the floor in rows. A cardinal at the head of the room in his vestments, all night. Standing. And a mage." He stopped, and corrected himself, as if somebody had asked him to be exact. "There were more of them. Eight, someone said after. I only ever saw the one at my head. The others were back in the dark past the lamps, taking turns."

"They put it into you from outside. Nothing first. No getting ready. It comes in waves, and you lie there, and it comes again. Nine hours. Fourteen, some of them, I heard." The stick had stopped turning. "Men gave out. Their bodies gave out under the pressure. It was not quick. Some of them died on the stone. Some they carried out still breathing and they died after. I don't know how many." He looked up then, for the first time, at nobody. "I don't know how many. Nobody ever told us. I think about one in ten walked out."

"In the morning the cardinal walked the room. He stopped at every one of the dead and said the man's name, his whole name, and that he was a failure under the Light. And went to the next one." Rylan looked at the fire. "I was near enough to hear every name."

Nobody said anything. A sound came from the wall, the boy on the gate tower walking his turn, and stopped.

"I was never devout," said Rylan. "What made me special is that I lived. A man in vestments stood over a randomness and told it it was a verdict." He put the stick in the fire. "That is randomness, dressed by men, and used twice. Once to make the ones who lived grateful. Once to make the ones who died deserve it."

The stick caught, and burned, and he watched it.

"I walked out at twenty," he said, "holding three messages. Because the other three were dead. I carried one, the next year, on foot, to a woman in a village two days out of the capital. I said it to her at her door, word for word, as near as I had it. The other two I've had so long now I don't trust the words. I don't know any more what's his and what's mine." He looked at his hands. "So I've never taken them."

"And yours?" said Emeric.

"Mine I gave to the three of them," said Rylan. "Nobody walked out holding mine."



Later, when the fire was lower, Emeric asked to see the marks.

He had seen them before, at Ward's measuring the summer before last, on forearms in the sun, and said nothing. He asked now. Osric pushed his sleeves up without a word and held his arms out to the fire, and his were the worst, ring over ring of raised silver from the wrist to the elbow on both arms, so thick in places that the lines ran together into a single shining welt. He opened his shirt at the neck and they went on across his chest. Declan's ran down one side only, the left, the arm and the left of the chest, and stopped as if somebody had drawn a line down the middle of him. Rylan's went higher than either of the others. When he pulled his collar down there was one line, single and fine, running up the side of his neck from the collarbone to just below the ear, where his collar had hidden it every day Emeric had known him.

Ward did not move his sleeves.

"The same," he said.

Emeric looked at the marks in the firelight and saw what they were, and could not have unseen it if he had tried. He had sewn wounds. He knew what a body did when it was made to grow faster than a body grows: it scarred. What the rite had left on these men was a record of how hard they had been forced, and somebody had taught them to read it as a sign that they had been chosen.

He did not say it. It was not his to say, not here, not tonight, to men who had been told for years that the silver on their arms was the Light's handwriting.

The other question was in his mouth too. Which cardinal. He held it there, and found that he did not want the answer, and did not ask, and let it go back down.

"Kaldo won't do that," he said instead.

Osric looked up.

"To anybody," Emeric said. "Not to make knights. Not to make anything. It won't happen here."

"Then how do you make knights?" said Osric.

"I don't know yet."

He had settled it before he had done the sum, and he did the sum anyway, sitting against the well, because he could not stop himself doing sums. Forty of Kaldo's young men, the brightest and strongest of every year, sent up to that room. One in ten walking out. Four knights, and thirty-six names said over a drain.

"Thirty-six," said Rylan, who had done it too. "And some more later."

The fire was nearly out. Declan got up and put a log on it without being asked, and sat down again, closer to Osric than he had been.

"Doran keeps the death book," Emeric said, after a while. "Every name, the day, where they lie." He looked at Rylan. "He could keep letters. Sealed. Any man on the roll who wants to leave one. To whoever he says. Doran keeps the box, and nobody opens anything unless the man doesn't come back."

Rylan did not say anything. He looked at the fire.

"Or nobody opens it at all," said Ward, "if he does."

"Or that."



That night, in his chamber, he took down a book that had nothing in it.

It was one of the books Ferra had bound for him in the winter, out of the grey paper and two boards, for whatever he wanted, and he had not wanted anything in it yet. He opened it at the back, the last leaf, and wrote, in a small hand, what Rylan had said by the fire. Not the whole of it. The four sentences. That a man who could not read had taken the whole of it apart in four sentences, sitting on a log with a stick in his hand, more cleanly than any tower in the kingdom ever would or could.

Under that he wrote that it was the most dangerous thing he had written down since he came to Kaldo, and that the danger was not Rylan, and was not the rite. It was the man in vestments.

He closed the book and put it in the locked drawer with Paxton's letter, and turned the key on its cord, and sat on the edge of the bed with his hands between his knees, the way Ward had sat by the fire, until the candle burned down to the stick.



Doran heard it in the vestry the next morning, standing, with the death book open on the shelf behind him and his hands folded in front of his cassock.

He did not ask why. He asked what kind of box.

"With a lock," Emeric said. "And the key yours."

"And a rule that I never read them," said Doran. "Written up where any man can read it before he puts his letter in. Otherwise no man will put his letter in." He considered the shelf. "It will want to stand beside the book. Not in a cupboard. Where it can be seen to be."

"Yes."

"Then I will need a box," said Doran, in the voice he kept for burial logistics, and offered him a cup of the parish wine, and apologized for it, and apologized for it again when Emeric drank it.

Bram made the box. It was oak, from a tithe house, with brass corners from the bottom of the box under his bench, and a lock he made himself and a key he gave to Doran and to nobody else. It stood on the shelf in the vestry beside the death book from the Thursday, with Doran's rule written on a board above it in Doran's own hand.

The first letter was in it before the week was out. Doran did not say whose, and Emeric did not ask.
