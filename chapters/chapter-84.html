Chapter 84 - The Unsold Hull

She had been built for a man named Aske, who ran cargo out of Wayford down the river to the coast and back, and who had ordered her the spring before the war and paid a third and never paid the rest.

Fletcher told it on the bank beside the outside foundry, with one hand flat on her side, the way a man tells the history of a horse he has been trying to sell for six years. Aske had wanted a river boat that could take the bar in fair weather and go down the coast to Duvrenne, broad and shallow and stiff, fifty feet on the deck, with a hold that would take forty tons and still clear the shoals of the lower river. Fletcher had laid her down that summer and planked her through the autumn, and the autumn had been the war. Aske had backed the wrong side of it with money he had borrowed against his boats, and lost the boats, and was not seen at the lower water again, and nobody had ever come down the water to ask after a half-paid hull on a beach at the bottom of the barony.

So Fletcher had finished her. He had not had to. He had done it in the gaps between smaller boats, over three years, because a hull that is half planked is a hull that is rotting, and he could not bear to watch it. Then he had launched her and moored her in the lagoon and tarred her every autumn, and nobody had bought her, because nobody at the lower water needed a boat that size and nobody anywhere else knew she existed.

"And you never ran her yourself," Emeric said.

"I build them." Fletcher looked at him as though he had asked why a cooper did not drink all his own beer. "Run her with what? Six men I'd have to pay, and cargo I'd have to find, and a trade I don't know the first thing about. A boat with no trade is a mouth. She sits in the water and eats tar and eats my time and gives me nothing back but the look of her." He slapped the planking, not gently. "Look of her's good, mind."

The look of her was very good. Even Emeric could see that, and he did not know boats. She was long and low, with a sheer that rose sweetly at the bow and more gently at the stern, and her planks lay against each other so closely that you could not have got a knife between them, and every fastening in her was a Kaldo nail. He had started her on coast iron, and gone back after the war and drawn every one of those and driven Kaldo nails in their place. He had not been paid for that either.

He had brought her up himself in the second month of the winter, in a mild spell when the river ran open, four days against the current, with a sail when the wind served and two horses on the towpath when it did not, and six men from the lower water who were going home tomorrow. He did not need to stay. He stayed the whole of the first afternoon. He showed Emeric her keel, which was elm, one piece, from a tree he had waited two years for. He showed him her knees, grown oak, every one cut from a bough that had already been the shape of the joint it was going into, and explained why that mattered in a way that was not necessary and that Emeric would not have interrupted for anything. He showed him the scarf joints in her keelson, and then, when Emeric had admired them, showed him again from the other side. He showed Bram. He showed Ward, who looked at the joints with polite interest and said that they were very straight.

It was vanity, and it was earned. The man who had come down the water in the autumn to commission his best work had come back to buy the work he had done before, and the old man was showing it to the one customer in forty years whose opinion he had begun, against his own better judgment, to want.

"Twenty-two gold," he said at the end of it. "As she stands. And you know what she cost me."

"I know what she cost you."

Ferra had entered it that morning, off a letter, in the year's column under the keel payment, and had said that as boats went it was the cheapest thing Kaldo would ever buy and she would like it in writing that it was a boat and not a start.

Fletcher went home in the morning with his six men and the horses, and did not look back at her from the towpath, and Emeric understood that he had deliberately not asked what they were going to do to her.

They had to get her out of the water, and that was the first problem, and it stopped the whole thing for three days.

A boat is repaired on a slip, a greased ramp running down into the water, and hauled up it on a capstan by a dozen men. There was no slip at Kaldo. Nobody had built a boat here since before the plague. There was the bank beside the outside foundry, a flat hard shelf of ground that ran down to the water with a drop at the edge of about a man's height, and Bram had built a set of oak stands on it, a cradle of timbers shaped to her bottom. And there was a hull that weighed, empty, what Fletcher said was about twelve tons, sitting in the water at the foot of the drop.

Bram costed a slip. It would take a month, and pilings, and he did not like the ground. Saban asked in the market for a crane and was laughed at. Somebody suggested digging a trench, flooding it, floating her in and then letting the water out, which was clever and would have taken all spring.

Emeric thought about it for most of the third evening, standing on the bank, and then he thought about the carriage.

He had come south in it ten years ago, a big plain carriage that was a lie in both directions, and it had been out of the coach house behind the keep only a handful of times since, the last of them the long road north to the Crown's court and back. Its four axle housings still held what they had held on the morning he left the capital. Four black stones flecked through with points of light, carved in a dense lattice, which he had crouched to look at in a courtyard at dawn when nobody else was looking at anything.

Gravity stones, cut to a threshold. They did not lift. They pushed back at a fixed weight, and gave, and pushed back again, and that was all they had ever done.

Bram took the housings apart in the morning, and the carriage settled onto its bare axles in the coach house and stayed there, and he carried the four stones down to Armand's bench in a wooden box lined with straw, and put the box down in front of the old man, and told him what he wanted.

Armand looked at the stones for a long time without touching them.

"You understand what these are," he said.

"Threshold stones. They resist and release at a fixed weight."

"Then you understand that they will not lift your boat."

"I understand that they won't lift it by themselves."

Armand took one of the stones out of the straw and turned it in the lamplight, and looked at the carving through a lens for some while, and set it down.

"No," he said. "They will not lift anything. Nobody has ever cut a stone that lifts. The carving tells a stone when to push back. It does not tell it to push." He laid a finger on the lattice. "What I can do is lift. With my own hands, in the tower's way, the way I was taught at nineteen. A man cannot lift a boat. But if I put one of these at each corner of her, and put my work into all four, then they do not have to decide anything. They only have to carry. They spread me. I will be lifting her in four places at once, and each of the four will be a thing I can hold." He put the stone back in the straw. "For some minutes. Not many."

"How many?"

"I have never done it," Armand said. "Let us find out."

They did it on the fourth morning, with the whole of the works on the bank.

Bram's men had got four slings under her in the water, one at each quarter, broad bands of doubled canvas, and in each sling, in an iron cup lashed where it would bear against her bottom, one of the four stones. From the slings ran eight lines up to the bank and round eight posts, and on each line four men. Armand stood at the top of the drop, above her midships, with his coat off and his sleeves rolled, which nobody in Kaldo had ever seen, and his hands out in front of him at the height of his chest, palms down.

He did not do anything for what felt like a long time. Emeric felt him begin before anything moved. It was like the change in the air before a storm, a pressure behind the ears, and it came from the old man in a steady pour that did not waver, and went down into the water, into four places, and spread.

The boat rose.

She came up out of the river slowly, the water sheeting off her strakes and then running and then dripping, until her whole keel was clear and she hung in the four slings with nothing under her but air and the river three feet below. She did not tip. She did not swing. She hung level, dripping, turning very slightly on the lines, twelve tons of oak and iron held up by an old man's open hands.

Nobody on the bank said a word.

"Walk her," Armand said, through his teeth.

The men on the lines walked her. She came in over the edge of the drop, slow as a cloud, and the stands were under her, and Armand let her down.

He did not let her down all at once. She came down onto the oak in a long cushioned settle, and came back up a finger's width, and went down again, less, and up again, less, and was still. Three movements, each smaller than the last.

Emeric had seen that before. In a courtyard at dawn, looking at the underside of a carriage as it took a man's weight on its step. The same three movements, the same dying-away, in the same stones, ten years and a week's road apart.

He did not say anything about it.

He stood on the bank and looked at the boat on her stands, and at the slings lying slack, and at the four black stones in their iron cups, and he thought about what he had just watched. Four stones. One man. A hull, held level in the air, above water, for as long as it had taken eight lines of men to walk it twenty yards. He thought about that for what felt like some time and was probably a few seconds, and he did not say what he was thinking, to anybody, and he did not let his face say it either.

Ward was watching him. Ward was always watching him. He looked at the boat, and at the prince looking at the boat, and at the prince's face saying nothing, and went on watching, and did not ask.

Armand sat down on the bank.

He sat down the way a man sits down when his legs have decided the matter for him, and stayed there with his forearms on his knees and his head hanging, grey in the face, breathing as though he had run up the keep stair. Imani, who had come down to see what the whole island was looking at, was beside him before anybody else had moved, with two fingers on the inside of his wrist.

"Four minutes, near enough," Bram said. He had counted.

"It felt longer," said Armand, to the ground.

Emeric crouched beside him. The old man looked up at him, and even grey and shaking he looked at him the way he looked at a baseline.

"You could have helped," Armand said. "Held one corner. You have the gift. I felt you standing there with it."

"I can't."

"Can't."

"I've never trained it." It was not a thing he had said aloud before, to anybody, and he found it came out easier than he expected, perhaps because the old man was sitting on the ground in front of him and had just paid for the thing he was admitting. "Water, air and stone, for eight years, every night I didn't sleep. The stone's a slab I lift and let fall, and I've been taking it smaller for years. A flagstone, a brick, a pebble. Everything I've ever held up, I've carried myself. I've never once put the weight into a stone and let the stone take it. It never left me short. There was always something else to do with the time." He looked at the boat. "I let it slide."

"For eight years."

"For eight years."

Armand looked at him for a moment longer, with an expression Emeric could not quite read, and then nodded, slowly, as though a figure on a sheet had just been corrected by someone who ought to have corrected it sooner.

"Threshold stones hold," the old man said. "A caster lifts. That is all there is in it. I held that boat for four minutes and I will be no use to anybody until tomorrow." He put a hand on Imani's shoulder and got himself up by it, heavily. "Write that down somewhere. You will want it."

He went up to the keep and slept until the next morning, and nobody saw him for the rest of the day.

The stationary engine stayed in its shed. The boat got a second one, smaller, off the same patterns, with a boiler half the size and a cylinder bored on the second lathe in a week because Jorrin had done it once already and knew where the difficult part was. That engine was the one that would travel. Bram said it was the first thing the works had ever made twice, and that the second one had taken a third of the time, and that he would like everybody to remember that.

The boiler went in amidships, on a bed of brick laid in iron trays, with a sheet of iron under the trays and a sheet of iron up the planking round it and a gap for air between the iron and the wood, and a pump that could flood the bed from the river. A fire, inside a boat made of fuel, on a river, a day from help. Nobody said it that plainly out loud. Everybody working on her thought it, and built accordingly, and Bram went round every evening with his palm flat on the planking near the bed to feel for warmth.

The shaft went out through her stern, low down, through a hole bored in her deadwood with a long auger, a day's work for four men. Where it went out through the wood it ran in a sleeve, and the sleeve had a gland at its inner end, packed with tallowed hemp in rings, to keep the river out the way the other gland kept the steam in. Rudd packed it. He came down from the engine shed with his sheet inside his shirt and packed it without being asked, and said it was the same job the other way round.

The propeller came off the hook on the shed wall and went on the end of the shaft, under her stern, and the whole works came to watch that too, and it was the first time anybody at the works had been told out loud what it was for. Tobe said he had known it all along. Wick said he had not, and that Tobe had not either, and that Tobe had said it was a fan.

It went wrong in the third week, and it went wrong the way he had half known it would.

They ran the engine on her stands, with the shaft turning the propeller in the air, to see everything run true before she went back in the water. For an hour it ran true. Then the shaft began to knock where it came through the stern. Bram crawled under her with a lamp and came out and said that the engine had moved.

It had moved about the width of a fingernail. The bed it sat on was bolted to her frames, and her frames were oak, and oak gave. Every stroke of the piston pushed the engine one way and pulled it back, and the oak under the bolts had given a little to each push, and the bolt holes had worn, and the engine had crept. The shaft, running from an engine that had crept to a sleeve that had not, was no longer straight. And the hull itself was not still. She had flexed as the engine ran, bending very slightly along her length with every stroke, the way a plank flexes if you jump on it, and a shaft that is straight when the plank is still is not straight when the plank is bending.

They fixed it the way it could be fixed in wood. Two long iron bearers, rolled on the mill and riveted up into girders, running the length of her engine room and bolted down through six frames on either side, so that the engine pulled against the whole of her middle and not against a few bolts. Iron wedges driven hard. The shaft taken down and trued and put up again. It took a fortnight, and it worked, and it would go on working as long as somebody went round the bolts every week with a spanner and felt the shaft for knocking.

He stood under her stern on the last evening of it, looking up at the shaft where it came out through the oak.

Wood and iron did not move the same way, and never would, and every place in this boat where one was fastened to the other was a place that would go on quietly wearing itself loose for as long as she floated. The iron hull on Fletcher's beach would not have that problem, because every piece of her would move the same way as every other piece. That was what she was for. He thought it once, plainly, standing under the stern with the lamp, and did not think it again.

Fletcher heard about the hole in her deadwood from one of his six men, who had heard it from a carter, and wrote about it. It was a short letter in a large hand, and it said that he was not going to ask what they had done to her and did not want to be told, and that if they had cut the deadwood across the grain he would like to know which fool had held the auger, and that she had been a good boat, and that he was sure she still was. It said nothing else. Ferra filed it under the boat.

The thaw came late that year, in the first days of the spring, and on the first morning that the ground would take a spade, Imani broke it.

She did it at the top of the long street, on the empty plot behind the grain factor's store where her fourteen beds were, which Ferra had bought from the factor's heirs in the autumn for a sum Ferra described as the only good price she had got all year. There were four of them there, and a boy with a spade. Imani, and Emeric, and Ward, who was always there, and Doran, who had come out of the church in his old coat because he had heard, and stood at the edge of the plot saying nothing.

Imani took a folded sheet out of the inside of her coat. It had been folded so often that the creases had gone soft and white. She opened it and read, aloud, not the date, which they all knew, but the name at the foot of it, in his hand, the way she had read it at the midwinter before last, as though it were a dosage.

Then she folded it again, and put it back inside her coat, and took the spade from the boy, and put it in the ground.

That was all. She did not make a speech, and he did not, and Doran did not, and after she had turned the first spit of earth she gave the spade back to the boy and told him to go on, and went back into the store to see to somebody.

The message came up the Wayford line two days later, on an ordinary afternoon, while the boat was still on her stands.

It came in Wayford's hand, from the Wayford end, through eleven posts, each of which logged a string of marks that meant nothing to the man writing them down, and it arrived at the Kaldo end, and the man at the Kaldo end logged it, and sent it up the stair to Ferra. She took it out of the marks with the Kaldo end's half of the key, in her head, and wrote it down.

The junction post at Wayford was down. A crack in the housing, the stone out of true. Repairs expected to take some weeks. Until further word, Kaldo was to carry all Wayford traffic by rider.

It was plausible. Posts went down. A stone went out of true in frost, and the first winter of any new line was the one that found out which housings had been set badly. It was the kind of message the relay had been built to carry, and it had carried it in a morning, and it decoded perfectly.

Ferra brought it to him with that last fact written in the margin, underlined once.

"It's clean," she said.

He read it twice. Then he read the margin.

"That's what's wrong with it."

"Yes."

A message that came up the line garbled could have been anything: a tired man at a post, a bad lamp, frost in a housing. A message that came up the line clean, in Wayford's hand, with Wayford's key, had been put onto the line by somebody who held Wayford's key. There were only three men in the world who could open the box at the Wayford counting house where Wayford's half lived. Pennock. The keeper of the post, a former courier Saban had hired. The night clerk. Saban had said it at the table the day the relay was costed: whoever sat at the Wayford end could tell them anything he liked in Wayford's hand, and they would believe it, because it came up their own line.

They did not believe it. They did not disbelieve it either. They did nothing, which was the Order's own rule: the riders kept running. Every one of them, on the same days, whatever the lamps said.

The ordinary rider came in from Wayford two days later, on the ordinary day, with the ordinary bag, and in it, as always, the paper logs of every post on the Wayford line for the week, copied out fair by each keeper and carried south by hand so that the stones could be checked against the ink.

The junction post's log showed it working every day of the week. It had passed forty-one strings of marks, with the hour against each. One of them, on the afternoon in question, was the message that said it was down.

Ferra laid the two sheets side by side on the table. Emeric looked at them. Saban, who had been sent for, looked at them longer.

Nothing had been lost. In the two days between, Kaldo had had nothing for Wayford that could not wait, and the riders had run as they always ran. Whoever had sent it had learned only that Kaldo did not stop its riders on the word of a lamp, which was worth something to somebody and worth rather more to Kaldo.

Nobody could say who had put it on the line. The box had three keys. The Wayford end kept a log of its own, and the log said nothing, because the man who had written the message had not written it in the log.

"Pay records," Saban said. "For the post. For the counting house. Every man who's been paid at the Wayford end since the line opened, and what, and by whom, and when." He was already standing. "And the keeper's. The one I hired."

"Tonight?"

"Tonight," said Saban, and went down the stair to write for them, and did not come back up until the lamps had been lit for some hours, and when he did, he had nothing yet, and said so.
