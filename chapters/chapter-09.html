Chapter 9 - The Ash Trade

"Boiling fixes the water," Imani said. "It doesn't fix hands."

They were standing at the north well's fire in the seventh week, watching a girl of about ten fill a kettle with her bare hands straight out of a bucket she'd just carried up from a privy-yard. Imani said it the way she said most things, as a fact that had been true for some time and that she was tired of being the only one to notice.

"Or cloth," she added. "They wash the bandages in the same water they boiled, with the same hands they wiped the baby with. Then they wonder."

Emeric had been thinking it for a fortnight. He'd been waiting to be sure before he said so, because a promise made too early in this town lasted a long time.

So first he checked. Soap existed here. He'd seen it in the capital: small scented cakes from the south coast, hard and pretty, that cost about a week of lower-ward food apiece. And the cloth trade used a grey, gritty scouring paste for wool, cheap and harsh and nothing you'd put on a child. What the lower ward needed was soap a household could buy every week without going hungry for it.

It didn't need inventing. It needed making cheap, and making the same every time.



He needed a soap maker, and Kaldo didn't have one. It did, Nessa told him, have a man who'd known how before the port died: Abloh, the sailmaker, the one who could do anything with cloth, who'd gone downriver with Fletcher.

It took Rylan a week to find him. He came back saying two days downriver, a reed village on the bank, Abloh living in a hut three streets from Fletcher.

It was the first time Emeric had left Kaldo since he'd arrived. Rylan came, and Ward, and they went down the river on a hired flatboat through country that was mostly marsh and heron.

Abloh was a lean, grey-whiskered man sitting on a stool outside a hut, mending a net. He'd heard of the prince; everybody downriver had, mostly the version where he was a drunk. He listened to what Emeric wanted with a fisherman's polite patience.

"Soap," he said. "I've made soap since I was fourteen. Soft soap. Fat and lye, in a pot." He shrugged. "Everybody's grandmother knows soft soap."

Emeric asked for a pot, a fire, some fat and some ash, and showed him two things. Lime, burned limestone, stirred into the ash liquor before you used it, so the lye came out stronger and cleaner. And salt, at the end, thrown into the hot soap so it came apart from the water and floated up hard, so you could cut it into bars that didn't go to slime in a week.

Abloh watched him do it once. Then he took the spoon, did it again himself, faster and better, and stood looking down at the pot.

Then he sat down on his stool, rubbed his face, and asked what the wage was.

"And Fletcher?" Rylan said, on the way back to the boat.

Emeric had been thinking about Fletcher since they'd tied up. A shipwright, three streets away. Kaldo had a river full of empty moorings and no ships, and a shipwright was exactly the man it would need, in a year or five. And there was nothing for a shipwright to do this year. He couldn't say when there would be.

So he didn't walk the three streets. A prince turning up at a man's door with empty hands was a promise, whatever he said, and he had nothing to promise.

Abloh came alone. Fletcher sent a message with him: that a man offered a wage takes it, and he didn't blame Abloh, and he'd hear if there was ever anything for him.



Soap needed four things, and every one of them came out of Emeric's own purse. If this failed, it would cost him and nobody else. That mattered here. A prince who lost his own money was a fool. A prince who lost the barony's was something worse.

Fat came from the butcher, and from the fishing boats, which rendered more of it than anyone wanted.

Ash came from every hearth in the town. He bought it. He put out word that the soap yard would pay for wood ash by the bucket, and for the first time in their lives the poorest households in Kaldo were paid for something they'd always thrown away. That changed how doors opened to him more than anything he'd said at them. In the lower ward, four hundred hearths. Four hundred doors that had a reason to know his name.

Lime needed a kiln. There was one, outside the walls, cold for ten years. It took nine days and an old man to light it again. He was past seventy and had worked it as a boy, and he refused to take a wage for the first week, on the grounds that he wanted to see whether it would still draw before he took money for it. It drew.

Salt came by boat from the coast at the bottom of the barony, once a month. The boatmen were paid badly, and said so. He didn't think about what else a working lime kiln might be good for. And it didn't occur to him that by buying salt from the coast he'd given Kaldo a reason to want a working harbor again. Fletcher, two days downriver, heard about the salt runs within the week.



The ash came in faster than anyone could count it, so somebody else counted it.

He found her at the scales in the soap yard one morning with a tally board and a stub of chalk. She was weighing bucket after bucket as the children brought them in, paying out coppers from a bag at her hip, writing it all down in a hand so even it looked ruled. It took him a moment to place her. Then he had it: the back corner of Bram's forge, the ledger, the red hair. Bram's daughter. In daylight the freckles were more than a dusting, and she'd tied a cloth over her hair against the ash, and it didn't do much good.

Bram had lent her, she said, when he asked. She didn't seem to think it needed more explanation than that.

"Your price," she said, while he was still deciding what to say next. "For the ash."

"Is it wrong?"

"It works now." She chalked a line. "In summer, people burn just enough to cook. In winter every house in this town burns twice that and more, to keep warm." She looked at him then, for the first time. "You'll have three times the ash by midwinter, at the same price. You can't sell three times the soap. The price won't survive the winter."

He worked it through in his head. She was right. He'd set the price for the season in front of him and never thought about the one after it.

He opened his mouth to thank her, and to ask her name.

Somebody screamed from the far end of the yard.



The third batch had gone out too strong.

It was his fault and he knew it at once. Demand had run ahead of what they could make, and that morning he'd let a batch go out to the street fires without testing the lye himself, because it had looked right and he was in a hurry. He knew exactly why you tested. He had known for twenty years of somebody else's career. He'd skipped it anyway.

A woman from the lower ward had washed her hands with it and the skin had come up red and raw across both palms.

Imani got there before he did. She had the woman's hands in a basin of clean water and was talking to her in a low, steady voice, and when Emeric came through the crowd she looked up at him and kept talking, to him now, in exactly the same voice, in front of the woman and everybody else.

What he'd done. Why it had happened. That he had known better, and that knowing better and doing it anyway was worse than not knowing at all.

He stood and took it. Every word was true.

The fix was a test anyone could run without him there. Float a fresh egg in the lye before you use it. If it sinks, the lye's too weak. If it floats and shows a patch above the surface wider than a copper coin, it's too strong: add water until it shows a copper's width, no more. Abloh had known a version of it already. They made it the rule.

Emeric paid the woman a month's wages that afternoon, before she or anyone else had asked.

Bram, who'd come down from the forge when he heard, watched him do it and said nothing. Emeric caught him looking, and couldn't read his face.

He didn't see Bram's daughter again that day. He realized that evening that he still didn't know her name.



The first two hundred bars went out free, on Imani's rounds, to every house that boiled.

Then a fisherman came to the yard and bought six. Then the inn sent a boy for a box. A tanner came, looked at the bars a long time, and took forty. And a carter who ran the Wayford road stopped his wagon at the yard gate, turned a bar over in his hands, smelled it, and asked what it would cost him by the hundredweight.

Emeric couldn't fill a hundredweight. Not yet. The salt came once a month, the kiln was small, and the pots took the time they took. He told the man so, and took his name, and said he'd send word.

Nine days later, in the lower market, a man stood up on a barrow and said it out loud.

The prince says the sickness comes from filth. Then the prince sells you the cure. Ask yourselves who that's good for.

It was fair. It was exactly what Emeric would have said, in that man's shoes, and the crowd turned to see what the prince would do about it.

So Emeric climbed up on the next barrow and told them how to make it.

All of it. At the volume of the market, so the back of the crowd could hear. The ash, and how to leach it. The lime, and how much. The fat. The salt at the end. The egg and the copper. Then he said it again, slower, from the start, because the first time half of them hadn't believed he was doing it.

Anyone could make it, he said. Anyone could sell it. He'd go on buying ash at the same price either way, whether they made their own soap or not. And if they didn't want the trouble, it was two coppers a bar at the soap yard.

The man on the barrow bought two bars on the way home. He was back the next week for more.

Imani found Emeric afterward, by the yard gate.

"Well done," she said. "You've just taught four hundred people how to cook caustic liquor in kitchens full of children."

He hadn't thought of that.

By the end of the month Abloh was teaching it, on a fixed morning, in the open part of the yard: how to make it, and how to test it, and how not to burn yourself or your children. He was paid for the mornings out of the yard's first takings. It was the first thing in Kaldo that had been taught rather than told.



The money started coming in, and he kept none of it.

It went to the kiln, to the wages, to the salt. What was left, he took to Wexler's signed order, the one that said the ground and the permission existed when the funds did. Now some of the funds did. The first two wells went into the ground above the rows, and men who hadn't had a day's paid work since the plague were digging them.

On a grey, wet afternoon near the end of June, Wexler came down to the soap yard.

He was pleasant. He looked at the pots and the drying racks and Abloh's teaching corner. He asked a few questions about the salt boats. Then, just before he left, he turned at the gate, as if it had only now occurred to him.

"How will your ash price hold," he said, "when the winter comes?"

He didn't wait for an answer. He went back up the hill, and Emeric understood, watching him go, that Wexler had stopped waiting for the prince to fail.

That night Emeric wrote it all down at the back of Armand's notebook. Wells begun. Kiln lit. Ash priced wrong. The rows, still where they were. Fletcher, downriver. Petram, two doors down and silent. Garrin, somewhere. Letters to the capital that nobody had answered.

At the bottom, after a while, he wrote one more line and underlined it.

Two of my father's men were not my father's men.

Wexler, that night, was making a list of his own. It was shorter, and every item on it could be counted.
