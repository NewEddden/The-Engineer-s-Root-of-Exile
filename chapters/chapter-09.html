Chapter 9 - What the Ground Does

The window in his room no longer fit its frame, and had not for some years, and there was a draught coming through the gap with a quality to it that no draught in the castle at Randillia City had ever had.

"Right," he said to nobody, at first light. "We're not in the castle anymore."

He asked to come along on Rylan's homecoming.

Rylan, to his credit, offered to go alone, which was the polite version and also the true preference. Emeric said he had not seen the town, and that Rylan had a reason to walk through it that was not a prince inspecting anything, and that the second part was worth more to him than the first.

It was not entirely honest. Rylan's face said he knew it. He let it go.

Ward was immovable and did not bother pretending to be asking.

He would not have Emeric walking an unmapped town with one guard and a title nobody in it respected, and he said why, which he rarely did: Rylan was a father going home to his children, he would not be watching the street behind them, and he should not have to be.

"You won't see me."

Emeric traded. There was a healer in the town. Have her look at the arm.

Ward said the arm was fine. The arm was eight days old and he had been sleeping on it.

Ward said he would not be seen, which was apparently his whole answer to that as well.

He came. By the gatehouse he had dropped back, and the second time Emeric looked for him he was not anywhere a guard is supposed to be.

The smell got worse downhill.

Not uphill. That was the first thing that did not fit, and he noted it without understanding it, and walked on with it sitting crossways in him.

Then he found the burial rows, and they were inside the walls, and he worked the whole thing out standing in front of them.

An island with one bridge cannot bury a plague.

You cannot carry four hundred bodies across a single span. You cannot open your gate to a mainland that is burying its own. And every day you wait, the problem gets heavier.

So they had used the churchyard. Then the ground beside the churchyard. Then wherever there was ground.

The people who made that decision had been terrified and correct and were mostly dead now.

He turned and looked downhill from the rows and the rest of it assembled itself without any effort on his part at all.

The wells sat downslope. The island was a knob of stone under a thin skin of soil, and everything that went into that skin travelled down through it toward the branches, and the wells were sunk directly into the path. The latrines were dug where digging was easiest, which was the low ground, which was where the wells were. Below the town the two branches rejoined, and the people too old to walk uphill drew straight from the bank.

Everything the town did to itself, it did upstream of its own mouth.

There was no away. There was no away anywhere on this rock.

And the one clean thing within reach was the entire lake sitting directly above them, which nobody drew from, because the wells were closer and always had been.

He walked down below the town, to where the two branches came back together, because he wanted to find out whether he was wrong.

There was a woman at the bank with a yoke and two buckets. She was old enough that the climb up to the wells was obviously the reason she was standing down here and not up there, and she filled both of them out of water that the entire island had spent the morning standing upstream of.

She nodded to him because he was a person standing near her. He nodded back.

He did not say anything, because there was nothing available to say that would not have been either a lecture or a lie, and because the nearest clean water in the world was four hundred feet above her head, at the top of a hill she could not walk up.

He stood there until she had gone, and then he went back up into the town, and he was not enjoying himself at all by then.

He kept passing rooms that nobody was in.

A town built for four thousand holding less than half that, and it had not shrunk evenly. Whole rows shuttered on the lower streets. Houses with the doors still hung and the hearths cold. Two of the four market halls standing dark on a market day.

A cooperage, shut. A dyehouse. A rope walk, intact, silent.

And at the head of the port, the old exchange. Two storeys, one long open floor built to hold a trading crowd, and high windows running the entire length of the upper wall because men reading tallies and counting coin need light to do it in.

The guild that raised it had dissolved years ago. The roof had gone over one bay and the rest of it was sound.

The door was not locked, because there was nothing inside worth locking it against.

He went as far as the threshold. The floor ran the entire length of the building without a post anywhere in the middle of it, which meant somebody had spent real money on roof trusses eighty years ago so that a trading crowd could see from one end of the room to the other.

Dust. Bird mess under the broken bay. A long counter down one side with the varnish worn through in a band where men had leaned on it for two generations and then stopped.

He read the windows before he read anything else, and thought, without deciding anything at all, that it was a building made for reading in.

He filed it, and did nothing about it for three years.

Past the second corner something pulled at him.

No direction in it, just a weight behind the eyes, and he stopped walking without having decided to stop walking. He told himself it was nothing. It was not nothing.

Then again in an alley narrow enough that he had to turn sideways past a stacked cart. Sharper this time, and with a direction, and sitting closer to his spine than to his ears, and thinning out the moment he looked straight at it. Empty tile and a strip of grey sky.

Three points now, one behind the other down the south road, arriving where he arrived.

Then the question that had made him useful on Earth and was about to make him wrong here.

By what mechanism?

Nothing that stood in a sealed hall in the capital crosses twelve days of open road unseen, past four guards, one of whom does not sleep properly even wounded. He could not sketch one. He could not get within a mile of one.

So he threw the line out and kept the convenient explanation instead.

An untrained mana pool. A mage for a mother. A capacity he had been offered instruction in at fourteen and had refused, because at fourteen he had been busy refusing everything.

A piano nobody had ever taught him to play, in a room he had stopped going into.

It accounted for the data and required only that he ignore the fact that the first instance had happened in a room he had stood in a thousand times.

Rylan's house was small and clean in the specific way that poor houses stay clean, cleanliness being one of the few things left that costs nothing.

Nessa went still half a second too long before remembering to bow. Emeric got down to her level to stop her doing it and felt, doing so, how new he still was to the customs of a world he had twenty-two years of memories in.

Finn hid behind her leg.

Wren did not. Wren looked at him with the flat unimpressed curiosity of a child who has not yet been told that princes are supposed to be interesting.

"Are you a real prince?"

"Yes."

"Where's your crown?"

"I left it in the carriage."

She considered that, decided he was either lying or poor, and lost interest entirely.

Finn, from behind the leg, was devastated on his behalf.

It was the most honest review of his standing that Emeric had received in three days.

On the way back out of the lane, a shutter on the upper floor of the house opposite stood fastened that had been open when they went in.

He did not look up at it. Nobody mentioned it.

He stepped back and let the reunion happen without standing in the middle of it. Neighbors came out for a guard's homecoming in a way they never would have for a prince's arrival, and even then there was a wariness under it, the caution people keep in reserve for nobility that turns up expecting gratitude.

Nessa talked while she worked.

Busier, before. One week the port had hands on every mooring and the next week it did not, and nobody had ever explained where the men went past the crown needing them.

He asked what the water was called, because a chart in the capital had said Lake Kaldo.

Nobody here called it that.

It was the Mere. It had been the Mere before there was a Kal to name anything after, and a surveyor had come through two generations back and written the barony's name on the water because the water did not have one he recognized.

Third correction in three days, after the land and after the family.

Somewhere in there he stopped being embarrassed about it and started taking notes, and filed the useful part, which was that anybody who says Lake Kaldo out loud learned this place from paper.

He asked who else had gone.

Fletcher, she said. The shipwright. Built half the hulls that ever left this water. And Abloh the sailmaker, who had a real gift for cloth of any kind and could have done very well working for nobles, if nobles had ever come looking for talent in Kaldo.

The two of them together, always. A package.

He asked if they had died out there.

She did not think so. They had come back from the war and they had not stayed. The port was silting by then and there was nothing here for a man who builds hulls, so they went downriver.

Where downriver?

She shrugged. Down the water. The coast, maybe.

To a woman who had never been off this island, downriver was another country, and the distinction between one stretch of it and another was not one she had ever had reason to make.

He filed the names and did not chase them, because he had no money, no yard, no timber, nothing to build toward, and no idea where downriver meant.

The plague came up the way it comes up in that town, which is quickly and once.

A couple of years after the war, while some of the men who had come back were still trying to put things right. It took the ones the war had not. Men, women, children, animals. It did not ask who had already paid.

Four thousand once. Half that now, or less.

The old folks talked about the town as if it were still that place underneath, just resting, and the rest of them had nowhere else to go, so they agreed whether they believed it or not.

He waited until they were walking back before he asked Rylan the question he had come out for.

"Who in this town does everybody know, and trust?"

"Two people." Rylan did not have to think about it. "Bram. Blacksmith. And Imani. She's the doctor."

He had the whole diagnosis and no standing whatsoever, and two names was everything he owned.
