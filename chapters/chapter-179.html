Chapter 179 - Livia

The House carriage came in at the South Gate on the morning of the midsummer quarter day, as it had come on every quarter day since the debt was paid: dark green, plain, with no arms on its door, two horses and a driver who never spoke and never got down. Vantic got out of it in the gate yard with her book under her arm and her gloves on, and looked round the yard, and up at the line room window in the gate tower where the wire went in, and nodded to Rylan on the gate as if he were a figure in a column she had checked last quarter and found correct.

She audited the counting room all morning, with Ferra, at the long desk. Emeric did not go in. He never did, on quarter days; the House's audit rights were the House's, and Ferra had said in the second year that a borrower who stood at the auditor's elbow was a borrower who had something to watch. He heard about it at noon, from Ferra, on the keep stair.

"She's seen the records hall," said Ferra. "She went across the square before she came up. She stood in the line."

"In the line?"

"For an hour. Behind a carter. She asked to see the House's line." Ferra's mouth moved. "The House has no line. It's not a household. My clerk told her so, very politely, and she thanked him and went out." She went on down the stair. "She said it was the most expensive thing I've ever built for nothing, and the House would have charged a copper a look."

At the bottom of the stair, without turning, Ferra said: "She wants to see you. This evening. On the wall, she says, not in a room." A pause. "She asked me first whether she might. Which she's never done."



She was on the river stretch of the wall walk at seven, where the evening sun came flat across the marsh, standing at the parapet with her gloved hands on the stone and her book under her arm, as if the book were part of the arm.

He came along the wall from the water stair and stood beside her, a little apart, as he always did. She did not look at him. She looked at the river.

"I am going home in a fortnight," she said. "To the family seat. On the coast. There is a Reading in July."

He knew what a Reading was. She had told him the first one, two years after it, in a sentence, on the steamboat, as if it had happened to somebody else: the senior partners of the House, in a room, with her father at the head of the table, going through her book line by line while she stood. She had lost that one. She had said so, and stated the losses first, and that was all she had ever said about it.

"Your second," he said.

"My second." She was quiet. "I would like to ask you for something. And to ask for it, I have to tell you something first, or the asking will not make sense. And I would rather tell you than have you work it out, because you would, and then you would be polite about it, and I could not bear that." She turned her head and looked at him, for the first time. "My name is Livia Ducatis."

He had half known it. He had known for years that she was more than a factor, that nobody wrote to a mere factor in the hand of the House's own partners, or travelled in the House's own carriage on a quarter day, or knew the founding ledger's clauses as if she had learned to read from them. He had not let himself put a name to it, because it was not his to name. Hearing it said aloud on the wall, in her own voice, plainly, was different.

"Ducatis," he said.

"The youngest of three. My father is the House. My brothers keep its two great books." She looked back at the river. "I keep the small ones. The bad ones. The books nobody else in the family wants, of borrowers the House expects to lose. Kaldo was one."

"And Vantic?"

"Is not a name." She said it without any expression at all. "It is a working name. In the House's founding ledger, at the back, there is a list of them, a dozen, written out by the man who founded the House sixty years ago. A factor in the field takes one, so that borrowers do not try to bargain with the family instead of the terms. When a factor leaves the post, the name goes back on the list, and the next one who goes out takes it." She was quiet. "Four people have answered to Vantic in sixty years. I am the fourth. The third was a man who died of a fever in a town in the west with a bad book. I never met him. I have his seal. My father gave it to me the day I left, in a box, with the ledger's copy of the clause, so that I would know what I was."

Emeric stood on the wall in the warm evening and felt something go cold in him, slowly, from the middle outward, the way cold water goes into a warm bath.

It was the coldest thing anybody had ever told him about a family. Colder than his own. His father had built him a mask, and the mask had been a cruelty, and it had been done to keep him alive. Nobody had kept her alive with this. They had given her a dead man's seal in a box so that she would know what she was.

He did not say any of that. She would not have thanked him for it.

"What do you want to ask for?" he said.



She told him on the wall, in the long light, with her book open on the parapet between them, in the voice she used for terms.

The House priced its loans the way every lending house in the kingdom priced them. A factor went to look at the borrower. He looked at the man, and the land, and the house, and the man's father, and what the town said in the taverns; and then he sat down and decided in his own head what the loan was worth, and at what price, and wrote it in his book, and the partners trusted his judgment because they had trained him. And sometimes he was right. And sometimes he was a man who did not like the look of a borrower's face.

"I want to price them the other way," she said. "Not from one man's judgment of one borrower. From what happened to all of them. Every loan the House has made in sixty years, and how it ended. Who paid and who didn't, and what they had in common, the ones who paid. Not their faces." She put her gloved finger on a column. "I have been building it for four years. In the evenings. From the House's own books. I can show the partners that half the borrowers the House calls dogs were never dogs. They were mispriced. They were charged too much because a factor didn't like them, and failed because they were charged too much."

"That's right," said Emeric. "That's how it ought to be done. It's how it's done where I..." He stopped. "It's right."

She looked at him, briefly, at the place where the sentence had stopped, and let it go.

"And the Reading," he said.

"And the Reading will ask me to prove it." She closed the book. "On the House's books alone, I can't. Not well enough. The partners will say I chose the books to suit me. I need books that aren't the House's. Many of them. Loans made by somebody else, to many people, small ones, and how they ended." She looked at him. "Milford's seed loans. Grayrock's tool advances to the miners. Wayford's rent books, from before you and after. Kaldo's tithe-house loans. Four baronies, every loan for five years, with the outcome. Pooled." She paused. "Nobody in the kingdom has a book like that but you. Because nobody in the kingdom lends to everybody but you."

"You could have asked as the House."

"As the House's factor I audit you. I cannot ask a borrower for his books for my own use. It would be a conflict, and the partners would say so, and they would be right." She held his eye. "So I am asking as Livia. Who has no standing to ask anybody for anything." She put the book back under her arm. "And who would rather you said no than gave it because she told you her name."

"It's not mine to give," said Emeric. "It's Ferra's office."

"I know." Something moved at the corner of her mouth. "I asked her first. This morning. Before I asked her whether I might see you."

"What did she say?"

"She said I should ask you, because it would be good for you to be asked for something you couldn't decide by yourself." Vantic looked at the river. "And then she said I would want the bad loans as well as the good ones, and she would have them copied for me by Thursday."



He went to say the other thing, and she said it first.

"There is one more." She did not look at him. "You will want to know why I went to Wayford in March. For the whole of the Visitor's fortnight. On House business that did not, if you looked at my book, take a fortnight."

"I'd noticed."

"I know you had. Ferra mentioned it to you in a line, among the wool prices, so that you would." She was quiet. "I did not care to be counted."

The sun was down behind the west hill now. The marsh had gone blue. Below them on the river an eel boat was coming home with its lantern lit, a small yellow light low on the water.

"At the shaft," said Emeric.

"At the shaft." She still did not look at him. "You saw."

"I saw."

"And said nothing. Not that day. Not to Ward. Not to Ferra. Not to anybody, in two years and a half." Now she turned her head. "I have known that you saw since that night at Bryda's table, when I looked down the table at you and you did not look back. I have never heard you say it." She looked at him in the dusk. "I wanted to hear you say it. Once. So that I would know I had not made it up."

"You didn't make it up. I saw your hand, and the frame stop, and a glyph on the air, the color of a lamp stone's." He kept his voice level. "I didn't say anything because it wasn't mine to say. The Church counts mages. Your family would have counted you out. I thought it was the only thing in your life you'd kept for yourself."

She looked at him for as long as it took to read a line.

"It was," she said. "It is." And then, very dry, turning back to the river: "The House would like it noted that the borrower has exceeded the terms of his agreement in the matter of discretion. The House has no clause for it. It is considering one."

He laughed. He had not expected to, and she had plainly meant him to, and it went out over the marsh in the dusk and an eel man on the boat below looked up.

"And since I am telling you things," said Livia Ducatis, "and will not have the stomach to do it twice, there is the last one." She did not smile now. "There is something under your castle that breathes. I felt it on a night in February of the fifth year, in the east tower, in my bed. At about two in the morning. A wind with no draft to it, coming up through the stone." She was quiet. "I said nothing. If I had asked you what it was, I would have been telling you what I could feel."

He stood very still on the wall.

"The night the forge first ran," he said.

"I don't know what it is. I don't want to be told." She looked at him. "But I want you to know that I know there is something. So that you never have to wonder whether I do, and be polite about it."



She signed the oath on the twenty-seventh, at night, in the cellar, on the cool lid of the feed cup, as all of them had.

It was Ferra's idea. Ferra said that a woman who had felt the forge in her bed and said nothing for two years was already inside the oath in every way except the signature, and that she would rather have the signature, because she was Ferra. Emeric asked the table. The table agreed, Ward last, after a long look at nothing in particular, and Imani first, before anybody else had opened their mouth.

So Vantic came down the narrow stair behind him at midnight with a lamp, and stood inside the forge's door, and looked at it.

She did not say anything for some while. She looked at the chamber in its glass skin, and the cage, and the cup, and the lattice, and the shelves of masters along the wall, the gauge blocks in their felt and the flats and the screws and the hob, and the box under the door where the sleeping stones had gone in March. She looked at it the way she looked at a book she had been sent to audit and found was larger than the House had been told.

"This is what the renovation allowance was," she said at last.

"Yes."

"I initialled that line." She was quiet. "I said it was the most reasonable thing the barony had spent money on since I came." She looked round the chamber again, slowly. "It was."

Ferra gave her the sheet, and the pen. She read it through once, all of it, carefully, the way she read terms; and then she took off her right glove, which Emeric had never seen her do in company, and laid it on the shelf beside the hob, and signed. Not Vantic. Her own name. Livia Ducatis, in a plain small upright hand, under Ward's.

Then she put the glove back on, finger by finger, not quickly, and picked up her book.



Ferra's clerks had the ledgers copied by the Thursday, as Ferra had said. Four baronies, five years, every loan the barony had made to anybody, and how it had ended. Twelve hundred lines. Vantic took them to the records hall's back room, where there was a long table and a window and nobody, and sat at them for four days with her own book open beside them and a pencil, and did not come to supper.

On the fifth day she sent for him.

He found her at the long table with the ledgers in stacks and her own pages spread in front of her, columns and columns of them in her small hand, and her gloves off, both of them, laid side by side on the corner of the table. He had never seen her with both gloves off. Her hands were thin and very white and there was ink on two fingers of the right one.

"It works," she said. "Look."

He looked. He knew what he was looking at; he had seen tables like it in the other life, thousands, on screens, built by machines that took a week to do what she had done with a pencil in four days. Borrowers in rows. What they had in common, in columns. And at the foot of every column the share of them who had paid, in her neat hand. The miners at Grayrock with families paid more often than the single men. The farmers who had borrowed for seed paid more often than the ones who had borrowed for rent. The ones the old baron's steward had written down as bad risks, with a cross against their names, paid more often than anybody.

"That's it," he said. "That's the whole method. You've proved it."

"I have proved half of it." She turned a page. "Now look at this."

It was the House's own book. Her copy of it, the borrowers of sixty years, priced from their outcomes the same way. And beside it, the same method run over Kaldo's ledgers. And the two did not agree.

"I nearly missed it," she said. "You did miss it. On the wall you said it was right. It is right." She put her finger on the House's column. "But the House's book is a book of people the House lent to. That's all it can be. The ones it refused aren't in it. They never borrowed, so they never paid or didn't pay, so the method has nothing to learn from them." She moved her finger across to Kaldo's. "So if you build the method on the House's book, it learns who the House liked. And then it goes on refusing the same people the House always refused. For the same reasons. Forever. Only now it's a column, and nobody can argue with a column." She took her finger away. "Your ledgers have the people the House would have refused. Because you lent to everybody. And they paid."

He looked at the two columns. She was right. He had built tables like this in the other life and never once thought of it, because in the other life the books had always been too big to see the hole in.

"You'd have been refused," he said slowly. "By your own method. If it ran on the House's book."

"Yes." She began to gather her pages. "A woman. A youngest child. Gifted, which the House does not count. On the House's book, sixty years of it, there is not one borrower like me who was ever given a chance to pay." She squared the stack. "A model built on the House's refusals would refuse me. It would be right to, on its own figures. And nobody would ever know it was wrong." She put on her gloves, one, then the other. "That's what I shall tell them in July. Not that I'm right. That their books can't tell them whether I am."
