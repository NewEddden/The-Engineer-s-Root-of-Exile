Chapter 179 - The Road North

He did not write anything down.

That was the whole of it, and all six of them knew it, and none of them said so. He had written down everything for thirteen years. He had written the rules for the watcher and the drains and the flasks and the charged pieces, and the covenant's reading, and the orders for the column, and the cells, and the net's five conditions with Ferra holding the pen. When he went to Wayford he had left Armand a list. When he went to Emberfell he had left Ward a list and Ferra two. There had never been a week since the first winter when Kaldo had not had a paper somewhere in the keep in his hand that said what to do if.

In the last week of the year he sat at the long-room table with the six of them round it and the summons in the middle, and said that he would be gone from the first week of the new year until the council rose, which might be spring and might be summer, and that Armand would sit as second, as the table had ruled, and that Ferra's veto on the purse stood as it always had. Ward would keep the garrison and the wall. Rylan would go north with him. And Saban, who had come to the table that night for the first time since the autumn, on his own feet, slowly, with his bound wrist in a sling and his face still the wrong colour, would go too, because the capital was Saban's ground and nobody else's, and because Imani said he could sit a carriage if he did not do anything else.

Then he stopped.

They waited for the rest. He saw them wait. Armand with his hands folded on the head of his stick. Bram. Imani, at the far end, with her register. Ward at his right hand. Ferra with her pen above her book.

There was no rest. He did not give them one.

After a while Ferra put her pen down beside her book, very precisely, the way she put it down at the end of a quarter, and did not pick it up again. Armand looked at the table in front of him. Bram nodded, once, to nobody. Ward looked at Emeric for a long moment and then looked away.

Nobody asked what they were to do.

He went down to the east tower on the last night, and told Marrow he was going, and where, and Marrow stood by the wall under the window and listened and said nothing at all.

He went to see Ward in the gatehouse after, late, and found him at his table with the map and the garrison roll.

"If you want me to write," Ward said, without looking up. "The other way. You only have to say."

"I know."

"You've never said."

"No," said Emeric. "I'm not going to say now."

Ward nodded, and went back to his roll.

Ferra came to the Western Gate in the morning.

He had not expected her to. They had said their business the night before at the long-room table with the others, and she had a quarter to close, and it was the first week of the year, when she was always at the book from first light. The carriage was drawn up in the road outside the gate: a hired one, from Milford, with a Milford driver and four hired horses and the barony's arms on nothing, because he was going to answer a summons and not to make a show. Rylan was on the box beside the driver, in his cloak, with his suit in a crate in the boot and his sword across his knees. Saban was already inside with a rug and a hot brick and a satchel of papers, looking out of the window at the lake as if he had never seen it.

The gate was busy. It was always busy in the morning. Carts coming in with the day's charcoal and going out with the day's paper, and the camp men walking through to the flat, and women with baskets for the market, and two of the fire company's first watch at the gate with their buckets, and Enno's line going past on the lake road to the lower field with their pieces on their shoulders, uncharged, and the smoke of the island's chimneys going straight up into a still grey sky. It was daylight, and half the island was there.

She came down the road from the keep with Hobb, in her winter cloak, with her book under her arm, and stopped at the carriage step, and Hobb stopped a little behind her.

"I've closed the quarter," she said.

"I thought you would have."

"I closed it last night. After." She did not open the book. She held it against her cloak with both arms. "It balances. I checked it from the top and the bottom."

"It always does."

"It does when I do it," Ferra said. "I'll close the year without you. At midwinter. On the shortest day." She looked at the book in her arms and not at him. "I've closed every one since the first. Every one. You've always been in the building."

"I know."

"The book will be here," she said. "When you come back. It'll be open on the table, at the page, and it'll balance, and everything that happened while you were gone will be in it, in order, and I will not have left anything out." She looked up at him then. "I want you to read it. All of it. When you come back. Not the totals. All of it. Every page."

"I'll read every page," he said.

She held his eyes for a moment longer than she needed to. There were a great many people at the Western Gate. A carter was waiting behind the carriage to get through with a load of rag. Hobb was looking very hard at the lake.

"Good," she said. "See that you do."

She stepped back from the carriage step. He got in. Rylan spoke to the driver, and the driver spoke to the horses, and the carriage went forward under the gate and out onto the Wayford road, and he looked back through the little window in the back of it and saw her standing in the road outside the Western Gate in her winter cloak with her book held against her with both arms, not waving, and the carter going round her with his load of rag, and then the road turned and the gate was gone.

Saban, beside him, did not say anything for a mile. Then he said, "She's never once come to the gate. Not in thirteen years. Not for anybody."

"I know," Emeric said.

It was twelve days.

It had been five to Wayford for the column, marching, and the carriage did it in four, on the frozen road, with the horses changed at the ford. They crossed Harrow Bridge on the fourth morning. The toll-house was gone. Somebody had pulled it down for firewood in the autumn and nobody had put it back, and there was a bare square of trodden earth at the north end where it had stood, and the pole still lying in the fern, black with frost. The relay post on the ridge was lit. He saw its lamps as they came up out of the valley, and heard the shutters go, and knew that Ferra would have the carriage's crossing on her table by dusk.

They went through Wayford without stopping except for Pennock, who came out to the counting-house door to hand Saban a satchel of copies. They went out past the stream ground, white under the frost, flat and empty, with nothing on it to show where the bank had been but a long low ridge in the snow. They went past the oak.

He did not ask the driver to stop. He looked at it from the carriage window as they passed. It was bare now, all its leaves down, black against the grey, with the stone trough under it and the frost on the trough. There was nothing on it. The board had been taken down and burned. He looked at it until the road went over the rise and it was behind them, and then he looked at his hands.

After that the road was Aldane's.

They went on it openly, under the king's summons, with the summons's copy in Saban's satchel and the king's great seal on it for anybody who stopped them, and nobody stopped them. The inns took their money and gave them rooms and looked at them sideways. On the sixth day, at a posting house on the Aldane border, the landlord's boy spat in the road behind the carriage as it left, and Rylan on the box turned and looked at him, only looked, and the boy went inside. On the eighth day they passed a church where a man was nailing a printed sheet to the door, and Saban leaned out of the window to read it, and sat back and said, "The duke's file. Articles one to five. They're posting it," and did not say anything else for an hour.

They went by road because the summons said in their own persons, and a man going to the capital in his own person went by road, in a carriage, with a driver and hired horses and a posting house every night, and took twelve days. Everybody in the realm who had ever answered a summons had taken twelve days. The other way would have had him in the capital in an afternoon, from the thorn hill, in the dark, with nobody to see him land. And then there would have been a prince at the capital's gate on the morning after he left Kaldo, with no carriage, and no road dust, and no posting house that had seen him, and every clerk in the chancery would have been able to do that sum.

Nobody could know he could. So he went by road, for twelve days, and let every posting house between Kaldo and the capital see him.

The last inn was a big one, a day's easy drive from the capital's south gate, on the high road where the Aldane road joined it, with a yard full of carriages and a tap-room full of men in good coats who were all going to the same place for the same reason. The spring council was a month off. Half the lords of the south were on the road.

He took a room at the back, over the stables, and ate in it with Saban and Rylan, and did not go down.

After Saban had gone to bed and Rylan had gone to sit on the stair outside the door with his sword, as he had every night, Emeric stood at the window.

It was dark outside. There was no light in the stable yard below but a lantern at the far gate. The window was a small one, of old greenish glass in lead, and with the candle behind him and the dark beyond it, it gave him back his own face.

He looked at it.

It was his face. Thin, and tired, and older than it had been. He had not looked at it much in thirteen years. There were not many mirrors in Kaldo, and he had never owned one.

He thought about the dinners at Wayford.

Two summers ago, at Placit's table, and Hollin's, and Strade's, night after night, with the five of them round him and their wives and their stewards, he had sat in a good coat and been a prince. Not himself. A prince: pleasant, idle, a little vain, a little bored, easily flattered, interested in nothing much but the wine and the talk and his own charm, with a smile he could put on and leave on for four hours and a laugh at the right places and nothing behind his eyes at all that anybody could see. He had learned it as a boy, in the capital, at his father's court, because it was the only safe face for a prince who was not the heir to wear there. He had put it away for eleven years in Kaldo and never missed it. He had taken it out again at Wayford to sit at a table with men who were running human beings dry in a cellar, and it had come back so easily that it had frightened him.

He tried it now, in the dark window.

He did not do anything with his face, at first. He only thought about the room he would walk into in a month. The long council hall with the high windows. His father at the end of it. His brother. Aldane. The lords of the realm on their benches. The file. And he thought about what the prince of the Wayford dinners would do in that room, how he would stand, what he would do with his hands.

And his face did it.

He watched it happen in the greenish glass. The tiredness went out of it first, smoothed away, as if somebody had drawn a hand down it. The mouth changed; it did not smile, not yet, but it was ready to, easy, a little amused at something. The eyes went pleasant and a little vague. The chin went up a very little. It was a handsome, idle, harmless face, the face of a young man who had never in his life had a serious thought or done a hard day's work or stood under an oak tree with five men hanging from it, and it had come up out of wherever it lived in him in the time it took to draw a breath, whole, without his having to do anything at all.

He looked at it for a long time.

It was as easy as it had been at Wayford. It was exactly as easy. He had hoped, a little, without saying so to himself, that it might have gone, in two years; that the war and the stream ground and the oak might have worn it away, so that he would have to work at it. It had not gone. It was there, perfect, waiting, as it had been at Placit's table, as it had been when he was fourteen.

It bothered him. It bothered him exactly as much as it had at Wayford. Not more. That bothered him too.

He let it go, and his own face came back in the glass, and he blew out the candle.

They came up the high road in the morning in a thin cold mist, among the other carriages, at a walk.

The capital's walls came up out of the mist a mile off: grey, very high, older than anything in Kaldo by five hundred years, running away on both sides of the south gate further than the eye could follow, with the towers of the upper city standing up behind them, and the cathedral's spire above everything, and the flags on the gate towers hanging limp in the still air. He had not seen them in years. They were exactly as he remembered. He had thought they would look smaller.

Saban was asleep with his head against the window. Rylan was on the box. The carriage went on at a walk toward the gate, in the line of carriages, and the walls came up and up.

He sat and watched them come, and felt his face, without his asking it to, begin to arrange itself.
