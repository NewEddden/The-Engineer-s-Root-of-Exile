Chapter 219 - The Dairy Page

The book was where it had always been, on the shelf in the long room between the volume on barm and the volume on the canal ring's water, with a strip of Ferra's grey binding tape down its spine and PELL. MILFORD. SEPT. written on the tape in a fifteen-year-old's capitals.

Emeric took it down on the fourth of January, a little after six in the evening, with the school empty and the stoves in the long room banked for the night, and stood with it under the lamp by the door without opening it.

He had come from the counting room. Ferra had had the day's figures in by five, down the wires from the three other baronies and the long wire from the stream ground, and had read them to him flat, the way she read rents, because that was the only way anybody could stand to hear them. The camp, closed since the ninth of December: no new cases in ten days. Grayrock: nineteen new. Wayford: six, all in one street by the tannery. Milford: two, in a carter's family who had bought a blanket at the Wayford market in November. Kaldo inside the walls: three. The first three inside the walls.

Ferra had written them in her book and ruled a line under them and looked up at him and said nothing at all, which from Ferra was a speech; and he had got up and come here.

He opened it. He did not have to look for the page. Pell's drawings of the blisters, a cow's teat in three views and a girl's forefinger in two; then the column at the back, the names and ages and answers one under another in Pell's round farm hand; and three-quarters of the way down, the line with the pencil box round it.

He had told himself, at Hester Mull's table, that every reason held. Imani had not proved it. There was no pox in the south. The Church would have a word for a prince who scratched a cow's sores into children's arms. He went through them again, honestly, and the second was gone, and the third weighed nothing against thirty new cases in one day, and the first was not his to keep deciding for her.

He had known since the ninth of December, since Imani came to the door of the surgeon's hut with her sleeves rolled and said the name of it to him and Ward and nobody else. He had waited for a way of saying it that was not a dead man in another world talking. It was the fourth of January.

He shut the book and put it under his arm and went to find the Seers' Table.



Ten of the twelve came. Pell walked in from her father's farm through the frost in her brother's coat, and Corin, the boy from the dock quarter who had named them in the first place and was seventeen now and taller than Emeric and still unrepentant about it, came from the paper mill white to the elbows. They sat down at the long table where they had always sat, on the side nearest the stove, and looked at the book under his arm, and nobody asked what it was for.

They knew what was in the barony; there was lime on the doorsteps in the lower ward. Pell looked at the binding tape with her own capitals on it and went slowly red, as if she had been caught at something.

He put the book down in front of her, open, at the box.

"Read it out," he said.

She looked at him. Then she read it, in the voice children in Sela's room used for reading aloud, a little too careful. The cow blisters some summers. Little ones that weep and crust. The girls catch them on their hands from the milking, a sore or two, a few days poorly. Every girl at Hester's had had it. Not one woman in the bay who'd had the cow blisters as a girl caught the pox in the speckled year. Their brothers had. Their mothers had. They'd nursed them. Not them.

She stopped. Nobody said anything. Corin had his elbows on the table and was looking at the page upside down.

"You've already found it," said Emeric.

There was a short, blank quiet at the table. A girl from the lower ward at the end, Rue, who drew better than any of them and spoke less, put her pencil down.

"Found what?" said Corin.

"The answer to the thing that's in Grayrock."

"That's a line," said Pell. "It's a line I wrote because it was odd." She put her finger on the box, as she had at Hester's table, as if she were holding it down. "I don't know what it means. I didn't then."

"Then let's find out what it means," he said, "out of your own book, and not out of my mouth. Turn back two pages."

The drawings of the blisters on the cow, and the drawings of the blisters on the girl's hand: the same shape, the same crust, the same days from wet to dry, written under each in Pell's small figures. "Same thing?" he said; and Rue, who had not seen them before, leaned over and looked from one to the other and said, "Same thing," and then, because Rue was exact, "same thing or its sister."

"How did the girls get it?"

"Milking," said Pell. "Off the cow. Off the teats. It's in the column." She found it. "Four of them said it was always the new girl got it first, because she milked rougher. It goes in at a crack in the hand."

"So it passes. Cow to girl. Does it pass girl to girl?"

Pell ran her finger down the column, frowning. "One," she said. "One said her sister got it off her, the summer they shared a bed, and her sister never milked."

"Hand to hand, then," said Corin. "Like the itch."

"Like the itch," Emeric agreed. "Now the other one. The speckled sickness. What does it do to somebody who's had it once and lived?"

They knew that. There was no street in Kaldo without a face in it marked from the speckled year, and not one of those faces had caught it again. Never twice, people said. Rue said it. Then a boy from the north well said it again, slower, as if he had heard himself, and looked at the page.

"So the body learns it," said Emeric. "Once, from the sickness itself, and it costs a fifth of a town to teach it, and it never forgets. You've drawn the small things in the canal water. You've drawn them in milk and in wounds. You know now that a sickness is a small living thing that gets into you." He put his hand flat on the table beside the box. "And here is a small sickness off a cow, that gets in at a crack in the hand, and costs a sore finger and a few days poorly."

Pell had stopped looking at him and was looking at her own drawing of the girl's finger.

"Its sister," she said, slowly. "Rue said its sister."

"Go on."

"If it's near enough the same," said Pell, and stopped. Started again. "If the body learns the little one. And the big one's near enough the same. Then it's already learned it, when the big one comes. So the girls never..." She looked up. Her face was no longer red. "That's why. That's what the line is."

Corin said a word that Sela would have had him outside for.

"Then you'd give it to people," said the boy from the north well. "On purpose. The little one. Off the cow."

"How?" said Emeric.

They argued it out among themselves while he sat on the end of the bench by the stove and kept his mouth shut, and it went where it had to go, because they were Imani's pupils at second hand and Ferra's census-takers at first, and neither woman had ever let them get away with a guess. In at a crack in the hand, said Pell; then make the crack. A scratch, said Corin, like a cat's. Where, said Rue; the upper arm, because nobody milked with it. From where, said the north well boy; from Hester's cows, said Pell, but it's January. They stopped at that. The blisters came in summer.

"Some summers," Pell said. "Not every cow. Not every year."

And then Rue, who had not spoken in a quarter of an hour, said, "The sister," and pointed at the one line in the column about the girl who had never milked and caught it in her sister's bed. "It went from a girl to a girl. It doesn't need the cow after the first one. Off one arm onto the next."

They all looked at her. Then at him. Pell looked at him longest.

"You knew," she said. "At Hester's. You read it at her table and you knew what it was."

"I knew there was something there," he said, which was true and not the whole truth, and she held his eye and he let her. "I didn't know you'd find all of it in an evening."

She did not look as if she believed him. But she looked down at the book again, and turned it the right way up on the table so that the others could see the box.

"It's our page," she said.

"It's your page."



Imani had been standing in the long room door for the last of it. He had sent the porter up the hill for her, and she stood with one shoulder on the frame and her arms folded and listened as she had listened to Pell over the crock, without interrupting. When the children turned round and saw her they went quiet as if a bell had gone.

She came down the room and stood at the end of the table and looked at the page, and at Pell's drawings, and at the box. She had not slept since the first; it was in the colour under her eyes and her shoulders, high and stiff, as if her coat were a size too small.

"No," she said.

Pell's chin came up.

"Not no, it doesn't work." Imani put a finger on the box. "No, I don't believe it. Yet." She looked down the table at the ten of them. "You know what I'm going to ask."

"How many," said Pell.

"How many."

And Pell turned the column round, and ran her finger down the margin where she had made her tally marks two years ago with nobody asking her to, because Ferra's census taught every child in Kaldo to total a page; and read it off. Thirty-eight women in the bay who had had the cow blisters as girls, before the speckled year or in it, by their own account and their mothers'. Not one of the thirty-eight had caught the pox. Sixty-one women of the same ages who had milked and never had the blisters, or never milked. Twenty-six of the sixty-one had caught it. Nine had died.

"Two columns," said Pell. "Alike in every way but the one." She did not quite manage to keep it out of her voice.

Imani looked at the figures for a long breath. Emeric watched her do the sum, which she did as fast as Ferra did, and then do it again.

"That's not a story," she said at last. "That's a column." She straightened. "It isn't a trial. I'd want a hundred, and a year, and the glass on it, and I've got none of those and nine dead in Grayrock this week." She looked at Pell. "So we do the trial while we do the work. Every arm written down. Whose arm it came off, whose it went on to, the day, the sore, the fever, whether it took. Every one. And if anybody we've done catches the pox, I want to know it the same day, and I want to know whose arm it came from." She looked at Emeric. "That's the price of me standing behind it."

"That's a lot of writing," said Corin.

"Then it's a good thing," said Imani, "that this barony's full of children who've been taught to ask everybody everything." She put her hand flat on the open book, beside the box, exactly where he had put his. "Who's got the steadiest hand?"

Nine heads turned to Rue.

"Right," said Imani, and took her coat off.



He went down to the cellar that night.

Ferra came down at nine with the forge book, as she came down for every run, and stood at the bottom of the steps with it open and the pencil out while he told her what he wanted, and wrote the stones down against each line before he had finished saying it.

"A draw plate," he said. "For wire. One hole, very fine, very true, hard enough to pull a hundred miles of wire through without wearing. A die and a former to go with it, to cut the wire short and bend it, and flatten the end into two prongs with a little gap between them." He held his finger and thumb apart to show her a gap a hair wide. "That's all. Three pieces of steel, none of them bigger than my hand."

"And the glass."

"Six syringes. For Imani. A barrel and a plunger ground to it so close that nothing gets past the plunger that you don't push past it." He looked at the chamber. "Those are the hard part. The glass is fussy."

Ferra wrote. "Bram's shop makes the wire?"

"Bram's shop makes the wire, and stamps the needles, and nobody who stamps them needs to know where the die came from. It's a die. It came from the standards room." Rule seven, at the head of the book, in his own hand. "The recipe for the plate goes in the book beside it the same week. Somebody in the open works can learn to cut one the slow way by spring."

"By summer," said Ferra, who knew the open works. She tapped the foot of the column. "Cheap. For you." It was the closest she came to approving a run. "I'll not stay. I've the Wayford returns."

"Go to bed."

"I'll go to the Wayford returns," said Ferra, and went up the steps with the book against her chest, and stopped at the top, and came down two steps again.

"She's not slept since the first," she said.

"I know."

"I'm telling you so it's written somewhere you'll read it." She went up again.

The steel took an hour. The plate first, a disc of the hardest steel the forge had ever given him, pierced through the middle with one bell-mouthed hole drawn down to the gauge of a fine sewing needle and true all the way through; then the die and the former, which were nothing, a block with a slot and a punch that pressed a wire's end flat and split it and set the two halves a hair apart.

The glass took four hours. He had known it would. The forge could make a flawless tube and a flawless rod; the fitting of one inside the other, so that the plunger slid on a skin of water and stayed wherever it was left, was a matter of the last ten-thousandth of an inch, and he did it six times, with the chamber's light on his face and the sweat running into his collar.

At half past two he put the last one in the felt and sat on the bottom step and looked at them: three small dull pieces of steel in a row on the bench, and beside them six slender things of glass in a case that the lamplight came through as if they were made of water.



Bram had the plate on the drawing bench at the open works by eight, and was swearing at it by ten, because the first wire broke at the hole and so did the second; and by noon he had worked out for himself that it wanted three plates, coarse and middle and fine, and that only the fine one had to come from the standards room. He did not ask where the standards room had got it.

By the evening of the fifth a girl from Grayrock, whose name Bram had to ask for, was feeding cut wire into the former at a bench by the stove and pulling a lever and dropping what came out into a tray: a stiff little wire as long as a finger, its end split into two short prongs that stood a hair apart. She did six a minute when Bram was watching and nine when he was not. By the end of the week there were three benches.

The needles went up the hill to the hospital in a tin box that had held Maren's raisins, with a few thousand in the bottom of it rattling like dried peas.

The syringes went up the hill in their case, in Emeric's coat.

Imani opened the case in the dispensary with her hands washed and dried and held one of them up against the window, and looked through it, and drew the plunger back an inch, and stopped it, and turned the whole thing upside down, and watched the plunger not move. She did not say anything for a while. Then she said, "Oh," very quietly, as if somebody had shown her a baby.

"There's six," he said.

"There's six," she said. "Two for the ward. One for the women's room. One for the surgery. Two locked up." She set it back in its felt as if it might wake up. "I'll want more."

"You'll have more. Slowly."

"I know slowly." She looked at the case again, and then at him, with the look she had given the clean water tower the first morning it ran, which was not a look she gave many things. Then she picked up the raisin tin, and shook it, and looked into it, and put it on the shelf over the dispensary hob with the bandages.

"What are these?" she said.

"Rue's," he said.



Hester Mull had one cow with the blisters.

It should not have been possible in January, and Hester said so, standing in the byre door at Milford in a sheepskin over the leather apron while Emeric and Pell and Rue and two of Imani's nurses came up through the frozen yard from the line. But Hester kept her byre warm in winter, because her mother had said a warm cow milked; and at the warm end of it there was a red heifer called Netty that had come in off the bench at the turn of the year sore on her teats, and Hester's newest girl had a sore on her finger to match.

"The girl in the book said," Hester said to Pell, "that if I ever had one in the cold months, I was to send to the castle. I sent." She looked at Emeric. "And now there's a prince in my byre again. I'm getting used to it. I don't care for it."

"She's a good cow," said Pell, who had milked Netty's mother.

"She's a sore cow," said Hester, "and you'll be gentle with her or I'll know why."

They were gentle with her. Hester held her head, and Rue did what Imani had shown her the night before on a willow stick, and the nurses did what Imani had told them about the cleanness of it, which was most of the work. Emeric held the lamp. He had decided on the way that holding the lamp was what he was for.

Pell was the first arm.

She had settled that at the long table before anyone could argue: it was her page, and she was not asking a child in Sela's room to go before she had. Rue went second. Then the other eight; and on the eighth day, when Pell's arm had come up in a single blister with a dent in its middle, as the milkmaids' fingers had, and she had run a fever for a night and gone to school sulking because Imani had sent her home, Rue took from Pell's arm to the first children.

The first children were Sela's.

That was Sela's doing. She came to the long room on the morning of the twelfth with her two youngest grandchildren by the hand and her sleeves rolled, and sat them down at the long table in front of Imani, and said that if her own were not done first, no woman in the north well street would bring hers; and if hers were done first, every woman in the north well street would be standing in the snow by noon. She was right. By noon the line went down the stair and out across the yard.

The bent wires came into it on the first morning, because there was nothing else to come into it with. The fork took up a drop by itself when you dipped it and held it between its prongs as a pen holds ink, the same drop each time; you laid it on the arm and pressed, a few times, quick, and a nurse could learn it by watching it done twice, and a fifteen-year-old in three. By the end of the first day the nurses were doing it with one hand and talking to the mothers with the other.

Imani did forty arms with one herself, at the end of the long table, to be sure of it, and wrote each of them down; and then stood up and looked at it, the little split wire between her finger and thumb, and put it in the pan, and went to see to the line.

The syringes stayed in their case on the dispensary shelf. Nobody needed them that month. Twice that week Emeric passed the shelf and saw the case and the raisin tin side by side, and the tin had been refilled twice from the open works, and was empty again.



Ottoline came to the counting room on the third day with a long wooden box under her arm and her magnifying glass bumping on its cord, and put the box on Ferra's table without asking whose table it was.

The census cards were in it: every soul in the four baronies, a card each, holes punched along the edge for who they were and where and what they could read, sorted through Ottoline's tabulator for the reading figures in October and put away again. The rest of the machine was on a cart in the yard.

"You'll want to know who's been done," she said. "And who it came from." She took out a card and held it up to the light. "I've left a row of the edge blank. A hole for the day. A hole for whether it took. And the number of the arm it came off."

Ferra looked at the card for a while, and then at the box, which held thirty thousand of them, near enough, and then at Ottoline.

"Every arm," said Ferra.

"Every arm," said Ottoline. "Your healer wants a roll of who carried it to whom. That's a roll. It's a long one, but it sorts." She put her glass up to her eye and looked at Emeric through it. "If one of your vaccinators writes it wrong, I'll find it, and I'll find who."

"Imani will see to that," said Emeric.

"I know," said Ottoline. "I've met her."

The tabulator went up against the counting room's back wall, and from the seventh day it ran every evening when the slates came in, the cards going through in their thousands with a noise like a loom and the sorted stacks coming out in rows: done, not done; took, didn't take; each arm's children, and theirs, down the generations of a week. On the tenth evening it found a vaccinator in Wayford who had written the same arm number on sixty cards running, and he was not wrong again. On the fourteenth an old man in Grayrock caught the pox four days after his arm was done, and Imani had his card by supper, and the arm it came off, and that one's, back to Pell's blister and Netty in the warm end of Hester's byre; and she read every one, and decided he had had it in him when he came to the line, and wrote so on his card in her plain square hand.

Ferra watched the dials turn over every evening at the end of her own day, and wrote the totals in her book under the deaths, in a second column. She did not say anything about it. The second column got longer than the first in the second week, and three times as long in the third.



They went out in pairs.

That was Corin's idea, on the second evening: one to hold the needle and one to write, and both to ask everybody everything; then the writer learns the needle, and the pair splits, and each takes a new writer from wherever they are. By the third week there were fifty pairs, and less than half of them had started in Kaldo: a clerk's girl in Wayford, two boys from the pit offices at Grayrock, a curate's sister in Milford who had read the notice on the church door and come down the bay with her own pencil.

They went down the river on the steamboat with crocks of matter packed in snow and sawdust in Fletcher's galley, and up the line to Grayrock in the guard's van with the mail; and to the hill hamlets that neither the rail nor a January road could reach, they went at night in hull two.

It was the first use of hull two that Ward did not object to in writing. She went over the baronies low and unlit with Ned Tallis at the levers and a pair in the hold, and set them down at the edge of a hamlet before light; and the hamlets saw two tired fifteen-year-olds walk in out of the frost with a box and a book and ask for the headman. Kaldo's own baronies, and only those. Ferra had written it at the head of the hull's log.

The K Discovery did the other thing.

The wires reached the four towns and nothing beyond them, and the pairs in the hills needed matter when theirs ran out, and needed to say where the pox was. So the K Discovery went up over Grayrock on the ninth of January, dark, and stayed there on a notch with her stones dim, and Fletcher's crew kept a lamp watch on her lower deck all night, every night, with Tess's shutter lamp, reading the flashes from the hilltops and passing them on to the pit office and back, and dropping what was asked for on a line. She did not come down to the ground in three weeks except for water and coal.

Where the speckled death used to take a town in a season, it was stopped in the four baronies in weeks. It was not stopped without dead. But the second column passed the first in the second week and the first stopped growing in the fourth, and in the fifth there was a day when nobody, anywhere on Ferra's sheet, came down with it, and she wrote a nought, and ruled under it, and looked at it.

That was later.



The worst of it was the third week, in Grayrock.

The pit villages under the tips were the last place the needles reached, three rows of miners' cottages back to back along the beck with one pump between them and twelve to a cottage; and on the twenty-first the lamp from the hill above the rows said half a row was down with it and the pair there had run out of matter. The K Discovery passed it to the pit office, and the pit office telephoned the hospital, and the hospital said Imani was already there.

She had been gone four days, from a street to a village to a second village to a third, where a child done eight days before had come down anyway and she had to see that card herself, and from there to the rows. Nobody had seen her sleep. Ilsa Renner, in the pit office, told the wire that the healer had eaten half a bowl of something standing up and gone down to the rows with the last of the matter in her coat, and had said to tell the castle, if the castle asked, that she was perfectly well and was not to be sent for.

Ferra read it to him off the tape at six.

"She says she's perfectly well," said Ferra, "and not to be sent for."

"She does say that."

"She does." Ferra tore the tape off, and folded it, and put it in her book between two pages, and looked at him over it. "I'll not write down that you went. I'll write down that you were sent for." She held out her hand without looking at it, the way she held it out for the ore figures; and he gave her the counting room key, because she would want it locked behind him; and went.



Hull two put him down on the spoil heap above the rows at ten, in the dark, in a wind that came straight off the tops with snow in it. Ned Tallis did not say anything to him the whole way except "Mind the step, Highness," and then, at the hatch, as Emeric swung down onto the black slope with a crock of matter in a sack against his chest and the wind tearing at his coat, "She bit the head off my brother last week, Highness. In Wayford. He'd brought the wrong crock." He considered. "He said she was right, mind."

The rows were lit in every window, and nobody in a pit village wasted a candle. There was a fire in an oil drum by the pump with three men round it, one of them holding a shovel and doing nothing with it, and lime white on half the doorsteps in the first row. The pair's lamp was in the end cottage of the second, and there was a line outside it in the snow, women with children wrapped against them, quiet.

He heard Imani before he saw her. It was her ward voice, coming out of the end cottage's door over the heads of the line, the flat carrying voice she used in the end room for the night nurse.

"No. Not that one. That one's had it, look at him, he's got the scar, take him home and give him his supper. Next. Who's next? Is that yours? Bring her here. No, bring her here, where the light is."

He went in.

The cottage was one room. They had pushed the table to the door with the lamps on it, the pair's book at one end and the boiling pan on a spirit stove at the other; the pair were a girl from Sela's room and a pit office clerk with ink on his cuffs, and both looked as if they had been crying earlier and had stopped for want of time. Imani was at the middle of the table with her sleeves rolled and her hair tied up in a kerchief and steam rising off her shoulders, a split wire in her fingers and a child's arm in her other hand, and she did not look up.

"If that's the matter," she said, "put it on the table and open it. If it's not the matter, go away."

"It's the matter."

She looked up then.

She had not slept in four days and it showed in all of her at once, as it had not shown at the long table at the start of the month; her face had gone thin and her mouth had gone thin with it and there was a smear of something on her cheekbone that might have been soot. She looked at him across the lamps for as long as it took to know him, and then her eyes went to the sack against his chest.

"Ilsa told you," she said.

"Ilsa told the hospital. The hospital told the wire."

"I told her not to." She looked back down at the arm. "Open it, then. Gilly, he's opening the matter. Take it off him. Not with those hands. Wash them." The girl from Sela's room went to the pan. Imani pressed the fork into the child's arm, quick, five times, and let go, and the child, who was about four, looked down at her own arm with enormous astonishment and did not cry. "Good girl," said Imani. "That's all. You'll get a sore on it and it'll itch and you're not to scratch it. Gilly, write her. Next."

He opened the crock. He did not wait to be told anything after that; the one thing she did not need was somebody she had to stop and talk to. He took his coat off and rolled his sleeves and washed his hands in the pan, and went to the door, and began bringing the line in.

That was most of the night.

He brought them in out of the snow one at a time, mothers with children, men with their caps in their hands, old women who said they'd had it in the speckled year and were sent home, and old women who said so and had not, and were caught by Imani at a look. He held arms still. He held children who would not hold still on his knee and told them the ship over the hill was a very large lamp on a very long string. He kept the pan on the boil. At one in the morning he carried a bucket of lime down the first row with the man with the shovel, who had stopped being able to go into the houses, and the man told him his whole life on the way without once asking who he was.

In the third house of the first row there was a girl of about twelve who had come down with it five days before any needle reached the rows. Imani went in to her at two with a lamp, and came out after a quarter of an hour and did the next arm, and the next, without saying anything; and at a quarter to three the girl's mother came and stood in the doorway of the third house with the lamp, and did not come any further, and did not need to. Imani saw her through the cottage window. She finished the arm she had. Then she put the fork down in the pan very carefully and went out into the snow and stood with the mother on the step, with her bare arms in the cold and her hand on the woman's shoulder, saying something he could not hear.

When she came back she picked up the fork out of the pan and said, "Next," in the same voice as before.

He brought her the next.

The line ended at half past four. It did not end because there was nobody left in the rows; it ended because there was nobody left in the rows who was not done, or had not had it, or was not already in a limed house, which Gilly worked out from the book at the end of the table and read off in a voice that went up at the end as if she did not believe it. Imani made her read it again. Then she made the clerk read it. Then she sat down on the bench by the hearth, quite suddenly, as if her legs had decided it, and put her hands flat on her knees, and looked at the floor.

"Go to bed," she said to the pair, without looking up. "Both of you. In the pit office, Ilsa's got cots. Go on. You were very good. You were very good, both of you."

They went, too tired to argue.

Emeric banked the fire, and turned the lamps down, and put the lid on the boiling pan, and the cottage was quiet, and warm, and smelled of lime and wet wool and the spirit stove; and Imani sat on the bench with her hands on her knees and did not move.

He went and crouched down in front of her. After a while she put out her right hand, without looking, and found his wrist, and laid two fingers on it, as she had done on every ward round in four years and on the north tower's leads with the town lit below them. He felt her start to count. He felt her lose it somewhere past ten and start again, and lose it again.

"I can't," she said. "I can't get it."

"Come up," he said. "Come up to the ship. She's right overhead. There's a bed and a stove and nobody wants anything from you."

"Ilsa will want the cards."

"Ilsa can have the cards in the morning."

She looked at the cottage door, and at the dark outside it, and the snow coming past the light. Then she looked at him.

"All right," she said.



He went out to the pump and lit the lamp he had in his coat, and pointed it up at the sky, and worked the shutter three times, and three times again; and up in the dark over the hill a light answered, and then the dark itself began to come down. The K Discovery came down out of the snow cloud with her stones dimmed almost to nothing and her lamps out, a long shape blacker than the sky, and stopped at a hundred feet over the spoil heap, holding, the snow swirling up off the tip in the down-draught of her steadies, and dropped the cage on its line.

Imani had been up in a cage before. She stood in it with her bag at her feet and her arms folded and her face set, and it went up with the two of them in it, swinging a little in the wind, out over the rows and their lit windows and the white doorsteps and the black line of the beck, and in at the open hatch, where Fletcher took her bag from her without a word.

Fletcher looked at Emeric over her head.

"I'll take her up," said Fletcher, "out of the cloud. You can see the stars up there tonight." He did not wait to be answered. "I'll be forward with the lamp watch. All of us will. All night. It's a long watch tonight." He turned and went up the gangway toward the control cabin and pulled the door to behind him, and Emeric heard him say something to the two men on the lamp, and one of them laughed and was shut up.

The dome cabin was warm.

The heater stones under the floor kept it so whatever the air outside was doing. It had the wide bed under the glass and a stove with a kettle on it, and the shutters were open; and as Imani stood in the middle of the floor with her arms still folded, the snow cleared off the glass all at once and the cloud dropped away under them, and there was the whole of the sky over Grayrock, black and cold, with the stars out from one edge of it to the other.

She looked up at it. Then she sat down on the edge of the bed.

And then she came apart.

He had never seen it. Nobody in Kaldo had, in four years of plague and the end room and the cellar and the camp and Wat; the nurses went to the side door to cry and Imani went to the side door to stand. She did not make much noise now. Her shoulders went first, a long shudder as if she were cold, and then she bent over her knees and pressed both hands over her mouth and shook; and when he sat down and put his arm round her she turned into him so fast it knocked the breath out of him, her fists in his shirt and her face in his neck, and wept as if something had been torn.

He held her and said nothing.

Some of it came out in words, after a while, against his neck. The girl in the third house was called Annet. She'd been done eight days late. Eight days, because the rows were last, because the rows were always last, because that was where the pump was and the pit and the twelve to a room. The old man with the card. The child in the third village who'd been done eight days before and come down anyway and was going to live and nobody yet knew why. She did not know why. She had told the mother on the step that she was sorry, and she was sorry, and it was not enough, and she was tired of being the woman who stood on steps.

"Every one of them's in a column," she said into his shirt. "Every one. Ferra's got them all. I keep thinking she'll have run out of paper."

"She won't."

"No." A breath that shook. "She never does."

He held her while it went through her and out the other side. The ship climbed and levelled, and he felt her settle on her notch under them, and somewhere forward the lamp watch clicked its shutter at a hilltop and was answered. Imani cried until she was empty, and then lay against him breathing as if she had run up a hill, with her face wet against his collar and her fists loosening in his shirt one finger at a time.

Then she sat up.

She did it the way she did everything: on purpose. She wiped her face with the heels of both hands, hard, and took the kerchief off her hair and blew her nose in it, which made him laugh, which made her look at him with her eyes swollen and say, "What," with such outrage that he laughed again; and she pulled her hair loose out of its pins and shook it out over her shoulders and pushed it back with both hands, and took a long breath, and let it out.

"Right," she said. "That's done."

"Is it?"

"For tonight." She looked at her hands in her lap. They had stopped shaking. "Annet. I'll want her written on her own card. Not just the number." She looked up at him. "Don't tell anybody I did that."

"Ferra?"

"Ferra I'll tell myself." She was quiet. Then she said, in a different voice, low, "You came."

"You said not to send for you. Nobody sent for you. I came."

"You held a bucket of lime for three hours."

"Forty minutes. And the man with the shovel held it most of the time." He looked at her. "His wife left him in the spring for a man who sells pies in Wayford. He thinks she's well out of it."

That startled a laugh out of her, a cracked one, and she put her hand over her face. When she took it away she was looking at him differently: in the starlight off the glass, with her hair down and her face blotched and her eyes red, looking at him the way she had looked at the syringe held up against the dispensary window.

She reached out and found his wrist again. Two fingers. He felt her start counting, and felt her get it this time, and keep it.

"Seventy-two," she said.

"Is that good?"

"It's tired." She did not let go. "You're tired. You've been up since five yesterday." She looked at his face. "You've got lime in your eyebrow."

"Have I?"

She brushed it out with her thumb. Then she left her thumb there, at his temple, and he felt her other hand's two fingers still on his wrist, and felt his own pulse change under them; and she felt it too, and her eyes came up to his.

"Eighty," she said.



She kissed him.

The kisses of the autumn had been quick ones, on the slip and against the coping, and the one on the stair had been over before he knew it had started. This one took its time. She kissed him the way she went back over a dressing she had not had time to check properly, and then again, having found what she was looking for; and when she drew back her hand was flat on his chest and her face was quite serious.

"I want to," she said.

He knew what she meant. He did not answer at once, because there was a page in Ferra's book with their initials on it, and he had been the one, on the berth, to say that the first time ought to be somewhere better than a berth.

She watched him think it. "It's in the book," she said. "You've seen the top of the page. You'll not have read the bottom." Her hand stayed where it was. "Under Ferra's line. If one of us needs you before, she has you, and she tells the other the next morning. Herself. Nobody keeps score." Her mouth moved. "Ferra wrote under protest beside it. Then she signed it anyway."

"I hadn't read it," he said.

"Well. Now you have." She did not look away. "Tonight. Here. Now. I've been wanting to since the cellar, and I've been too busy since the cellar, and I'm going to be too busy until spring. And tonight I'm not busy." Her mouth moved again. "I've written it down. Not on paper. But I've decided it."

"You've had four days without sleep," he said. "You've just..."

"I know what I've just. I was there." She tilted her head. "I'm a healer. I know what a body wants at the end of a night like that, and so do you, and it's not to lie on its own in the dark and think about the third house." She looked at him properly. "You're tired."

"I'm very tired."

"I counted." She was quiet for a breath. Then her face changed, and she looked down at her own hand on his chest, and for the first time in four years of knowing her he saw Imani not know what to say.

"I don't want to disappoint you," she said.

It came out low and quick, and she looked cross with herself for letting it. She had laid her hands on half the barony and, as far as he had ever known, let nobody lay a hand on her; and she was sitting on the edge of a bed under the stars telling him so with her chin up.

He took her hand off his chest and held it.

"You can't," he said.

"You don't know that."

"I do." He turned her hand over and put his mouth to the inside of her wrist, where she always put her fingers on his, and felt her pulse jump under his lips.

"That's cheating," she said, not steadily.

"Yes." Then, because she was still waiting, and meant it: "You've been on your feet four days. You don't have to hold yourself up tonight. Let me take the weight. I'll move you. All of it. All you have to do is tell me."

He saw her understand him. She had been up with him over the marsh, two hundred feet over the water in the dark, and made him let go of her for one breath to feel the fall, and come up again at the end of his arm swinging and helpless with it.

She looked at him for a long breath with her lips parted.

Then she took a fistful of his shirt and pulled him in.



They had to get her boots off first. They were ward boots, wet through from the rows, and the laces had frozen into knots at Grayrock and thawed into worse ones in the warm. She swore at the left one. He got down on the floor and did it for her, and she put her hand on his head while he worked at it, and left it there when the boot came away, with her fingers in his hair.

His own came off faster. He stayed where he was, kneeling on the warm boards between her feet. Her ward dress buttoned down the front, small bone buttons, and he undid the top three and stopped and looked up at her.

"Go on," she said. "You're slow."

He went on. The shift under the dress was worn thin at the shoulders from four years in the ward copper, and when the dress came open at her throat he put his mouth there, in the hollow, and then lower, on the bone, and heard her breath go in and stop. Her hand closed in his hair. She did not pull him away.

She stood to let the dress go, and stepped out of it, and pulled the shift off over her head in one movement, quickly, the way she would have done it at a basin; and then stood in the starlight with it in her hand and looked down at it, and at him, and seemed to remember what she had just done.

"Well," she said. Her colour had come up, all the way down her throat. "There. That's done."

He could not have said anything. She was smaller than she seemed in the ward, and all of a piece, and the day was still on her: a red line round each wrist where her cuffs had been turned back all night, and grey lime dust at her ankles. He reached up for her hand, and she gave it to him, and he pulled her down to him on the floor, and she came, and knelt astride his thigh with her hands on his shoulders and her face a hand's breadth from his.

She did his shirt herself. She was quicker with buttons than he was and had no patience with his, and when she got to the cuffs and found the lime on them she lost patience with those as well and hauled the whole thing off over his head and threw it behind her without looking where it went. Then she put both hands flat on his chest and left them there, and he watched her look at him the way she looked at anything new: carefully, all of it, before she made up her mind.

"You've a bruise," she said. Her fingers found it, low on his ribs, where the rim of the lime bucket had sat against him for forty minutes. "Here."

"The bucket."

"I'll look at it in the morning." She bent her head and put her mouth to it instead, and then to the skin beside it, and he forgot about the bucket.

The rest went the ordinary way, with a good deal of getting in each other's road: his belt, which she did not know how to work and would not be shown; the buttons of his trousers; a bad half-second on one foot, which ended with him sitting down hard on the boards and her laughing at him through her nose, the short dry laugh, with her forehead against his shoulder. Then there was nothing left on either of them. She sat back on her heels to look, quite openly, and did not hurry it, and he let her, though it cost him something to stay still while she did.

"Up," she said at last. "The bed. I'm not doing this on a floor."

They lay down facing each other on the wide bed under the glass, and for a while it was only that: her skin against his all down the length of her, warm from the heater stones and from the stove, her knee between his, her breath on his collarbone. She was trembling, very slightly, in a warm room. He put his hand on her back and held it there, and she pressed closer, and after a time the trembling went out of her by itself.

"Go on, then," she said into his shoulder. "Take it."

The glyph came up off the back of his hand, slow and pale, and lit the cabin.

He took her weight first. She was easy to lift; a willing body gives the pull nothing to push back against, and she came up off the mattress hair first, lifting off the pillow before the rest of her, and her hand clamped on his forearm. Then his own weight, straight up, the only way the pull would ever take him. Then they were a foot above the bed with nothing under either of them, and he let a little air into it, enough to turn them, slowly, out over the middle of the cabin.

Two holds and a draught of air. He felt the price of it settle under his breastbone, a steady draw, like the last mile of a long climb, and knew it would not get any cheaper as the night went on. He did not need it to.

Imani made a small sound in her throat. Her hair drifted round her face. She pushed it back, and it drifted again, and she let it.

"Is this how it's going to be?" she said.

"If you like."

"I'll tell you if I don't."

There was nothing to brace against. He found that out at once. When he drew her in by the waist, he went to her as much as she came to him; when she pushed at his shoulder to see his face, they came apart by a yard and turned slowly end over end, and she laughed through her nose and reached for him and missed. He opened his hand toward her and set the pull to it, and she came across the air to his palm and fetched up against him, and he slid the hand round to the small of her back and left the pull there, under it. She stayed where it held her.

"You're cheating again," she said against his mouth.

"It holds you better than I can."

"I'm not complaining. I'm writing it down."

He learned her slowly, and she let him. The long muscle beside her spine had been knotted for four days; he found it with his other hand and worked it with his thumb until it gave, and she made a noise into his shoulder that was very nearly a groan. Then he went where her breathing sent him. Down the side of her ribs, which jumped. The inside of her knee, when she brought it up round his hip and the air turned them over so that the stars were below her. The soft place under her arm, where she was ticklish, which she told him about, indignantly. Her breast, under his mouth; she took a breath and held it and let it out in a rush, and put her hand on the back of his head and kept him there.

She talked. He had half expected silence and got instructions instead, in pieces, as they came to her: "there," and "no, slower," and once "that is not fair," in the voice she used for a figure in a column she had not expected. He did what every one of them said.

Her own hands were less sure of themselves, and she told him that too.

"I don't know what I'm doing with these," she said, frowning down at them, at his chest, and lower. Then she put one where she wanted to know about, and watched his face while she did it, and learned from what it did, which he could not have stopped if he had tried. His breath went out of him all at once.

"Oh," she said. "All right." She did it again, more slowly, watching. "All right."

When his hand went down between them she caught his wrist and held it where it was, and said "Slower," and then "Yes, like that," and then nothing at all for a long time. He watched her face the whole while. He had seen it bent over a child's chest and over a ledger and over Pell's drawings of a cow, and he had never seen it come undone. He could feel her note, the way he had felt it beside him over the marsh, fast and bright against his own, and it climbed, and kept climbing. Her eyes closed and opened again, hunting for him. Her mouth came open on his name. And then something went through her, long and shaking, and the air carried it as lightly as it carried the rest of her, so that she lifted a hand's breadth against the pull and came back to him with her breath torn and her face wet and astonished.

"Oh," she said. "That's what that is."

She began to laugh, and the laugh broke in the middle, and she was crying, all at once and with no warning at all. The tears did not fall. They came off her lashes and hung in the glyph light, small and bright, and drifted past his face, and he held her and said nothing and let the air take them.

"She was twelve," Imani said into his neck.

He held her.

"I'm not stopping," she said, after a while. "I want you to know that. I'm not stopping. I'm only..."

"Both," he said.

She nodded against him, twice. "Both." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand and looked at him, wet-eyed and perfectly steady, and he saw that she had come back by herself, and had not needed him to fetch her.

When she was ready she told him so, quite clearly, with her forehead against his and her eyes open.

"Now," she said. "I want you. Now. Don't you dare be careful."

He was careful anyway, and she knew he would be and let him. There was no bed under them and nothing to lay her down on, so he did it the only way the air allowed. He turned them until she was over him, with her knees either side of his hips, and set the pull at her back so that it gave the two of them a down of their own, toward his hand; and she came down onto him herself, as slowly as she liked. She went slower than he would ever have dared to ask her. Then she stopped, with her breath held, and he stopped with her.

"It stings," she reported, in her ward voice. "Not badly. Don't move." Her fingers were working at his shoulder, steadily, the way they worked at a swelling to find out how far it went. "I'm going to breathe and you're going to wait."

He let the pull at her back go slack, so that there was nothing holding her to him but herself, and told her he had.

"I know. I felt it." She did not move away. She breathed. Her fingers went on working at his shoulder, and he held as still as he had ever held anything, in the air under the stars, with the glyph light on her face and the draw of the holds steady under his breastbone, and watched her, and waited. It was the hardest thing he had done that year. He did not mind it.

"Clinically," she said at last, "it's fine." She let a long breath out against his mouth. "It's rather more than fine. Put it back."

He put the pull back.

"All right," she said, in a voice he had never heard her use. "All right. Yes." Her hands came up into his hair. "Go on. Slowly. Like that."

He went on, slowly. Like that.

In the air every movement needed holding. Anything he did with his hips pushed the two of them apart unless he held her, so he held her, his forearm across the small of her back and the pull set under his palm, and moved under her; and she worked out, frowning, with her lip in her teeth, that she could answer him by pressing in with her knees. Between them they found it. A long slow roll, the air turning them over and round while they moved, so that sometimes the stars were over her shoulder and sometimes over his, and once the dark bulk of the bed went past, above or below, he could not have said which.

After a while she took the pace from him, as she had told him she would. Her heels came round into the small of his back. Her nails began to matter. Her mouth was at his ear, open, and every sound she made she gave to him there, and each one told him something he had not known and wished he had known sooner. He was as tired as he had been at ten o'clock, and could feel it under everything, in his arms and in the long draw of the holds; and his body wanted, badly, to go faster than she was letting it, and she would not let it, and he let her have her way, and the waiting turned out to be the point.

Her hand found his wrist.

She was counting. He felt her try. He felt her fingers press and her lips move against his neck, and get to six, and to seven, and lose it, and start again from one, and lose it at three; and then she gave up counting altogether and took hold of him with both hands and said his name instead, quietly, and then not quietly. She tightened round him, all the way down, and it went through him from the base of his spine outward and took the breath out of his chest, and the glyph on the back of his hand flared so bright that the whole dome cabin went white with it and the stars went out behind their own reflection in the glass.

He nearly lost the hold. He did not lose it. They dipped a foot in the air and the air caught them and turned them, and she held on to him through all of it, shaking, with her face in his neck and her breath coming in gasps against his skin; and it was a while before he could think of anything, and then he thought of nothing but her.



Afterwards he let them down onto the bed as gently as a leaf, and the glyph went dim, and the air in the cabin stopped turning and was only warm.

She lay half across him with her cheek on his chest and her hair everywhere, over his face and his shoulder and the pillow, and he did not move it. Their clothes were all over the cabin floor. His shirt had come to rest on the kettle.

After a long time she lifted her head and looked at him, and then put two fingers on the side of his neck, under his jaw, and held them there.

"Hundred and four," she said. "Hundred and two. Coming down." She did not take her fingers away. "Your colour's very good."

"Is it."

"Much better than it was at ten o'clock." She considered him with her head on one side. "I've prescribed worse things."

He started to laugh, and could not stop for a while, and she lay on him and let him and smiled down at him with her eyes still red and swollen and her face entirely clear, as if somebody had washed it.

"You didn't," she said.

"Didn't what?"

"Disappoint me." She put her head back down on his chest. "I thought you'd want to know. In case you were worried."

"I wasn't worried."

"Your pulse was." He felt her smile against him. "At the start. Ninety-six, before I'd done anything at all."

"You weren't counting at the start."

"I'm always counting," said Imani, and yawned enormously, and was asleep before she had quite finished it, with one hand flat on his heart.

He did not sleep for some while. He lay under the glass with her weight on him and watched the stars go round; and somewhere forward the lamp clicked at a hilltop, and was answered, and clicked again, and a pair of children in a hamlet he would never see asked for another crock and were told it was coming. Then he slept too.



Hull two took them home at noon, when Imani had read and signed Ilsa's cards and told Gilly and the clerk three times that there was nothing more in the rows they could not do; and she slept in the hold all the way down on a pile of empty matter sacks, and woke as they came down over the river, and sat up with her hair flattened on one side, and looked at Emeric across the hold, and went quite pink.

He had never seen Imani go pink either. It was turning out to be a week for it.

She did not go to the hospital first. She went straight across the inner ward and up the counting room stair with her bag in her hand, and Emeric followed, because he could not think of anywhere else he was supposed to be; and Ferra was at her table with the Wayford returns and the tabulator clattering against the back wall under Ottoline's eye, and looked up, and looked at Imani's face, and put her pencil down.

Imani pulled out the chair across the table from her and sat in it.

"Last night," she said. "On the ship. Over Grayrock." She did not look at Emeric. She looked at Ferra, steadily, with her chin up, the way she had looked at him on the edge of the bed. "I needed him. I had him. I'm telling you first."

Ottoline, at the tabulator, became very interested in a stack of cards from Milford.

Ferra looked at Imani for a long breath. Then she looked at Emeric in the doorway, briefly, all the way up and down, as she looked at a column she had not quite finished adding, and back at Imani.

Then she picked her pencil up and opened her book at the page from Emberfell with the two initials at the foot of it, and wrote under Imani's clause, in her small square hand, a line with the date at the start of it, and held the book out across the table for Imani to read.

Imani read it. Her mouth twitched.

"You've put the time," she said.

"You said over Grayrock. Fletcher's log will have the time. I'll get it from Fletcher." Ferra took the book back and looked at the entry, and closed it, and laid her hand flat on it, and looked at Imani across the table; and something in her face, which Emeric could not read from the door and was not meant to, was quite gentle.

"Good," said Ferra.

Imani let out a breath.

Ferra picked up the Wayford returns. She turned a page. Then, without looking up from them, in exactly the voice she used to read the rents:

"Then mine's the bigger occasion."

Imani laughed out loud, a real one, the first he had heard from her since the camp closed; and across the room the tabulator jammed on a Milford card with a noise like a dropped tray, and Ottoline said something under her breath about Milford's clerks, and went into it with a hook up to the elbow, and did not turn round.

Emeric, in the doorway, was not consulted, and did not expect to be. He went to see about the noon tape.
