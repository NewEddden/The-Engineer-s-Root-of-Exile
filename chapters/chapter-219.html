Chapter 219 - First Water

Fletcher had built boats on that beach for thirty-one years, and he had never once in all of them built one indoors.

He stood at the head of the dock on the first morning it was finished and looked down the length of it, and did not like it, and could not have said why, and knew that he would like it very much in about a month and would never admit to either.

It was a hole. That was the plain truth of it, and he had watched it be a hole for two years: a long straight trench cut into the shore of the lower water where his slip used to run down, deep enough to stand a church in, with the sea kept out of the end of it by an earth dam while the masons worked. The mages had cut the first of it, standing in pairs on the lip of the trench and lifting the shingle out of it in great slow slides while Fletcher's six stood by with their mouths open. Then Hewitt's setters had lined it, the walls in dressed stone and the floor in the grey lime that set under water, course after course, by day and then by night under the white lights on their masts, until the hole had sides like a cathedral's and a floor as flat as a table. Then the dam had come out and the gate had gone in.

The gate was iron, two leaves, each as wide as a barn door is tall, hung on pintles the size of a man's thigh that Bram had cast in the foundry and brought down on a dray with an ox team and a crowd. When the leaves were shut they met in the middle at an angle, like a pair of hands pressed together, and the sea leaned on them, and the harder it leaned the tighter they shut. Fletcher had not believed that until he had seen it. There were sluices in each leaf, and sluices in the walls, and a pump house at the landward end with two steam pumps in it on the Drop House's wire that could take the whole dock down to the floor in a tide.

And over it all, the roof. Iron trusses, riveted, spanning wall to wall, with glass in the roof lights so that the dock was lit by day like a church, and gas lamps on brackets down both walls for the nights. A crane ran the length of it on rails along the tops of the walls, a travelling beam with a crab on it that could lift ten tons and put them down anywhere in the hole to within the width of a finger. And on the landward side, through a door in the wall, Bram's fitting shop: a long room of benches and lathes and a forge, so that anything the dock wanted could be made fifty feet from where it was wanted.

Three hundred feet of iron would go in there. That was what it was for. Fletcher had drawn her, and the prince had drawn her, and they had argued over her for a year and a half across the long-room table and the yard table and the dock's edge, and her keel blocks were cut and stacked in the shed by the pump house waiting for the floor to be ready for them.

He was not going to put three hundred feet of iron into a hole he had never floated anything into.

"The Hod," he said, at the long-room table, the week before.

The prince looked at him. So did everybody.

"She goes in first," Fletcher said. "Before the keel. I want to flood that dock and float something into it and shut the gate on it and pump it dry and see it sit on its blocks, and I want the something to be forty feet of lighter and not three hundred feet of iron that took two years." He put his hand flat on the table. "If the gate goes, I lose a lighter. If a sluice lets go, I lose a lighter. If the blocks are wrong, I lose a lighter's bottom. I can build a lighter in five weeks. I did it once." He looked round the table. "And she wants the work. You know she does. She's been flying on a rack and a prayer for three years and coming down every night with her crew frozen and nothing but a toe-rail between them and three miles of nothing. I said I'd put a rail on her. I said it the first night she came down, with her crew frozen to the toe-rail. I've never done it."

"Hull two carries every run while she's in," Ferra said. "Alone."

"Hull two can carry every run. She's three times the size."

"And the Drop House runs on the store."

"There's a store now," said Bram. "Island pitch. It'll hold."

Ferra looked at her book for a moment and then wrote in it, which was how she said yes.

They floated her in on the top of the spring tide in the second week of the autumn.

They flooded the dock first, through the sluices, and that took the morning. Fletcher stood on the gate the whole of it, on the narrow iron walk along its top, and watched the sea come in through the sluices in six fat brown ropes of water and spread across the grey floor and climb the walls, course by course, until it stood level with the sea outside and the two leaves of the gate had nothing leaning on them at all. Then the men on the capstans walked the gate open, slowly, and it swung back into its recesses in the walls with a long groan of iron, and there was a way in.

The Hod came in under tow behind the yard's skiff, with Ned Tallis at her helm because he would not let anybody else, and Fletcher's six at her sides with poles, fending her off the walls. She looked very small in there. She looked like a coal barge that had wandered into a cathedral. She came up the dock under the glass roof with the light coming down on her in long bars from the roof lights, black and blunt and ugly, with her one hatch and her toe-rail and her racks under their canvas, and the whole yard stood along the tops of the walls and watched her come, and nobody said anything.

They brought her over the blocks, and held her there with lines to both walls while the six sounded under her with a pole to see she sat true. Then the gate swung shut behind her, and met in the middle, and the sluices were closed, and Bram went into the pump house and started the pumps.

The water went down. It went down a hand in the first quarter hour, and then faster, as the pumps found their stride, and the Hod came down with it, slowly, until she was standing on the blocks with a foot of water round her and then a hand and then none. She settled. Fletcher felt her settle through the soles of his boots on the wall above her, the way a man feels a horse settle under him when it stops fighting the bit. She sat on the blocks in the middle of the grey floor, dry, with the sea on the other side of the gate higher than her deck, and did not move.

Then Bram came out of the pump house and stood at the landward end and cocked his head.

"One's weeping," he said.

Fletcher had heard it too. It was a small sound under the pumps, a thin steady hiss, and when they stopped the pumps it was louder. Bram walked the floor of the dock along the foot of the gate with his head on one side, like a thrush on a lawn listening for a worm, and stopped at the third sluice on the seaward leaf, low, where a jet of water no thicker than a straw was coming through the edge of the plate and pattering on the floor.

"Casting," said Bram. "There's a flaw in the seat. It's not the gate."

He packed it. He sent to the fitting shop for oakum and tallow and a sheet of the new island rubber, and cut the rubber to the shape of the seat with a knife, and took the sluice's plate off with the crane's crab holding it and bedded the rubber under it and bolted it home, and stood back.

They flooded the dock again that afternoon, and pumped it again in the evening, and the Hod came down onto her blocks for the second time and sat there, and this time there was no sound under the pumps at all.

Fletcher walked the floor of his dock in the gas light, from the gate to the landward wall, under the Hod's bottom and round her, with his hand on the grey of the floor and then on the stone of the walls, all the way along, the way he walked a hull. It was dry. It was dry right through. The sea stood outside the gate, twenty feet above his head, and leaned, and could not get in.

He came back up the ladder and stood at the head of the dock with Bram and the prince.

"Well," he said.

That was all. The prince seemed to understand it. Bram certainly did.

The work on her started the next day and went on for five weeks, and most of it Fletcher could see.

The part he could not see went on at night.

The mages who had cut the dock had gone home in the summer, back to the wall and the flat and the tower, and none of them came down to the yard any more, and Fletcher's yard wall had been built high on the landward side for exactly that reason. So at night, when the six had gone home and the gas was turned down to a glimmer, the prince came down from the keep with Bram, and they went into the dock and shut the door in the wall behind them, and the roof lights went dark overhead, and they worked on her stones. Fletcher went in once, the first night, and stood at the foot of the ladder with a lamp, and watched the prince sitting on the Hod's deck in the dark with his hand flat on the plate and his eyes shut, not moving, for a quarter of an hour, while Bram waited beside him with a gravity stone in a housing on his knees. Then the prince opened his eyes and said something, and Bram fitted the housing into a seat he had cut in her frame that afternoon, and the prince put his hand on it, and shut his eyes again.

Fletcher went back up the ladder. He did not go in at night again. It was not his work, and a man who watched another man's work in the dark was a man who would one day be asked what he had seen.

But he saw what came of it in the mornings. New seats cut into her frame, low at the corners of her stern, with vents to the outside behind them, round and cased in brass, so that the air stones could push her along without a sail or a screw. That was the prince's. A screw at her stern on a hinged arm that folded up flat under her counter when she was flying and dropped down into the water when she was not. That was Fletcher's, and he was proud of the hinge. A small steam engine in the space forward of the hold, the same as the one in the K Discovery, with a short stack through the deck, to drive the screw on the water and warm her on the way up, because she had flown cold for three years and her crew had come down every night with their fingers white. And three legs, one forward and two aft, that folded up into wells in her bottom and came down on rams when she wanted to stand on a beach, with brass-rimmed caps on the deck over each one where the ram's head came through.

"No foils," said the prince, on the third morning, looking at her from the dock floor.

"No foils," Fletcher agreed. "She's a lighter. She doesn't need to be fast on the water. She needs to be on it and then off it."

"You'd have put an oil engine in her if I'd let you."

"I would not." Fletcher sniffed. "I'd have put an oil engine in the one I'm building next. She's for learning on, not this." He jerked his chin at the Hod. "This one gets steam, like the Discovery, because steam's what we know and her boiler's what's wrong with her. She's been going up with a cold boiler for three years because there was nothing on her to warm it but the crew. That's the one thing in her that the new one fixes. When the new one flies, and she's got a fire in her that doesn't need keeping in, and it's burning your oil off your island, every man on the lower water who ever froze on the Hod will know why." He looked at the keel blocks stacked in the shed. "That's what she's for. Showing them what they had, so they know what they've got."

Bram went away in the middle of the second week, for eight days, and Fletcher did not find out where until he came back.

He had been in the fitting shop the evening before, working late, on something Fletcher did not recognise: a short iron bar about as long as a man was tall, thick as a man's arm, with a chisel edge forged on one end and a socket bored in the other, and a seat cut halfway along it for a stone. The prince had been in the shop with him. Fletcher had seen the forge glow under the shop door until past midnight. And in the morning Bram was gone, with a cart, and a tripod of rail lengths on the cart, and a coil of rope, and the bar.

He came back on the ninth day with mud to his knees and a look on his face that Fletcher had last seen on it the night the Drop House first turned.

"Well?" said Fletcher.

"Water," said Bram. "At sixty feet. Hollis's rick yard." He sat down on a coil of line in the fitting shop and pulled his boots off. "Two men on a windlass. No engine. No mast. Eight days."

Fletcher did not know much about wells. He knew enough to know that a well sixty feet into the ground in eight days with two men and no mast was not a thing that happened.

"How?"

Bram looked at him for a moment. Then he nodded at the bar, which he had brought back with him and leaned against the wall by the door, with the mud of Hollis's yard dried on its chisel end.

"There's a stone in the middle of it," Bram said. "Light when you lift it. Heavy when it drops. You wind it up and it weighs nothing and you let it go and it comes down like the hammer of God. Every stroke." He rubbed his eyes. "Hollis stood and watched it the whole eight days, when he wasn't meant to be at the harvest. On the sixth day he brought his wife. She asked me whether it hurt the ground." He almost smiled. "He's got a pump in his rick yard now. First time in the farm's life the fire buckets haven't come up from the lake. He's told the whole road. They're calling it Bram's borer at the market."

"It's not for Hollis's rick yard," said Fletcher.

"No," said Bram. "It's not."

He did not say what it was for, and Fletcher did not ask him. But the next week the K Discovery went out on the ebb behind the Marsh Hen on two nights running, at hours Fletcher was only told the afternoon before, and on both nights there went down to her in the dark, on the yard's own dray, a stack of rail lengths and a bundle of iron pipe and a crate of plate and the bar, which was not a thing a man took to sea for any reason Fletcher could think of. And on the first night Bram went with them, and Ward, and Bram came back at dawn grey in the face and silent and smelling of the same foul rotten-egg stink that the knights had been scrubbing off in the yard's wash house all spring.

Fletcher had built the ship. He knew where she went. He did not know what they did when she got there, and he intended to go on not knowing it for as long as they let him.

The last of the work on the Hod was his, and he did it with his own hands, which he had not done on a hull since before the K Discovery.

He plated her deck again, over the old iron, in black. He laid timber over the plate, oak, so that a man's boots would not freeze to it at three miles, and trimmed the edges of it in brass, because the brass had come off a wreck on the bar and he had been saving it for something. He built a cupola over the forward end of her rack hatch, a little iron house with a glass in its door, so that a man going down into the hold to see to the racks could shut the cold out behind him and not let the whole of the band down into her belly after him.

And he put a rail on her.

A proper rail, all the way round, waist high, turned oak on iron stanchions, bolted through the deck plate and through the frame under it so that a man could fall against it with his whole weight and it would not give. It took him four days. He did every stanchion himself. When it was done he walked the length of it from bow to stern on both sides with his hand on it, and then went back and leaned on it by the hatch, where the crew always stood, with his whole weight, as hard as he could, and it did not move.

Ned Tallis came and stood beside him and leaned on it too.

"Took you long enough," Ned said.

"Three years," said Fletcher.

"I'd noticed."

The deckhouse was the last thing.

The old Hod had had no deckhouse. She had had a helm on an open deck and a canvas screen on a frame that the crew huddled behind in the worst of the cold. Fletcher took the screen off and burned it in the fitting shop's forge with some pleasure. In its place he built a house: a curved roof, iron ribs under planking under copper, with glass on three sides of it, the good new Kaldo glass that the prince said would not let the medium through. The helm went inside it. So did the engine's gauges and the rack gauges and the relay stone. The short stack went up through the roof at its back.

And in the after corner of it, behind a partition, he was told to fit something else, and nobody told him who for.

A bunk, padded, the length of a tall man, built into the wall with a lip on its outer edge so that nobody would roll out of it if she lurched. A stove, small, with a fire crystal in it in an iron box, that would boil a kettle and warm the corner and would never want feeding. A floor that was warm: the prince came down one night and did something to the plate under the corner with a dial and a battery of charged stones in a box, and after that the floor of the corner was warm to a bare foot even at three miles, and the rest of the deckhouse was not. A curtain on a rail, and behind it a privy that emptied over the side through a trap. And a shelf.

The shelf was the thing Fletcher thought about.

He had been told to put up a shelf, for books, in the corner. Nobody said how high. A shelf for books in a ship's deckhouse would go at the height of a man's eye standing, because that was where you put a shelf, and a man standing would reach up and take down what he wanted and read it on his feet, at the helm, between watches. That was what a shelf on a ship was for.

He stood in the corner for a while with his rule in his hand. Then he sat down on the bunk. He sat the way an old man sits, carefully, with a hand on the edge of it, and leaned back against the wall, and put his hand up, not high, the way an old man reaches who does not want to get up again once he has sat down. And he marked the wall where his hand came to, and put the shelf there.

Nobody told him who the corner was for. He did not ask. There was only one man in Kaldo who read the way that shelf was built for, sitting down, for hours, with his back to a wall; and only one man in Kaldo whose hands Fletcher had once been told got bad enough in the cold that he could not hold a pen; and only one man who had ever flown in the Hod and come down looking twenty years younger and wanting to go straight back up. Fletcher had never in his life made a shelf for a particular man before without being told who. He found he rather liked it.

She floated out on the top of the spring tide in the middle of the autumn, five weeks and two days after she floated in.

They flooded the dock and walked the gate open and the skiff took her line, and she came down the length of it under the roof lights on her own water, and she did not look like a coal barge any more. She looked like what she was, which was a small black iron ship with brass at her edges and a house on her deck with its glass catching the light, and a rail all the way round her that a man could lean on. Ned was at the helm inside the glass. He did not wave. He kept his eyes on the gate and his hands on the helm, and brought her through it with a foot to spare on either side, and out into the lower water, and the six cheered her from the walls, which they had never done for anything.

Fletcher did not cheer. He waited until she was clear, and the gate was shut behind her, and the sluices closed, and Bram had gone into the pump house and started the pumps.

The water went down. The floor of the dock came up out of it, grey and wet and clean, with the Hod's blocks standing on it in their row where she had sat. Fletcher went down the ladder while the last of the water was still running out through the sluices, and stood on the floor, and looked at the blocks.

Then he called for the crane.

They took the Hod's blocks up one at a time on the crab and swung them away to the landward end and stacked them. And they brought the keel blocks out of the shed by the pump house, the ones he had cut and dressed and stacked two summers ago, heavier than the Hod's by three times and longer by three times, and lowered them into the dock one at a time, and he walked each one to its mark on the floor, with the six, and set it there, and sounded it, and went on to the next. It took the rest of the day and half the night, under the gas.

When the last was down he stood at the head of the dock and looked along the row of them, forty blocks in a line down the middle of the grey floor, from the gate to the landward wall, and nothing on them yet at all.

Three hundred feet. He had drawn her and argued her and cut her blocks. He had floated forty feet of lighter into a hole to make sure the hole held. It held.

"Tomorrow," he said, to nobody, and turned the gas down, and went home.
