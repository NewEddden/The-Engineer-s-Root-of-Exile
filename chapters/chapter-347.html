Chapter 347 - The Crown's Bank

Ward's order went to the printer with a number in it that Saban didn't want printed.

It was a page and a half in Ward's hand, square and slow and pressed hard enough to read from the back of the sheet, with the last six lines in Ferra's. The six and Emeric had it on the long table on the morning of the twenty-third, and the number was in the third paragraph.

"Four hundred and ninety-four," said Saban.

"Yes," said Ferra.

"In an ordinary winter, on the floor in the capital, dead." He was standing, of course. "The leaflets printed the rate. A rate's a thing a man works out for himself. A number's a thing you can be wrong about in public."

"It's Armand's rotation books," said Ferra. "Every night he logged. Men a night, nights a winter, deaths a night. I didn't guess any of it."

"And the nights he didn't log?"

"Then it's out by a few."

"By a few," said Saban, "is what the Church will print in every pulpit from here to the border. Kaldo's figures are out. What else is out?"

Armand had been sitting with his young face in his old hand. He took the hand away.

"I looked for the Church's own count for thirty years," he said. "In the tower's library and theirs. They haven't one. If they say four hundred and ninety-four is wrong, they'll have to say what's right, and to say it they'll have to count, and when they've counted..." He spread his fingers. "Let them."

"That's what the last six lines are for," said Ferra.

Emeric read them again. Every candidate who came to the crown's yard would be written down by name on the day he came. Every death in the crown's yard would be written down by name on the day it happened. The books would be open to anyone who asked. And if Kaldo was wrong about anything in them, the mistake would go in the book too, with Kaldo's name beside it.

"It has to cost us something," said Ferra. "Or it's a leaflet."

It had cost them already. Nobody needed to say so. Ferra's honest page on the forcing deaths had gone into the heresy case in the capital, and half that case had been built out of Kaldo's own paper.

"I want the road in it," said Emeric.

Ward looked up.

"Not the seats. The road. The yard, the cold water, the bands, the drain and feed, all of it, printed. Velmoor puts its sons on its own floor every winter, and so does Solreth. If they had it written down..."

"They'd do it badly," said Ward.

"They'd do it."

"Badly. With no Band to feed them and no medic at every bed and nobody who's ever seen it done. They'd bury more than the floor does." Ward put his finger on the paper. "And then they'd say Kaldo's road killed them. And they'd be right."

"Imani?"

"He's right," said Imani, who had been reading the ward's book with her feet on the next chair. "I'd lose half of them in the first month to people who think more food is always better. You've seen what forcing does."

Emeric had. He had stood at the end of a row of beds after the forcing and counted. He let it go, and didn't like letting it go, and Ward watched him let it go and said nothing about it, which was a kindness of Ward's sort.

"It goes in," said Ward. "The number. Saban, I've heard you."

"Put on the record that I said it."

"It's on the record," said Ferra, writing.

Ward signed at the foot with the pen Bram had made him, a W and a line. Then he sat with the pen in his hand and didn't put it down.

"He asked me for this," he said. "In year eleven. Bring me a road, he said. I'll give it to them." He set the pen down beside the page, square. "Well."

Nobody said anything to that. Bram, whose eyebrows had grown back about two-thirds of the way and gave him a look of permanent mild surprise, took the order and read it through once more for spelling, because the printer had done knights without the k on the last leaflet and Bram had taken it personally.



The Kaldo News carried it the next morning on the front, the whole of it, under a line in plain type: BY ORDER OF THE MASTER OF THE KNIGHTS' BASE, UNDER THE CROWN.

Crown knighthood no longer required the rite. The knights' road was the crown's standard road, free inside the crown; the seats for Duvrenne's sons opened from this day under the treaty. The crown forces, Kaldo's garrison among them, were to take the crown's device on their plate. One unit kept its plate bare. Then the number, and Ferra's six lines.

By ten there were men in the base's long shed stencilling the crown on breastplates in blue enamel, and the whole base smelt of it. A garrison sergeant followed the painter's boy down the rows, complaining that it had gone on crooked, that it was always going to go on crooked, that thirty years in bare plate and now he looked like a tea caddy.

The three stood in the yard in bare plate.

It was the MK3s, black and plain, the helms under their arms. Arthur in the middle, Mira on his left, Bren on his right, already sweating in the July sun and not minding. Behind them the class stood along the fence in their drill shirts, and the knights off duty, and the cooks, and Imani on the rail by the gate with her bag, because she had decided that anything at the base involving the word ceremony was likely to end with somebody bleeding.

Ferra had struck one line from the old form of words, the line about the Light's choosing, with a single rule and her initials in the margin. The rest she had left as it was, because Ferra said you didn't throw out a ledger because one column was lies.

Emeric read it. He didn't use a sword for it. Ward had said a knight was made by his yard, not by a lump of steel on his shoulder, and Emeric had agreed with him. He said the words to each of them, Arthur first, and put his hand on each shoulder plate where the crest would have gone and didn't, and each said the answer back. Bren said his a little loud. Mira said hers looking at Ward.

Then Rylan stepped up beside Ward with the base's book open on his arm.

"First names," said Rylan, in the voice that didn't change. He wrote them as he said them. "Arthur. Mira. Bren." He looked at the three of them over the book. "The Crestless."

"Is that it?" said Bren.

"That's it," said Mira.

"I thought there'd be more."

"There'll be a great deal more," said Ward. "It's called work."

The class along the fence did not cheer, because nobody had told them whether they should. Then one of the cooks did, and after that there was no stopping it.



Mira took the yard in the afternoon.

Ward had told her to. He hadn't told her what to teach. Emeric stood at the fence beside him and watched her walk out into the middle with a wooden spear, and the class come round her in a ring, forty of them, the youngest about the age she had been when Ward first put a stick in her hand.

"Who's the strongest?" said Mira.

They looked at each other. They pushed a lad forward, a broad farm boy from the west road with hands like spades, and he came, red to the ears.

"Come at me," said Mira. "With the spear. Hard as you like. Try to put me on the fence."

He was strong. He came in low and fast, all his weight behind the point, the way she had gone at the old knight in the dyers' square. Mira took half a step sideways and back. Her left hand came off her haft and took his, just behind the head, and helped it on, and he went past her into nothing with his weight on his front foot, and she turned him with one hand. He sat down hard in the dust.

The ring laughed. Mira didn't.

"Don't laugh. That was me. In the war." She held a hand out and the farm boy took it and she pulled him up. "I was stronger than the man who did that to me. Much stronger. I pushed him a yard back on the first blow and I saw his face and I thought, that's done. Stronger doesn't see anything. He'd been reading my weight since I came over the cart. He knew where I'd be before I did." She looked round the ring. "You read the spear. He read you. Again."

The farm boy came again, slower, watching her, and she showed them at walking pace where his weight had been and how she'd known: the front knee, the back heel coming up, the way a body tells you it's committed half a breath before the arms do. She made them pair off and do it to each other, and went round the pairs moving hips and knees with her hands.

A thin dark girl with a Wayford accent asked the thing they all wanted to ask.

"Can we see?"

Mira looked at her. Then she put her spear down and lifted her right arm over her head, and there it was along the ribs under the arm: a long curved line, pink at the edges and white in the middle, with the stitch marks either side like the teeth of a comb, nineteen of them, where the plate had been cut away for the swing.

"Imani can take that off," said the thin girl. "Can't she? My uncle says she took a burn off a stoker's face and you can't tell."

"She can."

"Then why..."

"She offered," said Mira. She put her arm down. "I said no. I wanted something to show you." She picked up the spear. "Pairs. Walking pace. Watch the knee."

On the rail by the gate, Imani, who had offered three times, looked at the sky with the face of a woman not saying anything at length.

Ward watched the pairs for a while.

"She'll teach it better than I did," he said.

"You taught it to her."

"I told it to her. Every day for years. She didn't believe me till a man put a sword in her." Ward touched his strapped ribs through his shirt. "She'll not have to tell them twice. They've seen it."

Rylan came along the rail with a letter from Kesrath under the treaty's eleven lines: the sons of Duvrenne its crown would pay to send, for the master of the base to choose from. Ward read it standing. "Forty-two names. For eight places." He put it in his coat. "They'll have it when I've seen the lads stand. Not on paper."

Out in the yard the farm boy went past Mira again at walking pace, and this time, at the last step, he felt it, and stopped himself on his back foot, and stood there wobbling with his face lit up like a lamp. Mira hit him lightly on the helm with the butt of her spear.

"Better," she said.



The letter from Ostrel came on the twenty-fifth.

It had taken three weeks to go and come. Emeric had told Ostrel's envoy on the third of July, in the long room, with the envoy's two secretaries behind him and Saban at the window: Randillia would have Father Doran as its cardinal, or Randillia would have no cardinal at all, and any other man Ostrel sent to the capital in a red hat would find the border closed to him at the first post.

Saban had told him beforehand that it was a provocation. Armand had told him that a king could not make a cardinal, and that no king in four hundred years had tried.

"I'm not making him one," Emeric had said. "I'm telling them who we'll have."

"That's the same thing said slower," said Armand.

It was. He'd done it anyway.

The letter was in a chancery hand, on Ostrel's heavy cream paper, with Ostrel's seal, and Ferra opened it at the long table and read it and looked up.

"Sixty-one words," she said. "His name once. Yours not at all."

"And?"

"Confirmed. Cardinal of Randillia. From the feast of something I've never heard of." She passed it over. "It's the shortest yes I've ever read. It's shorter than most no's."

Saban read it over Emeric's shoulder and set out why, standing. The leaflets had said from the first that Kaldo made war on Augustine and not on the faith, and had been believed. Doran's letter had emptied the capital's cathedral without a single death. A pope who refused him would have to say why to every parish in a kingdom that had read Doran's letter aloud on a Sunday.

"He'd lose the kingdom's churches," said Saban. "Not to us. To Doran. So he's given them Doran and kept the kingdom." He looked at the seal for a long time. "He's not happy."

Emeric knew a little better than Saban how unhappy. The man who sealed Ostrel's letters wore Augustine's face, and it had been Augustine's cathedral Doran emptied. He didn't say any of it. Ferra turned her pen over once and didn't look at him, and Imani yawned and asked whether anyone was going to tell Doran or leave it in the post like a bill.

They found Doran in the bicycle shed behind the chapel, mending a puncture.

He had his sleeves rolled and his old black cassock hitched into his belt, and the tube in a bucket of water, looking for bubbles with the concentration of a man looking for sin. He was sixty, or near it. Since June he had ridden frame one off the Free Wheel's first list to every sick bed in the parish, ringing a bell Wren had filed down for him to the lowest note it would go.

He read the letter with wet hands, holding it by the corners.

"They've spelled my name right," he said. "That's a mercy. They didn't, the last time they wrote to me." He gave it back. "I didn't want this."

"I know."

"I said so. In the spring, and again in June." He found his bubble, and marked it with a thumbnail, and held the tube up to the light. "A king doesn't make a cardinal. I told you that too."

"Armand told me."

"Armand's right." Doran reached for the patch tin. "And you did it anyway, and Ostrel's swallowed it, and now I'm the Church in this kingdom, and I've a puncture." He roughed the rubber with the little file from the tin. "So. Two things, and then I'll fix my wheel."

"Go on."

"The season's mine now. In this kingdom. The floor in the capital, the rite, the roll. It was always the cardinal's to open, at first snow." He laid the patch on. "I shan't."

Emeric had not thought of that. He saw from Ferra's face that she had, some time ago, and had been waiting to see if Doran would.

"They'll say you've no right."

"They'll say I've no right to the hat either. Ostrel's said I have." Doran pressed the patch down with his thumb. "The second thing. Vantic's House opens its bank on the thirty-first. There'll be people at me by August to say the Church forbids interest and the cardinal must rule." He looked up. "I won't. Not this year. Not next. I've a kingdom of priests who've just learned they were preaching a floor of dead men every winter. I'm not starting on money."

"Nobody asked you to."

"Somebody will," said Doran. He let go of the patch, and tested it with a fingernail, and it held. "They always do."



Tess had the bank's door reader in pieces on a trestle in the half-built hall on the twenty-sixth, with a lamp on a hook over it and the plasterers working round her on the ceiling.

It was the same reader as the inner ward gate's, in the same brass case, with the same small stone in its throat that woke to a citizen's card. The bank's would do more: tell the counters whose card had come in at the door, and agree with every other reader in the hall, or the hall would not open.

"You can watch," said Tess, without looking up, when Emeric came in. "Don't help."

He watched. Bram had come over from the works with a bag of brass screws and stood behind her, and didn't help either.

She had the stone on a cork mat and her fingertips on it, and she talked while she wrote, not to them, to the stone, in the flat voice she had used since she was a girl in Bram's shop.

"Signed hand," she said. "First note. It doesn't take a note from anyone who isn't on its list. I'm on it. Armand. You." She didn't look at Emeric. "Not Bram."

"I don't want to be on it," said Bram.

"I know. That's why you're not." Her fingers moved. "Anyone else writes to it, it doesn't argue. It doesn't answer. Like nobody spoke."

"And if somebody makes it listen?"

"Tamper guard." Tess opened her eyes. "Second note. A write it doesn't like, a forced one, it locks. Shut. Won't read a card, won't open a door, rings at the watch house. You have to come and look at it with a lamp and a list." She closed her eyes again. "I copied it off the old races' sleeping stone."

Bram's eyebrows went further up than they had grown back.

"Off the golem."

"It worked."

"It worked on me. It threw me through a shed."

"That's how I know it works." Tess's fingers did something small and precise. "Theirs fails open, though. Wakes up and does whatever it was last told, in a mess. Ours fails dead. That's the only change." She opened her eyes. "Third note. Canary."

"Canary," said Emeric.

"Saban's word. Well, yours. You gave it to him years ago, for the toll house man. He says it's a stupid name." She sat back. "It's a note that does nothing. Nobody needs it. Nobody's got any reason to open it, ever. So if anybody opens it, or reads it, or even puts a finger on it from inside, it rings. Same bell. The first time somebody tries, we know."

She said it the way she'd tell an apprentice to oil a bearing. Emeric stood in the plaster dust and thought: a digital signature, a tamper seal that fails safe, a canary token, and every reader checked against every other, which was a checksum by any other name. She had got all four by herself, out of a dead golem and a grain trader's habit and years of Bram telling her that if a thing could go wrong in a shop it would go wrong on a Friday.

"Who's it for?" said Bram. "All that. On a door."

Tess looked at him as though he'd asked who a lock was for.

"Nobody," she said. "It's a door. You write a door properly or you don't write it." She turned the stone in the light. "I'm doing the gate readers the same way after. I did them badly the first time. I was younger."

A boy from the telegraph office came in through the plasterers with a slip for Tess. She read it, and her face did nothing at all, and she folded it into her apron pocket and picked up a screwdriver.

"What is it?"

"The Well," she said. "Their lab's grown a gravity stone. On Band grit. First one." She held her hand out to Bram for a screw without looking. "It holds."

Bram gave her the screw. Then he went out into the yard behind the hall, where nobody could see him, and Emeric, who could sense through walls when he wanted to and for once didn't need to, heard him laugh.



The Stewards' Council sat that evening for the first time under the Charter, as the council of the Root, in the long room with the windows open.

Bryda came down from Grayrock and Paxton and Aster from their holdings, with the members the holdings had elected in June, two of whom had brought their own pens in case Kaldo's were different. They did a water rate and a road and an argument about a bridge that went on until the lamps were lit, and Emeric said almost nothing, which Ferra had told him beforehand was the whole of his job.

Ferra kept the book at the side table. Partway through the bridge she turned back a long way, through years of her own square hand, to a page from year six, when the three stewards had sat at this table and asked for a mason, a nurse and a teacher, and he had said not yet, a year or two, and she had written one word in the margin. DEFERRED.

Grayrock had its clinic. Its school had opened in year ten. Every holding had its masons and its teachers now, most of them its own. Nobody had turned back to the page.

Ferra wrote the date beside the word, and under it DONE, and blotted it, and turned forward again to the bridge.

"What was that?" said Paxton, who missed nothing.

"An old page," said Ferra.



Vantic sent for them on the twenty-eighth, after dark, to the glass tower on the civic ring.

It was the same room as the other times: the long desk, the three lamps on their brass arms, the town below the glass in its rings of lights. She had a glass of something cold at her elbow and nothing on the desk at all, which in Vantic was a sign of great seriousness.

She waited until Ferra had shut the door.

"I can afford to know now," she said.

He had expected it since May. He sat down.

"Since when?"

"Since your Charter's been law for six weeks, and my House opens your kingdom's bank in three days, and there's a clause in the Charter about stones that I can't lend against or refuse to lend against without knowing what it covers." Vantic turned the glass a quarter turn. "Since Armand came into my counting room last year looking thirty and asked me, in the voice he had at seventy, about his tower's account. I've been not asking about that for a year. Then in May six more of you came back looking like you'd had a very long holiday. It's very tiring."

Ferra had a sheet of paper in her coat, a page in her square hand with nothing on it that didn't need to be there. She put it on the desk in front of Vantic.

The tree, in the dome, and what grew on it. What happened to a body that ate a control stone, and the long sleep, and what the nine had woken as. The clause, word for word, as it stood in the Charter: who might have a stone, who might not, who decided, and in what order.

Vantic read the clause first. She went straight past the tree to the bottom of the page and read the clause twice, with one finger beside the line, the way she'd read the terms of a loan before she looked at the asset. Then she read the rest.

It took her a long time.

"Healing first," she said at last.

"Yes."

"Service on the record. Ties by public lot." She put the page down. "And the Stone Board's an open book. Every name, every refusal." She looked at Emeric. "You know what a thing like this does to a bank."

"Tell me."

"Every man in the kingdom with money will try to buy one. Through me first, because I'm the House. Then through anybody." She tapped the clause. "This says he can't. And if he tries, it's in a book with his name in it, and a woman drawn by lot reads it out." Her mouth moved at one corner. "Prudence Fairley. She punched my ticket on the express in June and wouldn't take a bill."

She folded the page twice and handed it back to Ferra. She wasn't going to write any of it down. She would carry it the way she carried the K Amara and the Forge and the island: in her head, and nowhere else, and never lie about any of it, only stay silent.

"Now the card," said Vantic.



He had the chip in his pocket. It was the same one, card one's, that she had held in March and put down on the desk. He put it in her palm again.

It stayed grey. It would stay grey in any hand but its holder's, and he laid his own hand over hers, as before, and let her feel it answer him from inside, the cut waking to his note and showing it, small and bright, like a struck match seen through a closed hand.

Vantic sat with her hand under his and her eyes on the middle distance.

"There's no money on it," she said.

"No."

"Nothing on it at all. Only you." She took her hand away, and the chip went grey on her palm. "The book's the money. This is the key to the book. So if it's lost, nothing's lost. If it's forged, nothing's printed. You can't steal a balance off a man's card, because there isn't one."

"That's it."

"That's very good," said Vantic, in the tone of somebody saying a horse was very good when it had just won her a great deal of money.

He started on the books. He began at the beginning, because it was the way he'd learned it and because the rest stood on it: every sum written twice, once where it came from and once where it went, so that the two sides...

Vantic looked at Ferra. Ferra looked at Vantic.

"Yes," said Vantic, very kindly.

He stopped.

"My House has kept its books that way for two hundred years," she said. "Ferra's kept yours that way since she was a girl. We've been sitting here very patiently." She put the chip on the desk. "Tell me what I don't know."

Ferra had her face turned to the window. Her shoulders were shaking.

So he told her what she didn't know, and it took the rest of the lamp's first oil.

The journal would never be changed. No line scraped, struck or rewritten, not even by the clerk who'd written it. A wrong entry stayed, and a new entry reversed it, with the reason. The book only grew. Vantic said the House forbade erasure already. Emeric said the House forbade it for sums; he meant the clerks' own corrections too, so that a clerk who'd made a mistake had to write so in the book for anyone to read. Vantic thought about her clerks and said nothing.

Every line would be stamped. Ottoline Fesk had built the time stamp in the spring: a clockwork press at every counter that punched the day, the hour and the minute onto the line when the clerk pulled its lever, and beside them the number of the clerk's own card. Every opening of a holder's line would be logged the same way, and a holder could ask to see who had looked.

Three copies of each day's journal, written at once on carbon paper from the printing works: one at the counter, one to the House's vault under this tower, one up the line on the night mail to Grayrock. The day's totals by telegraph to the island station the same night.

"Why three towns?" said Vantic.

"A fire's one building. A flood's one valley. A thief's one door. It takes all three in one night to lose a day."

"And the copies?"

"Have to agree. Every night Ottoline's tabulator runs the counter copy against the vault copy. If they're one copper apart..."

"One copper," said Ferra, from the window, with real tenderness.

"...the bank doesn't open in the morning until it's found."

Vantic made a note on nothing, with her finger, on the bare desk. "And the vault?"

"Paper. Punched cards. In iron cases in a dry room cut into the rock under here, with a fire door. A dead zone does nothing to paper. Nor does a stone that's been run too hard. Nor does anything you could do to a stone at all."

She sat back.

"Now I'll ask," she said.



She had six questions, in order. She'd had them, he suspected, since March, carried about like coins in a pocket, unspent.

"What if I lose it?"

"It's grey to whoever finds it. You come to the counter and say so. The House voids its number in the book. A new one's keyed for you behind the curtain, with your hand on it." He shrugged. "The money never moved. It was never on the card."

"Can someone make one? Imitate it?"

"Only a delver. From inside the stone." He didn't want to say the next part and said it. "I've done it. Once. On my own card, to see if it could be done."

Vantic didn't move at all.

"Then the Church can," she said.

"The Church has delvers."

"Then a card isn't enough, and a hand isn't enough." She put a finger on the desk. "I want a third lock. A word. The holder sets it at the House, says it to the clerk once, and never writes it down. The card, the hand and the word. And the photograph, and a clerk who looks at a face." She looked at him. "You hadn't got a word."

"No."

"You had a very clever key," said Vantic, "and no word." She didn't smile. "Next. How do I stop thievery?"

"Pickpockets get a grey chip."

"And a man with a knife who walks the holder to my counter and stands behind him?"

Emeric opened his mouth and found he had nothing very good in it.

"The counter only," said Vantic, answering herself, "and only in daylight, and a limit on any one day. And a second word. A word the holder sets that pays out a little, slowly, and rings for the watch while it does." She tipped her glass at Ferra. "Write that down, would you."

"It's written," said Ferra.

"And my own clerks," said Vantic. "Which is where every bank's ever been robbed from, mine included. That's what your stamp's for, and your three towns, and your copper." She nodded once. "Good. Is thievery possible?"

He started to answer.

"Yes," she said, before he could. "Of course it is. Anything a person built, a person can rob. It's only harder, and louder, and when it's done there's a line in a book with a minute and a name on it." She moved on. "Can the resonance change? The note. Kaldo's notes go up, every year; I've read your own reports. Mine's gone up. You told me in March the whole town's had."

"The chip knows the shape of a note. Not how loud it is." He thought how to say it. "I'd know Bram's note in a crowd at a fair. I knew it when he was a smith with one gift and I know it now. It's louder. It's the same note."

"And these?" She touched the folded page in Ferra's coat without looking at it. "The nine. That's more than louder."

"They tested their cards the day they woke. They still answered. A shade brighter." He looked at Ferra. "The gate clerk at the inner ward has sent for Armand four times over Ferra's."

"Five," said Ferra.

"And the last," said Vantic. "Is the book safe in a dead zone?"

"The book's paper. Yes. A chip sleeps in a dead zone and wakes after; it doesn't forget. But a stone drained right to the bottom goes to dust." He held up his wrist. "Put a drain cuff on a man and you can kill his card. Then it's a lost card, and the money's safe in the book. Inside a dead zone the House pays by the photograph, the word and his signature, slowly."

Vantic sat for a while with her hands folded on the desk.

"Then the cards are sound," she said. "And the book's sound. And now the town."

"The town?"

"They'll be afraid." She said it simply. "Not of thieves. Of this. Money they can't hold. A card that knows them. A House that knows what they've got. For some of them the first year will last ten." She drank, finally, half the glass at once. "So these are my terms for the first year. Anyone may take every copper on his line out in coin, at the counter, any day the House is open. No fee. No questions."

Emeric shook his head before he'd thought about it.

"You'll have a run on the first morning."

"Yes."

"Everybody with buy-back credit on a line, at the coin window, by nine. You'll have to hold the whole buy-back in coin. In the hall. And every one of them who takes it out tells his neighbour the bank's not safe, or why would he have taken it out?"

"I have the whole buy-back in coin," said Vantic. "In the hall. That's what the House is for." She set the glass down. "A run you've invited isn't a run. It's a queue. They'll take it out and carry it home in a bag with everybody watching, and keep it under a floorboard for a month and lie awake listening to the floorboard. Then the works pays wages to a line instead of across a table on a Friday, and nobody gets knocked on the head walking home with a week in his pocket. By the second month most of them put it back." She looked at him with frank interest. "You didn't think of that."

"No."

"No. You thought of the card." She almost smiled. "You can't think of everything. It's a great relief to me."



The coin came up from the vault on the twenty-ninth, in the afternoon, in chests on a trolley, for the counting.

It was the buy-back reserve, the whole of it. On the first of July Vantic had let the Polytechnic alloy fall, in four lines in the Kaldo News, to a little over the price of tin, as Ferra had said three years ago it would and been off by one. The House would make good the difference to every buyer on what they had actually paid, factor's commission and all, because Ferra had found the Fairhythe widow in February. There had been pans and kettles and pram wheels of the aluminium for years.

The House would pay it as credit, on a line in each buyer's card number, from the morning the bank opened; anyone who wanted coin could have coin, and so the coin was here.

Vantic's clerks were counting it by scale at a long table in the hall under the new plaster, two to a scale and a third writing, and Vantic was walking the table with her hands behind her back. Emeric had come to see the time stamps fitted. Imani had come because Ferra was there.

Ferra stopped at the end of the table where the last chest was open.

She didn't touch the scale. She put both hands flat on the top layer of coin, the way she'd put her hands flat on a desk to steady it, and stood there with her eyes on the far wall.

Then she began picking coins out.

She did it fast and without looking down, two fingers, a coin, two fingers, a coin, from the top layer and then sinking her hand in to the wrist for the layer under. She laid them on the green cloth in a row. A silver piece of the old king's with its edge pared so neatly you'd have to hold it against a good one to see. A Kesrathi fish that looked right and rang wrong, if you knew how a fish was meant to ring. Another of the old king's. A copper with lead in its heart. She put her hand in to the elbow.

The clerk at the nearest scale had stopped with a coin in his tweezers.

Vantic had stopped counting. She stood at Ferra's shoulder and watched Ferra's fingers go in and come out, and said nothing at all, which in Vantic, Emeric was learning, was the loudest thing she did.

Ferra took her arm out of the chest and wiped her hand on her skirt.

"Thirty-one," she said. "In that one."

Vantic picked up the first silver piece and put it on the clerk's scale, and it was short. She put the second. Short. She went down the row with her own fingers, coin by coin, all thirty-one, while the clerks stood round with their tweezers in the air, and every one of them was short, clipped or sweated or cut with something cheaper, by a grain or by twenty.

"How long," said Vantic, "have you been able to do that?"

"A little, since the winter. Since before the stone. I could feel a light one was wrong before the scale said." Ferra looked at her hand. "Now I can feel how wrong."

"Through silver."

"Through anything. Stones too. I can tell you what's in a charged stone to the grain, if you put it in my hand."

Vantic turned to her head clerk, an old thin man with ink to the second knuckle.

"How many chests are left?"

"Eleven, Madam."

"Stop weighing them." She looked at Ferra. "Would you?"

"After supper," said Imani, before Ferra could answer. "She hasn't eaten since seven. She'll do your eleven chests on bread and soup or she'll fall in one, and I'm not fishing her out."

Vantic looked at Imani with the respect of one professional for another.

"After supper," she agreed.



They ate at a trestle at the back of the hall, out of a basket Tamsy had sent over: bread, cold mutton, a pot of pickled onions, a jar of plums. The plasterers had gone. The time stamps sat at their counters under cloths like birds put to bed.

Ferra ate with one hand and wrote with the other. She had her own book open, and Armand's register beside it, the tower's four hundred years of what each mage had read on the scale and how long each had lived. Under its last line she had pinned a slip with the new readings, the nine's and Tess's and Armand's, all above the top of the scale.

"What's that?" said Vantic.

"A sum I did in the winter. I'm doing it again."

"What's it a sum of?"

Ferra didn't look up. "How long we'll live."

Vantic put down her plum.

Emeric knew the sum. Ferra had done it the first time in the cold, on the back of a fuel sheet, and said that if it took a hundred years they'd still be early, and he'd been so glad to hear her say it that he hadn't asked for her figures. Now she ran the register down with her pen, and then went on past its last line onto the slip, where there were no deaths to count because nobody on it had died.

She wrote a number at the bottom.

Then, beside it, in smaller letters, she wrote extrapolated, and underlined it, and put the pen down.

"What does that mean?" said Vantic.

"It means I've drawn the line on past the last number I had." Ferra turned the book round so Vantic could see. "The register stops below us. Everybody it counts was weaker than we are now. So I know we'll live longer than them. I know that. I can show you." She touched the word. "I don't know how much. Nobody's lived it yet. This is a guess with a ruler."

"You'd never lend on that," said Vantic.

"No."

"Good," said Vantic. She picked up her plum again and looked at it and put it down a second time. "I'd want to see the slip in thirty years."

"I'll keep it," said Ferra.

Imani took the last of the bread off Ferra's plate and pushed the plums at her instead. Ferra ate a plum without noticing. Then she did eleven chests with her sleeve rolled to the shoulder and pulled two hundred and eight short coins out of them, and Vantic stood beside her for every one.



The card bank opened at nine on the thirty-first, and at a quarter to, the queue went twice round Lantern Square.

It went past the bakery and the coffee house under the South Gate arch and back again, and Prudence Fairley came off the tram and looked at it and got back on to fetch more ticket clerks, on the grounds that anything with a queue that long needed punching. There were works hands in caps and farm wives from Milford with baskets, and a widow with a black ribbon on her bonnet who told everyone round her that she had bought six pans through a factor in year twelve and had come to see the House pay her for them with her own eyes.

Harrold queued forty places back, between a cooper and a girl from the brickyard, and refused three times to be let ahead, and in the end the cooper gave up and told him about his wife's knees.

The doors opened at nine, and the brass reader in the doorway woke to each card as it came past.

By ten the coin window had a queue of its own, as Emeric had said it would.

They took it out. They gave the card and the word, and the clerk pulled the lever on the time stamp, and it punched the minute with a clack you could hear at the back of the hall, and they carried their credit out into the square in a bag where everybody could see. The widow counted hers twice on the steps, then went back in and asked to see who had opened her line that morning, and read the book with her finger, and came out looking satisfied in a way that suggested she'd hoped to be otherwise.

By noon the coin window's queue was shorter than the counters'.

By three o'clock a works hand who'd taken his out at half past nine came back in with the same bag and put it back on his line, because, he told the clerk, loudly, his wife had been looking at it all morning and he'd sooner have it where she couldn't.

Imani set her word at the counter at eleven with Ferra beside her. Vantic's clerk, a young woman with spectacles, explained the second word, the one that paid a little and rang for the watch.

"Can it be rude?" said Imani.

"It has to be a word you'd be able to say at a counter, Madam, with somebody behind you."

"Then it'll be rude," said Imani, "but quietly," and leaned over and said both words into the clerk's ear, and the clerk went pink and wrote nothing down, as she had been taught.

"What did you pick?" said Ferra, outside.

"You'll never know," said Imani. "That's the whole idea of it. Weren't you listening?" She took Ferra's arm. "What did you pick?"

"A number."

"Of course you did."

The board behind the counters had a column chalked on it that Ferra's clerks kept up through the day. Lines opened. By noon it said one thousand nine hundred and forty. By the last bell it said four thousand two hundred and sixteen, near enough every buyer in Kaldo with a citizen's card, and Ferra stood under it with her arms folded and looked at it the way other women looked at a sunset.

Beneath it, in Vantic's own hand, a smaller board said: River dam bonds. Closed.

The House had put the long bonds out in May at a rate Vantic chose and nobody argued with, against the gorge dam and the hydrogen works, and the last of them had gone that morning to a syndicate of Grayrock millers who wanted the power more than the interest. Bram had the money for the turbine blades and the last pour; by next summer the river dam would be turning. Vantic had decided all of it herself, and told Emeric afterwards in a note of two lines, as a courtesy.



Saban came in at four and stood by the door reader, watching. Emeric went and stood beside him.

He watched the coin window, and the works hand with the bag, and the widow, back a third time to check that her line still said what it had said at ten. He watched Vantic moving among her counters, never raising her voice. He watched for a long time.

"I voted against her," he said.

"I know."

"In February. Five to one. The one was me." He folded his arms. "Her House has branches the Church can reach. A secret a House knows is a secret a House can be made to give up. I'm still against it. Hold the vote again tomorrow and I'd vote the same way."

"I know."

"She told you in March I was right."

"She did."

Across the hall Vantic said something to her head clerk, and the head clerk went to the coin window and took the sign down from over it, the one that said COIN, and put up another that said COIN, ANY DAY, NO FEE, in larger letters.

"She was right about that," said Saban. He nodded at the window. "And about the word. You'd have opened this with a key and no word. I've spent thirty years watching men get what they wanted from other men who'd only locked one door." He paused. "I'd like it on the record that I said so. Against her, and still against her. And she was right."

"Do you want me to tell her?"

"No," said Saban. "I'll tell her myself. She'll enjoy it more."

He did, ten minutes later, standing. Emeric didn't hear what he said. He saw Vantic listen with her head a little on one side, and then say something short, and Saban, who did not laugh, nearly did.



Wren took the first night's copies out at seven.

The carbon copies, the third set, for Grayrock: a canvas packet sealed with the House's wax and the bank's time stamp punched through the flap. The night mail would have taken them at ten. Wren took them at seven, on the motorcycle.

The Motion Shop had built it in seven weeks. Wren had drawn it from the Free Wheel and argued with Tess about every part of it, and then built it with Hobb and two other apprentices: the frame made heavier, the wheels thicker, the forks sprung, a little engine slung low between the knees that coughed and roared and settled to a noise like a large cat that had been insulted. Emeric had named it the first time he saw it running, and the name had stuck because nobody had a better one. A motorcycle.

Half the town came out to watch her go. Rylan stood on the bank's steps with his arms folded and his face exactly as it always was.

"Nessa's going to kill me," he said, in the same voice he used for everything.

"She can ride it," said Emeric. "She built it."

"I know she built it. I've had seven weeks of watching her build it." Rylan's eyes didn't leave his daughter, who was strapping the packet to the carrier with three buckles and then a fourth because the fourth was the one that mattered. "I stood under a Kesrath mage at the drum. I minded that less."

Wren put on her goggles and her leather cap and kicked the engine over. It coughed. The crowd stepped back. She kicked it again and it caught, and the noise went off the front of the bank and round the square and came back, and a carthorse by the tram stop sat down in its shafts.

She looked at her father. Rylan lifted one hand an inch.

Then she was gone, down the Gate Avenue and out under the arch, the engine's noise going away up the Grayrock road in the long July evening until it was a bee, and then a thought of a bee, and then nothing.

Rylan stood on the steps until it was nothing. Then he went home to Nessa.

The telegraph came back from Grayrock at nine minutes to ten: packet received, seals whole. The night mail hadn't left Kaldo yet.

Bren had bought the second one at noon.

He had come into the Motion Shop with his pay in a bag, every copper of it, which he had drawn out in coin at the bank's window under Vantic's terms because he wanted to put it on the bench in front of Wren and see her face. He had been a crown knight for seven days. Mira came with him to make sure he didn't buy anything else.

"It's not finished," said Wren. She was under the first one with oil to the elbow, fitting the carrier for the evening. "The second frame. It's got no engine."

"I'll wait," said Bren.

"He's never waited for anything in his life," said Mira.

"I'll wait for this." Bren pushed the bag across the bench. "What's it cost?"

Wren looked at the bag, and at Bren, and named a figure. Tess, at the far bench, said "Wren," without looking up, and Wren named a smaller one. Bren counted it out in coppers, every one, and Wren gave him a receipt in pencil and stamped it with the shop's stamp, because Ferra had been in the shop in May and since then everything got stamped.



The tabulator found the copper at twenty past eleven that first night.

It had stopped on the last run, counter copy against vault copy, one copper apart, and the hall would not open in the morning until it was found. So the head clerk and four of his clerks and Ottoline Fesk sat under the lamps with the copies side by side and went down them line by line, and Ferra went down them twice as fast as all of them together, and Emeric sat at the end of the table and kept out of the way.

It was the time stamp. Counter four's had jammed at a quarter past two, Ottoline had freed it with a hairpin, and the clerk had pulled the lever again on the same line, so that a copper's change had gone through the counter copy twice and the vault copy once.

Ferra found it. The head clerk wrote a new line under it that reversed it, with the reason, the minute and his own card's number, and the book grew by one line. Ottoline went off to take counter four's time stamp apart, muttering, and Ferra took Imani home, because Imani had been asleep on a bench since ten.

Emeric stayed to see the lamps out.

Vantic was in her counting room over the hall. Her door was open. She was writing a letter by one lamp, the others turned down, and she looked up when he knocked on the frame.

"It's found," he said.

"I heard. One copper." She put her pen down. "Half my partners would have written it off and gone to bed."

"Ferra wouldn't let him."

"No. She'd have kept them up till two and put it on their lines with their names beside it." She took a sheet out from under the blotter. "Before you go. Something I've had done."

It was a form, fresh from the press, the ink still a little proud of the paper. A joint line: one account, the holders' cards keyed to it, either word to draw. He'd seen the proofs in June. This one had three columns for the holders' names, not two, and a third line ruled at the bottom for a third signature.

"Our joint lines take two names," said Vantic. "I had the press do a short run with room for three." She looked at the form and not at him. "Fifty. I don't expect a great deal of call for it."

He laughed, and meant it, and held the form up to the lamp.

"Thank you. That's very good of you. Ferra will want to read every word of it before anyone signs anything."

"I know," said Vantic. "I wrote it so she'd have to."

He folded the form into his coat. She picked up her pen again, and then didn't write anything with it, and after a while she reached up with two fingers and steadied the lamp stone on its brass arm, though it hadn't been guttering.

"It's a form," she said. "It isn't a present. Go home, Emeric. The copper's found."

He went down through the dark hall, past the time stamps under their cloths and the board with four thousand two hundred and sixteen on it in chalk, and out through the door, and the brass reader in the doorway woke to his card as he went past and knew him.
