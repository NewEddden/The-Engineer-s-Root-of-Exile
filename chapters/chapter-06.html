Chapter 6 - The Town, the Forge and the Healer

Rylan wanted to go home, and Emeric wanted to see the town, so on the second morning they went together.

Ward wanted to come. Emeric said a prince walking the lower streets with a six-and-a-half-foot knight at his shoulder would see nothing but people getting out of his way. Ward thought about that, said, "Then you won't see me," and went off somewhere, and Emeric didn't see him again all day.

The smell got worse as they went down.

The town fell away from the inner ward in every direction, but most steeply to the south-east, and the lanes followed the fall: narrow, muddy, crooked, the thatch on either side so close overhead in places that the sky was a strip. Nobody had planned any of it. Houses leaned on houses. A pig had the right of way.

Then the lane opened out, and the smell had its explanation.

The churchyard sat high on the shoulder of the inner ward, a little square of old stones behind a low wall. That was where the dead of Kaldo had been buried for four hundred years. It was full. And below it, spilling down the slope between the houses, in rows, in strips of garden, in the corner of a yard where children were playing, were more graves. Plain mounds. Wooden markers, some of them. Some with nothing at all.

"The plague," Rylan said. He didn't look at them. "They couldn't get the bodies out. One gate, and the whole town dying. So they put them in the churchyard, and then next to it, and then..." He lifted a hand at the slope. "Anywhere there was ground."

Emeric looked at the slope, and at where it went.

Downhill. Everything here went downhill. Rain on the rows would sink through thin soil and run on the rock beneath it, down toward the lowest part of the town, and that was where the wells were. He could see two of them from here, stone rings at the bottom of the fall, in the poorest streets, with the latrine ground alongside them in the same wet hollow.

An old woman was filling buckets at the lower one, by the south-east wall.

Four hundred feet behind her, up by the keep, the broken aqueduct ended in a stone cistern that got what trickle came through. Clean water, from the lake, all the way from the hills. Most of it ran away into the sand where the arch had fallen, and what reached the town wasn't enough for anybody but the keep. And around the keep's foot, the canal ring sat green and still, and nobody drank from that either.

The old woman lifted her buckets and went home with them.

They walked on. Emeric counted as he went, because he couldn't help it. Whole rows of houses with their shutters nailed. Two of the town's four market halls dark, their doors chained. A cooperage, shut. A dyehouse, shut. Along one side of a street ran a long, low rope walk, a building as long as a ship, intact, with nobody in it. The town had been built for far more people than lived in it now, and it wore what was left of them like a coat three sizes too big.



Near the east wall, where a narrow gate let out onto the river bridge, there was a building that stopped him.

It was two storeys of good stone, square, standing a little apart from the houses. The door was unlocked. Inside was one long room with no posts at all, nothing holding up the upper floor but the roof trusses, and the trusses were the work of someone who'd known exactly what he was doing. Eighty years old, perhaps. Expensive, even then. High windows ran along both sides, so the light fell evenly across the whole floor. One bay at the far end had lost its roof and there was a drift of old leaves under the gap.

"The exchange," Rylan said. "Where the merchants used to meet, when there were merchants."

A building made for reading in. Emeric stood in the middle of it, looking up at the trusses, and filed it.

On the way back up from the river, something touched his sense again.

It came as they turned a corner: the faint pull, the brush across something he had no word for, gone before he could look for it. A street later, in a narrow alley between two leaning houses, it came again, sharper. He stopped and turned all the way round. Washing on a line. A cat on a wall. Nobody.

He'd refused his training at fourteen. His mother had been a mage. He had a mage's sense in him, untrained, unused, and it was bound to misfire. That was the likeliest reading, and he took it. He didn't think about the fact that the first time he'd felt it had been in a hall he'd stood in a thousand times without feeling anything.



Rylan's house was in a lane of houses that all looked like it, and Rylan was through the door before Emeric reached it.

Nessa was a sturdy woman with flour on her forearms who said "Oh," once into Rylan's coat and then nothing for a while. A boy of six watched from behind her skirts, saw Emeric, and vanished upstairs. A girl of four did not vanish. She stood in the middle of the floor and looked Emeric up and down.

"Are you a real prince?"

"I am."

"Where's your crown?"

"I left it in the carriage."

She considered this, decided it was a poor answer but an acceptable one, and went to look for her brother.

They gave him bread and something warm to drink, and he sat at their table and listened while Nessa told Rylan everything that had happened in four months, which was a great deal and also nothing. The port had emptied in a week, she said, back when the crown came looking for men for the war. Just like that. Every boat with a man on it. The lake was the Mere, not Lake Kaldo. Anyone who said Lake Kaldo had learned the place off a map. Fletcher the shipwright, who'd built half the hulls on the river, had come back from the war, and so had Abloh the sailmaker, and the two of them had gone off downriver together when the port silted up, and she didn't know where. And then the plague had come, a couple of years after the war, and after that nobody had the heart to ask about anything.

"If you want something done here," Rylan said, at the door, "there's two people everyone trusts. Bram the smith. And Imani. The doctor."

When they stepped back out into the lane, an upper shutter on the house opposite was closed. It had been open when they arrived. Emeric was sure of it. He looked at it, and Rylan didn't, and neither of them mentioned it.



On the third day he went to the forge.

He'd found it by the noise, and because every lane in that part of town seemed to bend toward it. It was a big, black, open-fronted place with a stone hearth at the back, and the man at the anvil was broad and grey-bearded with forearms like hawsers. He looked up when Emeric came in, took in the clothes, and went back to work.

Emeric stood just inside the door, out of the way, and said nothing.

Bram was making a bracket. A simple iron bracket, for a gate or a shelf. He finished it and quenched it and set it aside, and took up another bar and began another exactly like it. Then another.

Emeric stood.

It was hard. Every instinct he'd brought from his old life said a silence was a space to be filled, that the way to get something from a man was to make a case to him. He held his tongue and made no case. The fire roared and dropped and roared. Sparks went up. The light moved across the floor as the morning went into the afternoon.

He looked at the forge, because he couldn't not.

The tuyere, where the air came into the fire, was set too high. Bram was getting less heat from his coal than the work at the bellows should have bought. The quench trough had worn a shallow dish into the floor in front of it, and the floor sloped, so every time Bram quenched, water ran back under his bench and into the wood. Small things. Each one cost Bram a little every day, and had for years.

Twice he opened his mouth to say so. Twice he closed it.

He wasn't the only one in the forge who knew where every number was. In the back corner, behind a half-wall that kept the sparks off, a young woman sat at a high desk with a ledger open in front of her. She had been there since before he arrived, and hadn't looked at him once.

The forge light didn't flatter anybody, and it flattered her anyway. She was pale, the kind of pale that never took the sun, with a dusting of freckles across her nose and cheeks, and her hair was red. Not auburn, not copper. Red, as bright as the coals behind her father, pinned up off her neck and coming loose in the heat. She was striking, and either didn't know it or had decided long ago it was none of her business. She sat very straight and very still, and the only part of her that moved was the pen.

Some time in the third hour Bram muttered a count to himself at the anvil, brackets made against brackets owed, and got it wrong. She corrected him without lifting her eyes from the page. Bram grunted and went on.

Emeric realized he had been looking at her longer than the moment needed, and turned back to the tuyere. He was standing in her father's forge trying to earn her father's trust. He kept his eyes on the iron after that, mostly.

Somewhere around the fifth hour a girl came in from the back, thin, perhaps fifteen, with a burn scar along one thumb and a sack of charcoal over her shoulder. She stopped when she saw the stranger. She glanced at Emeric, then followed his eyes to where they rested, on the tuyere. She studied it with a small frown, as though she had never thought to look at it before. Then she looked back at Emeric, said nothing, and went out again.

At the sixth hour, Bram put down his hammer.

"Most of your kind," he said, "would have sent a boy to fetch me. Or come in shouting." He wiped his hands on his apron. "What is it you want?"

So Emeric told him, plainly. He wanted the priest's records of the dead. He didn't know the priest. He wanted Bram to walk him there and tell the priest he was worth hearing.

"Why would I do that?"

"I can't give you a reason that isn't a promise," Emeric said. "And I don't want to promise you anything yet."

Bram looked at him for a long time.

"I'm not vouching for you," he said finally. "Not yet." He came round the anvil. "I'll walk you to Doran. What he does with you after that is yours to earn."

He held out his hand, black with soot to the wrist, without a moment's hesitation, as if it had never occurred to him that a prince might not take it. Emeric took it.



On the way back from the forge, with Rylan beside him again, a woman came round the corner ahead of them carrying a full bucket in each hand. She was past forty, lean and strong, with her sleeves rolled. She saw Rylan first and almost smiled. Then she saw the man beside Rylan, and his eyes, and what they meant.

She set both buckets down in the mud and walked away. She didn't hurry. She left them where they stood.

"Sela," Rylan said quietly, when she'd gone. "The crown took her eldest for the war. When they were choosing men." He looked at the buckets. "Nobody ever told her what happened to him. Not how. Not where. Not whether there was anything to bury."

Emeric looked at the buckets too.

"That one you don't fix," Rylan said. "You just don't make it worse."



The healer's house smelled of vinegar and dried herbs, and the woman who opened the door was twenty-five and tired in the bones.

She was brown-skinned and tall, with a long curling mass of dark hair tied up high on the back of her head and knotted there with a strip of cloth, so it wouldn't fall in her way when she bent over somebody. Some of it had escaped down the back of her neck and she'd stopped caring. She had the kind of face people stop talking to look at, a full mouth and a level, unimpressed gaze, and she knew it and had no use for it. Her sleeves were rolled to the elbow, and she stood in her own doorway like someone who'd decided long ago that nobody got past it without a reason.

She caught him looking. Her face said don't. He didn't.

"Whatever it is," Imani said, before he'd opened his mouth, "I'm out of it."

He said he didn't want anything from her pharmacy. He wanted to know how the town buried its dead.

She studied him, then let them in.

The priest's book, he'd realized on the walk over, would only hold the burials somebody had paid for. Standing in her doorway, he'd realized something else: in the capital he could have named four great houses that had their dead sealed in mana stone, preserved forever, at a cost that would have fed this street for a generation. He had no idea what happened to Kaldo's poor when they died. He'd never once wondered.

Imani's front room was arranged for working one-handed. Everything she might need in a hurry was within reach of a person standing at the table: knives, cloths, a jar of something, a bowl. It had been set up by somebody who had once had to work faster than they could think. A second chair stood by the fire. Its cushion had no dent in it. On a shelf above it sat six or seven small things that didn't belong to a healer: a carved bird, a child's spinning top, a man's pipe.

He didn't ask about the chair or the shelf. She saw him not asking.

"How many?" he said. "In the plague."

"Past nine hundred." She said it flatly. "I stopped counting after that. It got past what I could carry."

She'd taken the post at nineteen, from her mother, Mother Sabel. She'd been healer here six years. And in the plague's first year, when the dying started and didn't stop, she'd begun to notice where it hit and where it didn't, and built a theory out of it, and she told it to him now.

The sickness came up off the ground. Where water sat, where the land was low and wet, it rose out of the earth like a damp and got into people. That was why the low streets died and the high streets didn't.

She was right about every house. She was wrong about the reason. Emeric could see the exact shape of her mistake, the way you see a beam set one degree off true, and he didn't correct her. Not yet. Not in her own house, on the first day.

"I wrote to the capital," she said. "That first year. Three letters. Sent them up through the castle, the proper way." Her mouth went thin. "Nobody came."

He noted through the castle and kept it to himself.

Outside, as they left, Rylan said that Ward had walked past her door twice on the way to the forge and not knocked. Emeric said Ward's arm was healing clean and didn't need her.

"Doesn't matter," Rylan said. "He'd not go anyway. He'll come when it goes red and not an hour before."



That night he sat in his room in the keep with Armand's notebook open at the back, where the pages were blank, and wrote down Kaldo.

Water. Latrines. The wells, and where they sat. The rows. Fuel, because if he told three thousand people to boil their water they'd need something to boil it with. The broken aqueduct. The port, and the moorings standing empty. Fletcher and Abloh, somewhere downriver. Fifty-eight men in the garrison, answering to somebody who wasn't him. The prisoner two doors down who'd stopped talking. The baron. The healer with nine hundred dead and three letters nobody answered. The river, going past the town all day with all that power in it.

And his purse. Fifty Gold Rand. Eight Copper a day for four guards. Nothing more coming, ever.

Under all of it, in smaller writing, the night's count for the coin, which was still embarrassing.

He looked at the list. Somewhere in the writing of it he'd started enjoying himself, and he couldn't have said when. That unsettled him. A town was dying of its own wells, and he was sitting here pleased because it was a good problem.

He put the notebook away. Then he lay down, and slept properly, all the way through, for the first time since the fire.
