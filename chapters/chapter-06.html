Chapter 6 - Not Hollow

The Hollow Crystal hadn't left his side since the night of the exile. Under his pillow at night, in his coat pocket by day, close enough that he had stopped registering the weight of it the way he had stopped registering his own pulse.

His mother had put it in his hand. His brother had handed him a set of the same at the banquet, an insult dressed as nostalgia. Neither had ever done a thing. Every mage who had ever held one called it worthless, and Emeric had believed them the way you believe a physician about a joint that has ached your whole life. Not because you checked. Because nobody ever told you otherwise.

He didn't dream about Amara often, and when he did it was never the assassination. It was smaller. Her hands over his on a practice rune-slate, woodsmoke in the room, her voice correcting his grip without any heat in it.

Tonight was one of those. Not a nightmare. A memory his mind had decided to visit unprompted, the stone warm against his palm where his hand had closed around it in his sleep, as it did most nights without consulting him first.

He didn't feel himself cross into anything. No threshold, no falling, no announcement that something had begun. One moment he was somewhere that felt like his mother's room, and the next, underneath it, laid over the same frame like a second exposure on one plate, there was structure. Lines. Logic sitting where logic had no business being inside a dream.

He came awake without remembering the transition and the stone was still warm.

It took him a full minute in the dark to understand that what had just happened had not been a dream, and most of the rest of the night to understand why it had happened now.

He had not done a single thing differently. That was the whole of it. He had held the stone exactly as he had held it every night since the capital, and before that every night of a childhood he only had secondhand, a boy who took a grey rock to bed for four or five years because his mother had given it to him and then, somewhere around the age he stopped opening books, put it in a drawer and left it there.

The count had never reset. Whatever the thing measured, it had been measuring since he was six, and it had sat at some number just short of enough for a decade, and two months in a cold room in Kaldo had finished it.

Which meant the barrier had never been talent. It was time. Every mage who had ever tested one of these had held it for an hour, or a day, or a week, with instruments and a schedule and a report to file. Nobody had ever carried one against their skin for years, because why would you carry a worthless stone for years, and the answer, in the entire recorded history of the thing, was that one six-year-old had loved his mother.

He lay there turning it over, feeling the faint heat coming off it the way something feels when it has just been used, and decided he wasn't going back in tonight. He wanted to sit with the fact of the door before he started opening it.

The next night was uneventful in the way every night in that room was uneventful. He ran the day's problems, and when the problems ran out he missed things he had stopped letting himself miss out loud. Central heating. A blanket that held warmth instead of merely weighing him down. Fire crystals could handle the heat if he wanted to spend the attention on keeping one lit all night, which he didn't, because the instant his focus slipped it went cold again, no different from holding a space heater switched on with his own hand.

Eventually his thoughts wore thin enough to let him sleep, and this time he went looking for the door on purpose. He held the stone the way he had held it every night, and instead of letting his mind drift he pointed it, a lamp down a hallway he already knew had something in it.

Show me what you are.

Nothing, for what felt like a long time. Then the layering again, structure underneath awareness, except now with nothing covering it. No memory doing the work of getting him there. Just the stone, and whatever the stone was, sitting in front of him with nothing in between.

It didn't look like anything his eyes would have recognized. It read the way unfamiliar code used to read before the pattern clicked, a wall of syntax slowly resolving into shapes he almost knew. Not commands. Something underneath commands. Structure that things could be built from, waiting on somebody to build.

He found what felt like a branch. A fork, one path or the other depending on something he couldn't identify, a condition being checked. He pressed on it carefully, testing a door he wasn't sure was locked, and it held. Unmoved. Not resistance and not refusal. It simply did not answer force, the same way a variable does not answer being shouted at, only being addressed correctly.

Conditional logic.

The thought arrived with a vertigo he hadn't braced for, half excitement and half something nearer grief. This thing thought in if-then. It wasn't a crystal. It wasn't even a rune, not the way every rune anyone in this world had ever shown him was a rune. Every mage who had held one and called it worthless had been telling the exact truth about their own instruments and nothing at all about the stone.

He found a second shape near the first, something that repeated, folding back on itself in a way that felt deliberate rather than accidental, though he had no idea what it repeated for or what it counted or waited on. He didn't touch it. The branch had already taught him that recognizing a shape and knowing what it was for were two different things, and he had exactly one of those tonight.

So he stayed where he was and studied both from a careful distance, the way he would have stared at documentation for a language he was three weeks into, cataloguing what he recognized and using none of it. Branch. Loop, probably. Somewhere under both, likely, whatever held state. Whatever remembered anything between one use and the next.

He didn't find that tonight, and he didn't yet know that tonight was the right word for how long he had been in there. Time inside the thing did not announce itself the way it did outside. He never decided to leave. He simply became aware, at some point, that he was lying in bed with the stone loose in his hand and morning doing to the window frame what it did every other morning in that house.

It was not that the delve made him smarter. It gave him back what he already had, without the wait. The second he understood a thing well enough to finish the thought, the thought was finished. A card reading 2 + 2 needed no working; he would have known anyway, and the delve only cut the beat where he arrived at it. What color is the sky. Blue, already, before he decided to answer.

It read like precognition and it was not. It was his own understanding arriving on time for once. It could tell him nothing he had no way to know. A cipher he lacked the key to stayed a cipher. It did not think for him. It thought exactly as fast as he could have, and called the difference nothing.

And that was the floor of the room. He had not yet found the walls.

He got Armand's notebook out of the trunk in the morning, which he had been meaning to do for two months and hadn't, and read the whole thing standing up by the window.

It was better than he expected and it stopped exactly where he needed it to keep going. Forty years of a careful man's observations on lattice structure and charge behavior and why certain cuts hold a rune and certain cuts don't, and a full page near the back on the Hollow class, in which Armand had recorded eleven separate tests on four stones over nine years and concluded, honestly and with visible regret, that there was nothing in them.

Nine years. Eleven tests. Armand had come closer than anybody and had still been looking at it the way you look at a machine, which is to say from outside, with a schedule.

I never did finish teaching you properly, the man had said, not meeting his eyes.

Emeric sat down on the trunk and stayed there a while.

Hollow wasn't the right name anymore and he was done pretending otherwise even inside his own head. Hollow implied nothing was there. This had been full the whole time, waiting on somebody who knew how to look, or more accurately on somebody too sentimental to stop carrying it.

Control Rune. Not because it controlled anything he yet understood, but because the branch and the shape that was probably a loop both felt built to be directed rather than merely drawn from, the way every other rune in this world seemed to work. A working name. It would do until it earned a better one.

He didn't tell Ward, and he spent a while being unhappy about the reason.

Ward would keep any secret Emeric asked him to keep, right up to the moment keeping it stopped being consistent with keeping him alive, and a stone that thinks in conditions is not a secret that stays a secret when a cardinal takes an interest. Ward answered to the crown. Bram answered to nobody. That was the entire difference and it was enough, and Emeric noted, without enjoying it, that he had just done to Ward exactly what his father had spent fifteen years doing to him.

Bram took it the way he took most things now, with the stillness of a man deciding whether to be alarmed.

"You're telling me the tower, the mages, the lot of them, have been wrong. About a whole class of stone."

"I'm telling you I don't know yet. Something happened. I don't know what it means or what it can do or whether I can do it again on purpose." Emeric turned the thing between his fingers, plain and grey and inert-looking, exactly as it had looked every day since his mother put it in his hand. "I need somewhere quiet to try it awake. And I need you not to repeat it until I know what I'm holding."

"Wasn't planning to take heresy to the priest." Bram's mouth barely moved. Something behind his eyes did. "You've got the same look you had over the ledgers."

"Feels the same. Bigger."

Bram didn't ask how much bigger, which was one of the things Emeric had come to rely on without meaning to, the man's refusal to ask a question he couldn't yet give an answer to. He gave him the back room and asked nothing about what it was for.

Awake, the stone was a different animal. He held it the way he had in the dark, pointed his attention down the same hallway, and got nowhere near as far. The layering was there, faint, structure sitting under his fingers where a moment earlier there had been none. But the dream had dropped him into the middle of it, close enough to press on a branch and feel it hold. Awake left him at the surface with the depth visible below and no way down. Every time he reached for the branch, the thing thinned and slid off him, the way a word you are sure of comes apart the second you try to spell it.

He stayed with it until his eyes ached and got the same result every pass. Not nothing. A shallow read, steady and repeatable, enough to feel the shape was still down there. Just not enough to reach it.

Two depths, then, and he filed it the way he filed anything behaving like a rule rather than an accident. Asleep, the dream carried him down and he arrived deep with no say over the descent. Awake, he kept the say and lost the depth. The floor he could stand on by daylight sat well above the room he had found in the dark.

It was a worse tool than he had wanted and a considerably better one than he had owned a month ago. Shallow and waking, he could hold more at once than his hands could sort, and that much he could use tomorrow. The deep floor could wait until he understood what it cost to stand on it.

The metal problem had not waited politely for any of this.

"We buy our iron out of Grayrock," Bram said. They were walking the smithy yard while the apprentices banked the forge for the night. "Have for years. Little copper too, when their smiths have surplus. Nothing local worth digging since before the war, so it comes down the same road the grain does."

"How reliable?"

"Reliable enough, if you're not particular about price. Grayrock knows we've nowhere else to buy. Charges accordingly."

Emeric had expected some version of that before he asked, which didn't make it sit better. One supplier and no competition was its own kind of fragility, the sort of thing he would have flagged on a production line without thinking twice.

"Same road Imani's supplies come down," he said, and hadn't known he was going to say it until it was out. "Anything she can't grow herself."

"Aye. And the grain, and the salt. And every crystal worth cutting a rune into, Grayrock's hills or nowhere." Bram didn't slow. "You're starting to see the shape of it."

He was. One road in, one town at the other end of it, and everything Kaldo needed to stay alive travelling single file. He added it to the list without a solution attached, which was becoming a habit he did not love.

"For the farm work," Bram said. "What am I actually building?"

"Pipe. A great deal of pipe. Fittings, joints, something better than what the town's using now, which is mostly nothing. And whatever a proper irrigation run needs once we're past hauling buckets."

He had walked the floodplain twice and the arithmetic wasn't hard even without good instruments. Call it two thousand acres actually worked, less than half what the land could carry if there were hands for it, the rest lying fallow for want of people rather than want of soil. Whatever got built had to draw from a clean point upriver, well above the bad stretch, and run down through the worked fields in branches instead of one long spine, so that a break in one line didn't take the system with it.

No single points of failure. He had learned that somewhere that wasn't this world and it hadn't stopped being true because the world changed.

"That's a great deal of metal for one field."

"It isn't for one field. Armand's notes are good enough that I'm fairly sure I could grow one field myself. Magic would make it easy. Almost boring." Emeric shrugged. "It isn't sustainable and it isn't what this barony needs from me. It needs a system somebody else can run after I've moved on to the next fire. Something that doesn't need a prince standing in it to work."

Bram was quiet a moment, the particular quiet Emeric had learned meant recalculating scope rather than doubting the plan.

"That's a smith's work for months, not days. I can't do that and keep the shop running for the town's orders both."

"I know. That's why I'm not asking you as a favor."

"No." Bram stopped at the gate and looked back at his own forge a second longer than the question needed. "Then you've a smith. Whole shop, however long it takes. I'll want it in writing, and plain, because I've watched what happens to a man in this town who works on somebody's word."

"You'll have it by the end of the week."

"Then we're done arguing." He pushed the gate. "Come in. It's cold and I've been meaning to ask you something anyway."

Ferra was at the table when Emeric raised the other thing, which he hadn't planned and didn't mind. She had a ledger open in front of her, the household kind, and she didn't look up when he sat. He had learned by now that she heard everything said in that house whether she looked up or not.

"Bram. The smithing's settled. What I'm asking now is separate. I want to hire Ferra. Not to help you. On her own terms, doing her own work."

Bram's cup paused the way it had the first time Emeric asked him for something large. "Doing what work?"

"Records. Contracts. Whatever this turns into is going to throw off more paper than either of us can hold in our heads. Who owes who, who delivered what and when. I've seen what she does with numbers. That isn't a smith's skill. It's its own skill and I need it."

Ferra looked up for the first time.

"You've seen what I do with numbers," she repeated. Not quite a question.

"Your father mentioned the household accounts balance to the copper every month and you never write anything twice. That's not luck." He nodded at the ledger. "What's the total on that page? Don't add it. Look at it and tell me."

She glanced down. A beat, no longer than most people take to read one line.

"Fourteen silver, six copper. Assuming the fishmonger's third entry is a five and not a three. His hand's bad this week."

She looked back up with something sharp under the flatness, daring him to check rather than believe her. Emeric checked. It took him considerably longer than the beat she had used.

Fourteen silver, six copper.

"That's the skill," he said. "That's exactly the skill."

Bram set his cup down slower than he had picked it up. "You're asking my permission first."

"You're her father. It matters to me that you're asked and not informed." He meant it plainly. "But I'm not hiring you on her behalf either. If you say yes and she says no, that's still no."

Bram looked at his daughter a long moment. Whatever went between them Emeric wasn't equipped to read all of, and he didn't need all of it to recognize a father working out that his answer was no longer the one that mattered.

"I've no objection. Ask her, then."

"Same offer, direct. Records, contracts, order tracking, for whatever this becomes. Paid, your terms on how. Exclusive if you want it that way and not if you don't."

Ferra closed the ledger without hurrying.

"Exclusive," she said. "I don't do half measures on paperwork I actually respect." A pause, something almost amused under it. "You checked my arithmetic just now. Nobody's ever bothered. They believed me or they didn't."

"I don't believe things I can't check. No offense in it."

"None taken. I'd have thought less of you if you'd nodded along."

Petram had said three real things since the fire and two of them were about the weather.

The third came the week after the crystal, dragged loose more by boredom than by anything Emeric had done to earn it. A name half swallowed, cut off a syllable in. Something about coin moving where coin had no business going. Then nothing more, no matter how the questions came at it after.

It wasn't a confession. It wasn't an accusation that would survive anyone determined to argue with it. It was a thread, and Emeric had learned by now that a thread is worth more than it looks like on the day you find it.

Wexler's name never came up. It didn't need to. There were not many hands in this barony with the reach to move coin anywhere at all, which was exactly the kind of reasoning Emeric had spent two months warning himself against, and he wrote the letter anyway.

He wrote it three times. The first draft sounded like a request. The second sounded like an accusation. The third, the one that went out under his seal, sounded like neither: a standard inspection of barony revenue and expenditure, the sort of oversight any crown-resident royal was entitled to as a matter of course, phrased with the flat procedural boredom of something that happened all the time and needed no justification because it never had before.

Nothing in it asked. Nothing in it accused. It informed, the way a tax notice informs, and that was the whole point. A baron can sit on a request. Sitting on an instruction with a prince's seal, in front of a steward who then has to explain the delay to somebody, is a different kind of exposure.

It went to the steward's office rather than to Wexler directly, though both of them knew whose desk it would be on within the hour.

Wexler received him in the same room as before and didn't wait for him to sit.

"Ledgers." He turned the letter over once without reading it again, having plainly read it several times already. "You've decided you need my books."

"Crown books. You administer them. They aren't yours." Emeric kept his voice where he had kept it explaining the water survey. Flat, unhurried, a fact stated rather than a position defended. "It isn't a large request. Revenue in, expenditure out, the last two years."

"Standard practice." Wexler set the letter down between them, precisely, placing a piece rather than discarding a paper. "For a prince who intends to stay resident. Is that what you're telling me you intend?"

It had stopped being a question about ledgers, and Emeric knew that much without being able to name what it had turned into.

"I'd like to understand the barony I'm living in. That's all this is."

"That's all a great many things are, right up until they aren't." Wexler studied him with the same recalculating stillness as the entry hall, sharper now, a man running a total he had already run once and not liking that it came out the same. "You'll have your ledgers. I've no grounds to refuse a request that isn't, in form, a request. You wrote it carefully. I noticed."

"I try to."

"I'm sure you do." He didn't stand this time, and Emeric registered it without knowing what to make of it, whether it meant less concern or only less need to perform concern in front of him now. "You'll find the accounts in poorer shape than you'd expect for a barony this size. I'd ask that you keep whatever conclusions you draw to yourself until you understand why, rather than merely that."

There was a question sitting in Emeric's mouth, and it had been sitting there since a blacksmith told him about a satchel and an eleven-day ride, and this was plainly the moment for it.

He didn't ask it. He had one live thread and one seal and no second attempt at this conversation, and a man who has stopped talking about something for five years does not start because a boy asks him nicely in a cold room.

"I don't draw conclusions from numbers I haven't seen."

"No," Wexler said, and something unreadable moved behind the flatness a moment. "I don't imagine you do."

The ledgers came two days later, carried up by a page who clearly wanted no part of whatever was in them. Four bound volumes and a scatter of loose receipts that looked gathered in a hurry rather than pulled from any archive.

Emeric spread them across the desk that night and started where he had started with the parish records. Sorting before reading. Structure before content.

Then, because he had been waiting two weeks for a reason to try it on something that mattered, he put his hand around the stone and went as deep as waking let him go.

It did not tell him anything. It never told him anything. What it did was hold the whole shape at once, four volumes and six quarters and a hundred and some lines of expenditure, without any of it sliding off the edges the way it did when he tried to keep that much in his head unaided. He wasn't reading faster. He was simply not forgetting the first page by the time he reached the ninth, and against a set of books that had been built to be defensible line by line, that turned out to be the entire game.

It took him until the candle guttered to be sure he wasn't imagining it.

A barony this size, this population, running the numbers Wexler's own stewards had written down, should not have been sitting this close to the margin. The tithe income was there. The expenditure was accounted for, mostly. What wasn't there was the gap between them, a difference that should have existed and didn't.

Nothing stolen. Nothing so clean as a single missing line he could put a finger on. A slow distributed thinness spread across a hundred small entries that each looked entirely defensible on their own, and only stopped looking defensible when you could hold all hundred at the same time.

Which nobody in this barony had ever been able to do, and which was very probably the point.

He didn't have an answer. He had a shape, the same kind of shape the plague map had been at the start. Dots with no cause attached.

He added a line to the list he had started that first cold night at the writing desk. Six items had become nine, and none of the earlier ones had gotten any smaller in the meantime.

That was fine. That was how it always started.
