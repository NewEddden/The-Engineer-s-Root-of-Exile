Chapter 6 - Night Five

The fire had burned down to the point where a man could look into it without squinting, and the others were still seeing to the horses, and it was going to come out somewhere, and Ward was the only person alive to whom none of it would be news.

So he told it in order.

Age eight. Four attempts in fourteen months after the carriage, the fourth close enough that they had moved his rooms twice inside a week. His father sitting on the edge of his bed with a pleasant expression stretched over something enormous, saying that a threat gets removed and a joke gets ignored, and that he intended to make his son a joke, and that his son was going to help.

And he had. And he had been extremely good at it.

Drunk in the right places in front of the right people. Failing loudly, on purpose, where failing would be reported. Stupid questions asked in front of ambassadors, built to be repeated at dinner in three other kingdoms by winter.

"I thought it was the best game anyone had ever given me," he said. "I was eight. The prize was nobody putting anything in my food."

There had been a question he put to an ambassador from Vel at fifteen, in front of forty people, about whether it was true that her country's ships were towed by fish.

He had spent two days building that one. It had to be stupid enough to travel and not so stupid that it read as a performance. It had to be the particular kind of stupid that a man repeats at his own table, because a thing repeated at a table is in three kingdoms by winter and a thing repeated in a corridor dies in the corridor.

It worked exactly as designed. He heard it come back to him twice inside the year, once from a cousin who had it slightly wrong and thought the wrong version was funnier.

He had been proud of that for about six years.

Ward kept working the edge of a blade he had already finished working.

"And it worked. That's the part I keep getting stuck on. It worked, so there was never a year when somebody came and said the danger's finished, take it off, well done. It just kept working. So I kept doing it."

He watched the fire do what fires do.

"And one day I was nineteen and I hadn't opened a book since I was twelve, and I couldn't remember deciding not to."

He turned his hands over and looked at them and put them down.

"I stopped pretending to be an idle, useless, uninteresting man at some point I can't locate, because I became one. The act ate the actor. There's no seam. I've been looking for the seam for two days and there isn't one."

He had been circling that since a rock behaved like a spring in a courtyard at dawn. The thing he had felt under that axle and had not been able to name was curiosity, and it had come back to a man who had spent a decade making sure it could not find him.

"When," he said. "You were there. When did it stop being an act?"

Ward set the blade down.

He had not done that all evening. He had not done it, Emeric thought, in the fifteen years he had known him, and that alone told him the answer was going to cost something.

"Your fourteenth summer. You had a tutor come with a book on crystal lattices. You told him to leave it and you would look at it later." A pause. "You did not."

"You remember the book."

"I carried it up."

Eight years. Eight years of playing a part in front of a man who had watched him stop playing it and had never once said a word.

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"I am your guard. I keep you breathing." Ward picked the blade back up. "I was not given the rest of you to keep."

Osric came back from the horses then, and the fire was suddenly a place with five men at it, and Emeric did not answer, because there was no answer, and because a man who has been handed a sentence like that in a tone usually reserved for reporting a fence in need of repair does not get to have the last word on it.

The stories started because Osric started them, which was how it always went.

A bandit camp outside Grayrock, two summers back, and the camp grew as it was described. Three men at the outset. Five by the middle. By the end there was a defensive line in it and a lookout, and Declan said, without much heat, that the arithmetic had gotten away from him.

"I could have it entered in the official report," Emeric offered. "Final number. Whatever you say now, in writing, permanently."

Osric folded immediately.

Ward said they were all soft and should try a real posting sometime, and clearly meant it to end there, and nobody pushed. Something in the flatness told them not to.

Declan talked about his brother's wedding, which he had missed, and about the letter his brother sent afterward, which had been four lines long and had contained no reproach of any kind and had therefore been a great deal worse than a reproach would have been. He said it lightly and did not look up while he said it. Osric said something about the brother that Emeric did not catch, and Declan threw a boot at him, and missed, and Osric did not give the boot back.

Emeric sat outside the circle of it with his back against a wheel and let it happen around him.

It was, he worked out somewhere in the middle of it, the first evening in twenty-two years of memory that he had spent with men who were neither working for his father nor watching him on behalf of somebody. Nobody at that fire was waiting for him to be stupid. Nobody was waiting for him to be anything else either.

The absence of both took him most of an hour to identify, and when he had it, the word he arrived at was comfort, and he sat with it and did not say anything about it to anybody.

Rylan talked about Kaldo.

It had been good, before all that. His wife's family had been there three generations. When he was a boy the harbor still had real trade coming up from the coast, not just fishing boats patching their own nets on the bank. Then the war took the men. Then the plague took the ones the war had left.

His family had stayed through both of them, and he did not know whether that had been courage or nobody having anywhere else to go.

Nessa. Finn, who was six. Wren, who was four and had opinions.

Four months since he had seen any of them, and he had been counting on this posting for a real homecoming.

Petram said his cousin lived there.

A man named Corliss, same quarter as Rylan's people, which was how the two of them fell into working out the streets between them with obvious pleasure, neither having realized until that moment that he had been posted with somebody who knew the same three lanes.

They had not spoken in years, Petram said. Nothing bad in it. They had just never had much to do with each other.

Then, into the fire:

"Funny, though. Years go by, and you don't think about someone, then you hear their name again."

Out past the horses, where the treeline started, something brushed the edge of a sense he had no name for.

He looked, and found the ordinary nothing that a man always finds looking into the dark. A fox. A marten. One of the patient night things that drift to the edge of a fire's reach to weigh what the light is worth.

He was sitting inside a ring of six armed men. Whatever was out there was a guard's problem.

He filed it where he had filed the thing in the hall, which was to say nowhere, under no heading. A second stray number with no column to keep it in, and no reason yet to think the two were the same figure written twice.

It would occur to him later, with great precision and no use whatsoever, that two data points make a line.

Petram dug tea out of the carriage stores and steeped it over the fire.

Bitter, herbal, the kind of thing men drink on the road every night of their lives. Emeric did not think twice about the cup that came around.

There was nothing to think about.

He caught it in the fine movements first.

Declan's hand missed the cup, by very little, and corrected. Osric's head came up a beat late at something Rylan said. Ward was steady as a post, which was Ward.

Petram was steady. Garrin was steady.

He did the arithmetic in the time it takes to set down a cup.

Five men had drunk. Three were going. Two were not, and one of the two had made the tea.

There is a specific horror in watching your own body receive information and begin to comply with it. His hands were still working. His hands were going to keep working for a few more seconds and then they were not. The lead was coming up his arms from somewhere around the elbows, and there was a softness spreading behind his eyes that he recognized, with total clarity and no use whatsoever, from a chair that reclined farther than any chair needed to.

Rylan said something and it arrived slowly.

Emeric's whole chest went cold before his mind had finished the sentence, and he got two words out through a jaw that was already leaving him.

"Ward. Poison."

It was enough.

Ward turned into Petram's knife instead of taking it in the spine. The blade went through the bracer and into his forearm and stayed there for half a second before he took it out with the wrong hand, and then he put Petram in the dirt hard enough that Petram was not going to be running anywhere, and did not finish him, because a dead man tells nothing.

Garrin was already gone, boots into the treeline.

Ward could have gone after him. The rune in the bracer wanted a clean line of focus to pull the charge through and there was nothing clean anywhere in his head, but the reason he said out loud was the duty one, which was that going meant leaving Emeric alone in a camp with four unconscious men and one living traitor.

The other reason he did not say.

Emeric's vision went soft at the edges and his limbs filled with lead, and he waited to go down, and then something in him that had always run a little hotter than it should burned the fog off before it had finished the job.

He noticed that he should be unconscious and was not.

He had no column for that either.

He sat in the dirt for a long time afterward with his hands shaking, watching a man bind his own forearm with his teeth and one working hand, and he made himself go back through the evening, because going back through things was the one thing he was still good for.

Petram had raised the camp.

Petram had brewed the tea.

Petram had asked Rylan, easy, like it was nothing, whether his family was still in Kaldo. And had gotten names. And ages. And a street.

A careful man does not give up a real cousin's real name at a fire unless he does not expect anybody at that fire to repeat it.

He had held every piece of it an hour before it happened.

The gift only ever worked when he pointed it at something, and he had spent the entire evening pointing it at his own chest.

Then the worse part, arriving underneath. He had two strangers in his escort on a road his father had built, and he had filed them under his father's protection on day one and stopped looking at them, and had been pleased with himself while he did it.

His certainty about the plot was the thing that had made him careless.

He got as far as the shape of the fourth reading, the one that ran back to a small office and a whispered stretch of road, and stopped there the way water stops at a wall it has stopped at so long that it no longer registers as a wall.

On a street on another world, a man had run the odds on himself and gone anyway.

Tonight, at a fire, he had run them and had not moved until two words were the only thing left in him.

A mage for a mother and a knight for a father, and a cup of tea had taken him off his feet. Every useful thing done at that fire had been done by a man with his arm laid open.

He was delayed by years that he had chosen, and the delay had nearly cost him five men whose entire job was keeping him alive.

He decided, sitting in the dirt beside a fire that had gone out, that he was going to start again.

Late was worse than early and infinitely better than never.
