Chapter 47 - Upriver

Grayrock answered in the last week of October, seven months after it had been asked, and it answered a different question.

The letter came down on an ore barge, handed down with the bill of lading as the first one had been, in the same careful clerk's hand. It did not mention a meeting. It did not mention two pieces of ore off the same barge, or deeper workings, or any of the courtesies Emeric had put into his letter in March. It said that Grayrock regretted it would have no copper to sell downriver this season or, so far as could be foreseen, the next, its own works having need of all it raised. It hoped the barony of Kaldo would understand. It remained, with every respect, and the clerk had written the baron's name underneath and the baron had not signed it.

The ore on the barge was at the new price.

Ferra read the letter at her table, twice, and then took out the page she had ruled in March with its two lines and its empty column headed answered, and in the column beside Grayrock she wrote, in her small plain hand: No copper. And underneath, on a line of its own: Meeting, not mentioned.

Beside the crown there was still nothing.

"They don't need the copper," Emeric said. "Not all of it. Nobody needs all of it."

"No." Ferra blotted the line. "They need you not to have it."

He thought of the empty ring on Ferra's copper page, where the forty bars should have been.

He wrote to the crown again that night, setting out that the only copper on the river had been refused to a barony of the crown without a reason given, and that the barony would be grateful for the crown's view. Hale took it down to the north road with the rest of the week's letters and came back and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that he had not said, by saying nothing, the last time.

"Then we'll find it somewhere else."

"There is nowhere else on the river," Ferra said. "There's nothing upriver but Grayrock, and nothing downriver but the sea."

"Then not on the river."

She looked at him for long enough that he heard what he had said, and then she turned to a page he had not seen before.



The page was a list of houses.

Ninety-one of them, inside the walls, that the barony held against unpaid tithe because there had been nobody left alive in them to pay it. Most were shut. Some were let. Ferra had walked every one of them in June, when the iron came out of them for the wire, and against each one, in a column of its own, she had written what was in it that was worth anything, because she wrote down everything, and because a house standing empty with its door barred was still somebody's.

Kettles. Pans. A copper boiler in a washhouse. Candlesticks. The fittings of a pump. A preserving pan the size of a cartwheel, which had belonged, she said, to the woman who used to make the plum cheese for the whole lower ward, and had been sitting in a dark kitchen since the first spring of the plague because nobody could bear to go in and take it.

It came to a great deal of copper, when it was added up: nearer thirty bars than forty, and more than Grayrock had sent down the river in two years.

"The barony holds the houses," Ferra said. "It doesn't hold what's in them. Not all of it. Some of them have heirs." She put her finger on a second column, shorter, in which she had written names, and towns, and the word cousin, and the word sister, and once the word nobody with a question mark after it. "I've found twelve. There'll be more. Whatever's in their houses, we buy. At a fair price, off the scales, in front of them, if they'll come. Whatever's in the houses with nobody, the barony takes against the tithe, and I write it down. Both of them in the same book."

He looked at the list, all the way down.

"You've had this since the summer."

"I've had it since the summer," Ferra agreed. "You didn't need it till Grayrock said no."



It was ugly work, and it was done in the open, because Ferra said that was the only way it could be done.

Hale wrote the notices in the barony's form. Rylan read them out in the market on two mornings, every name and every house. Then for a fortnight, in the other dark market hall, the one the ward had not taken, Ferra sat at a table with Bram's scales on it, and a cart went round the shut houses with two of the gate crew and a key, and came back loaded, and the copper was weighed piece by piece in front of whoever had come to see it weighed.

Most of the time nobody came. When somebody did, it was worse.

A woman came down from the north side on the third day and stood at the table while they weighed her sister's kettles, two of them, and a little pan for milk. Ferra had the house down as one with nobody. She crossed that out and wrote the woman's name in, and did not say anything about it. The woman had not been inside her sister's house since the burial. She did not go inside it now. She took the coin Ferra counted out to her and looked at it in her palm, and then at the kettles on the scale, and asked whether she could keep the little pan, and Ferra took it off the scale without a word and gave it to her and wrote the weight again.

The plum woman's preserving pan came in on the fourth day. Nobody came for it. It sat on the weigh-room floor because it was too big for the scales, and Bram had to weigh it on the big beam in the forge, and he did not say anything at all while he did. Half the lower ward came past the forge door that afternoon to look at it, and some of them stood longer than others.

Bram melted it down with the rest, in a little round furnace he had built in the corner of the forge yard for the purpose, a squat chimney of firebrick as high as his chest, with a crucible in the heart of it and the bellows going in at the bottom. He melted the kettles, scraped of their tinning first, and the pans, and the washhouse boiler cut into pieces, and the plum pan last, a piece at a time, skimming off what floated as he had skimmed Grayrock's slag, and poured it into bars in a sand mold on the ground. The candlesticks and the pump fittings he did not put in at all. They were brass, and Emeric said brass would not do for wire, and they went into a box under the bench for something else.

Twenty-nine bars, when it was done. Good bars. Cleaner than Grayrock's, Bram said, though he said it without satisfaction, and did not look at the place in the yard where the plum pan had stood.

Ferra entered them in her book in two columns, bought and taken, and the bought column came to more than the barony had meant to pay, because she had paid a fair price, and she wrote the difference at the bottom in a line of its own and showed it to him, and he signed it.



Saban came back up the river at the end of October, on the last of his autumn round.

He came to Ferra's table on the first evening, as he had in March, with his hat in his hands and road dust on the shoulders of his mended coat, and before he would say anything about the arch bar or the tallow or the price of salt on the coast he told them about the questions.

"Four of them," he said. "In five weeks. Different towns. Different men." He sat down. "I've been on that round twenty years. Nobody asks me anything on it but the price of what's in the cart."

A factor in Wayford, with a cough, who bought the arch bar off Saban's tailboard by the case and had sold it on at twice the price, had asked him where it came from. Saban had told him. Kaldo. The factor had laughed, the way everybody on the round laughed at the name, the plague-town joke, and then he had stopped laughing and gone quiet, as if he were doing a sum.

A man on the northern road, a day out of Wayford, who had a horse and a good coat and nothing at all to sell, had asked him over a fire at a waystation how much of it there would be in the spring. Saban had told him what he thought, which was more.

A steward in Milford, a polite one, in the yard of a house Saban did not name, had asked him who owned it. The soap, the yard, the shop. Saban had said the barony did, and the steward had asked which barony, and Saban had said Kaldo again, and the steward had smiled the same smile as the factor.

And a clerk in the Grayrock scale house, where Saban's carts were weighed going north, had asked him, while the clerk was writing down the weight of Saban's empty cart, who was paying for all of it.

"All of what?" Emeric said.

"That's what I asked him." Saban turned his hat round once on his knee. "He had his book open. The scale book. Every barge that goes down the river is in it, by weight, and every coin that comes back up. He turned it round to show me the column with Kaldo at the top of it." Saban looked at Ferra. "Iron, every month, more each quarter than the one before. At a fifth over. Paid in coin. Never argued. He said that in his experience a barony that buys more every quarter at a price it doesn't argue with is either very rich, or it is about to be."

Nobody said anything.

"I answered every one of them true," Saban said. "That's my trade. A man who lies about where his goods come from on that road doesn't have a round. But I'll tell you what I heard, under it." He put his hat on the table. "Every one of them laughed first. The plague town. And every one of them stopped laughing and did a sum. And the sum was always the same sum." He looked at Emeric. "They know Kaldo was weak. Everybody on that river has known it for four years. They don't know yet that it's stopped. They've got an old picture."

Emeric thought of the old map off the guardroom wall, going out under the arch in June with nine men who had been the barony's, and up the north road, toward whoever would buy a drink for a man who could talk about Kaldo.

"How old?" he said.

"Old enough to be wrong. New enough to be interesting." Saban stood up. "They're hungry up there, Highness. Not angry. Hungry. And Kaldo's on the table."

Emeric told Ward that night, on the wall walk above the South Gate, all of it, the four questions and the scale book and the old map.

Ward listened to the end without turning his head from the road.

"Where did the nine go," he said. It was not a question, the way he said it, and Emeric did not answer it, because neither of them knew.

The next week there were two men on the wall walk above the South Gate after dark instead of one, and two above the river tower, and the gate crew's board had a new line chalked under it in Ward's square hand, which only said that the gate was shut at sunset and opened for nobody before light without the keep's word down the wire. Ward did not explain it to the garrison, and the garrison did not ask.



The autumn sowing went in through the first fortnight of November, in the second year of the rotation, and it was the worst fortnight he had had in Kaldo, though nothing in it went wrong that anybody else could see.

He was tired. He had been tired since the still, and before the still, since the zinc, and he had not noticed it arriving because it had arrived by inches. He was drawing copper in the dyehouse at night, out of the plum woman's pan. He had been up to the mines twice since September, a week each time, because the crew still would not light without him, and they wanted him again before the lower working flooded. He was in the yard at dawn because Ward was. He was in the hall for the petitions and at Ferra's table for the books and in the fields for the sowing, and he was holding a fire still for seven hours every time anybody needed a handful of zinc, and he did not sleep, and he did not notice that he did not.

Other people noticed.

He snapped at Tess on the second day of the sowing, in the dyehouse, over a jar she had filled the wrong way round, and she went white and did not cry and did not speak to him for the rest of the afternoon, and he did not understand until he was halfway up the hill that night that the jar had been filled exactly as he had told her to fill it the week before.

He told the cooper's journeyman that the barony would pay for the hazel for his hoops, and the journeyman thanked him and said that the prince had told him the same on Tuesday, and that it had been paid on Wednesday, and was there something else?

He stood in Farris's field on the fifth day watching the drill go up and down, and could not for the life of him remember whether they had agreed to put the clover under the barley this coming spring or the one after, and asked Farris, and Farris looked at him with real concern and told him they had agreed it at the pier a fortnight ago, with Hollis there, and that Emeric had been the one who said this spring.

And he watched Hollis sow his own strip. All of it. The sound half of the seed, and then, at the bottom of the strip, by the hedge, the brown, out of the sack, broadcast by hand, swinging, the way his father had sown, steadily, while Emeric stood at the end of the strip and did not say anything, because he had said it in September, and Hollis had heard him, and this was Hollis's strip.

On the last evening of the sowing he sat at Ferra's table with the season's figures and could not get a column to come out. He did it three times, and got three answers, and none of them was hers.

She waited until he had finished the third.

"You agreed the clover on the twentieth," she said. "At the pier. Farris told me."

"I know. He told me too."

"And Tess's jar was yours. Bram told me." She did not say it unkindly. She did not say it kindly either. "And you've been at the mines twice since September, a week at a time, and in the dyehouse every night you were here, and Abloh says you've gone grey under the eyes, which Abloh has never said about anybody."

"I'm tired. It's the sowing."

"It's not the sowing," Ferra said.

She did not say anything else. She took his three wrong answers off the table and did the column herself, once, and wrote the right answer at the bottom, and then she turned to the back of her own book, to the pages at the very end, past all the barony's business, and wrote something there, short, and the date beside it. He saw her hand move. He did not see what it wrote, and he was too tired to ask, and he went home.
