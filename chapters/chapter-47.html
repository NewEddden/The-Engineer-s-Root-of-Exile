Chapter 47 - A Law of Nature

There was nothing to hit all season and it turned out that he was very bad at that.

He had been good at August. That was the shameful part and he had written it down in March so that it could not be improved later: he had been better in the four days before an assault than he had been in any four days since, because an assault is a problem with a shape and a date on it and men you can put in places, and a price is not.

April went into May and May into June. Velmoor took its fifth and paid better than the coast and did not matter. The Grayrock cart road ran up and the Grayrock ore came down and the arrangement held, and Bryda wrote every quarter in a hand that had not changed by a letter. Two houses on the coast came off nine to seven, which Saban said was worth nothing and was the ordinary drift of a number nobody was watching, and Ferra put the two of them in the book anyway.

Emeric spent the whole of it being reasonable, in rooms, at tables, about numbers, and had no idea until the middle of June how much of himself he was spending on it.

What set it off was nothing.

He had written to the contracts house in the capital on the second of May, the fourth letter in eighteen months, a good letter, short, with a price in it that was worth their while. On the twenty-second of June he found himself standing in the yard doing arithmetic about it: seven days up if the road was dry, a day or two for a clerk to put it in front of somebody, four days for a man to make up his mind, seven days back. Twenty. Twenty at the outside. He had done it in his head, standing up, without deciding to, and had then noticed that he had done the same sum on the eleventh, and on the second, and he had a small flat feeling in his chest that he recognised and could not immediately place.

He placed it about an hour later, sitting on the wall, and when he had placed it he sat there for a long time.

It was the feeling of waiting for an answer that had not come, and he knew it very well, and he had not felt it since he was somebody else.

Marcus had sent messages the way a man breathes. Not letters, nothing with a fold in it: a line of text, a figure, a drawing, a question with three words in it, and the thing went out of his hand and arrived, and the arriving was not a separate event from the sending. It was the same event. He had a man in a facility four thousand miles away that he had never once stood in a room with, and he had worked with that man for six years, and there had never been a single hour of those six years in which he had wondered whether a thing he sent had got there.

He tried to put a number on it, on the wall, in the sun, because putting a number on things was the last habit that had survived the crossing intact.

Say ten a day, and that was low, and say he had done it from about sixteen. Say thirty thousand. Say thirty thousand messages, and out of thirty thousand, the number of times he had asked himself whether one had arrived was a number he could count, and every one of those was a failure so rare that it had been an anecdote afterward, a thing you told people about, because the system had broken and the system did not break.

Thirty thousand sendings and about six wonderings.

And he had not brought a machine through with him and he had not brought a road, and he had been perfectly clear about that from the first month, and had built a barony out of the perfect clarity of knowing exactly what he did not have. He had never once had to be told that there were no engines here. He had looked at a field and seen no engine.

What he had brought through, without noticing it, without ever once looking at it, was the thing underneath: that when you send a thing, it arrives. Not a tool. Not a fact. A reflex laid down thirty thousand times in a world where it had been true every single time, which is exactly the number of repetitions it takes for a habit to stop feeling like a habit and start feeling like a property of the universe.

He sat on the wall and went back through two and a half years and could not find one instance.

Not one. Not one time, since a courtyard and a cart and a road south, had he asked whether a letter had got where he sent it. He had asked whether it was answered. He had asked constantly whether it was answered, he had been asking about it in the yard an hour ago, and he had never in his life understood that these were two different questions with two different answers and that he had been silently collapsing them into one since before he could shave.

A man in this world would have asked. A boy in this world would have asked. It was the first question anybody here would ask and it was the one question he had never had a reason to learn.

Then he got to the second thing, which took another quarter of an hour and was much worse.

He had been told.

Not hinted at. Told, across a table, in his own long room, on the fourth of December in his second winter, by a man who had ridden eleven days to be there. Four letters out of five. Armand had sat and worked out loud that of the five things he had written, one had arrived, and that of the two Emeric had sent, one had arrived in September dated in April, and he had said out loud that hearing it as a sum was worse than living it in instalments.

And then Armand had said what the roads have always been.

And Emeric had written that night about the residue in the door frame, and about the gallery, and about one in two, and had written nothing whatsoever about the letters, because the letters were the part of the evening that had been explained.

He went and found Armand in the second room off the long room, which had four crates in it in December and now had shelves, and asked him to say it again and to say it exactly.

Armand said it again. Four out of five, in the year before he came, on that road.

"And when you said the roads are what the roads have always been," Emeric said, "what did you mean? Not what you'd say now. What was in your head."

"That a letter is a thing you hand to a road." Armand looked mildly baffled at being asked. "You give it to a carrier, and the carrier gives it to a post, and the post gives it to another carrier, and somewhere in that there is a river and a junction and a man with a bad chest and an inn, and a proportion of what goes in does not come out. That is not a complaint. It is the arrangement. Everyone alive has grown up inside it."

"What proportion?"

"How would anyone know that?"

"Somebody must have counted."

"Counted what?" Armand had his hand out flat, honestly at a loss, in the exact way he had been at a loss in December when he was asked what an operator measured. "You cannot count what did not arrive. A letter that is lost does not announce itself. It is not there, and you do not know it was ever sent, and the man who sent it does not know it was lost. He knows only that you did not write back, and the ordinary explanation of a man not writing back is that he did not care to."

Emeric sat down on the corner of the shelf-crate.

"Say that last bit again."

"That he did not care to."

"So when four of your five didn't reach me, what did you conclude?"

Armand was quiet for a second.

"That the roads were the roads," he said, "and that a young man in a barony had a great deal to do, and that if he had wanted to write, he would have written."

"You thought I'd cooled on you."

"For a period in June, yes, and I was ashamed of it and I did not write it in the July letter, which is why the July letter was short." He put his hands together. "You are looking at me as though I have said something remarkable and I have said the most commonplace thing in the world."

"You've said the whole of it," Emeric said. "I came here with a law that says a thing sent is a thing arrived, and it isn't a law, it's a habit off thirty thousand repetitions in a place that had the machinery to make it true. And you've all got a law that says letters are lost, which is also true, and which is also a habit, and yours is worse than mine, because mine only makes me stupid about the road."

"And mine?"

"Yours means nobody counts. Ever. Anywhere." He was up now. "If losing is normal and losing is random, then a lost letter is not information. It's weather. Nobody keeps a book of the weather that didn't happen. So there is no man in this kingdom, not one, who could tell you whether the road that eats his letters eats everybody's, or eats his in particular, or eats only the ones with something in them, because the answer to all three of those looks precisely the same from where he is standing, which is at his own end of it, holding a silence."

"You are describing a thing that cannot be found out."

"I'm describing a thing that can only be found out by somebody who thinks it's strange that letters go missing," Emeric said, "and there is exactly one of those in this kingdom and he has been here two and a half years and hasn't asked the question once."

Paxton came on the fourteenth of July with no cattle at all, which Emeric noticed on the stair and did not understand until the man was sitting down.

"I am going to do this badly," Paxton said, "and I would like you to let me get to the end before you say anything, because if you interrupt me I shall lose my nerve and go home and talk about wool."

"Go on."

"One. I know a thing. I have known it since the spring and I have spent the spring and half the summer deciding, and I have not decided, and I have come anyway, which is what a man does when he cannot decide."

"Two. I will not tell you how I know it. If you work it out you will not say so, not in this room, not to your guard, not to the woman with the books, not in your own hand on a page." He was not looking at the fire. He was looking at Emeric. "Because the man who told me believes I am his friend, and I intend to go on letting him believe it for as long as he will have me, and if that ever stops, the reason it stops will be that somebody wrote it down."

"Three. What I am going to give you is one sentence. It is not evidence. It will not stand up in front of anyone. You cannot act on it, you cannot repeat it, and if you took it to the crown tomorrow the crown would be entirely right to send you home." Paxton let out a breath. "It is the most useless thing anybody has ever given anybody, and I have ridden a day to hand it over, and I am aware of how that sounds."

"Say the sentence."

"Your letters are not being lost," Paxton said. "They are being taken. It is not the road. It is a place on the road, and it is done on purpose, and it has been done for longer than you have been here."

The room was very quiet.

"How long."

"That is a second sentence and I am not going to give you a second sentence." Paxton stood up. He looked every one of his years doing it. "I have thought about this a great deal and here is where I have got to. If I tell you a name you will do something with it, because you are young and you are quick and you have never once in three years been given a fact and sat on it. And whatever you did would be built on a thing you cannot show anybody, and it would come apart in your hands, and then I would be a man who was told a secret and betrayed it, and I would have bought you nothing."

"So what am I meant to do with it?"

"Go and find it out yourself," Paxton said, "properly, in your own hand, out of your own paper, so that on the day you stand up and say it, not one word of it came from me. That is the whole of what I have to give you and it is a year of your life and I am sorry."

He went to the door and stopped there.

"You are going to want to write this evening down," he said. "Don't."

Emeric got the list out that night and sat with it open for some time.

Twenty days. I did the sum in the yard on the twenty-second and I had done it on the eleventh and on the second and I did not know I was doing it, and the feeling underneath it was not a feeling I have had in this world.

Thirty thousand sendings and about six times I ever wondered. I did not bring an engine through and I have never once forgotten that I did not bring an engine through. What I brought through was the thing underneath the engine, which is that sending is arriving, and I have been carrying it for two and a half years the way a man carries the floor being solid. You do not check the floor. Checking the floor is not a discipline you have failed at. It has simply never come up.

Armand told me in December of my second year and I wrote three pages that night about a door frame and a training bench and one in two, and not one word about the letters, because the letters were the part that had been explained, and I have spent two years being the only man in this kingdom who refuses to accept a local explanation of a local fact, about wells, about iron, about a foot of length, about a fist in a sack of grain, and I took this one whole and did not chew it once.

And theirs is worse than mine and that is the actual finding. If letters are lost, and lost at random, then a silence carries no information, so nobody keeps a record, so the difference between a road that loses everybody's and a road that loses only mine cannot be seen by anyone standing at their own end of it. It is not that the answer is hidden. It is that both of the available worldviews make the question stupid to ask.

Ferra started her book of silences on the twenty-ninth of April because I asked an idle question in a room, and told me it would be worth nothing for a year, and she is the only reason there will be anything at all to look at when I finally know what I am looking for.

There is a thing that happened this afternoon that I am not writing down, and I am writing down that I am not writing it down, and that I have thought about it for four hours and can find no version where writing it costs me nothing. This is the first page of this book that has a hole in it deliberately. I have kept this list since my first November for the single reason that a thing written cannot be improved later, and I have just found the one class of true thing that gets a man killed by being on a page in a barony whose letters somebody else reads.

I am going to go and find it out myself. It is going to take a year and I have been told so by a man who did the arithmetic before he got on the horse.
