Chapter 46 - What It Costs to Win

The war sent its bill the way bills arrive in a barony that keeps good books, which is in pieces, over a winter, and every piece of it was a policy Emeric had chosen, coming due at a size he had not.

The wounded came first, because they had never stopped coming.

He had put it on the board in the first year in words nobody could misread. If the Works breaks you, the Works keeps paying you. And on the flank, before the war, he had told the crews and the garrison and every volunteer who picked up a crossbow the same thing, as policy and not as charity. A man hurt in Kaldo's service is paid and housed until he mends. He had meant it. He meant it still. What he had not done, until that winter, was see it at size.

It was a long list, and it did not get shorter as fast as anybody wanted. Burns off the mages that Imani was still dressing at midwinter. Broken forearms that had set and ribs that had knit and a shoulder that nobody had been able to put back for two days and that did not go back right. Men who could stand and could not lift. Imani told him in the first week of winter that half her list would be back at work by the spring and the other half would not, and that he should stop asking her which half, because she would tell him when she knew and not before. The harnessed men had come out of it beaten rather than cut, and most of them were back on the wall by the solstice, sore and irritable and paid. The volunteers were the expensive part. The volunteers had never been on any payroll. Kaldo was now paying wages to people it had never employed, for work they could no longer do, and it was going to go on paying them.

Hollis was a line item.

He was on Ferra's page, in her hand, between a quarryman with a crushed foot and a woman off the soap rota with burned palms, at a weekly figure that made up the difference between what he could earn on the quarry gang with one arm and a half and what he had earned with two. Both men knew it. Hollis collected it at the yard on the day and said thank you in a voice that made it sound like a receipt, and never once referred to it otherwise. Emeric signed the sheet every week and never once referred to it at all.

The farm was next, and the farm was worse, because the battle had been fought on it.

Farris walked it with him in a hard frost, and did not talk, which with Farris meant it was bad.

The lane had been dug through Emeric's own divisions. That had been the point of it, and it had worked, and now it was a ninety-foot scar across good ground with the spoil from it lying in long banks across three divisions, and every bank had to be moved by hand before a plough could cross it. Two hundred men had camped and dug and trampled across the whole west bank. The sluices were broken, some by the fighting and some by the digging and two by nobody could say what. And under all of it was the burned crop, a mile and a half of black stubble and charred straw matted into the ground by the autumn rain, which had to be cleared before anything could be sown, because seed sown through that would come up thin or not at all.

"It'll come back," Farris said, at the far end of the sixth division, where Renner had been found. "The ground's not hurt. It's buried."

"How long to unbury it."

Farris named a number of men and a number of weeks. Emeric did not like either.

The main was the piece nobody could have seen coming, because nobody could see it at all.

It ran under four feet of spoil from the tower to the far edge of the ninth division, clay pipe laid by hand in the third year, a section at a time, every one of them passed through Colm's hoop. Men had stood and fought forty feet from it. Carts had stood on top of it. Two hundred men and their horses had gone back and forth across the line of it for the best part of a week, and there is no way on earth to survey buried pipe from above. You find out what has happened to it by digging it up.

They dug it up in the second week of winter, and it was materially worse than the estimate.

Emeric went down into the trench when they opened the first stretch, because a number is easier to believe once you have stood in it. The pipe came up in pieces. Colm lifted the first crushed section out with both hands and turned it over and looked at the break for a long while, the way a man looks at a letter from a relative he has not heard from in years.

"It did what it was made for," Colm said. "Nobody made it for this."

It had not failed. It had been stood on by something the weight of a loaded cart, day after day, and it had held for most of those days, and then it had not. They opened the next stretch the following morning, and the one after that, and the figure on Ferra's sheet went up every evening, and Emeric went down into the trench every morning regardless.

A long run of crushed sections, all along the stretch where the coalition had parked its carts. A collapsed run near the tower base, where the ground had given under something heavy. The pump-house feed, cracked across in two places. Every one of those was a trench, a crew, new pipe, and a test, and every one of them had to be done in frost.

It was the worst way for a number to move. It moved after the labour had already been committed, when the men were in the trench and paid and the only thing left to decide was whether to finish.

Ferra said so, in almost exactly those words, and wrote the new figure under the old one without crossing the old one out.

Colm, meanwhile, had spent the autumn making a better pipe than the one in the ground. Harder fired, with a deeper collar, for no reason except that he had worked out how and could not leave it alone. Emeric had seen the first ones and approved of them. He did not yet know what that approval was going to be worth.

The deadline was what made all of it one problem instead of three.

The spoil had to be moved, the stubble cleared, the sluices set and the main relaid and tested before the first sowing. There was no negotiating with that date. It belonged to the ground and the weather, and neither of them had ever been interested in Emeric's arithmetic. Miss the planting window and Kaldo lost the following year as well as this one.

And the following year was the one that would actually finish the barony. The grain in the cellars was the reserve. It was the only reserve. There was not a second one standing behind it.

He had spent a week framing the choice as debt against hardship. Ferra took that frame away from him in one sentence.

"It isn't debt or tightening," she said. "It's debt or a second year with nothing in it. You won't survive the second year, so it's debt."

The obvious question came from one of the venture men, in the long room over the threshing floor, and it was a fair one.

"You've a cellar full of grain. Sell some."

Emeric had been waiting for somebody to ask, because if nobody asked it out loud, somebody would ask it in a kitchen, and a question asked in a kitchen grows.

"Food isn't money," he said. "Not this food. Some of what's in those cellars is yours, not mine, and the rest is what this town eats until there's a harvest. I can't sell the town's dinner. The seed in there is the only seed there is. If I sell it we plant nothing. And the only buyer anywhere in reach who wants grain is the hill, and the hill pays in ore, and nobody on the hill will answer a letter."

Ferra had written to Grayrock's steward twice. Nobody at Grayrock held the authority to answer and nobody had.

The venture man thought about it and nodded, slowly, the way men nod when they have been told the truth and do not enjoy it.

That was the shape of it, and it was an ugly shape. The largest thing Kaldo owned could not be turned into coin. The only customer who wanted it paid in something Kaldo could neither eat nor bank.

The foundry was worse than idle.

It ran. It ran on the ore that had been bought and paid for before the war, Renner's ore, sitting in the yard in long heaps, and it ran well, and every single thing it made went into the store and stayed there. Hinges. Ploughshares. Bar. Fittings. A rack of pipe collars. Tools in their dozens, good ones, off the pattern.

There was nobody to sell them to. The hill was silent. Wayford hated Kaldo and had just paid for the privilege. Milford had levied its own drovers for a war it had not fought and was, as far as anybody could tell, broke. The capital was five days off by a rider with fresh horses and twelve by cart, and nobody in the capital had ever bought a hinge from the south. The halls that had bought the soap were still deciding what they were, and the soap had not started again.

So Kaldo was paying a trained crew, at the posted rate, to turn good ore into goods that nobody would pay for.

Ferra called it the pile. She said an idle foundry costs you its wages. The pile cost the wages and the ore, and gave back a store that had to be counted.

It took him longer than it should have to see what the pile was telling him.

He had spent three years on capacity. Every problem Kaldo had ever brought him, he had answered by building. More water, more grain, more iron, more trained hands. And now he was standing in a store full of good iron in a town full of good hands, and what he was thinking about was how to make more of both, and Ferra had to say it twice.

"It isn't the making," she said the first time. "You can make. You've proved it. Nobody is buying."

He started to answer and she said it again, slower, in the voice she used for a figure he had refused to look at.

"Kaldo has no market."

The name Duvrenne had been in his head for six weeks. He did not reach for it. He did not even think of it, sitting there. Nobody further off than a cart could reach had ever bought anything from Kaldo, not once, in all the time he had been there, and at some point in that time he had stopped treating it as a fact that might change and had started treating it as the weather.

What he tried first was the right instinct. He knew it was right while he was doing it, and that did not help.

If nobody would buy the iron that existed, he would sell the iron that did not exist yet.

Two years of the foundry's future output, sold now, as contracts. Tools, fittings, plate, delivered over two years at a fixed price, paid for in advance. No lender. No interest. A holding that needed iron got it cheaper than it could get it anywhere else and got it certain. Kaldo got its money inside the planting window. It avoided debt entirely, and it was honest, and he was quietly pleased with it for about a week.

Ferra drew up the terms. Saban carried the letters, west and north, to every holding and house he had a name in, which was most of them within a sensible ride.

It was undersubscribed, and it was not close.

The answers came back across the winter, and the ones that came back at all were courteous. Nobody quarrelled with the price. Nobody said the iron was poor, and several of them had seen it. What they said, in a dozen different forms of words, was that they would not sign a two-year contract with a name that had no history.

Kaldo had been a plague town three years before. It had been a treason case. It had been, as far as half the region was concerned, the barony that started a war. A holding that paid for two years of iron in advance was betting that the seller would still be there in two years to deliver it. Nobody in the region had any way to price that bet, and a bet you cannot price is a bet you do not take.

It was not the product. It was not the price. It was the name, and there was nothing standing behind the name.

One answer came back with money in it.

Paxton subscribed what Milford could carry. His letter was four lines in a square, unornamented hand.

Milford takes forty ploughshares and six score hinges on your terms, delivered as you can make them. The sum is enclosed. It is what I can carry this year. It is not what you need, and I expect you know that as well as I do.

It was signed with an initial. It was the first thing Paxton had done for Kaldo since the field.

Emeric read it twice and wrote back a line of thanks, and chose the words of the line with some care, and they said nothing that either man would have wanted a third person to read.

Ferra read him the whole stack in a single sitting, in the small room, at night, flat.

"Wounded," she said. "Injury pay, housing, dressings, through the spring at the least and longer for some. Farm. Clearing, spoil, sluices, the whole west bank to be ready by the sowing. Main. The crushed run, the collapsed run, the pump-house feed, and the crew for all three. Haulage. Payroll."

She turned the sheet.

"Against. No harvest. No soap. The pile. Milford's subscription, which is kind and small. And Wayford's money, which I told you in the autumn would buy months, and which has now bought most of them."

Then she did the thing she had not done in the autumn.

She wrote a date at the foot of the page, turned the page round, and put it in front of him.

It was the day the barony would no longer be able to pay the Works. It was nine weeks off. The planting window closed after it.

She put the wage on the table next. It was her job to put everything on the table, and she did it the way she did everything else, with a figure beside it. If the Works went to half pay, the date moved out by so much. If the injury pay went to half as well, it moved out by so much more.

She was not arguing for it. She was not arguing against it. It was a line, and lines go on the page.

"No," said Emeric.

He did not make a speech. He had put both of those on a board in a public square, and they were the reason the town worked. They were the reason men who had watched three barons come down the road had picked up crossbows and stood on a bank. A town that had been paid through a war would work through a winter. A town that learned its wage could be cut the first time the lord was short would never again believe a word painted on a board, and he had priced that, as well as he could, and it came out higher than any figure on her page.

"I would rather owe money than owe that," he said.

Ferra wrote it down and did not comment. She had the date she needed. She did not need him to be admirable about it, and he was not trying to be. It was a calculation, and he knew it was a calculation, and in the month that followed it was going to cost him, in writing, a measurable sum.

Saban was in the room, because Saban had carried every one of the letters, and Emeric had wanted him to hear how they had come out.

He waited until Ferra had closed the book.

"There's one house that lends against a thing like this," he said. "Ducatis."

The largest lending house in Randillia. Emeric had known the name since before he came south. Half the noble houses of the capital that had ever overreached had their paper in that house, and he had watched some of them, at dinners, grow careful when the name came up. He had hoped, with some specificity, never to need it.

"They'll own us," said Ferra.

"They'll lend to us," said Saban. "The owning is in the terms. Terms can be read."

Emeric looked at the date at the foot of the page for a long time. His pride had an opinion on the matter. The arithmetic had a better one, and it was louder.

"Yes," he said. "Write to them."

He was about to borrow from the only institution in the kingdom large enough to own him, and he had just said yes to it in the same voice he used for ordering lime.
