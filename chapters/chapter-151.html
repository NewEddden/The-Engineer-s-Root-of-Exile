Chapter 151 - Four Baronies

Paxton's first message on the Milford line was an inventory.

The line opened on the third of September, thirty miles of it, the third of Kaldo's lines and the shortest, across the river and west over the fields along the cart road to Milford's gate, where Paxton had had a room cleared for the sounder in his own steward's house and a clerk sat by it day and night. The Kaldo end clicked for the first time a little before noon. The clerk in the gate tower wrote it down. It took him most of a quarter of an hour.

It said that Milford had in its granary, as of that morning, four thousand two hundred and six bushels of wheat, twelve hundred of barley and three hundred of beans; that the west herd stood at ninety-one head, the east at sixty-four; that three ploughs were sound and one was broken at the share and would be mended by the Thursday; that the sluice on the lower field was leaking and had been since August; and that the steward of Milford sent his respects to the holder and his compliments to the chancellor, in that order, and would report again on Monday.

Ferra read it standing at the back of the telegraph room with her book open, checking it against her own figures for Milford line by line as she went. She did not find anything wrong with it. She put a small mark beside the sluice. Then she closed the book and said, to nobody, that it was the first time anything had come up a wire to her that she would not have to send back, and went down the tower stair.



The adding machine came down the capital road the same week, in a crate of straw, from Wayford.

It was the one Emeric had stood in front of in Ottoline Fesk's shop under the clock a year ago: a box of brass and walnut the size of a bread oven, with a row of eight little wheels behind eight little windows on its face, numbers round their rims, and below them a bed of keys, and a crank on the right side. Ottoline had sent it with a letter. The letter was four pages long and was about how to oil it. On the last page, at the very bottom, in a smaller hand, she had added that she had sold the barony of Kaldo a second one for its counting room at the price written below, and a third for Wayford's under Aster's office, and that if the barony wanted more she could make one a month, and that she hoped the chancellor would find it adequate.

It went on the clerks' table in the counting room. Nobody said anything about it. The three clerks looked at it from a distance for a day, the way a dog looks at a new dog. On the second day, at about four in the afternoon, when the quarter's carts had come in and the columns were long and the light was going, Ferra got up from her desk with a page of figures in her hand and went over to the clerks' table and sat down in front of the machine.

She put the first figure in on the keys. She turned the crank. She put in the second. She turned it again. She went down the whole page like that, steadily, without looking up, the little wheels clicking round behind their windows with a sound like a clock in a hurry, and at the bottom she read the eight windows, and wrote the number on her page, and looked at it beside the total she had already made in her own head on the way across the room.

They agreed. She got up and went back to her desk and did not say anything.

The clerks looked at each other. The next morning all three of them were at the machine before she came in, and there was a queue for it by noon, and Ferra, at her desk, went on doing every sum in her head first, and checked every one of them on the machine after, and never once said a word about it to anybody, which from Ferra, Emeric thought, watching her from the door, was a speech.



Rylan wore the first glyph on his own skin on the fourteenth, in the keep yard, at night, with the gate shut.

It had been coming since the spring, since Wayford. It had begun with a question Emeric had asked Rylan in the yard one morning, sitting on the mounting block: how does a knight do it? How do you make yourself fast? And Rylan had looked at him as though he had asked how a man breathes, and thought about it, and said that you thought of it. That was all. You thought of the spell, the shape of it, the way a mage sees his glyph; and you saw yourself fast; and you were. It came from inside, from your own mana, the way your own arm moved when you thought of moving it.

So a man with no mana of his own to speak of, who could not make himself fast from inside, needed two things. He needed the shape of the spell somewhere he could feel it. And he needed the mana from somewhere else.

The mana was on his wrist already. Every knight in Kaldo wore a band now, with its row of blue battery beads against the skin. The shape was the hard part. A glyph was a glyph whether it was in the air or etched on a stone or drawn in ink on a page; Tess had shown that, two years ago, on a stone the size of a coin. It could be drawn on a man.

Imani drew it. She would not let anybody else put a needle into a man in her barony, she said, least of all a prince who had never held one, and she sat Rylan down in the cutting room of the hospital on a stool under the north window and drew the glyph into the inside of his left forearm from Emeric's drawing, line by line, with a fine needle and a cousin of the barony's own ink, the black that held a charge, cut thinner. It took her three hours. Rylan sat through it with his arm on the table and his jaw tight and talked the whole time, to keep from thinking about it, about horses, and the worst inn on the capital road, and a dog he had had as a boy, until Imani told him that if he moved his arm once more while he was talking she would draw the dog on it instead. At the end of it there was a glyph on the inside of his arm, the size of a palm, black and raised and sore, and from its lowest line a short black thread of the ink ran down to his wrist and under the edge of his band, to the grey bead.

A glyph tattoo. Emeric named it at the table in the morning, so that it would have a name before it had a reputation.

In the keep yard at night, with the gate shut and Ward on it and a lamp on the mounting block, Rylan stood at one end of the yard and held his own power still.

That was the test. Rylan was a knight. He could make himself fast from inside without any of this, and if he did, nobody would ever know which speed was his and which the glyph's. So he held his own still, the way knights did when they slept, damped down in him like a banked fire, until Ward, standing beside him with a hand on his shoulder, said that he could feel nothing coming off him at all; and then Rylan looked at the far end of the yard, forty feet away, at the sand sack hanging from its post, and thought of the spell.

The glyph on his arm lit.

Faint. Very faint, a line of grey light running round the shape of it under the skin. Emeric felt it draw. He felt the grey bead on Rylan's wrist give, and the blue beads behind it give, and the charge go up the black thread into the glyph, and out of the glyph into Rylan.

And Rylan was at the sack.

He was at it and past it and into the wall behind it, with his shoulder, hard, and down on the cobbles. He had crossed forty feet in less time than it took the lamp's flame to lean. Then he was lying on the cobbles at the foot of the wall with the sack swinging above him, laughing, and holding his shoulder.

"Too much," said Ward.

Rylan sat up. The glyph on his arm was dark again. He looked at it, under the lamp, and then held it up for Emeric to see, and Emeric saw what he had been afraid he would see and what the plan had said he would. The lines of the glyph were paler. Not much. A shade. As though the ink had been very slightly washed.

They ran it ten times that night. Ten uses. Each a second, or less. And after each the glyph was a shade paler, and the band's gauge a little lower, and by the tenth Rylan said he could feel the difference, a burst that came on softer, like a horse that has been run hard and still goes when you ask it but goes a little less. That was the cost. It was in the plan, and in Armand's book, and now it was on the inside of Rylan's arm. The glyph was a patch, not a body. It wore out of the skin with use, as overdraw scars wore into it, only backward. In a while it would need drawing again. It gave seconds of a knight's speed, not hours, and none of a knight's resistance to a spell, and in a dead zone it would give nothing at all.

It was still a second of a knight's speed, on a man who had none of his own.

"So you don't use it to run," said Emeric.

Rylan, on the mounting block with his sore arm across his knees, looked up.

"You use it to step," said Emeric. "One step. Half of one. The step that closes the gap, or gets you out of the way of the thing coming. A man who spends his ten seconds running across a yard has spent them. A man who spends a tenth of a second at a time has a hundred." He looked at Ward. "Show him."

Ward showed him.

Ward did not have a glyph on his arm and did not need one. But he had been a knight a long time, and he had learned, a long time ago, in fights that went on for hours, what every knight who lived long enough learned: that you did not pour yourself into a fight, you spent yourself on it a drop at a time. He stood in the middle of the yard under the lamp with a practice blade, and Emeric threw a pebble at him, and Ward was a foot to the left of where the pebble went, and had not seemed to move. Emeric threw another. Ward was a foot to the right. Then Ward came across the yard at Rylan at an ordinary walk, slow, a big grey man strolling, and in the middle of the walk, for no longer than a blink, he was not strolling, and the practice blade was resting on Rylan's collarbone, and then he was strolling again, and stepped back.

"Like that," said Ward. "Feather it."

Rylan spent the rest of the night feathering it, a step at a time, under the lamp, and by the time the glyph was too pale to draw from he could take the half step and stop. It was not Ward's. It was a start.

The tattoo stayed inside Kaldo's own men. That was the table's rule and Emeric's, and Ward's above both. A commoner with a precast glyph on his own skin, fed from a stone on his own wrist, was the thing the Church had spent its whole long history making sure no commoner had, and there would come a day when that mattered more than anything else in the south. It was not today. Today it was a sore arm on a knight in a shut yard.



The baths opened on the twenty-second, beside the water tower, at the top of the new main street.

The street had been finished on the Friday. It ran from the South Gate to the keep, the whole length of it, drained under its crown by a stone culvert that fed the new sewer, and cobbled from wall to wall with grey Grayrock setts bedded in Kaldo's cement, so that a cart went up it now on stone instead of axle deep in mud, and a woman could cross it in the rain in her house shoes. It had taken all summer. The town had walked round the works for four months, complaining. Then it had walked on the finished stone for two days and stopped complaining, and begun to complain instead about everywhere that was not cobbled yet.

The baths were at the top of it. Two long rooms of brick under one tile roof, one for men and one for women, with a tiled pool in each, and the water came into the pools hot. It came from the water tower beside them, down a pipe, through a copper heater in the cellar where three of the recharged fire stones sat under a plate over a bed of Grayrock coal, so that when the stones were spent the coal took over and the water never went cold. Imani had drawn the drains. Ferra had set the price: a copper for a grown body, nothing for a child, nothing at all on a Sunday, and Ferra had looked at Emeric across the table when she said nothing on a Sunday and dared him to say anything about it, and he had not.

The whole town came. By turns, by streets, by families, with Sela at the women's door with her roll as if it were the school, and the garrison at the men's, and a queue going down the new cobbles all afternoon past the water tower almost to the gate.

At dusk, when the last of the queue had gone in, the three of them were on the steps outside, arguing about the water.

It was a real argument. Ferra had been told by four separate women coming out of the women's pool that the water was too hot, and four was a sample, and she had written it down. Imani had been in the women's pool herself at noon, to see, and said it was exactly right, and that four women out of four hundred who had never in their lives been in water warmer than a puddle were not a sample, they were a complaint.

"Four is four," said Ferra.

"Four is four people who've never been warm," said Imani. "Skin goes red in hot water. That's blood coming up to the top. It's good for them."

"Then you'll write that on the door. That their skin will go red and it's good for them."

"I'll write it if you like."

"I'd like a number," said Ferra.

They both looked at him.

He had a thermometer. Ivo had made it in the spring for the dyehouse vats, a long glass tube with a silver thread of mercury in it, marked off in degrees along a strip of brass, because the dyers had complained that they could never get the same blue twice and Emeric had said that was because they had never once known how hot the vat was. It was in the cellar of the baths, by the heater, where he had hung it a week before for Bram to set the stones by. He went down and got it. He came back up the steps with it in his hand, and the two of them were standing at the top of the steps under the lamp over the door, side by side, with their arms folded, exactly alike, waiting for him.

He went into the women's pool, because it was empty now and because it was the one in dispute, with Imani on one side of him and Ferra on the other, and knelt at the edge of the tiled pool in the steam, and put the bulb of the thermometer in the water, and held it there, and the three of them watched the silver thread climb.

It stopped.

He read it. He read it twice. He looked up at Ferra on his left, who had her book open, and Imani on his right, who had her arms folded, and he understood that whatever number he said next he was going to lose.

"Well?" said Ferra.

"Hot," said Emeric. "Not too hot. Hot."

"That's not a number," said Ferra.

"That's the right answer," said Imani.

And they looked at each other, over his head, while he knelt between them at the edge of the women's pool with a thermometer in his hand and steam in his hair. He saw them do it. He saw something pass between them, quick and dry, over the top of him, that he was not a part of and was not meant to be, and that was entirely about him. Then Ferra wrote something in her book. Imani leaned down and took the thermometer out of his hand, and read it herself, and handed it to Ferra, who read it and wrote the number down beside whatever she had already written, and closed the book.

They went out together. He heard them on the steps, going down the new cobbles in the dark, arguing about the price of the coal under the heater. Neither of them had told him what the number was. He found he did not mind at all.



Armand read the bands on the twenty-ninth, the quarter day, as he had said he would.

He did it in his room off the hall, in his chair, with the window open onto the gate yard and the book on the board across his knees. Every wearer came in turn. They sat on the stool in front of him and held out their wrists, and he put his thin dry fingers on the inside of each wrist, above the band, and closed his eyes, and read them, three times, slowly, a breath apart, and opened his eyes and wrote three numbers in his book in his small cramped hand, and the next one came in.

Emeric came last. He sat on the stool, and held out his wrist, and while Armand had his eyes shut he looked at the book.

He read it upside down, the way he read Ferra's. He could not help it; he had been reading books upside down across tables for five years. Armand had ruled it in columns: the name, the age, the three readings, the date. It went down the page in the order they had come in. Bram. Ward. Saban. Osric. Declan. Rylan, with a small note beside his name in a different ink that said arm. Imani, twenty-seven. Ferra, twenty-six. The numbers after the names meant very little to Emeric, upside down; Armand's scale was his own. But the first column did not need reading the right way up, and he found that he had stopped on it, on the two lines one under the other, without meaning to.

Twenty-seven. Twenty-six.

He had known. He had known roughly, the way a man knows the ages of the people he works beside, without ever once having asked or been told. He had not known it in a column, in ink, with a date beside it. It looked different in a column. He thought of Imani at nineteen, with her mother's post and that year on her, which she had told him about once, quickly, the way the town told everything about that year. He thought of Ferra at twenty-one at her father's table, with thirty years of the barony's ledgers in three clerks' hands and no index, telling a prince what she would be paid. He thought of himself at twenty-two in a cold gallery in the capital, with somebody else's memories coming up through him feelings first.

Armand opened his eyes.

"You're reading my book," he said.

"Yes."

"Upside down."

"Yes."

Armand wrote three numbers on the next line, and then, in the second column, without looking up, a fourth: twenty-seven. He blotted it.

"There," he said. "Now you're in it too. Go away."



The low flights stopped at the first frost, on the sixth of October.

The K Discovery had been out at night more than forty times since May. She had never once climbed. She had never been higher than the trees on the edge of the marsh, and she had never been out over anything but water, and nobody but the people who had flown her had ever seen her do anything but go down the river under oars and come back. Tess's data book had a page for every night, and on the last night of the season, with the frost white on the shed roof and her legs up in her slots and her doors shut behind her, Tess drew a line across the foot of the last page and wrote the total under it, and wrote under the total: Next year, up.



Saban brought the other thing on the tenth.

It came by the new wire, from his man at the Wayford posting house, the one who had been sitting in the same corner of the same taproom for years watching who went by on the capital road; and for all those years the man had sent what he saw the slow way, by post, at nine days a letter. Now it came in a quarter of an hour. Saban read it out at the table with his hat on his knee.

Two crown riders had come through Wayford on the capital road in the last week of September, going south. They had stopped at the posting house for the night. They had asked, in the taproom, over a good deal of ale, how far Kaldo's boats went, and how fast, and whether it was true that one of them went without sails and without horses on the bank. And three days after them, a third rider, on his own, in plain clothes on a crown horse, going north, had asked the posting house's keeper what the Grayrock fifth came to, and whether the keeper had any idea what was coming out of the Grayrock shaft that the shaft had never let out before.

Saban put the slip down.

"That's all," he said. "Riders ask things. It's what they're for." He turned his hat. "But it's the crown asking. Not a lord. Not a buyer. The crown. And it's asking about the boats and the fifth in the same week." He looked down the table at Emeric. "Somebody in the capital has started to add us up."

Nobody said anything.

Emeric looked down the long table at them. At Ferra with her book, and Imani with her list from Grayrock, and Bram and Ward and Armand in his chair at the end with his eyes shut. At Saban's slip of telegraph paper on the table, which had come nearly four hundred miles down a wire that had not existed in March. Four baronies. Grayrock's iron and Grayrock's coal and what was under Grayrock's east hill. Milford's grain. Wayford's road and Wayford's vats. Kaldo's works and Kaldo's lamps and Kaldo's school, and a long brown ship in a shed under the north wall with her legs drawn up and her doors shut and frost on her roof. No debt. Not a copper owed to anybody.

And the capital, asking.

"Then let it add," he said.

Ferra wrote it down. The date, and the words, and under them, after a moment, a line she did not let him see; and the first snow came in November, and the year turned, and the river froze at its edges and thawed again, and by the time anybody in Kaldo looked up from the work, the paper for the next year's lanterns was already drying in the loft above the mill.
