Chapter 151 - Out of Rooms

Bram looked at his wrist when he thought nobody was watching him.

He did it the way he looked into a furnace. He turned the band a little on the bone so the beads came round to the lamp, and he looked at the blue ones, the faceted ones between the gold barrels, and he looked at the light in them the way he looked at the colour of iron in a fire, to know how hot it was without asking it. Then he put his cuff back down over it and picked up his cup and looked at something else, a man who had not been looking at anything.

Emeric saw him do it three times before the table sat.

The bands were a week old. There were seven of them in the long room that morning, on seven wrists, and not one of the seven had said a word about them since the supper where they were given out. Ward wore his under the sleeve of his coat and did not touch it. Saban had put his next to the old chain of beads he had worn since the coast, so that anybody who looked at his wrist saw a merchant who liked beads. Imani wore hers on the left, because she washed her right hand forty times a day. Armand had turned his inward, so the gold was against the pulse, and Emeric had not asked why. Ferra wore hers the way she wore the key on the cord round her neck, as a thing that was there and did not need looking at.

And Bram looked at his, and pretended he did not.

Emeric understood it. A band was a new tool in a hand that had held the same tools for forty years, and Bram had never trusted a thing he could not see the inside of. The light in the beads was the only inside he had. He could not read the charge the way Emeric read it, as a figure, and Emeric had not yet found a way to give him one that did not need a door opened for him. So Bram read colour, because colour was what he knew.

It was midwinter. There was frost on the inside of the long room's windows at the corners, and the fire at the far end had been built up before light, and the table was the table, with its scars and its ink rings and Ferra's end of it squared off by her books.

Emeric had a case under his chair.

Ferra began, because it was her figure.

"I can't count them," she said.

She said it plainly and put her hand flat on the book in front of her, which was the census book, the long one with the grey boards, and she did not open it.

"I have three clerks," she said. "Hobb, and Wenna, and Dace. Since the notice went up in Wayford I have had them at the Western Gate in turns with a board and a book, taking names as they come over the bridge. They took six hundred and twelve names between the first frost and last night. That's heads. Not households. Hobb thinks it's half of what's come. Dace thinks less than half. Wenna won't guess, which is the right answer, and I'm inclined to believe Dace." She looked down the table. "They come over the bridge in the afternoon with a cart and a copy of the notice folded up in somebody's coat, and a cousin's name, or no name, and by the time the clerk looks up there's another cart. Half of them don't stop. They've been told they don't have to. It's printed."

"It is," said Emeric.

"Milford, mostly. Paxton's thirty-one owing, and their families, and the families of men who owed Paxton's neighbours. Grayrock, some. Bryda's men who were held on the old baron's books and never got off them. A few out of Wayford who didn't want to stay in Wayford after the square, and I don't blame them." She turned the book a quarter turn on the table. "I had four thousand two hundred and some at harvest. I'll tell you near five thousand now and I can't tell you nearer, and a figure I can't tell you nearer isn't a figure. It's a feeling in a coat."

Saban smiled at that, not very much.

"Where are they?" said Imani.

"Everywhere." Ferra did not look at her. "On the island, three households to a house. In the lofts over the market stalls. In the old boat sheds on the east bank. And outside, round the foundry, in anything with a roof and some things without. The foundry has no wall and never had. They've built against the slag heap because it's warm."

Imani wrote something down.

"Rents," said Ferra, "on the island. A room off the market that let for eight Copper the month at harvest lets for a Silver and six now. That's double, and it's double because a man who owns a room can see the carts coming over the bridge as well as I can. The people paying it are the people who came here to stop being held for rent." She let that sit for exactly as long as she meant to. "Nobody's broken a law. I've looked."

"What does it cost us," said Ward, "not knowing who they are."

Ferra looked at him and nodded, once, because it was the right question.

"It's three things," she said. "It's a tax problem, because a household I haven't got in the book pays nothing and draws on everything. It's a levy problem, because if you ask me tomorrow how many men between sixteen and fifty are sleeping in this barony, I will give you a number off the harvest census and it will be wrong by some hundreds. And it's a spy problem." She did not raise her voice for the last one. "If I were anybody who wanted a man inside Kaldo this winter, I would put him on a cart out of Milford with a copy of the notice in his coat. And I would not be able to tell you he was here. And neither would Saban."

Saban did not argue with that.

"I need more clerks," Ferra said, "and I need somewhere to put the people I count. The second one isn't mine."

It was the opening he had been waiting for, and he did not like how well it fitted.

Emeric reached under his chair and brought up the case, and opened it on the table, and took out the sheets.

There were not many. He had not let himself make many. He had drawn them on the good paper, the big sheets Jorrin cut for the pattern store, at night in the keep's east room over two months, with the door open and Ward's chair in the passage, and he had drawn them in pencil first and ink after, and there were still places on every one of them where the ink stopped and the pencil went on alone.

He laid the first one flat and weighted its corners with Ferra's inkwell and Bram's cup and two of the books.

It was a circle.

It was a circle drawn with a string and a pin, a big one, and inside it another circle, and at the centre a square with a round thing drawn inside the square. Lines ran out from the centre like the spokes of a wheel and met the rings. Outside the big circle, on the right of the sheet, there was a long line with ties drawn across it at intervals, which anybody at the table who had seen the Grayrock line knew was rail. At the top of the sheet a double line came in from off the edge of the paper, high and straight, and stopped at a small square on the outer ring.

He put the second sheet beside it, which was the same circle cut through from the side, so that you saw the wall standing up and the ground under it, and under the ground a great many lines that did not go anywhere you could see from above.

He did not put down the third.

"That's a city," said Bram.

"Yes."

"Where?"

"I haven't chosen." Emeric put his hands flat on the edge of the table, on either side of the sheets, not on them. "South. Near. Not on the island. On flat ground, with stone and a road and the water coming to it. There's more than one place it could go and I haven't walked all of them."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"This is what I've been drawing," he said. "Since before the ship came home from the west, in my head, and on paper for the last two months, because I didn't trust the head. I'm going to tell you two rules about it before anybody tells me anything, because the rules are the part I'm sure of and the drawing is not." He looked at the sheets. "The first is that nothing is built until the whole of it exists. On paper. Every street, every drain, every wall, every well. All of it, before anybody breaks ground. I will not have a city that's built in the order people ask for things. That's what the island is. A church and a keep and a wall, and then whatever fitted in the gaps, for longer than anybody's grandfather could remember."

"And the second," said Ferra.

"Nothing goes over ground until what goes under it is laid." He touched the edge of the second sheet, where the lines were. "Drains. Water. The mains. The street's cut, the pipe goes in, the street goes back, and then you build the house. Not the house and then somebody digs up the street in twenty years to find out where the house put its waste. I've seen that done. I've seen what it costs."

He had seen it done in another world, in a city nobody at the table would ever see, where they had dug the same street up four times in his memory for four different pipes. He did not say that. He never said where he had seen things.

"How big," said Saban.

"Big enough that the island fits in it three times over, with the fields between."

Saban looked at the circle for a long moment, and Emeric watched him price it, and watched him stop pricing it because there was nothing to price it against.

Ward was the first to say no, and he said it the way he said most things, without heat and without leaving any room round it.

"It's flat," he said.

"It is."

"The island is why Kaldo's alive." Ward did not look at the sheet. He looked at Emeric. "One crossing. Water on every side. Every plan I've made since the first year starts from that, and every plan anybody's ever made against us has had to start from it too. They came with four hundred men and five mages and they had to come over one bridge, and that's why you're sitting here." He put one finger on the table, not on the drawing. "A wall on flat ground is a wall anybody can walk round. You can build it as high as you like. They'll walk round it and sit down, and you'll be inside it with everybody you've got, eating what you brought."

"The wall's not the whole of it," said Emeric.

"The wall's never the whole of it. It's the part that's still there when the rest isn't." Ward sat back. "I'm not saying you're wrong about the room. Ferra's right about the room. I've been on the wall at night. I'm saying you're giving up the one thing we have that nobody can build, for a thing anybody can."

"I agree with him," said Declan.

Declan came to the table when the garrison was on it, and sat at Ward's end, and had not spoken yet. He said it without looking at anybody and without apology. Ward did not look at him either. They had been saying things to each other without looking for as long as either of them had worn the Crown's plate.

Emeric did not answer Ward. He did not have an answer that was better than Ward's argument, and he knew it, and there was no point pretending at a table where everyone could see him pretend.

"What does it cost," said Saban.

"I don't know yet."

"Roughly."

"More than we've got. More than the barony earns in ten years. More than we've borrowed." Emeric looked at him. "I can tell you what it costs when the drawing's finished. Not before. That's the first rule."

"The first rule's very convenient," said Saban, and he did not say it unkindly.

"What does it eat in iron," said Bram.

He had leaned forward over the side sheet and was looking at the lines under the ground, not at the wall at all. He had found the pipes before anybody else had found anything.

"If those are iron," he said, "that's more pipe than I've made in my life. That's more pipe than every smith in the south has made in his life. And I've got the converter and it makes steel in a morning and it's the best thing I've ever stood next to, and I still haven't rolled a sheet of plate off it, because I haven't got a stand that'll do it." He did not look up. "If they're iron, you'll want a works for nothing but pipe. And one for nothing but rail, to get the stone to it. I'm saying so now so nobody says later I didn't."

"Some of them are clay," said Emeric. "Fired. The big ones are stone."

"Some of them," said Bram.

Imani had not looked at the wall either.

"Where does its water come from," she said, "and where does it go."

"From the river, upstream. Higher than the city, so it comes down by its own weight and nobody has to pump it." He touched the double line at the top of the first sheet, the one that came in off the edge of the paper. "That's an aqueduct. A channel on arches, where the ground drops."

"And where does it go."

"Out below. Treated first."

"Treated how."

"Beds," he said. "Settling. Then land."

"Where."

He did not know where. He had drawn the outfall as an arrow on the second sheet, going off the edge of the paper the same way the aqueduct came on, and he had not drawn where the arrow went, because he had not chosen the ground.

"I don't know yet," he said.

Imani wrote it down. She did not say anything about it. She wrote it on the line under the last thing she had written, in the same hand, and Emeric had known her long enough to know that a thing Imani wrote down without comment was a thing she would come back to.

"Who pays," said Ferra.

"You'll tell me," he said. "When it's finished."

"I'll tell you now, then, so you've got it." She did not open the book. "Nobody pays. There's nobody to pay. The barony can pay for the drawing, and it can pay for the men who make the drawing, and it can pay for the first season of anything that doesn't need stone. After that it's the House, or it's the Crown, or it's the lane, and the lane's a boat with sulphur in it that comes up the coast when the weather lets it." She looked at him down the table. "I'm not saying no. I've got the veto and I'm not using it. I'm saying that on the day you put a spade in, you'll be building it on paper I haven't seen yet, from people who haven't lent it."

"I know."

"Good," said Ferra.

Armand said nothing.

He had not said anything since the sheets came out. He sat at the end of the table beside Imani with his hands folded on the wood and his thumbs moving very slightly against each other, and he looked at the circle, and at the second sheet where the wall stood up out of the ground, and back.

Emeric watched him and knew what he was doing, because he had watched Armand do it before, at a pit face in Grayrock and at the hull on the stands. He was counting.

He was counting the wall. Not in stone. In men. He was counting how many earth mages it would take to lift a wall of that length out of the ground, and how many days, and how many would come, and how many of those were still his. He was doing it with his thumbs, one against the other, a man near eighty telling off a figure the way a boy tells off his fingers, and he had not noticed he was doing it.

Emeric did not ask him. Not here.

Armand looked up, and saw him looking, and stopped his thumbs.

It went round the table once more and nobody moved.

Ward did not give ground, because Ward did not give ground to a drawing, and he was right not to. Declan did not give ground because Ward did not. Saban had stopped pricing and started thinking, which was worse. Bram was still looking at the pipes. Imani had her line written and was not going to say it again until she had something to put next to it. Ferra had said what the money was, as she always did, first, before anybody could start spending it.

Emeric could have ended it.

He knew exactly how. He could have said it was decided, and it would have been decided. Every one of them would have gone out of the long room and done their part of it, and done it well, because they were the best people he had ever worked with and they would do a thing well that they disagreed with. Ward would have built the city's wall better than any man living, and gone on believing it was a wall anybody could walk round, and been right.

He did not want a city Ward thought was wrong. He wanted Ward to want it. He had never in his life got anything worth having out of a man by telling him to have it.

"I'm not asking for a decision," he said. "I'm asking you to look at it. That's all this morning is."

He rolled the sheets, the two he had put down and the one he had not, and put them back in the case.

"That's not nothing," said Saban.

"No."

"A man shows you a city and says he's not asking for anything." Saban leaned back in his chair. "In my trade that's the most expensive thing a man can say."

"I know," said Emeric.

Ferra had taken her pen out while he rolled the sheets. She wrote a line, which Emeric could not read upside down from where he sat, and then she put the pen back in its case and looked up the table, not at him.

"Two months," she said to nobody in particular. "He's had a city in that case for two months. I've watched the case get heavier every week. I thought it was the Grayrock accounts." She squared the census book with the edge of the table. "I've been carrying the Grayrock accounts myself."

It got a laugh from Bram, a short one, and something from Imani that was nearly one.

Saban did not laugh. He looked at Ferra, down the table, with an expression Emeric had seen on him before and not placed, a look that was not quite amusement and not quite anything else, the look of a merchant who has watched a customer handle the same bolt of cloth three market days running and not buy it. Then he looked at Emeric, briefly. Then he looked at his own hands.

Ferra did not look at Saban at all. She had the census book open now at the page with no heading, and she was reading it.

The table rose.

Ward went out first, as he always did, to see that the passage was what it should be. Emeric heard him on the stair, and heard him stop at the turn and speak to somebody, and go on down. Armand went next, slowly, with Imani's hand not quite on his arm. Bram stayed long enough to finish his cup and look at his wrist once more, the fourth time, and then went.

Ferra stayed with her books until the room was empty except for the two of them and the clerk at the door, who was Hobb, waiting for her. Then she stood and gathered them.

"You'll need a surveyor," she said.

"I'll need three."

"I'll find them. Not this week." She put the census book under her arm. "Ward's right, you know."

"I know he is."

"That's not the same as him being right about the rest of it." She went to the door, and Hobb opened it for her, and she stopped in it and looked back at the empty table, at the place where the sheets had been. "Six hundred and twelve," she said. "Half. Less than half. I want the city. I want somewhere to put them. I'd just like to know who they are first."

She went out.

Ward walked the island wall that night.

He did it most nights. It was not a watch. The watch was kept by the garrison, from the towers and the gate, and he had written its hours himself years ago and did not interfere with it. It was a walk. He went up at the keep stair after the last bell and along the north run of the wall, over the lake side, with the ice at the edge of the water below him grey in the starlight, and round the east run over the Eastern Branch, and down the south, and along the west to the Western Gate, and across the gate's top, and back.

He did not look at the lake tonight. He looked in.

The island below him was a dark mass of roofs, the way it always was after the bell, with the church tower up out of it and the keep's black bulk behind him and the new tower over the market well, pale timber against the dark, with its sails still. And in the dark mass, lights.

He counted them.

He had counted them before, on other nights, without meaning to, the way a man who has kept watches counts anything that stays still long enough. He knew about how many lit windows the island showed after the last bell in winter. It was not very many. Lamp oil was dear and candles dearer, and people went to bed with the dark.

There were more tonight. Many more. There were windows lit in the lanes behind the market that he had never seen lit, and lofts over stalls, and a boat shed on the east bank with light coming out between its boards. And when he stopped on the south run above the tannery and looked straight down into the lane there, into one house, he could see through its window that there were people asleep on its floor in a row, and a woman sitting up among them with a rush light, mending something, and a man asleep sitting against the wall because there was no room for him to lie down.

Three families, he thought, in a house built for one.

He stood there for some time.

Below him in the lane, a child coughed, and the woman with the rush light put down her mending and bent over somewhere he could not see, and the light moved with her.

Ward went on along the wall. At the Western Gate he stopped again and looked out, over the bridge, at the dark road beyond it, where he could see the glow of fires round the foundry that had not been there at harvest either. Then he looked back in at the island, at the lanes, at the lights three and four to a house where there should have been one, and he stood on the wall that had kept Kaldo alive for longer than anybody could say and thought about what it was keeping in.

He did not change his mind. He was not a man who changed his mind on a wall at night.

But he walked the rest of it slower, and he counted every window, and when he came down at the keep stair he had a number, and he did not like it.
