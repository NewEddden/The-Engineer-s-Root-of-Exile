Chapter 236 - Double Casing

Ivo held the vial up to the window of his glass house and looked through it at the hill.

It was the size of Emeric's little finger, a little longer, a tube of thick clear glass closed at one end in a round and drawn out at the other into a neck as thin as a straw. Ivo had made a hundred of them in two days at the bench by the third cone, one after another, a gather and a pull and a turn and a snip, and they lay in a tray of sand at his elbow in rows like a box of new candles; and he had picked this one out of the hundred and held it up, and was looking through it, with his one good eye, at the light.

"You'll fill it," he said. "And then close the neck."

"Yes."

"With what?"

"Fire."

Ivo lowered the vial and looked at him. Everybody in Kaldo knew the duke's magic was fire; it was the one thing about him that had never been a secret. Ivo looked at Emeric's right hand, and back at the vial.

"Not here," he said. It was not a question.

"Not here."

"Where you go at night in the iron boat."

Emeric did not answer, and Ivo did not wait for one. He put the vial back in its row in the sand, exactly where it had been, and picked up the next.

"The neck's thin so the fire can close it quick," he said. "Thin glass closes in a breath and doesn't crack. Thick glass you'd be there a minute and it'd craze." He turned the second vial. "Don't let it get cold before you close it. Wherever that is."

"It'll be cold."

"Then be quick," said Ivo, and went back to his bench.



The reason it had to be done in the Band was a small one, and it had taken Emeric most of a night on the floor of the stone shed to see it.

The liquid in Bram's trap sat still because the trap was cold and the shed's air was only the shed's air. Seal a spoonful of it in a vial on the ground, at the lake's density, and it went on trying to be what it had been at four miles: a little of it stood up off the surface as a gas into the space above it, and pushed, and pushed harder as the vial warmed, in a space the size of a thimble, until the glass gave or the liquid was gone. He had watched it happen to the first two vials he had sealed in the shed, on the Thursday; the first had cracked in an hour with a sound like a knuckle, and the second had gone dull in two days, a little blue skin at the bottom and the rest of it gone through the glass at the neck where his seal was thinnest.

Sealed in the Band, the space above the liquid was the Band already. There was nothing for it to try to become. It sat.

So on the Saturday, the seventeenth, the Hod went up with a small copy of Bram's shed frame lashed in her hold beside the flasks, a cold stone in a cradle in a felt-wrapped tub and a short glass coil and a trap; and Armand sat three hours in his corner with the raw Band coming down his pipe, and Imani sat the mark with her card on her knee; and Emeric went down the trap at the first hour, in his mask and his fur, into the cold black hold, with Tobe after him and a lamp shuttered to a slit.

It was bitter down there. The racks lay asleep under the shut hatch on either side, and the flasks stood in their quilted waistcoats at the helm post's foot, and the iron round them was rimed white; and he coupled one of the flasks to the little frame and cracked its valve the quarter of a hair, which in that cold could only be done at all because the frame's coupling sat in its own felt with a walnut of fire stone against it, and waited.

It came faster up there. The stone had less to pull against in the cold of four miles than in a shed by the lake, and the flask's Band was the Band outside, and in half an hour there was a pool in the trap the size of a coin.

He filled the vials from it with a fine glass dropper Ivo had drawn him, one at a time, kneeling in the hold with the lamp's slit on his hands. A drop, two, three; a vial's worth, a little under the neck. Then he took the vial in his left glove and held its neck to the fingertips of his right, bare, and let the fire come.

Not much. A thread, a candle's worth, at the end of the first finger, where nobody but Tobe could see it and Tobe had seen worse. The thin neck went soft in the thread of flame in a breath and he drew it, and turned it, and it closed, and he took the fire away, and the vial lay in his glove, sealed, with a little pale blue pool in its round end glowing faintly in the dark of the hold.

He did eight. He could feel each of them when it was done, through the glove, small and hard and bright and very still: a little of the Band, in glass, on its own, that would sit.

Tobe held the tray. He did not say anything until the eighth, and then he said, through his mask, that a man could get used to most things, but he did not think he would ever get used to a duke lighting his finger like a match in the bottom of a lighter.

"You'll get used to it," said Emeric.

"I'll get used to not mentioning it," said Tobe. "That's not the same."



The canister was Bram's, and Bram made it on the bench at the end of the long shop where nobody went who did not have to, with the window shuttered and the door bolted.

Emeric had drawn it. A ring of eight vials standing upright between two round end caps, like the bars of a small round cage, the caps a hand across, iron, enamelled by Ivo inside and out in white with a band of blue round the rim, because Ivo said if a thing was going to be enamelled it might as well be enamelled properly. Three ports on the top cap, brass, with screw plugs: one to draw from, one to fill by, one for a gauge. On the bottom cap a coupling with two feed tubes, so that it could sit on a pipe like a lamp on a bracket and give to whatever was on the other end. And every vial in the ring held at both ends in a soft brass collar, so that a knock to the cage would not crack the glass.

"And when one goes?" Bram had said, looking at the drawing on the Monday.

"It won't go."

"Everything goes." Bram had put his finger on one of the eight. "Somebody drops it on the deck. Somebody puts a boot through the rack. A spar comes down in a blow. One of these goes, it's the stone shed on the Monday, wherever it is, with whoever's next to it." He tapped. "Case it."

"It's cased. The caps."

"The caps keep the cage together. They don't keep that in." Bram picked up a pencil and drew, round the vial under his finger, a second line close outside the first. "A sleeve. Every one. A vial inside a tube. If the vial goes, it goes into the tube, and the tube holds it, and nobody standing by the rack knows anything's happened till somebody looks."

So the sleeves were Bram's. He made them himself, on the Tuesday and the Wednesday, from glass tube Ivo drew him to his own measure, a little wider than the vials, so that a vial slid into a sleeve with a hair's room round it, and the sleeve was closed at its foot and gathered at its top round the vial's sealed neck and fused to it, one piece. It was fiddly work for a big man's hands. He did it at the bench with Ivo's little lamp and a pair of tongs with their jaws wrapped in leather, slowly, one at a time, the way he had done the needle valve for the shed, the way he did anything he did not trust anybody else to do.

Emeric sat with him through the Tuesday afternoon, because Bram had asked him to hold the lamp.

Bram did not talk. That was not unusual. He talked when there was something to say and the something was usually about iron; on a fiddly job he did not talk at all, and Emeric had learned in nine years to sit with him through it and hold whatever wanted holding and say nothing either. So he held the lamp. The shop was quiet but for the hiss of it and the small clink of the tongs. Through the shutters the afternoon went from white to yellow.

It was an hour, about, before Bram spoke. He had a sleeve in the tongs and was turning it in the flame, watching the glass at its top go soft round the vial's neck, very carefully, with his whole face; and he did not look up.

"Mind the fourth step," said Bram.

Emeric held the lamp.

It was the cellar stair. The fourth step down from the passage, the one with the loose stone in its tread that rocked under a man's heel. Bram had said it from under Armand's chair on the night of the oath, nearly five years ago, with one of the chair's poles on his shoulder going up out of the cellar: mind the fourth step, it's loose. It was still loose. Nobody had ever mended it. Ferra went down that stair at the second bell most nights, or up it, and stepped over the fourth, and he had watched her do it and thought nothing of it.

Bram had not been talking about the cellar stair.

Bram fused the sleeve to the neck, and took it out of the flame, and held it up to the shutter to look at the seam, and put it down in the sand tray with the others, and reached for the next.

That was all. He did not look at Emeric. He did not say anything else, then or for the rest of the afternoon, or at supper, or ever. Emeric sat with the lamp in his hand and his face doing he did not know what, and understood, slowly, the way a man understands a sum he has been carrying wrong for months, that Bram had known since the roses. Since before. Since some night when his daughter came up the fourth step a little after the second bell and he was there in the dark of the passage, or was not and did not need to be. And he had said nothing to her. And would not.

And had said it to Emeric, once, in four words, over a sleeve, so that Emeric would know he knew.

Emeric did not say anything either. He held the lamp until the light went, and in the evening he went up to the counting room, and Ferra was at her table with her book and looked up at him and asked him what was wrong with his face, and he said nothing was wrong with it, and she said it looked like he'd been told a joke at a funeral, and went back to her book.

He did not tell her. He found, sitting across from her with the lamp between them, that he did not want to. She was enjoying it too much. She had a secret from her father, the first thing in her whole life she had kept off the books, and she ran it like a ledger in her head, the times and the stairs and the creaking boards; and she thought she was winning.

He let her.



The first sleeve failed on the Wednesday, the twenty-first, at four miles, on deck.

They did it outside, on the open deck forward of the house, in their furs and their masks and their harnesses clipped to the line along the rail, because nobody was going to break a vial of the Band inside a caulked house to see what happened. Ned held her into the westerly from the helm with the door shut behind Emeric and Cade. The canister sat in a wooden cradle lashed to the hatch cover, with one of its eight vials marked in red; and Emeric had a brass rod with a hard steel tip that went in through the top cap's fill port and down inside the cage to the marked vial's shoulder, and a small hammer.

"Ready," he said through the mask.

Cade, at the rail, nodded. He had volunteered. He always volunteered.

Emeric tapped the rod.

The vial cracked. He heard it, faint, through the cap and the cage: a small dry tick. And the sleeve round it, which was as thin as the vial was, cracked with it, at the same instant, from the same tick, as one pane cracks the pane against it; and the little pool of pale blue in the vial's round end met the air of four miles through two broken shells at once, and went up.

It went up the way the trap had gone in the shed, all at once, but here there was no shed to fill. There was the open sky, and the wind, and the deck; and for a breath the deck round the canister was thicker than the Band itself, much thicker, a knot of four miles of sky squeezed into a spoon and let go all at once at the Band's own foot. Emeric felt it hit him like a hot wind off a furnace door. Cade, at the rail, made a sound inside his mask and put a hand to it. The probe on the pole above the cupola, which had been as bright as a lamp, went past bright into something Emeric had never seen it do, a hard white, and the lift stones under the deck drank so hard all at once that the Hod rose a foot under them like a boat on a swell.

Then the wind took it. It was gone in a breath, east, toward the sea, a knot of thickness tumbling away in the dark; and the deck was only the Band again, and the probe came down to a lamp, and the Hod settled.

Cade had his mask off and his glove at his nose. It came away red.

"Down," said Emeric. "Now."

Ned had her going before he finished the word. Inside the house, he heard afterward, the drawer's needle had gone round the dial in one jump as the knot went past the crown, and Imani had shut the bypass before anybody asked her to; and in the corner Armand had opened his eyes and said, quite calmly, that something had just walked past the window that was not the Band, and asked what it was. They went down through the Band's foot and below it, an hour early, and nobody minded.

Cade's nose had stopped by two miles. He sat on the bench with his head forward and Imani's cloth pinched to it and looked at the cracked canister in its cradle, which Emeric had brought in through the door when the deck cleared, and which lay on the floor of the house with its red-marked vial and its sleeve in pieces inside the cage like a broken egg in a cup.

"It went through both," said Cade. "Like they were one."

"They were as thin as each other," said Emeric. "One goes, it takes the other with it. It's not a sleeve if it breaks when the thing inside it breaks. It's a second vial."

"Bram'll want to know," said Ned, from the helm, without turning round.

"Bram will want to know," said Emeric, and found that he was not looking forward to it at all.



Bram wanted to know. He wanted to know standing at his bench on the Thursday morning with the cracked canister in front of him on a cloth and the pieces of his sleeve laid out beside it in the order they had come out, and he went over every one with his spectacles on and his lens, and said nothing for a quarter of an hour.

Then he put the lens down.

"Thin," he said.

"As thin as the vial."

"As thin as the vial." Bram looked at the pieces. "I made it as thin as the vial because I wanted the vial to slide in with a hair's room and I wanted it light, and I was thinking of a sleeve on a man's arm, that's thinner than the arm." He picked up a curved piece of it between finger and thumb. "That's wrong. A sleeve's stronger than what it holds or it's not a sleeve. A boiler's lagging is thinner than the boiler. The boiler's shell is thicker than the water." He put the piece down. "Stronger than what it holds."

He made eight more on the Thursday and the Friday. Ivo drew him tube twice as thick in the wall as the vial's, and Bram fitted them as he had fitted the first, slowly, in the leather-jawed tongs, and they were heavier in the hand, and the canister weighed half again what it had.

"Saturday," he said, on the Friday evening, with the eighth in the sand.

"Saturday. Armand's night."

"Then I'll come."

Emeric looked up.

"I'll come," said Bram. He did not look up from the sand tray. "It's my sleeve. I'll break it myself." He took his spectacles off. "I've built the half of everything that's gone up there. The tank under the floor in the ship. The flasks in this one. The case on the wall and the drawers in it. I've never been to the Band." He put the spectacles in their case. "I'll go once and see what I've been building for."

"You'll need a chute."

"Then tell Abloh," said Bram. "He'll want to fit it. He'll want to tell me I've a back on me."



Ferra came down to the reeds on the Saturday with the book and the lamp, as she always came, and stopped at the top of the bank when she saw her father at the foot of the plank in a fur that was not his and a chute Abloh had let out at the shoulders for him that afternoon, standing very square and looking at the Hod as if she owed him money.

"You're not going up," she said.

"I am," said Bram.

"You've never been up to the Band."

"That's why I'm going." He looked up the bank at her. "It's a sleeve. Mine. I'll not have Cade break it for me twice."

Ferra stood at the top of the bank with the lamp in one hand and the book under her arm, and Emeric, at the foot of the plank, watched her face do four things one after another, very fast, and settle on none of them.

"Ninety, four, six minutes," she said to her father.

Bram looked at her.

"Ned makes everybody say it," said Ferra. "Before they step on her. Say it."

"Ninety," said Bram. "Four. Six minutes." He put his foot on the plank. "And chutes below a mile and stay with her above it, and masks at the door, and nobody goes in the hold without his mask, and the healer turns the brass and nobody else does." He went up the plank. "I've read the book, girl. I built half the things it's about."

Ferra did not say anything. She came down the bank and stood beside Ward with the lamp and the book, and when the Hod went up she watched it go until the dark had it; and then she opened her own book at the back, under A. ALOFT and THE HOD and HOURS AT THE MARK, and wrote something, and Ward, beside her, did not look to see what.



Bram sat on the bench in the house with his hands on his knees all the way up and said nothing. At a mile he looked at the probe on the wall. At three he looked at the frost in the corners of the panes. At four, when the probe on the pole outside went bright and Ned said "Hm" and the house stayed at the mark, he looked at the brass case on the wall under the crown, at his drawers and his lever and his gauges, and watched the top drawer's needle begin to move.

"It's working," he said.

"It's working."

"I'd not seen it working." Bram watched the needle. "I'd seen it on the bench." He was quiet a while. Then he said: "It's warm."

Emeric looked at him.

"In here." Bram moved one hand, a small movement, at the air of the house. "Not the floor. The tank under the floor's warm, I made that. This." He frowned at nothing. "Like a forge in the morning, before the fire. When the bricks are still holding last night."

"That's the mark," said Imani, from the bench opposite, with her card on her knee.

"That's the Band," said Emeric. "A little of it. Let in on purpose."

Bram said nothing for a while. Then he looked at his own hands on his knees, the big black-seamed hands of forty years at an anvil, and opened them, and closed them, and opened them again, slowly, as a man does in the morning to find out how bad they are.

"Hm," said Bram, exactly as Ned said it, and did not explain.

At the first hour he went out on deck with Emeric, in his mask, clipped to the line, and knelt by the canister in its cradle on the hatch cover, with the new red-marked vial in its new thick sleeve; and he took the brass rod out of Emeric's hand without asking, and put it through the fill port, and found the vial's shoulder by feel through the cage, his eyes shut behind the mask's glass, as Emeric had once watched him find a crack in a casting with his fingertips in the dark.

He tapped it.

The vial cracked. Emeric heard the small dry tick through the cap. And nothing else happened.

Nothing went up. Nothing hit them. The deck stayed the Band and the probe stayed a lamp, and the Hod sat in the dark with Ned's hand on her air as if she had been set down on a table. Inside the cage, inside the thick sleeve, the little pool of pale blue in the broken vial had gone up, as it had to, meeting the sleeve's air; and the sleeve held it. Emeric could feel it there: a small hot knot of thickness inside the glass, pressing, turning, finding nowhere to go, and staying. Warm. Held.

Bram knelt by the cradle with his gloved hand flat on the top cap.

"It's in there," he said, through the mask.

"It's in there."

"Pushing."

"Pushing. And held."

Bram took his hand off the cap. He stayed kneeling a breath longer, looking at the canister in its cradle in the dark, four miles up, with the stars very hard and very many over the shut cupola behind them and the cloud lying silver and flat below; and Emeric saw him turn his head, once, and look out over the rail at all of it, the dark and the cloud and the Band he could not see, and the curve of the world's edge where the cloud met the sky, which was faintly, very faintly, curved.

He looked at it for as long as it took to breathe twice. Then he got up, on his own, with one hand on the rail, and unclipped, and went back in through the door to the house, and sat down on the bench with his hands on his knees.

"Stronger than what it holds," he said, to Imani, who had not asked.



The canisters went into iron.

That was settled on the Sunday in the counting room, and it took five minutes. Liquid mana glowed. Faintly; you could read by it only if you held the vial to the page. But a mage looking through a canister's glass from across a hold or a cellar would see a faint blue where no faint blue should be, and know at once there was something in it, and want to know what; and every mage in the realm could feel a charged stone a few paces off, and none could feel anything behind iron. So the canisters rode in iron racks, closed, with a slot in the front of each the width of a finger so that a man could read the level and nothing else, and the racks went into the Hod's hold behind the flasks, and into the cellar.

"And the latch," said Ferra.

She said it to her book, without looking up. She had been waiting for it all through the racks.

The ship's latch held a drogue, and its bellows was full while her stones worked, and when her stones stopped, in a dead zone, the bellows went slack in three counts and the drogue went out and she fell slowly under it into whatever was below. That was the latch as it had been since the first voyage. In the plans in his head it had always had a second half: a tank of the stuff in the canisters, behind a seal that the same slack bellows would break, so that when the stones stopped the tank let go and filled the ship's own air with the Band, and the stones woke inside it, in a dead zone, without waiting for the zone to clear.

He had drawn it on the Saturday night, after the Hod came down, with Bram's sleeve still in his head. It lay in front of him on the counting room table now: a tank, glass, in an iron jacket with a sight slit; a thin glass disc across its throat; a spring and a spike behind the disc, held off by the latch; and a short horn below the disc for the stuff to leave by.

"When does it go in?" said Ferra.

"Not yet."

"It's drawn."

"It's drawn." He looked at it. "I'll not put a sealed bottle of that aboard a ship that people sleep in until I've watched one break on purpose, in a room, and seen the stones come back. Not on paper. In front of me."

Ferra wrote something.

"Where?" she said.

"The cellar. When there's a room big enough to do it safely."

She looked up at him then, and he knew she was thinking of a page at the back of her book with a heading on it and a date and nothing under it; and she did not say the heading aloud, and neither did he.

"Then it's drawn," said Ferra, and wrote that, and closed the book. "And in a drawer."

It went in a drawer.



Arthur passed Ward's test on the Monday, on the first day of it, and said nothing about it at supper.

It was the suit test. Ward had made it for Osric and Declan and Rylan five years ago and changed it every year since, and it had three parts and no mercy in any of them. The falls first: off the keep's inner wall into the canal ring, with Bram's suction pads on the palms and the knees, from the top of the wall to the water, catching on every third course on the way down, which nobody but Rylan had ever done cleanly on the first morning. Then the glide, in a harness on the long line from the keep's west tower to the far side of the ring, where a man in a suit had to hold himself level on the line with his own arms and his own nerve for the whole length of it and not swing. Then the drill Ward called the dead crystal, which was being dropped from the tower's foot on a short rope with the suit's crystal pulled out of its slot by Rylan halfway down, and landing anyway.

The suit was a spare. Ward had taken it out of its press on the Sunday night without asking anybody, one of the two that had been built for knights still to come, and said, when Rylan raised his eyebrows, that a suit that sat in a press learned nothing.

Arthur did the falls on the first morning, cleanly, every third course, and came up out of the canal ring dripping with his hands still stuck to the pads, and stood on the edge in the cold with the water running off him and waited for Ward to say something, and Ward did not. He did the glide at noon and did not swing. He did the dead crystal in the afternoon, and when Rylan pulled the crystal at the halfway he dropped like a stone for the length of a man and then caught, on his own legs, on the cobbles, knees bent, the way he had been taught, and stood up.

Ward wrote something in his book. He did not say anything to Arthur. He did not need to.

Emeric saw it all from the stair, where he always stood.

At supper in the long room Arthur sat at the class's end of the table with Mira on one side and Bren on the other and ate his soup and said nothing about the suit at all. Bren asked him how it was. Arthur said it was wet. Mira asked him what the dead crystal felt like. Arthur said it felt like falling, and went on eating.

Sela heard it from Rylan, at the school door, on the Tuesday morning. Rylan told Nessa afterward, and Nessa told Imani, and Imani told Emeric on the keep leads, because Imani told him things about Sela when she judged he had earned them, that Sela had heard it all the way through without a word, with her hand on the door, and then said: "He didn't tell me." And Rylan had said: "No. He wouldn't. He'd want you to hear it from somebody who'd seen it." And Sela had gone into the school and taught the morning, and at noon she had gone to the yard and stood at the gate and watched her son on the rail with the class for a quarter of an hour, and gone back to the school without going in.



The first lane was finished on the Wednesday, the twenty-eighth, and lit on the Thursday, and Bram's house was at the end of it.

It ran from behind the old corn exchange downhill to the forge, the old forge where Bram had worked before there was a works, and his house stood behind the forge where it always had, close enough that the kitchen wall was warm on the side it shared with the fire. The lane had been dug once from end to end in July and everything put into the trench before it closed, and in October the concrete had gone over it, and the lamp posts stood along it in the wet, green iron with their white globes; and on every house a wire ran in from the post to the eaves on two white knobs, and in through a hole bored in the wall. Tess's men had done it, house by house, a week of it. On the corner by the corn exchange, on a post of its own, an iron box stood with a door in it and a handset inside on a hook, the first call box in the lower ward; Tess had put it there on the Tuesday and two boys had already been caught ringing the exchange to ask the operator what her name was.

The wire ran along the beam over Bram's table on its little white knobs of porcelain, two strands side by side and never touching. It went down the wall by the door in a groove Tess's men had cut and plastered over, and came to a brass plate with a black knob on it; and from the beam over the table a cord hung down to a glass bulb no bigger than a pear.

Bram stood in the doorway with his arms folded and looked at the holes.

"You'll have the wall full of them," he said.

"Two," said Emeric.

"Two today."

By the door, at the height of a man's chest, a black box with a little window in it sat on two screws. Ottoline had put it there herself that morning, with her magnifying glass on its cord and her lips moving, and checked it against something in a case before she let the men close it. Behind the window a thin copper disc stood on its edge, still.

Ferra came in at a quarter past six with her book under her arm, as she came in most evenings to eat with her father, and stopped on the step.

She looked at the wire on the beam. She looked at the box by the door. She did not ask what any of it was, because she had paid for it, line by line, and she knew.

"Well," said Bram. "Go on, then."

Emeric had the switch under his hand. He took his hand off it and stepped back from the wall.

Ferra looked at him. Then she put her book down on the table, very square to its edge, and went to the wall and turned the black knob a quarter round, and it went over with a small hard click.

The bulb lit.

It did not hiss, as the wall's arc lamps hissed, or burn white, or throw the long hard shadows the arcs had thrown over the town since the second winter. It was small and steady and a little yellow, and it filled the kitchen to its corners and no farther, and it made no sound at all.

Emeric did not look at it. He had seen bulbs; he had seen ten thousand of them, in another life, and had never once looked at one. He looked at Ferra, standing under it with her hand still on the switch and the light on her hair, all of it, the red of it gone to copper under the yellow, the one loose strand that was always loose at her temple lit at the edge like a wire in a lamp; and her face perfectly still, the way it went still over a figure she had not expected to come out right. She had a smudge of ink on her jaw. She did not know. He would not have told her for the world.

She was not looking at the bulb either. She was looking at the meter. The little disc behind its window had begun to turn, slowly, edge on, catching the light once each time round.

"It's counting," she said.

"Yes."

"Every turn."

"Every turn."

She watched it go round three times. Then, without turning her head, she said: "You're looking at me."

"Yes."

"Look at the bulb. You paid for it."

"You paid for it."

"I paid for it," agreed Ferra, and her ears went pink, and she went on watching the meter, and did not move out from under the light.

Bram came in off the step at last and sat down in the mended chair by the hearth, the one with the new leg that did not match, and looked up at the bulb with his spectacles pushed up on his forehead. Then he looked at his daughter under it. Then, for a breath, at Emeric, standing by the wall not looking at the bulb; and Emeric saw him see it, and saw him look back at the bulb.

"What's it cost?" said Bram.

"Less than the candle," said Ferra, without turning round. "I've done the sum."

"Less than the candle by the week, or the hour?"

"Both."

Bram grunted. He looked at the bulb a while longer. Then he reached up, without getting out of the chair, and turned the knob back with one black finger, and the kitchen went dark but for the fire; and turned it on again; and off; and on, and left it on.

"Doesn't flicker," he said.



They switched the river street on at dusk on the last day of the month.

The cable had gone in along the line of the old steam trench, which Linus had drawn on the town map in red with the cable a hand's width beside it in blue, so that nobody would dig anywhere else; a cable as thick as a child's wrist, a skin of lead over oiled paper over copper, laid on sand with red tiles over it so that the next man with a pick would hit the tile and stop. The lamps stood along the street on black iron posts, one every forty yards, from the bridge foot up the hill to the hospital steps.

Emeric was on the bridge. He saw it go along the street from the far end, lamp by lamp, faster than anybody could have walked it: white globes coming on in their order up the hill until the whole street lay lit under them in the drizzle.

The lamplighter was halfway along with his pole and his ladder. He had been doing the river street every evening since before Emeric came to Kaldo; Emeric had last noticed him a year ago from the ward's step, coming along the street below with his pole and his ladder while Imani stood beside him in her ward dress and shawl.

He stood under the third lamp with the ladder on his shoulder and looked up at it. Then he looked along the street at all the others, lit, and nobody at any of them.

Emeric walked down off the bridge to him. He did not know what he was going to say, and when he got there he said the plain thing.

"There's a switch in the works. They come on together."

"I saw." The lamplighter did not take his eyes off the lamp. "And they go off together?"

"Yes."

"And when one doesn't come on?"

"Somebody has to go and see why."

"Somebody has to know it's out first," said the lamplighter. He shifted the ladder on his shoulder. "From the bridge you'll not see it. You'll see a dark bit and not know which. I know every one of these." He nodded along the street. "That one by the chandler's leans. The one at the hospital steps has a crack in the glass, I've told them twice. And that one." He pointed with his chin. "Fourth from the top. Look at it."

Emeric looked. It was a little dimmer than the others, and a little yellower.

"That's not right," said the lamplighter.

Emeric looked at it a while longer. Then at the man with the ladder, who was still looking at it, with the face of somebody who has found the one thing wrong in a street full of things that are right and cannot stop looking at it until it is mended.

"No," he said. "It isn't." He turned up the street. "Show me the one with the crack."

The lamplighter looked at him sideways. Then he took the ladder off his shoulder, and carried it, and walked up the hill with the duke beside him in the drizzle under the lamps, pointing at each one as they passed it and saying what was wrong with it, which in most cases was nothing; and at the hospital steps he leaned the ladder on the post with the cracked globe and went up it, out of habit, and stopped at the top with nothing to light, and looked down.

"I'll want a wage for this," he said.

"You'll have one," said Emeric. "Every night. The whole street, and the next one when it's lit. Walking it. Telling Tess what's out."

The lamplighter came down the ladder.

"Then I'll want a better ladder," he said.
