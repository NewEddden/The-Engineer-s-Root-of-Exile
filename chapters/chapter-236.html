Chapter 236 - The Outer City

In the morning, which was only a word down there, they went to look at where they were.

They kept the ship's hours. That had been the plan and it was the right one: the surface slept by day in the band and worked by night, and the line only talked at night, so the five under the ridge slept when the line was still and worked when it could hear them. But the dark did not know what hour it was, and neither, after the first sleep, did any of them. Emeric woke with no idea whether he had slept an hour or ten, and lay on his cot under the tall stair and looked up at it, and it was exactly as dark as it had been. Ferra was already up. She was sitting on the step at the foot of the stair with a lamp stone beside her and the sand-glass beside that, and she had turned the glass every time it ran since they came up out of the water, and made a stroke in the book for each, so that she could tell him, when he asked, that it was a little after sundown on the island, and that it had been a hot one.

"How do you know it was hot?"

"It's always hot on the island." She closed the book. "I'm guessing. I wrote guessing."

It was a city. By the second hour none of them doubted that, and by the fourth none of them could have said how big it was.

The hall at the top of the steps was a street. It went away from the water in a long straight line into the rock, wider than any street in Kaldo, with its roof a long way up, three or four times the height of a man, arched, smooth. Along both its sides there were doorways, and every doorway was the wrong height. That was the thing the eye went to first and came back to, every time, however long you had been looking: not the size of the street, not the dark, but the doors. Each one was tall. Taller than a door should be by half again, and narrow for its height, so that it was not a big door for a big man but a door for a tall thin one, a man the height of a man standing on another man's shoulders, and slender. And the lintels over them were cut with the same care as everything else, clean, square, with a line of the signs running along each one at about the height a tall thin man's eyes would come to, and a little above.

Zale stood under the first one with his lamp held up and read along the lintel, moving his lips, the way he had read the margins in the small room at Kaldo: slowly, like a child of six. Not the signs; he could not read the signs, nobody could. He was reading them the way Ansel Vey had read them, as shapes, and matching them.

"That one," he said, and put his finger on it. "That's in the book. The first page. And that one. And that." He looked round at his brother. "It's the same. It's the same hand. Not the same man's hand. The same way of cutting." He was quiet. "He copied these off a headland on the other side of the world, in the fog. And they're here."

They went into the rooms behind the doors. There were a great many rooms. They were empty. Not stripped; empty, swept, as a house is empty when its people have moved out of it on purpose and taken what they meant to take and left it clean behind them. There were stone benches along the walls of some of them, cut out of the rock, and they were the wrong height too; Sir Gavin sat on one and his feet did not reach the floor, and he sat there with his boots swinging like a boy on a wall and did not laugh. There were shelves cut in the walls, empty. There were niches, at a tall man's shoulder, with a smoke mark above each where a lamp had burned once, a long time ago, for a long time. There were channels cut in the floors of some of the rooms that ran to holes in the walls, and Emeric put his hand in one of the holes and felt the air move, very faintly, cool, from somewhere else: the rock breathed. Someone had made it breathe.

And there were stairs. Everywhere, going up and down between the levels, off the street, out of the rooms, along the walls of the halls to galleries; and every step was the height of a man's knee, and a little more.

They put hand lines on all of them. That was the day's first work and most of its sweat. Sir Aled and Sir Gavin went ahead up every stair with the rope, and drove a peg at the top and a peg at the bottom, and strung the line between, so that a man going up used his arms as much as his legs, and a man coming down had something to hold, because a fall down a stair like that was a fall down a cliff. On the third stair the steps had gone; a stretch of the flight, a dozen steps, was simply not there, as if a great bite had been taken out of the wall, and the gallery it had led to hung over their heads with its lip thirty feet up and nothing under it but smooth rock.

Emeric looked at it for a while. Then he unclipped the harpoon from his thigh.

"Stand back," he said. "Behind me. All of you. Hold your lamps."

They stood back. He put it to his shoulder and aimed at the gallery's lip, at a crack he could feel in the stone there better than he could see it, and told the stone in the breech to go.

It went with the sound of a door slammed in the next room, very loud in the stillness, and the echo of it came back to them off the far walls of the hall a long time after, and kept coming, fainter and fainter, off walls further and further away, until it was a whisper, as if the city were passing it from room to room. The spear went up with its line whipping out behind it off the reel and struck the crack and held. And every lamp in the hall went out.

For a breath. Every light stone, his at his chest and his wrist and his belt, Ferra's, the knights', Zale's; the whole of the dark came in at once, total, the dark of a place that had never seen the sun; and in it nobody moved, because they had been told not to, and they had drilled it. And then the lights came back, one after another, a little dim and then bright, as the medium came back into the hole the stone had torn in it, and Sir Gavin let his breath out in a long whistle that went away down the hall like the echo had.

"That's the cost," said Emeric. He handed the gun to Sir Aled and went up the line hand over hand to the gallery and made it fast.

It was on the gallery, looking down at the street from thirty feet up, that he said the wrong thing.

He had not meant to say anything. He had been thinking aloud, which he did not do often and should have done less. He was standing at the rail of the gallery, which was a rail cut out of the solid rock and came to his chest, with his lamp held out over the dark street below, and the tall narrow doors going away along it on both sides as far as the light went, and he said, to nobody:

"Whoever they were. A people who lived underground. Primitive, maybe. Or another kind of."

"Primitive?" said Ferra.

She was beside him at the rail. She did not raise her voice; she never raised her voice. She held her lamp down over the stair they had just come up, the one with the bite out of it, where the steps that were left went down in the lamplight, and she put her other hand on the edge of the nearest step, the top one, where it met the gallery floor.

"Look at the stair cuts," she said.

He looked. He had been looking at them all day. He looked again, the way she was looking, the way she looked at a column of figures, and saw what she saw. Every step on that stair was the same height as every other step on it. Not near enough. The same. He had climbed thirty of them and not noticed, because they were the same, and a man does not notice a thing that is the same. Every tread the same depth. Every riser the same height, to the width of a hair, as far as the eye could tell, all the way down the flight into the dark; and every edge square, and every corner clean, and the whole flight running down the wall at one slope, straight as a rule, without a waver in it anywhere, cut out of the living rock.

Bram's masons could not have done it. Not in stone. Not in a year, with the best tools Kaldo had. He knew that as surely as he knew anything.

"Primitive," Ferra said again. Not unkindly. As a figure she was checking. She took her hand off the step and wrote something in her book. "I'd want to see the tools."

He did not answer that. There was no answer. He had said a foolish thing out of a lifetime of stories where the people under the ground were always smaller than the people on it, and she had put her hand on a stair and made it foolish, and the word stood there in the dark between them, and he let it stand, because it was his, and because in a quarter of an hour it did not matter at all.

The great hall was at the end of the street.

They did not know it was there until they were in it. The street ended, or seemed to; the lamps showed the tall doors on either side stopping, and then nothing ahead, and the floor going on, and the walls on either side going away, out, out of the light, until there were no walls. Just the floor, smooth, and the dark, and the sound of their boots coming back to them very late and very faint off something a long way off.

"Lamps," said Emeric. "One at a time. Round the edge. Wall first."

They had brought a dozen extra lamp stones for this, in iron cups on short spikes, for setting on a floor. Sir Aled and Sir Gavin went out into the dark with them, one to the left, one to the right, each with a hand line paid out behind him from the street's end so that neither could lose his way back across a floor that had no landmarks on it at all, and walked until they found the wall, and set a lamp at its foot, and went on along it, and set another.

The wall came up out of the dark a piece at a time.

At first it was only colour. A patch of dull red where the first lamp stood. A patch of green, far off to the right, a long way from it. Blue, a deep blue, faded, at the second lamp on the left. Then the lamps went on round, and the patches of colour met, and ran into each other, and became shapes, and the shapes became figures, and the figures went up the wall out of the lamps' reach into the dark overhead, and they stood in the middle of the floor and turned slowly round, the five of them, with their own lamps held up, and did not say anything at all.

The hall was round. The wall went all the way round it, and all of it was painted, from a man's height above the floor to as high as the lamps would reach, and higher, into the dark. It was plaster laid on the stone and painted, in colours that had faded to the colours of old cloth, dull reds and greens and ochres and a blue that must once have been the blue of a summer noon; and in places the plaster had gone, and there was bare stone; and in places it was so faint that it was a stain and nothing more. But most of it was there.

In the middle of it, on the wall opposite the street's mouth, facing the way they had come in, there was a tree.

It was huge. It went up the wall from the floor into the dark, the trunk as wide as a house, and the branches spread out from it on both sides along the curve of the wall until they reached halfway round the hall in each direction, and the whole of the rest of the painting hung from them, or grew round them, or stood under them. Its trunk was painted grey, a pale grey, and over the grey, very fine, done with the point of something, there was a net of crossing lines. Emeric knew that net. He had seen it on the broken face of a stone in his mother's comb-case and in the stem of a thing growing in a glass bowl in the middle of his house. In the branches there were pods. Long pods, a great many of them, and he went close to the wall under the lowest branch and held his lamp up to one, and counted the bumps along it, the way he had counted them on the drawing in the book, and on the stone in the dealer's tray. Eight. There were eight. Under the tree, on the ground at its foot, painted as small as they would be at that scale, there were husks: split, empty, the two halves of each lying cupped and open, holding nothing.

They had got it right. Everything he could check, they had got right. Whoever had painted this had stood in front of that tree.

Round the tree were the people who tended it.

He would think afterwards that he should have seen them first, and that he had not because he had been looking at the pods, as a man looks at a figure he can check before he looks at one he cannot. They were everywhere. In the branches, on the ground, at the roots, going away along the wall in both directions into the seasons that the branches ran through: for the wall ran through the year, he saw that later, budding on the left and full in the middle and bare on the right, and the people went through the year with it.

The tall ones first. They were most of them. Tall, slender, long in the arm and the neck, drawn in a few graceful lines as if the painter had liked drawing them, standing among the branches and on the ground, reaching up into the pods, carrying baskets, talking to each other with their hands. Their heads were narrow, and their ears were drawn long, and came to a point, and lay back a little along the skull. And their heads, when he looked at them against the scale of the tree, and the scale of a doorway painted in the wall's lower corner, sat exactly where the lintels of the tall narrow doors said a head should sit. These were the ones the city had been cut for. He knew it the way he knew the steps were even. There was no arguing with a door.

At the roots, the short ones. Broad. Heavy in the shoulder, heavy in the arm, with short thick legs set wide, and big square hands; and every one of them had a tool in those hands. Mattocks. Wedges. A hammer with a head like a fist. Something long and flat with a notched end that he did not know. They were working at the tree's foot, round the great roots, in the earth, and some of them were painted half into it, as if the ground were a cut-away and they were under it, in tunnels, among the roots. And one of them, near the middle, near the trunk, was standing with his back half turned, looking over his shoulder at something, with a hammer resting on one shoulder and the other hand on his hip; and the set of those shoulders, and the way he stood, square on his feet, the whole weight of him down through his heels, was Bram. It was Bram at the bench at the end of a day, with the hammer on his shoulder, turning to see who had come into the shop.

Emeric stood very still in front of him.

Above the tree, over the crown, over the pods, in the dark where the lamps barely reached, something was curled. He had to walk back across the floor to see it whole, and even then it went up out of the light. A beast. Long. Winged; the wings folded along its back like a bat's, or like the cape-wings of the suits folded along the frame, great ribbed things of a dull green that had once been bright. A long neck. A long tail that went round the top of the tree's crown and came down the far side of it. A head, turned down, looking at the tree and the people under it, with its eyes half shut. It was drawn to the same scale as the tree. It was nearly as big as the tree. And it was not doing anything. It was not attacking anybody. There were tall ones in the branches right under it, reaching up for pods, and one of them had its hand on the beast's foreleg, lightly, the way you put your hand on a horse's shoulder going past it in a stable. It was among them. That was all. It lay round the top of the tree the way a cat lies round the top of a warm stove.

And others. Many others.

They went round the wall in both directions and he could not take them all in, and did not try to, then. A kind of figure with a body like a man's and a head like nothing; a kind that went on four legs and carried things on its back, and was not a beast, because it was talking to one of the tall ones with its hands; a kind that was small, very small, a hand high on the wall, in crowds, like mice. Things he had no word for. Things he had words for from nowhere and would not use. And in the places where the plaster was worst, where the damp had got at it, figures that were nearly gone, that could have been anything: a shape that might have been a body or might have been a cloak hung on a peg; a head that might have been horned or might have been crowned; a pair of what might have been arms, or might have been wings, or might have been the branches of something else, growing.

And gaps.

He saw them last. Ferra saw them first.

She was standing at the wall on the left side of the tree, in the spring of it, where the branches were budding, with her lamp held close to the plaster and her book open against the wall. She had been there for some time. She said, "Here," not loudly, and he went to her.

There was a place in the painting where the plaster had gone. It was the size of a man, a little less, and it was bare stone, grey, smooth, the stone of the wall under the painting. Round its edges the painting went on as if nothing had happened: a branch, the pale legs of one of the tall ones standing beside it, a basket on the ground. And at the edge of the bare place, at its bottom corner, where the plaster had not quite gone, there was a heel. A foot's heel, painted, pale, with the line of an ankle going up from it into the bare stone and stopping.

It was not a tall one's heel. It was too short in the ankle, and too broad, for the tall ones; and too narrow, and too long, for the short ones at the roots.

Ferra moved her lamp. Along the wall, a little further, another bare place. At its edge, the fingers of a hand, reaching in from the bare stone toward a pod, painted pale; four fingers and a thumb, the thumb set where a thumb is set, the fingers the length that fingers are. And further, another, and at its edge the line of a shoulder going up to where the neck would be, and the neck was bare stone.

She went along the wall with her lamp, all the way round the spring and into the summer, and he went with her, and she found eleven of them. Eleven places where a figure had stood, a little less than the size of a man, where the plaster was gone to the stone; and at the edges of four of them a hand, a heel, a shoulder, a hip, left behind. And the proportions of every one of them were the proportions of the people standing in front of the wall looking at them.

"Damp," Emeric said, after a long time. His voice sounded strange to him in the big dark room. "It's the damp. It's got in behind the plaster. Look." He pointed. There were stains all over the wall, where the damp had got in; there were places where the painting was nearly gone; there were a dozen other bare patches, round the wall, where there were no hands at the edge of them at all, only branch and sky. "It's wear. It's old."

"It's wear," Ferra said.

She did not move her lamp from the last of them, the one with the shoulder. She was looking at the edge of the bare place, where the plaster stopped: at the line of it. Emeric looked where she was looking. The edge where the damp had taken the plaster off elsewhere on the wall was ragged, crumbling, soft, like the edge of a bite taken out of bread. The edge here was ragged too. Nearly as ragged.

"I've seen a great many erased lines," Ferra said. "In a great many ledgers. You can take a line out of a book so that nobody can read it. You can't take it out so that nobody can see where it was. The paper's thinner there." She looked at the edge of the bare place a long time. "I don't know," she said. "I don't know if it's wear."

She wrote something in her book. She did not say what, and he did not ask, and neither of them said anything else about it, then or afterwards, to anybody.

They each saw something different. He watched it happen, and he could not stop it, and he did not try.

Ferra numbered them. It was the first thing she did, when she had finished with the bare places, and it took her the rest of the night. She went round the wall with her lamp and her book and a clean sheet, and for every kind of figure on it, every kind that she could tell apart from every other kind, she drew a small plain outline on the sheet, a few lines, the shape of it, and put a number beside it. One: the tall ones. Two: the short ones at the roots. Three: the beast. Four: the ones on four legs who talked with their hands. Five, six, seven, eight. Eleven. Nineteen. She did not name any of them. When Sir Gavin asked her, looking over her shoulder, what number four was, she said, "Four," and went on drawing. "I don't know what they are," she said, when he did not go away. "So I won't say. I'll say what I can see. That's what I can see." And at the bottom of the sheet, after twenty-three, she drew eleven small empty oblongs in a row, one for each of the bare places, and put a number under each, and nothing in any of them.

Zale went quiet. He stood in front of the tall ones for a long time, with his lamp held up, and then went and sat on the floor in the middle of the hall with his back to nothing and his knees drawn up and his arms round them, and looked round at the whole of it, slowly, all the way round, and did not say anything, for the first time since Emeric had known him, for more than an hour.

The knights said the only word they had.

It was Sir Aled who said it. He had been round the whole wall once with a lamp, dutifully, as he did everything, and come back to the middle of the floor, and stood there with his lamp hanging from his hand, looking up at the beast curled round the top of the tree with its eyes half shut. He was a big plain man who had been a knight for twenty years and had fought at the coalition war with his head held down in a river, and he looked at the beast for a long time, and then at the tall ones in the branches with their long pointed ears, and then at the short broad ones under the ground among the roots with their tools, and his face did not change at all.

"Demons," he said.

He did not say it with fear. He said it the way a man names a thing he has been told about all his life and never expected to see. Sir Gavin, beside him, said nothing; but he did not say no, either, and after a moment he put his hand flat on his breast, over his heart, the way a man does in church when the priest names the Light, and kept it there.

"It's what they are," Sir Aled said. "In the Book. The ones under the ground, that the Light put down. With wings, and ears like that. My mother had a picture." He looked at Emeric. "Highness. What are they?"

He knew what they were.

That was the thing. That was what he stood with in the middle of the floor while Sir Aled waited for an answer. He had known since the lamps came up on the beast. He had had words for three of them since he was a child, from a world that had only ever told stories about them, a world where every child knew what a thing like that curled round a tree was called, and every child knew it was not real. A dragon. The broad ones at the roots with their hammers and their mattocks, under the ground, among the roots: dwarves. The tall ones with the long pointed ears, in the branches, graceful: elves. He had read about them in books. He had watched them in plays. He had never once in that whole life thought that he would stand in a room under the ground and see them painted on a wall by somebody who had not thought they were stories.

He did not say any of the three words aloud. He did not say them to Ferra, or to his brother, or to the knight in front of him.

"I don't know," he said.

Sir Aled nodded, slowly, as if that were a reasonable answer, and it was the first lie Emeric had told in that city.

And it was not, entirely, a lie. That was what he told himself later, lying awake under the tall stair, and it was true, and it did not help much.

Because he did not know. He knew what they looked like. He knew what his old world had called things that looked like that. He did not know what they were. The wall had got the tree right: the lattice on the trunk, the eight bumps on the pod, the husks lying cupped and empty at its foot, everything he could check. Whoever had painted it had stood in front of that tree. But he had stood in front of a great many painted walls in his old life, in great cold churches, and on those walls there had been harvests painted exactly, the right corn, the right sickles, the right way of binding a sheaf, every detail a farmer could check; and over the harvests, in the sky, saints with rings of gold round their heads, and angels with the wings of swans, and once, over a field of wheat, a great green beast being killed by a man on a horse. The corn had been true. The painters had stood in front of it. And the beast over it had been a story they told.

A true tree could stand under a legend. The same wall that got the pod right could have put anything it liked above it.

It might be a record. People who had painted what they saw: the tree, and the ones who tended it, and whatever lay round the top of it in the sun. It might be art. People who had painted the tree they knew and the stories they told about it. It might be both, and there was no way, in that hall, to tell where one stopped and the other started. The wall could not tell him. It was a wall.

He thought of Armand's books. The bones in them that nobody could explain, that Armand had shown him once, years ago, in the old study, drawn by some patient man a long time dead: great bones, out of the ground, of beasts nobody had ever seen, that some said were giants and some said were God's first try at a world and some said nothing at all. He had thought, looking at those drawings, of beasts from his old world that had been real and had died out long before anybody was there to paint them. He thought of the bones now, standing under the thing curled round the top of the tree, and stopped there. Right there. Because nothing in that hall could tell him which it was, and he was not going to decide it on a wall.

He found the seed on the way out.

It was over the street's mouth, on the inside, above the arch they had come in by, where nobody standing in the hall facing the tree would see it unless they turned round to leave. A band of carving, not painting, cut into the stone over the arch: a row of the signs; and in the middle of the row, larger than the rest, a seed. An oval, with a net of crossing lines cut over it, very fine, and a line down the middle of it where it would split, and above it, three short lines going up, like rays. Or like a shoot.

He had seen it before. Not in the book. In a church.

He stood under it with his lamp and knew exactly where. The altar stone in Doran's church on the island, the old one, that had been in that church longer than Kaldo had been Kaldo; the stone under the altar cloth, at the front, that nobody looked at, because the cloth covered it except at the great feasts, when it was taken off and the stone washed. He had seen it washed once, in his first year, standing at the back of the church because it was expected. A seed, cut in the stone, with a net over it and three lines going up. He had thought it was a lamp. Everybody thought it was a lamp. The lamp in the window. It was in every church he had ever been in, now that he thought of it, somewhere, on something, worn.

He did not say anything. He did not point it out. He looked at it for as long as he could without anybody noticing him look, and then he went out under it into the street, and it went into the room behind his eyes, onto a shelf, beside the book, and stayed there.

They found Ansel Vey at the end of the night, in a small room off the great hall, on the tree's right, in the autumn of it.

It was Zale who found him. He had got up off the floor at last and gone round the wall, slowly, on his own, with his lamp; and at the place where the wall's autumn began, where the branches went bare, there was a doorway in the painted wall, one of the tall narrow ones, that nobody had noticed because the painting went round it and made it look like part of the picture. Zale went in. And after a while he came out again, and stood in the doorway with his lamp hanging from his hand, and said, "Emeric," in a voice Emeric had not heard him use since they were boys.

It was a small room, for that city. A bench cut in the wall, and a niche over it with a smoke mark. And on the floor under the niche, against the wall, sitting, with his back to the stone and his legs out in front of him, a man.

What was left of one. The dry air had kept him, the way it had kept everything in the city: not flesh, but not only bones either; a shape, brown, very light, in the rags of a coat of a cut that nobody in Randillia had ever worn, with its collar up. His head had fallen forward onto his chest. His hands were in his lap. In his lap, under his hands, was a satchel of leather gone as hard as wood, and beside him on the floor, in a row, very neat, as a man sets things out when he knows he is going to be found by somebody, a candle stub in a tin, a flint, a pen, a little bottle of ink gone to a black crust, and a book.

Nobody touched anything for a long time.

Then Zale knelt down in front of him and put his lamp on the floor, and reached out, very carefully, and took the book.

It was small. Much smaller than the one in Ferra's box, the size of a man's hand, bound in oiled leather that had kept most of it dry. Zale opened it. The pages were close-written in the same brown ink as the margins of the other book, gone pale, in the same small cramped hand, the hand Zale had read by lamplight in the small room at Kaldo two pages a night; and between the writing, drawings. The hall. The tree. The tall ones in the branches. The beast. The bare places in the plaster, drawn as bare places, with a mark beside each. The seed over the arch.

"He was here," Zale said. "He stood where we stood. He drew it all." He turned a page. His voice was not steady. "He's got a word for them. He writes it every time. On the first book, in the margins, I never knew what it meant. It's the same word."

"What is it?"

Zale's finger was on it. "It's his tongue. Merrow's. It doesn't go into ours very well." He looked at it for a long time. "The ones before," he said. "That's as near as I can get. The ones who were here before. The ones who came first." He looked up at the wall through the doorway, at the tall ones in the branches in the lamplight, and the broad ones at the roots. "He crossed the world after them. From a headland in the fog. He knew what he was looking for. He knew they'd been somewhere. The ones before."

He turned to the end. The last pages. Emeric could see, over his shoulder, that the hand changed on them: got larger, and looser, and ran downhill.

Zale read it aloud. Slowly. Like a child of six, finding the words one at a time in a tongue he had learned in harbours.

"The water has come up behind me. It came in the night. When I went down to the steps this morning to go out, to go back to the boat, it was over the steps, and over the roof of the passage, and I could not see the end of it." He stopped. He went on. "It was not there yesterday. The passage was dry. I walked down it. There was no water in it but a little in the bottom, in a trickle, going down. Now it is full. It rose in one night. It rose faster than the sea rises. I do not understand it. There has been no rain. There is never any rain." He turned the page. "Third day. It has not gone down. I have candles for five days and water for more. I have drawn the hall again. I wanted to have it right."

He turned the page. There was a drawing on it: the whole wall, small, very fine, the tree in the middle and the beast round its top. Under it, a few lines.

"If this is the last," Zale read. His voice went. He waited. "If this is the last page, I would like whoever finds it to know that they were beautiful. And that I was not afraid, at the end. Only sorry not to know the rest."

He closed the book.

Nobody said anything. The lamp on the floor burned without a flicker. Sir Aled, in the doorway, had his hand flat on his breast again, and his lips were moving.

Emeric looked at the dead man sitting against the wall with his hands in his lap, and at the book in his brother's hands, and thought about water that rose in one night in a passage under a ridge on an island where there was never any rain. And about the old riverbed under the sand south of the outcrops, that he had felt on the first night on the island: its edge sharp, cut, the edge of a river that had not crept sideways over a hundred years but jumped, all at once, and gone somewhere else.

Water on this island did not creep. It jumped. Once, long ago, a whole river out of its bed, before anybody had drawn a line down the middle of it; and once, in a dead man's lifetime, in one night, into a passage that had been dry the day before, and had stayed. He could not say they were the same water. He could not say what moved under the island's rock, or when, or why. But he had known, on the first night, that nobody could ever check the edge of the old bed and no court could ever hear it, and here was a man who had stood at the other end of the island's water on the night it moved, and written it down: the day, the hour, the rain that had not fallen. Evidence. The only evidence of its kind there would ever be. In the dead hand of a man nobody had ever heard of, in a tongue nobody in Randillia could read, at the bottom of a flooded hole under a ridge that two kings claimed and a third was digging, in the one place in the world that Emeric could never, ever tell anybody he had been.

He almost laughed. He did not. It was not funny. It was only the same thing again, the thing that kept happening to him: the truth, lying in plain sight, in the one place nobody could be allowed to look.

"Wrap it," he said to Zale. "The book. Oilcloth. It goes home with us. It goes in the box with the other one." He looked at the dead man. "He sent the first one home. He'd want this one to get there too."

"And him?" said Zale.

Emeric looked at the dead man a long time.

"We can't take him," he said. "Not through the water. Not up the line. Not home." He knelt, beside his brother, in front of Ansel Vey of Merrow Sound, who had crossed the world after the ones before and found them, and was not afraid at the end. "We leave him with them. It's where he wanted to be."

Zale nodded. He did not get up for a long time.

He told the knights before they slept. Both of them, together, at the camp under the stair, with the lamps turned low and the others on their cots, pretending to sleep, which was the only privacy there was.

"What's on that wall doesn't leave this place," he said.

They looked at him.

"Not a word of it. Not to the ship, not to the others, not to your wives, not to your priest. Not ever." He looked from one to the other. "You said a word in there, Sir Aled. You said it honestly, and I'm not saying you were wrong to say it. But you know what that word does, outside. There's a file in the capital with Kaldo's name on it, that a duke wrote, and you've both heard what's in it. It has articles. One of them is that word." He let that sit. "It's a word that hangs men. It doesn't matter if the man who says it believes it. It doesn't matter if it's true. It matters who hears him say it, and what they want. And there are people who want very badly to hear a man from Kaldo say that word." He looked at the knight's hand, still flat on his breast. "If one of you says it in a tavern, in two years, about a wall under an island, I hang for it. And so do you. And so does everybody who was in the room."

Sir Aled was quiet for a long moment.

"I understand, Highness," he said. And then, heavily: "I don't want to know what they are. If they're demons, I don't want to have seen them. If they're not, I don't want to know what they are instead." He took his hand from his breast. "I'll not say it. On my oath."

"On mine," said Sir Gavin.

They understood it. That was what Emeric thought, lying on his cot afterwards under the tall stair, with the city dark and still round him and a dead man sitting in a room off the great hall with his hands in his lap. They understood it better than they would ever have understood anything true he could have told them about the wall. The Church had given them a word for it, and he had told them what the Church did with the word, and that was a thing they had known all their lives.

Topside, a mile to the west, Jibari coupled the hose.

It came down out of the black above him like a snake let down out of a tree, the iron coupling on its end swinging a little, and he caught it on the second swing and walked it to the hatch in the dune's foot with the weight of it pulling at his shoulders, and got down on his knees on the sand and fitted it to the pump's outlet at the crawl-way's mouth, and turned the collar, and felt the threads take, cut to Bram's gauge, the way they always took. He pulled one on the signal cord that ran up beside the hose. All well.

Above him, eighty feet up, hanging over the dunes with her bulk blotting out a great piece of the stars, the Wain held. There was no lamp on her. There was only the shape of her, and the faint hum that came down off her, and the hose going up into her belly.

Inside the dune, in the round room, Tamlin set the stone beam going. It rocked, slow, light and heavy, light and heavy, the way the drops rocked in the Drop House at home, and the pump on the drum's floor under the black oil began to push, and Jibari, kneeling at the hatch with his hand on the hose, felt it go stiff under his palm, and swell, and throb, like a thing alive.

The oil went up. He could hear it going up, inside the canvas and the rubber, a long low gulping sound like a big animal drinking. Up eighty feet into the dark. Into the Wain's belly, into her baffled tank, where Ned was watching the gauge by a lamp stone and saying nothing.

It took twenty minutes.

Twenty minutes, by the glass. Jibari had carried casks up the winch on the old runs, the winter before, a cask at a time on the slings, a whole night of it for forty casks and a crew half dead at the end of it and the ship hanging low over the pools for two hours where anybody looking might see. Twenty minutes, and the hose went slack under his hand, and Ned's two pulls came down the cord, and Tamlin stopped the beam, and the hose drained back down itself into the drum with a long gurgling sigh, so that when Jibari uncoupled it there was not a drop on the sand; and it went up out of his hands into the dark like a snake going back into its tree, and was gone.

Then the bales. That was the slow part. The grey shrub, cut and pressed on the flats, off the silver-leaved stands in the dark, into bales bound with twine, and each bale into its sling with its little stone set to lighten it, and each sling up on the winch, one at a time, because the hose could not take bales, and nothing could hurry a winch. It took the rest of the night, and what the winch could not take by the last hour went into stacks in the lee of the dunes under sailcloth dyed the colour of sand, for the landing. Their backs ached. Their hands were cut by the shrub's twigs through their gloves. The stinking black pools lay in the starlight behind them, and the dune had to be dressed at the end of it, by hand, like a bed.

Nobody complained. Jibari looked along the line of them, at the end of it, the six of them on the flats in their Skins in the last of the dark, waiting for the boat door, and saw that nobody was going to complain, not tonight, not any night. They had come down to this out of the sky. They were going back up into it. They would be doing it again tomorrow.

He stood on the flats and waited for the line to come down, and found he was thinking, for no reason, about what was in the water in the towers at home, and whether it was in him now, the thing that would let him do this, and whether he minded; and found that he did not know, and that he did not mind not knowing, tonight, with the stars going out one by one over the sea, and the line coming down out of the dark to take him home.
