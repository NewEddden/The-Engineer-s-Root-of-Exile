Chapter 7 - The Discharged Debt

Keep whatever conclusions you draw from that to yourself, until you've understood why.

Emeric turned the sentence over, and the crystal with it. He'd had the thing out of his pocket for an hour now, more habit than intent. Either the baron had a real reason for a barony this thin on its margins, or he was hoping a prince would read guilt as incompetence and stop looking. Both of those were consistent with the same words, which was exactly the sort of thing the man seemed to enjoy doing.

He started the way he'd started with the parish books. Category first, structure before content. Revenue in one pile, expenditure in another, then finer. Tithe remittance. Garrison pay. Castle upkeep. Procurement.

And one small pile that kept growing faster than it had any business growing. Debts forgiven. Obligations discharged.

Debt didn't usually evaporate. Most of it got collected. What didn't get collected got written down as a loss, on the books, traceable, because a lord who forgives a debt quietly is a lord who has to explain himself later. A cluster of quiet write-offs stood out precisely because it had been built not to.

The Control Rune warmed against his fingers without him deciding to hold it tighter, and the pages in front of him resolved into something cleaner. The shallow read, the same one he'd found in Bram's back room. Structure surfacing under what his eyes were already doing.

It told him nothing the pages didn't already say. It let him hold all of it at once, sorted and filtered in a way his hands couldn't manage fast enough on their own. Discharged debts by date instead of by page number. That was the entire trick. Speed, not insight.

Ferra's head for numbers wasn't magic either. It was just faster than the method everyone else was stuck with, and he had started to suspect that most of the difference between people got made in exactly that gap.

Sorted by date, one entry landed exactly where he didn't want it.

A debt, discharged in full, dated to the same week the carriage had been ambushed on the road into Kaldo.

He read the line again in case the date changed on a second pass. It didn't.

He had no name on the other end of it. Only a date that lined up too well to be nothing, and a date is not a lead. It was a coincidence with a shape, and a coincidence with a shape means nothing at all until there's a second one standing next to it to lean on.

He went to Bram's with the documents under his arm. By now the whole arrangement was ritual. Emeric left with Rylan, Ward followed without being seen, and everyone involved pretended it was still a secret.

Ferra took the ledgers off him without comment, the way Bram used to take a cracked blade, like the problem was the only thing in the room worth acknowledging. She didn't sort by category the way he had. She sorted by hand. The actual handwriting, entry cross-referenced against entry, until she found what she was after.

"This one." She turned the page toward him and tapped the line he'd already flagged. "Whoever wrote this wasn't hiding the amount. They were hiding who it went to."

The name on the entry was a merchant house, plain as anything, except the ink sat a shade off from the entries around it. Added later. Over something scraped away first.

"Somebody wanted a name here badly enough to write one in after the fact. That's not sloppy bookkeeping. That's covering a gap."

"Can you get under it? What was there before?"

"Given time." She didn't look up. "This isn't the only page that's been touched. Give me the rest of the quarter, not just this line."

It wasn't a name yet. It was a shape with a name-sized hole in it, which still beat what he'd had that morning.

Tam was on the door when they came for Petram, same as most afternoons since Ward set the rotation.

He was maybe nineteen and stood like a man who had been raised to expect someone to tell him to move along. He didn't ask why twice. He stepped aside the way he had the first time, the asking having been settled somewhere Emeric wasn't present for.

"Cold down here," Emeric said, because the silence wanted something in it.

"It is, Your Highness." A beat, and then, apparently deciding a prince who had spoken first could be spoken back to, "Warmer than the wall, though. I did the wall two winters."

"Which is worse?"

"Wall's worse. Wall's got wind in it."

Emeric almost asked him his name, and then the door was open and Petram was sitting up on the bench inside, and the moment went past the way small moments do.

Petram looked worse than he had a week ago and better than he had any right to. Imani had been in to see him twice, which was more than the man had earned and exactly as much as she gave anybody. He didn't waste breath pretending not to know why they'd come.

"You're not here to kill me. You'd have done that already."

"No."

"Then you want a name." Flat, working out the shape of the trade before either of them put terms on it. "You know I can't give you the one you actually want. I never met the man. I met the one he sent."

"Garrin knew him."

"Garrin knew faces." A shrug that cost him something in the ribs. "I knew the road. That's why he ran and I didn't. He had somewhere to be."

"Give me the one you met, then."

"Cobb." He said it like a man handing over the last coin in his pocket.

A courier's name. Somebody who moved between towns often enough that nobody thought twice about him turning up in any of them.

Behind them, close enough to the bars to be doing his job and not close enough to be obviously listening, Tam went very still.

Emeric caught it and said nothing. Neither did Petram, though the look he gave the boy said he'd caught it too, and had filed it as a problem for later, the way a man in a cell files everything as a problem for later.

The pull found Emeric on the stairs back up, sharp, off to his left where the wall gave onto the yard.

He didn't turn around. He had a rule for it now, and the rule was three afternoons of readings that had all resolved into one very large man who was professionally difficult to see, and a rule you've tested three times is a rule you stop testing. He went up to the desk and worked until the light went.

Word came before dinner the next day.

Tam hadn't shown up to relieve the evening rotation. They found him near the mill road, close enough to town that it read on paper like exactly the kind of opportunistic violence a barony this poor produced with grim regularity. Nothing missing that a robbery wouldn't have taken. Nothing pointing anywhere.

Imani looked at him because somebody had to. One strike, from behind, by a hand that knew where to put it. She said that much and then said she wasn't going to say more, because more than that wasn't hers to say, and she had watched what happened in this town to people who guessed out loud.

Except the timing, which pointed everywhere.

Emeric stood out in the cold by the guardhouse a long while before he trusted himself to say anything, and what came out when it came was not worth repeating.

He didn't say Wexler's name. There was no need. Underneath the anger, cold and separate from it, the engineer's part of his mind was already doing what it always did, sorting the loss into what it cost and what it proved. Tam had heard enough to be worth killing over. Which meant the name Petram gave up was real, and worth more than Emeric had let himself hope two days ago.

He hated that the thought had arrived that fast, and he didn't stop it arriving.

Rylan slowed on the walk back through town without being asked, looking down the same street Emeric was.

"That's Sela's," he said. "The house with the shutter off."

"Should I know the name?"

"You've met her." Rylan didn't look at him. "First week. The well by the burial ground. She took her bucket and went."

Emeric stood in the road and let that assemble itself.

Her first son went for a war nobody had explained to her and never came back and nobody had ever written to say why. Her second one had stood on a cold door for a prince who had spoken to him once about the wind.

"I never asked him his name," Emeric said. "I had the chance yesterday and I let it go past because the door opened."

Rylan didn't tell him it didn't matter. Emeric was grateful for that.

The rage came back sharper on the walk up the hill, and it took him most of the road to place where it was actually aimed. Not at Wexler. At the version of himself from yesterday morning, who had approved a duty rotation in a single sentence without reading past the shape of it, and had never once thought to ask who exactly he had posted over the only piece of leverage in this barony worth killing for.

Ward met him at the gatehouse and asked, without preamble, whether Emeric had gone anywhere unaccompanied in the last two days.

"No. Stairs and the desk." He said it automatically, already somewhere else. "You'd know. You were behind me."

"Not yesterday," Ward said. "Yesterday I was in the muniment room with your ledgers from noon to dark, at your instruction."

Emeric heard it. Some small part of him even reached for it, the way a hand reaches for a word it's about to need.

Then he noticed the blood on his own palm and couldn't say when he had made a fist, and by the time he had it open again the thought had gone wherever thoughts go when there is something louder in the room.
