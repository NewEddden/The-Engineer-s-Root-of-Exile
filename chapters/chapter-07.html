Chapter 7 - The Discharged Debt

Keep whatever conclusions you draw to yourself until you understand why, rather than merely that.

Emeric turned the sentence over, and the stone with it. He'd had the thing out of his pocket an hour by then, more habit than intent.

Either the baron had a real reason for a barony sitting this thin on its margins, or he was hoping a prince would read guilt as incompetence. Both required him to know the accounts were bad. Only one required him to have made them that way.

He sorted the way he had sorted the parish books. Category first, structure before content. Revenue in one pile, expenditure in another, then finer: tithe remittance, garrison pay, castle upkeep, procurement.

And one small pile that kept growing faster than it had any business growing.

Debts forgiven. Obligations discharged.

Debt did not usually evaporate. Most of it got collected. What didn't get collected got written down as a loss, on the books, traceable, because a loss on a ledger is a thing a steward can be asked about later. A cluster of quiet write-offs stood out precisely because it had been built not to.

The stone warmed against his fingers without him deciding to hold it tighter, and the pages in front of him settled into something he could keep all of at once. It told him nothing the ledgers didn't already say. It let him ask a question the ledgers had been arranged to make expensive.

Sort the discharges by date instead of by page.

That was the whole trick. Speed, not insight.

One entry landed exactly where he did not want it.

A debt discharged in full, dated to the same week his carriage had been ambushed on the road into Kaldo.

"No," he said out loud, to an empty room, and read it again in case the date changed on a second pass. It didn't.

Then he did the thing he had been training himself to do since the night he wrote apathy in a column and made himself leave it there. He went looking for the ways he was wrong.

He counted every discharge in the two years he had. Sixty-one. He counted the weeks. If sixty-one entries scattered evenly across a hundred and four weeks, the odds that at least one of them landed in any particular week were not remotely interesting. They were, in fact, better than even. One entry near one date was not a finding. It was arithmetic behaving normally.

So he sat with that, and was disappointed, and then noticed the second thing.

The sixty-one were not scattered evenly. Fifty-three of them sat in a band between the harvest and the year's turn, which was when a barony forgave a debt it was never going to collect off a farmer who had nothing. Ordinary. Seasonal. Boring.

Eight sat outside the band. His was one of the eight.

That was still not proof. It was a coincidence with a shape, and a coincidence with a shape means nothing at all until there is a second one standing next to it to lean on.

He took the documents to Bram's.

By now the trip had turned ritual. He left with Rylan, Ward followed without being seen, and all three of them maintained the arrangement was still a secret.

Ferra took the ledgers off him without comment, the way her father took a cracked blade, as though the problem were the only thing in the room worth acknowledging.

She didn't sort by category. He watched her not do it and had to sit on the urge to correct her, which he recognized, unhappily, as the urge of a man who has one method and mistakes it for the method.

"You're going by hand," he said eventually.

"Six clerks wrote these across two years and every one of them has a way of making a seven." She didn't look up. "Categories are what the writer wanted you to see. Handwriting's what he couldn't help."

It took her most of an hour. Then she turned a page toward him and tapped the line he had already flagged.

"This one. Whoever wrote it wasn't hiding the amount. They were hiding who it went to."

The name on the entry was a merchant house, plain as anything, except the ink sat a shade off from the entries around it, and the letters ran slightly closer together than the same hand managed anywhere else on the page. Written later. Written into a space that had been made for it.

"Somebody wanted a name here badly enough to put one in after the fact. That isn't sloppy bookkeeping. That's covering a gap."

"Can you get under it? What was there before?"

"Given time." She still hadn't looked up. "And this isn't the only page that's been touched. Give me the whole quarter, not the one line."

It wasn't a name yet. It was a shape with a name-sized hole in it, which still beat what he'd had that morning.

The boy on the door had been there three weeks and Emeric had never asked.

That was the sort of fact he only assembled afterward, and he assembled this one on the walk down, without attending to it, the way you notice a step you've taken a thousand times.

Six had ridden east out of the capital with him. One of them was in a locked room two doors from his own. One of them had gone into the trees the night of the fire and had not been seen since, by Emeric or by anybody who would admit to it.

That left four, and one of the four spent his days on rooflines above a prince who went out every single day.

Three men cannot hold a door around the clock for three months. Ward had said so, plainly, in the fourth week. Ward had set a rotation with local men in it, vetted them himself, told Emeric which ones and why, and Emeric had said that sounds right and gone back to the parish map.

He had spent his first evening in this castle refusing to put a prisoner in another man's cells. Three months later half the men on that prisoner's door drew their pay from the same castle, and it had happened by increments, for good reasons, each one of them defensible on its own.

He would think about that arrangement quite a lot in the years afterward, and about the fact that it was exactly the shape of the ledgers.

Tam was eating when they came for Petram, and stood up too fast, and put his elbow into the stool behind him. He caught it before it went over and apologized twice for a noise nobody had minded.

"Evening, Rylan."

"Tam."

He stepped aside for Emeric with the careful over-correctness of somebody who had been told exactly once how to behave in front of a prince and had been holding onto it since. Nineteen, maybe. Bad shave. The sword belt sat on him like a borrowed coat.

Emeric went past him and into the room and did not say a word to him.

Petram looked worse than he had a week ago and better than he had any right to. Imani had been in twice, which was more than the man had earned and precisely as much as she gave anybody.

He didn't waste breath pretending not to know why they'd come.

"You're not here to kill me. You'd have done that already."

"No."

"Then you want a name." Flat, working out the shape of the trade before either of them put terms on it. "You know I can't give you the one you actually want. I never met the man. I met the one he sent."

"Give me that one, then."

"What do I get."

"You get to still be alive in a room three months from now, which is more than the men who hired you have offered you since the fire."

Petram considered that with the thoroughness of somebody who had already considered it several hundred times.

"Cobb," he said, like a man handing over the last coin in his pocket.

A courier's name. Somebody who moved between towns often enough that nobody thought twice about him turning up in any of them, which was, Emeric thought, the entire job description of the person you would use for this.

By the door, close enough to be doing his job and not close enough to be obviously listening, Tam went very still.

Emeric caught it. He caught it clearly, and he registered it, and what he did with it was nothing at all, because he was holding a name he had wanted for three months and his whole attention had gone to it the way water goes to a drain.

Petram caught it too. The look he gave the boy said so, and said that he had filed it as a problem for later, the way a man in a locked room files everything as a problem for later.

Word came before dinner.

Tam hadn't shown up to relieve the evening rotation. They found him near the mill road, close enough to town that on paper it read as exactly the sort of opportunistic violence a barony this poor produced with grim regularity. Nothing missing that a robbery wouldn't have taken. Nothing pointing anywhere.

Imani looked at him, because somebody had to.

One strike, from behind, by a hand that knew where to put it. She said that much and then said she was not going to say more, because more than that wasn't hers to say and she had watched what happened in this town to people who guessed out loud.

Rylan was the one who told him the rest of it.

Nineteen. Took the guardhouse post in the spring because it paid a half copper over the docks and his mother had asked him to take something that wasn't the water. Second son.

"Sela's boy," Rylan said, to the street rather than to Emeric.

The name did what Rylan had known it would do.

A woman at the north well on his fourth day in this town, filling a bucket, starting a nod for Rylan and killing it halfway when she saw who was walking beside him. Leaving faster than a full bucket allowed, water going over the rim with every hurried step.

Crown took her boy. Crown never said what happened to him. You're the Crown today, whatever else you are on other days.

"That's the same woman," Emeric said.

"That's the same woman."

"How many did she have."

"Two," Rylan said.

Emeric stood out in the cold by the guardhouse for a while after that, and the part of him that had never once stopped running kept running, because it did not know how to do anything else and because it had nowhere better to be.

He did not say Wexler's name. He wanted to. He could feel the shape of the sentence sitting ready in his mouth, and the readiness was itself the reason not to say it, because a conclusion that arrives already assembled is a conclusion somebody else built.

So he ran the only question that was actually his to run.

Who knew? Four people had been inside that room or its doorway when the name Cobb was said out loud. Himself. Rylan. Petram, who was locked in it. And Tam, who was now dead.

Which meant the information had left that corridor in the four hours between the name and the mill road, and it had left it by one of three routes. Somebody had been listening who he hadn't counted. Or Tam had gone and told somebody himself, which a frightened nineteen-year-old who has just recognized a name might very plausibly do, and would have done to whoever he trusted most in that castle. Or Tam had said nothing to anyone, and had simply been seen going still by a man who then had to assume the worst.

The first route meant the corridor was not his. The second meant the boy had died running toward help. The third meant Tam had been killed for a reaction he never spoke, which was a thing Emeric was going to have to sit with for a long while.

All three of them pointed at somebody with reach inside this castle, and there were not many, and one of them had told him three months ago to understand why before he understood that.

He wrote none of it down. He noticed himself deciding not to and understood that he had just started keeping a second set of books in his own head, which was what everybody in this building appeared to be doing, and that he had learned it in under a season.

Underneath all of that, cold and running separate from it, the engineer sorted the loss into what it cost and what it proved, and the proving part came out clean and horrible.

Tam had heard something worth killing him for within four hours. Which meant the name Petram gave up was real, and current, and worth more than Emeric had let himself hope that morning.

A boy he had walked past twice a day for three weeks had just confirmed his lead for him, and had paid for it, and the confirmation was going to be useful.

The rage came back sharper on the walk up the hill.

It took him half the slope to place where it was actually aimed, and it wasn't at Wexler. It was at the man who had come down that same hill four hours earlier, holding a ledger, past a nineteen-year-old standing at a door, and had not asked his name, and had not asked who was on the roster, and had not once in three months looked up from the numbers long enough to notice that the one arrangement he had made on his first night in this place had been dismantled around him a fortnight at a time, with his own agreement, for perfectly good reasons.

He had seen a fall three steps before it happened, once.

He had not seen the car.

He noticed the blood on his palm at the gatehouse and couldn't say when he had made a fist.
