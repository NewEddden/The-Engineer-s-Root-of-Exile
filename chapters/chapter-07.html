Chapter 7 - Seven Days and a Bound Man

Breaking camp took twice as long with four men.

Four guards, one of them wounded, one prisoner, seven days of road left, and a watch rotation that did not have enough bodies in it to be a rotation. Ward named it once, flatly, standing over the traces, and then said nothing further, because there was nothing further. That was the whole of the discussion.

Before any of it, Emeric made them boil water.

Rylan, grey and furious with himself, built the fire back up. Declan, who could not yet walk in an entirely straight line, found the smallest pot in the carriage stores. A strip of linen went into it, and a needle from the sewing kit, and a length of thread.

"It's boiled," Osric said, the second time.

"Boil it again. And the cloth."

"The cloth's clean."

"It isn't."

Osric looked at the cloth, which was visibly clean, and then at his prince, who had been poisoned about ten hours ago and had slept perhaps two of them, and made a decision that Emeric could read across ten feet of grass.

He tried to explain. That was the mistake.

He got about three sentences in, things too small to be seen, living things, on hands and on cloth and in water, and heard the shape the words were making in the air and stopped in the middle of the fourth.

Declan was nodding. Declan had the particular attentive expression of a man agreeing with a drunk.

Osric was not nodding. Osric had privately concluded that the poison had gotten into something and was being polite about it, and was going to boil the water because he had been told to boil the water.

Ward held his forearm out over the pot and said nothing at all, and did exactly what he had been instructed to do, down to the second boiling, because his prince had told him to. Which is not the same thing as being persuaded of anything.

The knife had gone through the bracer's leather and out the far side of the muscle, a clean in-and-out a finger long. Emeric washed it with water that had boiled and cooled, and washed his own hands the same way, twice.

He had never stitched anything in his life.

He knew how it was done the way he knew his own name, every motion in order: the angle of the needle, the bite on either side, the point past which tight stops being closed and starts being a second injury. His hands had never done any of it. They followed what he knew, slowly, each motion thought through before it was allowed to happen, the way a man crosses his own house in the dark.

Eight stitches. They came out even.

When he tied off the last one Ward looked down at his arm, and then at Emeric, and did not ask.

The arm did not go bad.

Over seven days it did not swell, did not heat, did not go to streaks. It closed at the edges and stayed closed.

Not one man at that camp or at any halt on that road changed his mind about a single thing. Osric went on believing the water had been a strange day. Declan went on humoring him about it and stopped mentioning it. Nobody connected the second boiling to the arm, because the arm was fine, and an arm being fine is not evidence of anything to a man who expected it to be fine.

Emeric noted it as an irritation.

Being right, apparently, was not the hard part.



He asked Ward the other thing while the pot was still cooling.

"Teach me again," he said. "The sword."

Ward was buckling the bracer back on over the bandage, and did not look up. He finished the buckle and tested it. Then he went to his kit, and from the very bottom of it drew out a long bundle in oiled cloth and unrolled it on the grass.

Two practice blades. Wood, weighted with lead along the spine, the grips worn smooth and dark by hands. They were old. They had been oiled recently.

Emeric looked at them, and then at Ward, and did not ask how long they had been riding at the bottom of that kit. He had a fair idea, and the idea was not a comfortable one.

Ward handed him the one whose grip was wrapped narrower, for a smaller hand than his own, and kept the other, and put his wounded arm behind his back. Emeric's hands closed on the grip before he had thought about how, the left low and the right above it, thumbs laid where somebody had once laid them. Ward looked at the hands for longer than hands deserved, and said nothing about them.

"Feet," Ward said.

He put Emeric on the ground four times before breakfast. One-handed, with a hole through his forearm and eight stitches in it, barely moving his own feet.

He could feel his way through a stitch at the pace a stitch allows. Nobody allows you that pace with a blade, and by the time Emeric had thought through what his feet ought to be doing, Ward had already decided what they would be doing instead.

The fourth time he lay on his back in the wet grass and looked at the sky. Ward stood over him and said, "Better," in a voice that made it plain that it was not, and offered his good hand.

Over by the fire, Rylan, sitting with his head in his hands, laughed for the first time that morning.



Petram rode bound in the back of the carriage, where none of them had to look at him directly in order to know he was there.

Rylan drove, quiet in a way that had not been in him before the fire. Declan and Osric rode close flank instead of their usual loose spread, and watched the treeline more than the road, and had stopped talking to each other about anything that was not immediately in front of them.

Ward wanted to open Petram up, and did not soften how.

"He'll talk inside an hour."

"He'll say something inside an hour."

"That is the same thing."

"It is nearly the same thing," Emeric said, "which is worse than it being a different thing, because you can't tell them apart afterward."

He gave both reasons, because giving one of them would have looked like squeamishness and he could not afford to look squeamish in front of this man for at least a month.

A confession beaten out of a man is worth nothing standing in front of a magistrate, and he intended to hand this one over intact, with a witness, to somebody whose job it was.

And he had seven days, a man tied in a carriage, and absolutely nothing else to do with either of them.

Ward accepted it without agreeing with it. His objection had been practical rather than moral and he did not raise it again.

It was the first time Emeric had overruled him on anything in fifteen years. It was about method rather than safety. Both of them registered both of those facts and neither of them mentioned either.

The first pass came at the second halt of the sixth day, in the back of the carriage, with the flap tied open so that Rylan could see in from the box.

Petram sat against the far panel with his wrists bound in front of him and his boots off, because Ward had taken his boots and nobody had explained why and nobody needed to.

Emeric sat on the step. He did not go in.

"What were you paid?"

Petram looked at him for a while.

"You're not going to ask who."

"You don't know who. If you knew who, Garrin would have killed you on the road somewhere between the capital and here, and he wouldn't have needed a reason past the one he already had." Emeric let that sit. "What were you paid."

It came out.

Coin. Half up front, in a purse, in a stable yard, in the capital. The rest on completion. A sum he said out loud without any shame in it at all, and it was larger than fifteen years of a guard's wage and it was not larger than twenty.

Emeric did the sum on the step. Better than a hundred Gold Rand. More than twice the purse his father had handed him at a banquet to live on.

"When were you approached?"

"Before the roster."

"We'll get to that tomorrow. In what order did the pieces arrive? The money, the target, the road."

"The money. Then the road." A pause. "Then you."

Emeric turned that over and liked it. It told him something about how the organization thought, which was that it did not tell a man who he was killing until it had already bought him, which meant it had learned at some point that telling them first cost more.

And underneath that, warm and quiet, was relief.

A purchased man has a price. A price is a number. Numbers he could work with. He had spent the night before sitting in the dirt wondering whether the whole world was full of men who would do this for nothing, and it turned out the answer was that this one had done it for a figure he could write down.

He approved of the clarity, and was reaching for the next question when Petram spoke.

"It wasn't only the coin."

"I didn't ask what it was for."

"No." Petram looked at him. "You didn't."

There was something in the look that Emeric could not classify. Not anger, which he had been braced for. Not pleading, which he had half expected and had a plan for. Something quieter and further back, and it stayed on him a second too long.

Emeric got down off the step and had the flap tied shut and spent the rest of the afternoon deciding it had been resentment, and was satisfied with that, and rode on.

They passed through Wayford in the middle of that afternoon, the halfway mark, a town of inns and stables strung along a crossroads. Emeric kept his hood up the length of the main street. Osric came out of a baker's with bread and an opinion about the baker, and shared both.



At night the carriage was his.

Petram slept outside it, tied, under whoever had the watch. Emeric fastened the shutters, set a candle on the floor between his boots, and practiced.

The stylus first, because it cost nothing: a slate on his knee and the silver point drawn across it dry, no stone and no ink, only the motion, the small repeated strokes he half remembered from childhood. His hand wandered. He corrected it, and it wandered somewhere else.

Then the candle.

On the fourth try the ring of light came up over the wick and closed, lopsided, and the flame leaned a finger's tilt toward his finger and held for a count of three. It was crooked and small and it had come, and he sat back with an expression he was glad nobody could see.

Then the coin, which was harder.

A Copper Rand on his open palm. The pull came from somewhere lower and heavier, like holding a door shut against wind, and a ring of light opened above his hand with a mark at its center, and the coin rose a finger's width off his skin and hung there.

He counted. At six it dropped.

He tried again. Eight.

It was, by any honest measure, embarrassing. He had presumably done exactly this at seven with people saying words like genius over his head. At twenty-two a count of eight bought him a headache that sat behind his eyes like a thumb.

He did it four more times anyway, because eight was a number, and a number can be made to go up.

When he opened the door to empty the wash basin into the grass, Ward was sitting with his back against the rear wheel, awake, looking at the strip of ground under the carriage door, where a faint light had been showing in short pulses for the better part of an hour.

A sheet of paper, written close and folded small, was going into the inside of his coat as the door opened. A letter, then. A woman somewhere, Emeric supposed, and was pleased for him.

Ward did not say anything. Neither did Emeric.



The second pass took most of the seventh day, in three short visits, because he had worked out overnight that a long conversation lets a man find his footing and a short one leaves him standing in whatever you left him standing in.

"Tell me about Garrin."

"He's the other one."

"He's the first one." Emeric had had a night with it. "You didn't recruit him and you didn't bring him in. You take from him. How long?"

Petram did not answer the first time. He answered the second time, at the noon halt, and what he said was that it had been the better part of a year.

Emeric made him say it a third time, in the middle of the afternoon, before he let himself have it, because he wanted to be certain that he was not building a structure on a word.

The better part of a year.

The roster had been drawn eleven days before they left.

Somebody had bought a man for this journey months before anyone in that palace was told the journey existed. Which meant that either the guard assignment had been penetrated after the fact by somebody with reach into the roster office, or the roster itself had been written to order by somebody with reach a good deal higher than that.

There was no third door.

He rode with it through the rest of the afternoon and felt the whole shape of it settle, and understood that this was the fact that everything after it was going to be built on, and that he could do nothing at all with it.

He wanted to write it down. He wanted it very badly, in Armand's notebook, dated, with the three things it implied listed underneath it.

Write nothing down.

He had taken that from his father in a small office in a whisper and had not thought twice about it at the time, and now it was a week later and the single most important fact he had ever been handed was sitting in his head with nowhere to go and no way to check it against tomorrow's version of itself.

He went back once more before the evening halt.

"What did he look like. When he was recruiting you. What was he doing with his face."

Petram thought about it for a long moment, and Emeric had the sense that he was trying, which was interesting in itself.

"Ordinary," he said. "That's the thing about him. He looked like the rest of us."

The third pass was at the evening halt, and it was short, and it cost more than the other two together.

"Why you?"

"Because I had family there."

"Which you assumed meant you knew the ground."

"I do know the ground."

"You haven't been to Kaldo in nine years and you told me this morning you couldn't name the man holding the barony."

Petram had nothing for that, and after a moment he stopped looking for something.

Emeric sat on the step with the flap open and did the thing he did, which was to take it apart and look at what it was actually made of.

Somebody had gone through the palace rolls looking for men with people in Kaldo. That was a search. Searches leave a shape, and the shape of this one told him what the search had been for, and it had not been for local knowledge, because local knowledge is cheap and can be bought in any tavern within a day's ride.

The value of family in Kaldo, to an organization like this one, was that family in Kaldo can be reached.

He got down off the step.

Twenty feet away Rylan was checking a hoof he had already checked, on a horse that was fine, because Rylan had been finding things to do with his hands since the fire. He had a wife and two children in that barony. He had been counting the days out loud since the capital. He had said their names at a fire two nights ago in front of a man who was being paid to be there.

Emeric stood and looked at him for some seconds.

Then he went and sat down by the wheel and said nothing at all to anybody about it.

The fourth pass was that same night, and it was an accident.

He had taken Petram his water after supper, boiled twice like everybody's, and Petram chose that cup to object to. It tasted of the pot. Emeric let him win, and asked in passing about the shape of the plan, how it had been supposed to go, and Petram gave it to him still half in the argument, and one piece of it was the timing.

It had not been meant for that campsite.

It had been meant for the forest past Wayford, where the road is poor and runs under cover for most of a day and a carriage cannot get up any speed at all. The ninth night. Petram had moved it forward by four days because Ward had started tightening the watch and he had not believed the arrangement would survive to the treeline.

Emeric went very still.

His father had whispered him that exact stretch of forest. Past Wayford the road is poor and the trees run most of a day. Rest the horses before it, not after it.

Wayford was a day behind them. The forest was tomorrow.

He sat on the step of a carriage on a road in the middle of his own kingdom and could not make the thing resolve.

Either that was a king with good intelligence about a dangerous road, which every king has about every road in his country, and the coincidence was not a coincidence at all but simply the fact that a bad stretch of road is bad for everyone who uses it, including the men waiting in it.

Or it was something else.

The evidence supported both readings exactly equally. Not slightly in favor of one. Equally, to the digit, which is the worst possible outcome of any investigation, because it means the investigation has told you nothing and has also made it impossible for you to stop conducting it.

He had every part of the man laid out on the table in front of him, in order, labeled, and all he had to do was run the load down through that one member and find out whether it carried.

He sat there for a while.

Then he got up and climbed into the carriage for the night.

Ward's cloak was hanging over the gap at the bottom of the door, pinned at both corners.

He lit the candle and put the coin on his palm, because it was that or lie in the dark with the forest, and the coin would not go above four. It was a bad night. He had known it would be, and did not sleep much after.

In the morning he did not mention any of it to anybody. He asked Ward to rest the horses an hour at the edge of the trees, and Ward looked at him once and did it.



On the eighth day Petram stopped talking.

Not gradually. Completely, between one halt and the next. He answered nothing, volunteered nothing, and sat against the panel looking at the boards with his bound hands in his lap.

Emeric worked on it for an hour and had it.

He had assumed from the first afternoon that a man who sells his loyalty has none left to spend, and he had treated Petram as a transaction the whole way down the road, and the transaction model makes a prediction. A man in that position keeps talking, because talking is the only commodity he has left and because he is trying to establish a price for it.

So the silence was fear, arriving late, the way it does when a man finally works out what is waiting at the end of the road.

Or it was a man reaching the bottom of what he had actually been told, and going quiet rather than admit that the well was dry.

Both were consistent. He checked it twice and it held.

He was pleased to have it settled, and he moved on to the next thing, and neither Ward nor anyone else on that road had any reason to ask him whether he had gotten it right.

The forest was everything his father had said it was.

Ruts deep enough to stand a boot in, roots across the road, trees close on both sides. Emeric watched the trees for two days until his neck ached, and nothing came out of them. On the evening of the ninth day they came out the far side with a cracked spoke, which Rylan bound with cord and swore at in a way that was very nearly like himself.



The count went up.

Slowly, the way a seized joint gives up a degree and then sits there daring you to ask for another. Ten on the third night, at the height of his hand. Twelve on the fourth. The candle came easier than the coin. The stylus did not come at all.

Every morning Ward put him on the ground, and on the eleventh morning Osric watched and offered advice, all of it confident. It did no harm for an escort to see its prince lose to his knight every day before breakfast. It was very much in character.

On the twelfth day the land fell away.

They came over a long rise in the middle of the morning, and Rylan, who had been counting the days out loud since the capital, said, "Today," from the box, to nobody.

Below them the road ran down to a long dark wood, and Kaldo was somewhere past it, out of sight. Beyond that the ground ran away south in a long green drop, down and down, past farmland and then past nothing at all, toward a grey line at the limit of what the eye would do.

The sea.

It was the first thing he had seen in twelve days that was not a road.
