Chapter 7 - The Map

He went to Bram first.

That was deliberate. If he turned up at the priest's door with the smith beside him, he'd look like a man who'd been given advice and taken it. If he turned up with the smith and the healer and a guard, he'd look like a man assembling something, and on his seventh day in Kaldo he didn't want to look like that to anyone.

Bram put down his tongs, took off his apron and came without a word.

Imani took more persuading. She stood in her doorway with her arms folded and listened to him say that he thought there was a way to find the dead she hadn't been able to count, and when he'd finished she looked at him a long moment.

"I'm billing you for it," she said, and went for her shawl.

Rylan carried the writing box. It held paper, ink, two pens and a ruler, and Rylan carried it the way a man carries something he's been told is important and doesn't understand.

Halfway to the parish house, Bram said, conversationally, "Your knight's been on the tannery roof again."

Emeric looked at him.

"Over the south road. And on the rope walk, the day you stood in my forge six hours." Bram didn't look up from the lane. "Big man to sit that still. He's been on one roof or another every day you've been in the town."

So the pull in the alley had been Ward. And at the corner. Emeric felt something in his shoulders let go that he hadn't known was tight. Ward, watching him from above, and his own untrained sense catching Ward like a stare felt from across a room.

That explained the town. It didn't explain the hall in the capital, or the treeline on the fifth night, or the gallery above the kitchens, or the long wood on the last day of the road. He took those, all four, and put them in a drawer at the back of his mind, and shut it, and didn't look at why he was so glad to shut it.



Doran the priest was in his fifties and looked older. He lived in two rooms beside the church with a fire that smoked and a table covered in the debris of a man who had stopped expecting visitors. He poured them wine without being asked. It was terrible. He apologized for it, then apologized again a minute later, as if the first apology hadn't covered it.

Bram introduced Emeric in four sentences.

"This is the prince. He stood in my forge all day and didn't say a word, which is more than I'd have managed. He thinks he knows something about the dead. I don't know if he does."

Emeric thought it was the best introduction he'd ever been given.

He asked for the book of the dead. He'd expected to have to argue for it. Doran got up, went to a shelf, and came back with a thick ledger bound in boards and cracked leather, and set it on the table with both hands.

"Twenty-six years," Doran said. "I've kept it twenty-six years." He touched the cover. "Nobody's ever asked to read it."

It held everything he'd promised and more. Each burial had a name, a day, and a cause written in Doran's words: the flux, a fall, old age, the fever, childbed. And in the plague years, in a cramped and hurried hand, beside every name, where in the town the body had been laid.

"There's a tithe map, too," Doran said, turning pages. "Old. Decades old. Every house in the barony marked on it, for the church's tenth. It's the only survey of the place there's ever been."

Emeric went still.

He didn't mean to. A survey of every house, already made, and nobody had thought of it as anything but a tax document. He kept his face where it was and said that might be useful, later. Across the table Imani looked from his face to Doran's and back, and he saw her store something away.

He didn't need the tithe map for this. He took a sheet from Rylan's box and began to draw.

He drew Kaldo from memory. The circle of the wall. The inner ward rising in the middle, with its ring of water around the keep. The churchyard on the south-east shoulder of the ward, and the rows spilling below it. The lanes, and how they fell. Every well in the town, because he'd walked to every one of them over the last six days and knew where each sat and how far below the rows it lay. The latrine ground. The low south-east quarter where the ground bottomed out against the wall.

Bram leaned over to watch. After a while he said, "That's right," about one corner, in a tone of mild surprise. After another while he stopped saying it.



It took four hours.

Doran read from the book. Emeric marked. Imani named.

It settled that way without anyone deciding. Doran would read out a name and a day and a cause, and where the body lay if he'd written it. Emeric would find the house on his sheet and put a dot beside it. And half the time, before Doran had finished the name, Imani would say who it was: that's the cooper's boy, the one with the cough; that's the widow by the north well, she was ninety if she was a day.

Over one man on Cooper's Row, Bram and Imani argued. Bram said the man had been ninety and died of being ninety. Imani said she'd sat with him, and it was the sickness, and she knew the difference. They went back and forth until Doran found the entry and read it out: the sickness, in his own hand. Bram accepted that under protest and said so.

Halfway through, Imani mentioned that she'd almost been late. She'd been delivering a Tarrow baby three streets away and come straight from it, and she was still faintly surprised to be here at all.

In the third hour Rylan, who'd been reading over Doran's shoulder, stopped breathing for a second.

"I knew him," he said.

It was a name two streets from his mother's house, a boy Rylan had grown up with. Dead in the plague's first year. Rylan had been in the capital that year, drawing a guard's wage, and nobody had written to tell him.

No one spoke. Bram reached over and put one big hand on Rylan's shoulder and left it there, and Doran waited, and then read the next name.



It was Imani who saw it first.

She'd stopped naming. She was leaning over the sheet with her arms folded on the table, looking at the whole of it instead of the next dot, and then she unfolded one arm and put her finger down on the two lowest wells.

"There," she said.

Emeric looked where she was pointing, and saw what she was seeing, and for a second forgot anyone else was in the room.

The high streets were nearly clean. Since the rows began, two or three dots a street, no more. The kind of dying any town does. But below the rows, from the edge of the burial ground down to the two wells in the bottom of the south-east quarter, the dots crowded so thick they ran together. A wedge, with hard edges, as if someone had laid a ruler on the sheet and drawn it. And it stopped where the ground stopped falling, where the rise of the inner ward turned the water a different way.

The older pages showed something too. The low streets had always died a little more than the high ones. But before the rows the difference was small, the kind you'd expect, and after the rows it was a wedge.

Doran looked at it for a long time.

"The poor live low," he said at last. "And the poor die."

It was the first thing any honest man would say, and Emeric said so.

"It's a fair objection," he said. "Poor streets have more sickness. Worse food, more crowding, less of everything." He moved his finger across the sheet, all the way to the far side of the town, near the west wall. "Foulard Street."

It was as poor as any street in the wedge. Low, wet, crowded, with the worst houses in Kaldo.

It had four dots on it. In four years.

"Its well's on the far side of the ward from the rows," he said. "Nothing buried above it. Nothing draining down into it." He took his finger away. "Poverty doesn't do that. Water does."

Imani reached for the pen, then stopped, and pushed a clean sheet across the table to Emeric instead.

"Write these down," she said. "Nobody else ever has."

She had dead the book didn't. Nobody had paid the parish for them, so nobody had entered them, and nobody had said a word over them either, because the rite cost money too. She'd washed most of them herself, for the box someone bought, before the someone dug them in. She didn't say who. He didn't ask. Across the table, Doran didn't look up.

She could place ninety-odd of them from memory, house by house, and Emeric wrote down every one: a name if she had it, the house, the month. Where she wasn't sure of the month he drew a small ring instead of leaving them off. Then he put them on the map. When he'd finished, the wedge was darker, and its edges hadn't moved.

It was the first list of them that had ever existed. He folded it into the back of Armand's notebook, by itself.

Emeric looked at the sheet, and at her.

"I had a guess," he said, to the table, "and a third of the writing. She had all of the writing and the wrong name for the thing."

Imani didn't look up. Something in her jaw changed, slightly, and stayed changed.

Then he told them what he thought it was, and he used her words to do it.

"You're right that it comes from the ground where the water sits," he said. "But it goes down, not up. From the rows, and the latrines, down through the soil into the wells. Into whoever drinks. Out of them again, and back into the ground, and round." He looked at Imani. "It's alive. Too small to see. It travels in water, not in air."

"You can't prove that."

"Not yet. So far it's only a pattern." He put the pen down. "Give me a month. And some households willing to try something. Then I'll be able to show you, or I'll be wrong."



Doran had said nothing for some time.

"If it's a creature," he said finally, slowly, as though each word cost him, "and not a judgment." He looked at the book. "Then what were we praying about?"

Nobody answered.

"I don't know," Emeric said. "I won't pretend I do."

Doran looked up at him then. Properly, for the first time all afternoon. Then he looked back down and turned the page to read on, because there were still entries left.

He'd come to the start of the rows.

Emeric watched him read it. The first plague spring. The churchyard full. A decision about where to put the dead next, made fast, by the man whose book this was, because somebody had to make it and nobody else would. The entry was in Doran's own hand. He'd chosen the ground.

He sat back. The colour went out of his face in a way that had nothing to do with the bad wine. He asked, quietly, if they could stop for today and go on tomorrow.

They gathered up the sheet. Bram didn't get up.

"Go on," Bram said. "I'll stay a while."

Emeric didn't ask what was said in that room after they left.



Outside it was getting dark. The lamps were being lit in the lanes, and the smell was there, as it always was.

Imani stopped on the step and pulled her shawl tighter.

"A month," she said. "You said give it a month."

"Yes."

"I'm not waiting a month. People are drinking from those wells tonight." She was already turning away. "I'll start in the morning."

Bram came out behind them, slower, and shut Doran's door gently.

"The forge district's mine," he said. "Every man in it owes me work. If she's going door to door, they'll open."

Emeric looked down the lane toward the bottom of the town, where the two wells sat in the dark with the rows above them.

Door to door, then. Three thousand people, one door at a time, to tell them that the ground they'd buried their families in was killing them.
