Chapter 7 - Seven Days and a Bound Man

Breaking camp took twice as long with four men.

Four guards, one of them wounded, one prisoner, seven days of road left, and a watch rotation that did not have enough bodies in it to be a rotation. Ward named it once, flatly, standing over the traces, and then said nothing further, because there was nothing further. That was the whole of the discussion.

Petram rode bound in the back of the carriage, where none of them had to look at him directly in order to know he was there.

Rylan drove, quiet in a way that had not been in him before the fire. Declan and Osric rode close flank instead of their usual loose spread, and watched the treeline more than the road, and had stopped talking to each other about anything that was not immediately in front of them.

At the first halt Emeric made them boil water.

"It's boiled," Osric said, the second time.

"Boil it again. And the cloth."

"The cloth's clean."

"It isn't."

Osric looked at the cloth, which was visibly clean, and then at his prince, who had been poisoned about fourteen hours ago and had slept perhaps two of them, and made a decision that Emeric could read across ten feet of grass.

He tried to explain. That was the mistake.

He got about three sentences in, things too small to be seen, living things, on hands and on cloth and in water, and heard the shape the words were making in the air and stopped in the middle of the fourth.

Declan was nodding. Declan had the particular attentive expression of a man agreeing with a drunk.

Osric was not nodding. Osric had privately concluded that the poison had gotten into something and was being polite about it, and was going to boil the water because he had been told to boil the water.

Ward held his forearm out over the pot and said nothing at all, and did exactly what he had been instructed to do, down to the second boiling, because his prince had told him to. Which is not the same thing as being persuaded of anything.

The arm did not go bad.

Over seven days it did not swell, did not heat, did not go to streaks. It closed at the edges and stayed closed.

Not one man at that halt or any halt afterward changed his mind about a single thing. Osric went on believing the water had been a strange day. Declan went on humoring him about it and stopped mentioning it. Nobody ever connected the second boiling to the arm, because the arm was fine, and an arm being fine is not evidence of anything to a man who expected it to be fine.

Emeric noted it as an irritation.

Being right, apparently, was not the hard part.

He did not draw the conclusion that sat directly underneath that observation, and he would not draw it for a very long time.

Ward wanted to open Petram up, and did not soften how.

"He'll talk inside an hour."

"He'll say something inside an hour."

"That is the same thing."

"It is nearly the same thing," Emeric said, "which is worse than it being a different thing, because you can't tell them apart afterward."

He gave both reasons, because giving one of them would have looked like squeamishness and he could not afford to look squeamish in front of this man for at least a month.

A confession beaten out of a man is worth nothing standing in front of a magistrate, and he intended to hand this one over intact, with a witness, to somebody whose job it was.

And he had seven days, a man tied in a carriage, and absolutely nothing else to do with either of them.

Ward accepted it without agreeing with it. His objection had been practical rather than moral and he never raised it again.

It was the first time Emeric had overruled him on anything in fifteen years. It was about method rather than safety. Both of them registered both of those facts and neither of them mentioned either.

The first pass came at the second halt of the first day, in the back of the carriage, with the flap tied open so that Rylan could see in from the box.

Petram sat against the far panel with his wrists bound in front of him and his boots off, because Ward had taken his boots and nobody had explained why and nobody needed to.

Emeric sat on the step. He did not go in.

"What were you paid?"

Petram looked at him for a while.

"You're not going to ask who."

"You don't know who. If you knew who, Garrin would have killed you on the road somewhere between the capital and here, and he wouldn't have needed a reason past the one he already had." Emeric let that sit. "What were you paid."

It came out.

Coin. Half up front, in a purse, in a stable yard, in the capital. The rest on completion. A sum he said out loud without any shame in it at all, and it was larger than fifteen years of a guard's wage and it was not larger than twenty.

"When were you approached?"

"Before the roster."

"We'll get to that tomorrow. In what order did the pieces arrive? The money, the target, the road."

"The money. Then the road." A pause. "Then you."

Emeric turned that over and liked it. It told him something about how the organization thought, which was that it did not tell a man who he was killing until it had already bought him, which meant it had learned at some point that telling them first cost more.

And underneath that, sitting warm and entirely unexamined, was relief.

A purchased man has a price. A price is a number. Numbers he could work with. He had spent the night before sitting in the dirt wondering whether the whole world was full of men who would do this for nothing, and it turned out the answer was that this one had done it for a figure he could write down.

He approved of the clarity and put it away without looking at it.

"It wasn't only the coin," Petram said.

"I didn't ask what it was for."

"No." Petram looked at him. "You didn't."

There was something in the look that Emeric could not classify. Not anger, which he had been braced for. Not pleading, which he had half expected and had a plan for. Something quieter and further back, and it stayed on him a second too long.

Emeric got down off the step and had the flap tied shut and spent the rest of the afternoon deciding it had been resentment, and was satisfied with that, and rode on.

The second pass took most of the third day, in three short visits, because he had worked out overnight that a long conversation lets a man find his footing and a short one leaves him standing in whatever you left him standing in.

"Tell me about Garrin."

"He's the other one."

"He's the first one." Emeric had had a night with it. "You didn't recruit him and you didn't bring him in. You take from him. How long?"

Petram did not answer the first time. He answered the second time, in the afternoon, and what he said was that it had been the better part of a year.

Emeric made him say it a third time before he let himself have it, because he wanted to be certain that he was not building a structure on a word.

The better part of a year.

The roster had been drawn eleven days before they left.

Somebody had bought a man for this journey months before anyone in that palace was told the journey existed. Which meant that either the guard assignment had been penetrated after the fact by somebody with reach into the roster office, or the roster itself had been written to order by somebody with reach a good deal higher than that.

There was no third door.

He sat with it at the fire that night and felt the whole shape of it settle, and understood that this was the fact that everything after it was going to be built on, and that he could do nothing at all with it.

He wanted to write it down. He wanted it very badly, in Armand's notebook, dated, with the three things it implied listed underneath it.

Write nothing down.

He had taken that from his father in a small office in a whisper and had not thought twice about it at the time, and now it was six days later and the single most important fact he had ever been handed was sitting in his head with nowhere to go and no way to check it against tomorrow's version of itself.

He went back once more before dark.

"What did he look like. When he was recruiting you. What was he doing with his face."

Petram thought about it for a long moment, and Emeric had the sense that he was trying, which was interesting in itself.

"Ordinary," he said. "That's the thing about him. He looked like the rest of us."

The third pass was on the fifth day and it was short and it cost more than the other three together.

"Why you?"

"Because I had family there."

"Which you assumed meant you knew the ground."

"I do know the ground."

"You haven't been to Kaldo in nine years and you told me yesterday you couldn't name the man holding the barony."

Petram had nothing for that, and after a moment he stopped looking for something.

Emeric sat on the step with the flap open and did the thing he did, which was to take it apart and look at what it was actually made of.

Somebody had gone through the palace rolls looking for men with people in Kaldo. That was a search. Searches leave a shape, and the shape of this one told him what the search had been for, and it had not been for local knowledge, because local knowledge is cheap and can be bought in any tavern within a day's ride.

The value of family in Kaldo, to an organization like this one, was that family in Kaldo can be reached.

He climbed down out of the carriage.

Twenty feet away Rylan was checking a hoof he had already checked, on a horse that was fine, because Rylan had been finding things to do with his hands since the fire. He had a wife and two children in that barony. He had been counting the days out loud since the capital. He had said their names at a fire five nights ago in front of a man who was being paid to be there.

Emeric stood and looked at him for some seconds.

Then he went and sat down by the wheel and said nothing at all to anybody about it, and the day ended.

The fourth pass was on the sixth day and it was an accident.

He had gone back for something else entirely, the shape of the plan, how it was supposed to have gone, and Petram gave it to him without much prompting, and one piece of it was the timing.

It had not been meant for that campsite.

It had been meant for the forest past Wayford, where the road is poor and runs under cover for most of a day and a carriage cannot get up any speed at all. Petram had moved it forward by four days because Ward had started tightening the watch and he had not believed the arrangement would survive to the treeline.

Emeric went very still.

His father had whispered him that exact stretch of forest. Past Wayford the road is poor and the trees run most of a day. Rest the horses before it, not after it.

He sat on the step of a carriage on a road in the middle of his own kingdom and could not make the thing resolve.

Either that was a king with good intelligence about a dangerous road, which every king has about every road in his country, and the coincidence was not a coincidence at all but simply the fact that a bad stretch of road is bad for everyone who uses it, including the men waiting in it.

Or it was something else.

The evidence supported both readings exactly equally. Not slightly in favor of one. Equally, to the digit, which is the worst possible outcome of any investigation, because it means the investigation has told you nothing and has also made it impossible for you to stop conducting it.

He had every part of the man laid out on the table in front of him, in order, labeled, and all he had to do was run the load down through that one member and find out whether it carried.

He sat there for a while.

Then he got up and went to find something to eat, and did not sleep much, and in the morning he did not mention it to anybody.

On the eighth day Petram stopped talking.

Not gradually. Completely, between one halt and the next. He answered nothing, volunteered nothing, and sat against the panel looking at the boards with his bound hands in his lap.

Emeric worked on it for an hour and had it.

He had assumed from the first afternoon that a man who sells his loyalty has none left to spend, and he had treated Petram as a transaction the whole way down the road, and the transaction model makes a prediction. A man in that position keeps talking, because talking is the only commodity he has left and because he is trying to establish a price for it.

So the silence was fear, arriving late, the way it does when a man finally works out what is waiting at the end of the road.

Or it was a man reaching the bottom of what he had actually been told, and going quiet rather than admit that the well was dry.

Both were consistent. He checked it twice and it held.

He was pleased to have it settled, and he moved on to the next thing, and neither Ward nor anyone else on that road had any reason to ask him whether he had gotten it right.

On the twelfth day the land fell away.

They came over the last rise in the early afternoon and the water was there, wide and flat and split around a spine of rock, with the town sitting on the island in the middle of the split.

Below it the ground ran away south and west in a long green drop, down and down, past farmland and then past nothing at all, toward a grey line at the limit of what the eye would do.

The sea.

It was the first thing he had seen in twelve days that was not a road.
