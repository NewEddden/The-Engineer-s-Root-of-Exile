Chapter 101 - The Quiet Man

"You said not without you," Emeric said. "So sit with me."

Armand was in his chair by the fire in the room off the hall, with the blanket over his knees and the board across it and a book on the board that he had not been reading. He looked up. He looked at Emeric in the doorway, the way he had looked at him over the drawings of the stones in August, and Emeric could see him trying the question from three directions before he asked it.

"Why now?"

"A man I sent down a road died on it." Emeric came in and shut the door. "Out of my sight. A long way out of it. I didn't know for three days." He sat down on the stool across the fire. "I want to be able to see further than I can."

"This won't let you see down a road."

"I know."

"It won't let you see past this house, at first. Perhaps not past this room." Armand closed the book. "And it costs. You've watched it cost me."

"I know."

Armand looked at the fire for a while. Then he reached down beside his chair to the basket where he kept the drained stones Linnet brought him to practise his reading on, and took one out, a dull grey lamp stone the size of a hen's egg, empty, and held it out.

"Hold that," he said. "In your fist. Hard."

Emeric took it.

"When you go out, you go thin. Thinner than you've ever been. And when you're thin, you're not sure where your edges are, and you can forget which part of you is you." Armand settled back in the chair. "That's what happened to the man in the tower. He went out to the walls of the room and couldn't find which part was him to come back to. So you hold something hard, and you hold it tight enough to hurt, and when you want to come back, you come back to that. The hardest thing you can feel." He nodded at the stone. "Not a word. Not a thought. Your hand, and a stone in it."

"And if I can't find my hand?"

"Then I'll find it for you," said Armand, "and it will not be pleasant for either of us."



The first night he got as far as the room.

He did what Armand had told him in July and had told him again now, slowly, twice. He let it out the way a man lets his breath go on a cold morning, with no glyph and no shape and no pushing, and let it hang. He had felt mana go out of him a thousand times, for fire, for the stones, for the lift. This was not that. This had no road and no end. It went out of him in every direction at once and very thin, and kept going, and he had to keep letting it.

For a long while there was nothing. Then there was the room.

It came back to him the way the walls of a dark room come back to a man who claps his hands: not seen, but there, all at once, the shape of the space and what stood in it. The hearth, cold and hard at its edges and nothing at all in the middle where the fire was, because fire carried none. The chair. The basket of drained stones, each a small dead weight. And in the chair, Armand.

He had felt Armand for years, the way he felt everyone, at the edge of his own sense, a warmth. This was different. Armand was enormous. He was a great slow bright presence, wide as the room, deep as a well, and in the middle of him, where his heart was, there was something wrong: a place where the brightness stuttered and caught and went on, and stuttered again. Emeric drew back from it without meaning to, as a hand draws back from a stove.

"There," said Armand's voice, from a long way off. "You've found me. Don't look at the middle. Everybody looks at the middle. Come back."

He looked for his hand.

It was not where he had left it. For a breath, two, three, he did not know which of the hands at the ends of his arms was his, or that he had arms, or where the stool was, and the room was all round him thin and wide and he was the room, and then the stone. The stone in his fist, hard, a dull grey ache in his palm where the edge of it bit. He went to it. He went back into his hand, and up his arm, and was sitting on the stool by the fire with his fist clenched so tight round a dead lamp stone that his knuckles had gone white.

His head hurt as though somebody had pressed a thumb behind each eye. His hands were cold to the wrist.

"Three breaths," Armand said. He was watching him with no expression at all. "You were gone three breaths after I told you to come back. That's not bad, for the first time." He picked up his book again, and did not open it. "It's not good. Tomorrow."



The second night he got the corridor, and the hall.

It went out of him easier, now that he knew which way was out. The room came back, and Armand in it, bright and wide and stuttering at the heart, and he did not look at the middle. He let it go further. Through the door, which was oak and thick and came back to him as a hardness with nothing in it. Into the corridor.

Somebody was in the corridor. A man, walking, with something in his coat that was much brighter than he was, a small hard glow at his hip. A half-charged lamp stone. And the man round it was quiet and narrow and precise, and moved in short exact steps, as if he were measuring the corridor for a report.

"Hale," Emeric said, aloud, without meaning to. "With a lamp stone. Half full."

Armand laughed, softly, and it hurt him, and he stopped.

The hall. It was wider than he could hold all at once, and he held it in pieces. The long table, and the benches, and at the far end, by the door to the yard, men. Four, five. The knights coming in off the wall for their supper, cold, stamping, talking. He knew they were talking because he could feel them move, not because he could hear them. And they were not the same.

He had known that already. He had found it on the wall in September, with his eyes shut, Ward coming one way and Rylan the other, the same amount of mana, a different beat. Ward slow and even. Rylan quick, with a half stop in it. He had told Tess, and Tess had asked whether a stone could learn it, and he had said not one they could cut.

Now, out here, thin as breath, he could feel what that beat had been the edge of. It went further than how they walked. It was all of them. Each of the men at the far end of the hall was a sound he would have known in a crowd. Osric was loud even here, a wide careless brightness that spilled. Declan was neat and close and kept to himself, as Declan did. Ward was a wall. A great slow even weight that did not change, and would not.

"Everybody sounds different," Armand said, from somewhere. "When you're out far enough. The tower calls it a man's note. I knew a woman who could tell you who'd been in a room an hour after they'd left it, from what was still hanging in the air."

On his other world there had been something like it. Every man's fingers left a pattern on whatever he touched, a whorl of lines that no other man's fingers had ever made, and men had been hanged on them. A fingerprint. This was a fingerprint that a man carried round him, that he could not wipe off.

Somebody came in from the yard, late, alone, and crossed the hall toward the keep stair without stopping at the table.

Rylan. It was Rylan's beat, quick, with the half stop in it. But the note round it was wrong. He had felt Rylan a hundred times on the wall and in the yard and at the board, and Rylan had always been a thing like a rope, strong and easy and a little slack. This was not slack. It was drawn tight, tighter than he had ever felt anything in a man, like a wire on a winch wound a turn past where it would hold, humming with it.

It went up the keep stair toward the line room, and was gone out of his reach.

He lost some of his hold, enough that the hall went grey and wide and he did not know for a breath where in it he was. Then the stone. The stone in his fist. He came back to it, and up his arm, and was sitting by Armand's fire with his head splitting and Armand leaning forward in his chair, one hand half out toward him, ready.

"Two breaths," Armand said, and sat back. "Better." He looked at Emeric's face. "Who was that, at the end? You went white."

"Rylan."

Armand waited.

"He felt wrong," Emeric said, and heard how little that meant, said aloud, in a warm room, by a fire.

"Men do," said Armand. "Men are cold, and tired, and their wives are cross with them, and their teeth hurt. You'll feel a hundred men feel wrong before you feel one that matters." He picked up his book. "That's the other thing it costs. You'll want to know why. Every time. And mostly it's teeth."



It was not teeth. Over the next ten days, everybody but Emeric seemed to know it was not teeth.

Rylan had stopped eating with the knights. Declan told it as a joke, the first time, at the dawn drill: that Rylan had taken against Osric's stories at last and nobody could blame him, and now ate his bread on the wall walk alone like a heron. Osric did not think it was funny. Osric said he had told the same stories for years and Rylan had never minded them before.

Rylan was on the board nearly every night. Ward's rota for the board, on the guardroom wall in Ward's square hand, had Rylan's name against six nights out of seven, and the seventh crossed through and his name written over it.

And Rylan had asked Declan two questions, on the wall, one cold night, while they were walking the river stretch. Declan told Emeric about them afterward, not as a joke, because by then he had stopped finding things funny. Which stretch went blind, if the river stair's stone was taken out for cutting, the way the prince had taken it out in September for the dog. And when the card said to wake the prince, did the prince come down to the fault himself. Alone. Or did he bring somebody.

"I told him the prince comes himself," Declan said. "Because that's what you do. And that he'd bring the man who woke him, because that's what you'd do." He looked at the wall. "He said that's what he thought."

Emeric crossed the market square that afternoon on his way to the exchange, and there were three men at the door of the inn, travellers down from the north road, in good plain coats, with a cart in the inn yard that had a few bales of wool on it. Late in the year to be buying wool. One of them was bargaining with the innkeeper over the price of a second night. Emeric went past them into the exchange, and Ferra had a column for him, and he did not think about them again.



Sela brought the third thing.

She did not bring it to Emeric. She brought it to Ward, at the guardroom door, on the second Monday of December, standing very straight in her dark school dress with her hands folded in front of her and her face set, and Ward brought it up to the keep himself that evening with the other two.

Finn had not been at school for a week.

Wren had. Wren came every morning, on time, with her slate and her bread, and sat in her place, and when Sela asked her where her brother was, said he was at their aunt's. Sela had let it go for three days. On the fourth she had sent Arthur to the house after school with a message, and Arthur had come back and said that Nessa had come to the door and said Finn was at her sister's, up the west road, for his chest, and shut the door.

"Nessa has a sister on the west road," Ward said. "Sela knows her. They have not spoken since before the plague. Sela says Nessa would not send a dog to that house." He stood in the middle of Emeric's room with his hands behind his back. "Sela says the boy had no cough."

He laid it out. He did it the way he laid out a road: the board, six nights in seven. The knights' table. Declan's two questions. And the boy, away from school a week, at the house of a sister his mother did not speak to, with a cough nobody had heard.

"I do not like them," Ward said.

Emeric sat at the table under the window.

He did not like them either. He had felt Rylan cross the hall below him, tight as a wire, and had told Armand about it and been told it was mostly teeth. He sat and looked at the three things Ward had brought him, and they made a shape. He could see the shape. It was a bad one.

He had seen a shape like it before.

A column in the garrison's book, and a column in the barony's payments, and nine years of small regular draws by a man called Corliss on the garrison's share, and the coffin-maker's bills, and the dates, all of them true. He had held every one of those dates in his own hands and checked them four ways, and every fact had been true, and the shape they made had been a thief. And he had stood Corliss up in the yard in front of the whole garrison and given him a choice between thief and sexton, on a column. And Corliss had been burying the plague's dead for nine years out of the garrison's share because nobody else would, and had said nothing, and had waited both times for the second question, which Emeric had not asked.

Three facts, every one of them true. And a man's name on them, in front of everybody who would ever have to stand a wall beside him.

"What do you want to do?" he said.

"Take him off the board. Put a man on his house."

"Watch him."

"Yes."

"My own knight. Who drank Petram's tea beside me on the road and got up the next morning and rode on, and showed the whole garrison how to fight on its back, and told us at the yard fire what they did to him in that room." Emeric shook his head. "On three facts. Because his boy has a cough and he's quiet."

"The boy has no cough."

"Then ask him," Emeric said. "I'll ask him. Myself. Tonight."



Rylan was on the board.

He was on the stool in the line room with his feet on the floor, not on the bench, and the lamp turned low, and the twelve shutters up and the twelve needles lying still. He stood up when Emeric came in. He had never stood up for Emeric in the line room before.

Emeric did not sit down.

"Finn's not at school," he said. "Sela's worried."

Rylan did not look away. That was the first thing Emeric noticed, and he held on to it afterward, because it was the thing he had wanted to see. Rylan looked straight at him, steadily, the way he had looked at the card on the wall in October, reading it a letter at a time.

"He's at Nessa's sister's. On the west road. He's had a cough since the frost, and she's got a cow and a dry house and a fire in it all day, and we haven't." He said it plainly, with no more in it than a man puts into his children's coughs. "Nessa wouldn't have him in the damp. They'd words, years back, Nessa and her sister. But it's a cow and a dry house. You'll swallow a lot for a dry house."

"Imani could look at him."

"He doesn't need Imani. He needs a cow." Rylan nearly smiled. It did not get as far as his eyes. "He'll be home by midwinter."

"And the board? Six nights."

"I can't sleep in the house with him out of it." Rylan looked at the shutters. "I lie there and listen for him. So I come up here and listen for the wall instead. It's the same thing, near enough, and it's some use." He was quiet. "And I eat on the walk because Osric talks. He's always talked. I've no stomach for it this month, that's all."

"You asked Declan about the river stair."

"I'm on the board every night. If you take that stone out, I want to know which stretch I can't see." Rylan said it without any change in his voice. "And the card says wake the prince. I wanted to know if I wake anybody else. It doesn't say."

Every word of it was plausible. Every word of it was the kind of thing a tired man says, plainly, to a question he has been expecting. Emeric stood in the line room and listened to it, and it fitted, and it was the answer he would have given in Rylan's place, to the word.

And under it, without going out at all, without the stone or Armand or anything but what he had always felt of the men near him, he could feel Rylan's note. Tight. Wound past holding. Humming.

A man whose son was coughing in a house his wife would not send a dog to. A man who could not sleep.

Mostly it's teeth, Armand had said.

He chose. He knew he was choosing, and he chose anyway, because the other choice was a man on Rylan's door and Rylan's name in every mouth in the guardroom by morning, and he had done that once, to a man burying the dead, and been wrong.

"Bring him to the ward when he's home," he said. "Let Imani hear his chest."

"I will."

"Goodnight, Rylan."

"Highness."

Ward was at the foot of the keep stair when Emeric came down, standing in the dark of the hall where the knights had been at supper, with his hands behind his back. He did not ask. He looked at Emeric's face, and Emeric told him, all of it, the cough and the cow and the dry house and the board and the card.

Ward heard it to the end. He looked up the dark stair, toward the line room, for a long while.

"As you say," he said.
