Chapter 101 - Nothing Touching

Bram built it on the ladder, which meant that it took him most of the early summer and that nothing in it was made by eye.

It went into the pattern store, against the back wall, where the hold bench had stood on its own since the winter. It was a chamber of firebrick about the size of a baker's oven, laid from the stock left over when the glass furnace was relined, on a stone footing, with its floor a hand deep in dry sand. At the back a flue went up beside the high window and out through the roof, and in the throat of the flue, where it narrowed, Bram set an air stone the size of a walnut in an iron collar to draw the smoke up and away, so that whatever burned in the chamber would not stay in the room with the men. At the front was a port the width of two hands, with an iron shutter that slid in a groove, and inside it a second, smaller shutter holding a pane from the glassworks, smoked over a lamp until it was nearly black.

Inside, standing in the sand on four short iron legs, was the hold bench again: a plate of iron scraped flat on the three plates, a little larger than the first, with four housings under its corners made off the any-size drawing, and Bram's four iron fingers bent up and over them. He had cut the cups himself and given the collars to Jorrin. Nobody had asked him for the fingers. He put them on anyway.

He asked what the sand was for, once, and Emeric told him: if anything falls, it falls on sand. Bram looked at the sand, and at the legs under the plate, and at the fingers over it, and did not ask what the legs and fingers were for, because he could see that too. Everything in the chamber had somewhere to land.

The stones under the plate were what Emeric spent the same weeks on.

He had written two words under a question in the spring, and it had taken him some days after that to see that he had been carrying the answer since the autumn, in the yard, from Armand. You do not leave your work in a stone, ever. An acolyte who leaves himself in a stone and walks away is tied to it. When the stone runs dry, it runs dry through him.

Every first-year in the capital was shown once what that did to a man, so that he would never do it. Nobody in the tower had ever looked at it as anything but a warning. But it was also a description, and an exact one. A stone with a thread still on it did not spend itself. It drew what it spent up the thread, from whatever stood at the other end. The acolyte had only been standing where the fuel went.

Put a charged stone where the acolyte stood, and the blank would run dry through the charged stone instead of through itself. The black stone would not spend at all. The charge would.

And a stone on a thread was not set. It could be given more, or less, or given the other way, for as long as the thread was on it. That was the whole difference between Armand's night on the bench in the autumn and Emeric's own the night after. The old man had held the thread and could have changed his mind at any moment until he let go. Emeric had let go, and could not.

He tried it on the hold bench first, because a thing is tried small before it is tried large.

He had only one way to carry a charge from one stone to another, the one he had found for the wall. Bram cut a groove along the top of a slab of slate, half an inch wide and half an inch deep, from end to end, and bedded it with the wall's mortar, with charged stone ground into it sand-fine. At one end of the groove Emeric seated a blank in a housing. At the other, in a cup of its own, he put a charged stone from the spares box under the board, one of the stones the wall was kept alive on, signed out in the board's book in his own hand. Then he went down into the delve and joined them. It was not like setting a stone. It was like tying a knot and leaving both ends in somebody else's hands.

The blank came up off its pan the width of a fingernail and hung there.

He had the medium open, and he watched it for an hour. At the end of the hour the blank sat in the medium exactly as dense and strange and hot as it had at the start. At the other end of the groove the spare had thinned by a sliver, the way a candle is a sliver shorter after an hour on a table.

Nothing touching it. Nobody feeding it. A stone feeding a stone.

He took the spare out of its cup at the end of the hour, and the blank came down onto the pan in one small movement and lay there, no lighter than the day Nevin cut it.

Armand wrote the forge's four blanks against the stock in the strongroom ledger, an ounce apiece, and then stopped with his pen over the column where he had always written what a blank was spent on.

"How long do they hold?"

"As long as they're fed."

Armand looked at him over the book. He had seen the slate and the groove and the blank on the pan, with the spare in its cup at the other end, and he had not said anything at the time. He said something now.

"That is the thing they show the first-years."

"It's the same thing."

"They show it to them so that they will never do it."

"Nobody's tied to it," Emeric said. "It's what happens to the acolyte, with a stone standing where he stood."

The old man was silent for some time. Then he wrote in the column where he had always written spent: Four ounces, for a forge. Lent. It was the first time the word had appeared in that book, and he looked at it after he had written it as though he were not certain it was allowed.

That was the feed. The feed was not enough.

A plate on four fed blanks would hang exactly as a plate on four set blanks hung, until something moved on it. Then the low corner would go on being low, however faithfully it was fed, because nothing would tell it to take more. Who tells it twice. He had looked at that line for weeks before he saw that the answer was already standing in the finishing shop, running primers for Orrin.

A deciding stone was told once. He had bound the printer's pages into stones, and they would never hold anything else. But a page was not all a deciding stone could hold. The printer's stone told the carriage here, and then here, and then here, a hundred thousand times from a single telling. A stone could just as well be told a rule once, and then tell the blanks what the rule said a thousand times an hour.

It needed eyes. On the old bench the eyes had been Bram's dish and Emeric's own, and he had been looking at the steel. So he made a reading stone. It was a small grey node like the ones in the wall's joints, cut the same way and bound the same way, at the same cost to him, one night in the delve. Where the wall's nodes looked along a street, this one looked down, from a bracket on the frame, at the dish in the middle of the plate, and it saw one thing: whether the oil touched the scribed ring all the way round, and if it did not, which side it had left. The dish held oil now instead of water. Bram had changed it without being asked, because water in a hot chamber would be gone in an hour.

The deciding stone took two nights. It was a single short rule, and it was harder to write than a whole page, because a page did not have to be right about anything that had not happened yet. When the oil leaves the ring, feed the corner it has left more lift, and the corner across from it less, and if less is not enough, heavy. When the oil sits on the ring, feed each corner what it carries and nothing over. He bound it into a stone the size of a thumb and set it outside the chamber, in a housing on the wall beside the port, where the grooves met. Thick ones ran from the cups for the spares to the deciding stone, and from it through the joints of the firebrick to the four housings under the plate. One thin scratch ran from the reading stone back to it, the way the wall carried a report home.

They ran it on the first warm night of the summer, with the door locked on both keys and the outer shutter open.

Ward stood at the door. Bram stood at Emeric's shoulder with his arms folded, looking in through the port, which was as close as he had ever chosen to stand to anything Emeric did with the medium. Armand had not come. He said the ledger would tell him in the morning, and Emeric understood that he did not want to watch the first-years' warning turned into a machine.

Emeric took four charged stones out of the spares box, put them in their cups beside the port, and signed for them in the board's book: four, to the pattern store, back by morning. By his arithmetic from the slate, an evening would cost a sliver of one.

He woke the deciding stone.

The plate came up off its legs a finger's width, all four corners together, and hung. The oil in the dish ran round to its ring and stopped there. Nothing had set it. Nothing was holding it but four charged stones on a wall, a rule in a fifth, and a small grey stone looking down at a dish.

He laid the billet on its three points, and the plate went down a hair under the weight, level, and came back up, level, and hung again.

Then he took up the heat and the form. The chip of fire stone on its copper wire went in through the port to the one place on the billet's end he wanted, and the spot went dull red, and he put the form in behind it and pushed.

In the winter the window corner had begun to go down under that push and had never come back. He felt the plate give under it now, at the corner nearest the push, by a hair. He felt the reading stone see it. It was the smallest thing he had felt in the medium in months, a flicker along the thin scratch in the firebrick, like a word said under the breath, and the deciding stone answered it before he could have. The near corner took more. The far corner took less, and then for a breath it took heavy, and the plate came back up under the billet like a hand steadying a cup. The oil ran back to its ring.

He pushed harder. It went down and came back. He pushed from the other side, and it went down there and came back there. He worked the billet's end for most of an hour, the way he had worked the printer's head, and not once in the hour did he have to think about what was under it.

Bram, at his shoulder, said nothing, and after a while unfolded his arms.

When he had finished with the billet, he wanted one thing more.

The plate was a floor. It hung on nothing, but it was a floor for whatever lay on it. Iron hot enough to shape still had a shape of its own, and sat on its three points without argument. Iron hot enough to run would take the shape of whatever it lay on, the way water takes the shape of a cup, and a floor was only a mould nobody noticed. If he wanted a bar with no mould at all, nothing could be under it.

Nothing he had built yet could hold a thing without touching it. He could.

So he took a slug off the plate, a stub of Bram's steel no bigger than a fingernail, and took it onto himself the old way, with the third drill, and held it in the middle of the chamber over the sand, touching nothing. Three ropes, on a thing the size he had always been able to manage. If he dropped it, it would fall on sand. That was what the sand was for.

He gave the chip more than he had ever given it. The slug went red, and orange, and yellow, and then a white that came through the smoked glass like the sun through cloud, and then it stopped being a slug.

He had the form ready, a bar, long and square, laid round the melt like a mould made of nothing. The heat and the form had held steel at red heat for two years without once losing it. By every number he had, the form would hold the melt where it put it.

It did not. A push only works on a thing that pushes back, and a liquid does not push back. It goes round. He felt the melt close over the edges of the form the way water closes over a hand, and draw in from the ends, and gather itself up out of every corner he gave it, pulling in toward its own middle until there was no corner left anywhere on it. It was round in less time than it took him to understand why. It hung in the middle of the chamber, a white bead, touching nothing, turning very slowly, and round it his form still stood in the shape of a bar with nothing inside.

He let the form go, and then the heat. The bead went yellow, and red, and dark. He let it down onto the sand, and the sand smoked under it.

Molten iron with nothing under it did the one thing it wanted to do, and what it wanted was to be round. Hot iron could be shaped. Molten iron could only be kept. He would work hot from now on, and leave molten to moulds. It was the first thing the forge had taught him, and he had taught it to himself in about a minute.

Bram fished the bead out of the sand with tongs when it had gone grey, and cooled it in the slack tub by the door, and carried it through to the finishing shop without a word and set it down on the surface plate.

He rolled it. It went away from his finger in a line so straight it might have been ruled, to the far edge of the plate, and he caught it and rolled it back. He put the callipers on it one way, and turned it, and another way, and another, and his face did not change at all, which with Bram meant a great deal. It was very likely the roundest thing ever made in four baronies, and it had been made by mistake. Then he took down one of Neta's balls from the bearing box, one of the dozen she had rolled between grooved plates for days on end, and put the callipers on that, and looked at the two side by side on the plate.

"She rolled for days to get them that close," he said.

"I know."

"This is closer. And it took you about as long as boiling an egg."

"It took me holding all of it," Emeric said. "The steel, the heat, and the other thing. That wasn't the forge. That was me."

Bram turned the ball over in his fingers. In the winter of the Standing Order, in the room above Kaldo, Emeric had shown him a bearing turning in the air, opened up like a cut fruit, and Bram had stood and watched it and understood in one look how much of his life he had spent pushing against his own shafts. Every shaft in the works still ran in brass and grease. Every one of them could run the way the bearing in the box ran, if there were balls enough, and true enough.

"Then it's a master," Bram said. "Neta rolls against this. Every ball she rolls, she gauges off this one." He weighed it in his palm. "Same weight of iron, same size of ball. Cut the slugs on the old man's bench to a grain and they'd all come out the same."

Emeric had not said it. He had been about to.

Bram put the ball in his apron pocket. He looked back through the door of the pattern store at the chamber, with its shutter open and the plate still hanging in the dark behind the smoked glass, level, over the sand.

"So what will it make," he said, "that mine can't?"

"Almost nothing," Emeric said. "Only things that need stones in exact places, where a hand can't put them."

Bram considered that for as long as he considered anything.

"Then it'll stand idle."

"Mostly."

Bram nodded, as though that were the right answer for a machine to have, and went home with the ball in his pocket.

Ferra came up to the long room in the morning with the four stones in her coat.

She had carried them up from the gatehouse herself, which was not her work, in her coat pocket, which was not where she carried anything, and she took them out one at a time and set them on the table in a row, square to its edge. They were grey. He did not need the medium to see that they were empty, but he opened it anyway, and there was nothing in any of them, not a sliver.

"Four, to the pattern store," Ferra read from the board's book. "Back by morning." She closed it. "It's morning."

He picked one up and turned it over. The black stone had not spent a grain. Armand's ledger would say so in the old man's hand: four ounces, lent, and every one of them still in its cup.

"If two nodes had gone dark in the night," Ferra said, "the man in the chair would have gone to the box, the way the card tells him to. He'd have found it four short, and nothing in the book to say why but your initial." She did not raise her voice. "They'll be charged again by dark. That isn't the point."

"No."

He knew why, sitting there with the dead stone in his hand, and he had known it, in a way, since the moment the deciding stone first answered the reading stone faster than he could have. The slate had been a hold that held still. The forge was a hold that kept itself still. Every push he made with the form, it answered, and every answer it made overshot by a hair and was answered back, lift at one corner and heavy at the other pulling against each other, a thousand times an hour, for an evening. And for the whole of the melt, while he held the bead himself, the plate had hung in the dark behind him on four fed stones, correcting nothing, and costing all the same. He had written the rule to answer every hair. A hold that held still cost a sliver. A hold that kept itself still paid for every correction, and paid for it twice.

His arithmetic had been right. It had been right for the slate.

"Next time," Ferra said, "sign for what it eats."

"I didn't know what it ate."

"You do now." She picked up the board's book, and then, after a moment, one of the grey stones, and put that back in her pocket as well, and he did not ask what for. "So does the book."

She went down the stair. He sat with the other three.

He had built a thing that did not spend the Crown's stone. It had spent the wall's instead, in a single evening, doing nothing more than keeping a plate level, and it had done that very well.
