Chapter 101 - Carrying the Carrier

Ferra came up to the long room eight days after the foundry with one sheet of paper, and she put it down in front of him without a word and waited.

He knew the wait. He had seen it perhaps a dozen times in ten years, and never for anything small: the night the first quarter came in above the line, the evening the census came to a number that was true. It was what she did instead of saying well done. He had learned to look forward to it more than he had ever told her.

He did not look forward to this one. He could see from the door that it was not that kind of sheet.

It was ruled in her hand, in columns, and it had been worked on for eight days. At the head of it were two figures from her own scales: the pack, full, fastened, with the buckles through it, and the pack, empty, the morning after. They were the same to a grain. Under them was Armand's log from the foundry, copied fair, every twenty of Ward's counts and the reading Emeric had given for each, and under that the count they had flown and the count the arithmetic had promised. And under all of that, where the sheet had been ruled most carefully, was a column he had not asked her for.

"Charge weighs nothing," Ferra said. "So every grain of that pack is stone, or iron, or felt. It's carried the whole way and it does nothing but hold."

"Yes."

"And every grain it carries costs it something to carry. The pack lifts Ward. It lifts itself as well. That's in Armand's log if you read it the right way round." She put her finger on the new column. "So I asked what happens if you give him more."

She had done it the way she did a quarter: with nothing assumed that could be counted instead. A pack twice as heavy held twice the charge, and it also had twice as much of itself to lift, on top of Ward. So it did not fly twice as long. It flew a good deal less than twice. Four times the pack flew not quite three times as long. A pack the weight of Ward himself, which would put an eighth of a ton of stone on a man's back and a second eighth of a ton of man under it, flew six times as long, and no more, and the whole of that was about a third of an hour.

After that the column flattened. She had carried it down the sheet anyway, because she carried everything to the foot of the page, and every line was a heavier pack and a longer count and a smaller difference between them, until the last two lines were a pack twice the weight of the one above and a count that had barely moved.

At the foot, under a ruled line, she had written the figure the column was bending toward and would never reach. A little over two thousand four hundred of Ward's counts. Forty minutes, give or take.

"There isn't a pack that flies an hour," she said. "Not on these stones. Not at any weight. You'd run out of man long before you got near it, and if you had all the stone in the world and a man made of iron to carry it, it still wouldn't fly an hour. Every stone you add has to carry itself as well as him, and after a while it's carrying almost nothing but itself."

He read it twice. There was nothing wrong with it. He had known the shape of it since he stood at the chalk line with the pack thinning three times too fast, but he had not done the arithmetic, and she had, to the foot of the page, from nothing but her own scales and an old man's log.

"You did this from the weights," he said.

"I did it from what's written down." She took her finger off the sheet. "That's what it's for."

He sat back.

It was the section again, with a man in it. In the thaw two stones off the new face had held on three times longer than the arithmetic said, and the arithmetic had been wrong because it had been done on a bench. Now the arithmetic had been done on a man in the air, and it was not wrong, and what it said was worse. There was no amount of stone that would do it. The ship on Fletcher's stocks weighed more than a hundred Wards, and the same column would bend over for her exactly as it bent for him, at exactly the same place, because the place had nothing to do with what was being carried. It was a property of the stone.

"The barony has plenty of stone," he said slowly. "Saban can bring up lamp stones by the cartload at four copper apiece, and Orrin can charge them. That was never the problem. You've found the problem."

Ferra waited.

"It's how much a pound of it holds. And how fast it can give it up without cooking its own joins." He put two fingers on her last line. "Those two. That's all this is. If a pound held three times as much, that figure goes three times as far. If the joins stopped eating it, further again. Nothing else on the page matters."

"Then those are the two you want."

"Those are the two I want."

She picked up the sheet, looked at it once more, and gave it to him to keep. He saw, as she handed it over, that she had written the heading at the top of it after all, above everything, in the same hand as every trial's book since the first. WHAT WENT WRONG. And under it, smaller, the answer, which was the first time he had ever seen her put an answer under that heading on the same day she wrote it. The stone.

The ship waited.

He had never said aloud that it was waiting for anything, and he did not say it now. Fletcher's carter came up at the end of the week with a note in the square hand that said the plating was on to the sheer strake on both sides and he was fitting her out, and that the engine Bram had sent down in the summer was bedded and turned over sweet, and nothing else. Fletcher kept building. He had been told somebody would come down and tell him something when there was something true to tell, and he had taken that at its word, and gone on.

Emeric took out the notebook that night.

Under Flight there were five lines now: the floor, heavy, the forge, the harness, and a line in the column for things he could not answer that he had crossed out in the summer. He looked at them for some time. Then he turned back up the page, above Flight, where there was a little room left between it and the rule for the matched set, and wrote one word there, a word from nowhere, like most of his words.

Cells.

He wrote nothing under it. He knew what went under it. A pound of stone that held three times what a lamp stone held. A way to take it out that did not run hot. It went above Flight because it came first. Ward had flown with nobody pouring, and the ship would fly with nobody pouring, and neither of them was going anywhere that mattered until the thing on his back weighed less than the thing it was lifting.

He took the first of the two to Armand the next morning.

"How much does a lamp stone hold?"

The old man looked at him over the week's readings. "As much as Orrin puts in it."

"And how much can Orrin put in it before it won't take any more?"

Armand considered that. It was, Emeric saw, not a question anybody had ever asked him, which was a thing he was coming to recognise on the old man's face. The tower charged a stone until it was full, and a stone was full when it began to warm under the hand, and every mage from the first year knew the feel of it. Nobody had ever weighed the stone first and then asked what it had taken, a pound against a pound.

"The bench will read a stone's charge," Armand said, "near enough. It was not built to. It reads what a stone is doing to the needle, and a full stone does more to it than an empty one." He set the readings aside. "Near enough is not a measure."

"It's more than I've got."

They did it over nine days. Orrin charged a dozen of Saban's lamp stones to warm, one at a time, and Armand weighed each empty and read each full, and wrote the two against each other in a column, and at the end of the nine days there was a figure for what a pound of lamp stone would hold that Armand would put his name to, with a note beside it in the crabbed hand to say that it was near enough and not a measure.

On the fourth of the nine days Emeric took two of the stones to the finishing shop.

Neta was at the grooved plates, rolling balls against the master Bram had brought out of the sand earlier in the summer, as she had been every afternoon since. He asked her to put a lamp stone between the plates and roll it the same way, with emery and oil, until it was round. She looked at him for a moment the way she had once looked at him across three iron plates she had been told to scrape for three weeks, and did not ask why, and did it. It took her two days. It came out a little smaller than it went in and a great deal rounder, a pale bead like a pebble off a river bed.

Orrin charged it to warm, and Armand weighed it and read it, and it held more than its weight of rough stone. Not a great deal more. Enough that the old man read it twice, on two days, before he wrote it.

"Round holds more," Armand said, "than rough, weight for weight." He looked at the bead on the pan. "I have no idea why."

"Nor have I."

"You have an idea," said Armand. "You do not yet have the reason for it. They are not the same." He wrote the figure, and the note, and put the bead in the drawer with the lead from the spring, where it sat beside the square hole the heavy blank had left, and Emeric did not ask what the drawer was for, because he had begun to understand that it was the old man's own column for things he could not answer.

Vantic came on the summer quarter day, as she always did.

She took the chair by Ferra's table, and Ferra put the barony's books in front of her one at a time, in the order they always went, and Vantic went through them with her own open beside her, and copied what she needed from every one of them into hers. It took most of the day. It always took most of the day. Emeric came in towards the end of the afternoon on a question about the ore contract and found himself staying, in the doorway, because the strongroom ledger was on the table.

She had it open at the summer's pages. He knew what was on them, because Armand had shown him each line as he wrote it. Four ounces, for a forge. Lent. Six ounces, for a harness. Lent. Four spares off the board's box, drawn and returned empty, in the board's book in his own initial, and Ferra's note beside it. And a page of the works' accounts, in Bram's hand, for firebrick, an air stone off the floor's stock, four iron legs, a slate, a groove, eleven leather tubes at four Silver the lot, and a box of forty lamp stones from the coast at four copper apiece.

Vantic read the word lent, both times. He saw her pen stop over it for the length of a breath. Then she copied it into her own book, exactly, the word and the weight and the date, and turned the page, and copied the next.

She did not ask what had been lent, or to whom, or what a forge was, or why the barony's accounts had a line for eleven leather tubes. She did not ask what a harness wanted with six ounces of the only stone in four baronies that the Crown bought at two Platinum the pound. In all the years Ferra had known her, she had asked a question about every line in every book that ever cost more than a Gold Rand. There were eleven such lines on the summer's pages. She asked about none of them.

When she had finished she closed her book and squared it on the table, and looked up, and found him in the doorway.

"Your Highness has been lending a great deal of stone this summer," she said.

"To myself."

"That is the only kind of loan my house has never been asked to write." She stood and gathered her things, and at the door she paused, the way she sometimes paused at doors, with her book held flat against her. "I have written it as you have written it. Lent. It is a very good word. It says the stone is still yours and says nothing whatever about where it is."

"That was the idea."

"I thought it might be," said Vantic, and went down the stair.

Ferra said it that night, in the long room, at the end of the day's work, when the lamp had been turned down once and not yet turned up again.

She had come up with the ore contract, which did not need her, and sat across from him with it while he read it, which it did not need either. He read it. He signed it. She took it and did not go.

"Eleven lines," she said. "Over a Gold. She asked about none of them."

"I saw."

"It isn't that she doesn't want to know." Ferra turned the pen in her fingers, his pen, end over end, the way she had once on a night in the thaw when she had not opened her ledger. "She knows. She could see from the word what the word was for. She read lent twice and copied it once and turned the page. She isn't avoiding knowing. She's avoiding writing it down."

He thought about that. He thought about a column in a dispatch book, one line a week, on the same day of the week, for six years, when the foundry was lit and when it was cold, and a woman who wrote when the week ended and not when anything happened.

"Somebody reads her book," he said.

"Every week. On a day they chose." Ferra put the pen away inside her coat. "If she writes a question, the question's in the book, and whoever reads the book reads the question. If she writes lent and nothing else, then that's all there is to read. She's keeping us out of her own ledger." She was quiet for a moment. "I'd do the same. If it were me, and somebody was reading mine."

"Nobody reads yours."

"No," Ferra said. "Nobody reads mine."

She said it as she said everything, plainly, as a figure. But she said it looking at him and not at the page, and she stayed where she was for some time after she had said it, with her hands folded on the shut ledger and the lamp going down between them, and neither of them turned it up.

When she went she stopped at the door, as Vantic had, with her hand on the frame.

"Cells," she said. "You wrote it above Flight."

He had not shown her the notebook. Nobody could read the notebook.

"You could see the page from where you were sitting," he said.

"I could see where on the page." She did not smile. "It was the right place for it."

She said goodnight in the voice she said it in every night, and went down the gallery, and he heard her door close. He sat on with the arithmetic in front of him, her sheet with its column bending over at the foot toward a number it would never reach, and read the answer she had written under her own heading, twice, as if it might have changed. It had not. The stone.
