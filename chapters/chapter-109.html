Chapter 109 - The Room Above Kaldo

Ferra brought the candle.

She had marked it herself that afternoon, in her new room, with a rule and a penknife: a plain tallow candle of the kind Maren bought by the gross, with a notch cut into it at every half hour of burning, which she had found by lighting another just like it at noon and watching it with her pen in her hand until the first notch, and then the second, and then blowing it out. She set it on the table in Emeric's room in a brass stick and lit it from the fire and sat down beside it.

"Half hours," she said. "I'll not be able to read it from in there. Somebody out here will."

"Declan."

Declan was on the door, outside it, on a stool in the passage with his back to the wood and a crossbow across his knees, because the first act of the Standing Order said that nothing in Kaldo woke one man, and seven people with their eyes shut in one room at the top of the keep was, Ward had said, a thing that wanted a man awake outside it. Declan had been told that the six would be at a long meeting and must not be disturbed for anything short of fire, and had nodded, and not asked what kind of meeting needed a crossbow on its door, because Declan did not ask things.

It was the thirteenth of February. A fortnight since the reading. Emeric had chosen the day by the moon, because there was none, and by Saban, who had come in off the Milford road the night before and could be kept in Kaldo by nothing but a promise.

They sat where they could. Armand had his own chair, carried up by Arthur, by the fire. Bram had the end of the bed, Imani the other end. Ward stood, then sat on the floor with his back against the wall under the window, with his long legs out, because Ward would not sit on a bed in a prince's room. Saban had the stool from the table. Ferra had the chair at the table beside her candle.

Emeric sat on the floor in the middle of them, cross-legged, on the rug, with his mother's stone in his hand.

"Hold it," he said. "In your hand. Shut. You don't need to squeeze it. When I go, you'll feel me go, and you'll feel a place behind the stone, like a door at the end of a passage with a light under it. Go to the light. Don't push. Pushing doesn't work. Nobody's ever got in by pushing." He looked at Saban. "You'll fight it. Everybody does the first time. When you've stopped fighting it, you'll be through."

"And if I don't stop fighting it?" said Saban.

"Then you'll sit on a stool for an hour with your eyes shut," said Bram, "and feel a fool."

"Did you?"

"No. I put my hand on his shoulder at my own table and fell in. First time. I've never been sure that was better."

Saban looked at Bram as if that were the most reassuring thing anybody had said to him in a year.

"I was four tries," said Ferra, without looking up from her candle. "Three nights I sat and felt a fool. It passes."

Emeric shut his eyes.



Bram came in almost at once, the way a man comes through his own forge door, without looking at it.

Then nobody, for a while.

Emeric stood in the room and felt for them at its edge, the five of them still outside, each a weight on a thread that ran back out of here to a stone in a hand by a fire. Ferra, small and hard and close. Imani, further off, wide, warm. Ward, a wall. Armand, enormous and slow and stuttering at the heart. And Saban, whom he had never felt from here before: a quick dry thing, light, like a dead leaf blown along a lane, never settled.

Saban came in.

He came in on his first try, all at once, between one breath and the next, and stood in the room beside Bram blinking and turning his hat in his hands, which he had somehow brought in with him, and Bram looked at him with his mouth open.

"That's not fair," said Bram.

"Is it not?" said Saban. He was looking round. "I've been through worse doors than that. In worse company." He turned his hat once more. "A door's a door. You see a light under one, you go in quick before somebody shuts it."

Armand came next, as easily as he had come in the night of the frost, standing straight without his stick at Saban's shoulder. Emeric felt him begin to count at once, the drain going down the thread out of him, the steady draw on the lamp stone on the long night, and felt him keep counting it, every breath, and did not say anything, because there was nothing to say to Armand about counting that Armand did not already know.

Ferra came in on her second try. She came in sideways, as she had the first time, years ago, as if she had found the door by walking round the back of the house, and stood with her hands folded in front of her and did not look round at anything. She looked at Emeric.

"Imani's gone," she said.

She had. He had felt her at the edge, wide and warm, and then not. Not out. Not back in her body on the end of his bed. Somewhere else, at the edge, wandering, the way a person wanders out of a house they have been shown round to look at the garden. He felt for her, and found her, a long way off, near and not near, and did not pull.

"She'll come back," he said.

"How do you know?"

"Because she's Imani, and she's looking at something."

She came back. He did not know how long it was; it was not the kind of place where he could tell. She came in through the door that was not a door with her sleeves pinned up and a crease between her brows, and stood at the edge of the room, and looked round it as she would have looked round a ward someone else had laid out, finding everything in the wrong place.

"Where did you go?" said Emeric.

"I don't know." She was still looking round. "Out. There's out. You didn't say there was out." She looked at him. "I'll go again sometime and find where."

Ward was last. Four tries. Emeric felt each of them at the edge: Ward coming down the passage to the door in a straight line at a steady pace, and stopping, and standing outside it like a man waiting to be told to go in. The fourth time he did not stop. He came in, and stood, and looked round once, the way he looked round a gate yard, and then, because there was nobody in the room who was not his, let his hands come out from behind his back.



The room was not the room they knew.

Bram had seen it with a bench and a light. Ferra had seen it with the bench and a ledger on it. Imani had seen it with a scrubbed table and a shelf of jars and an old chair. Armand had seen it with twelve opened stones in a row. Emeric had spent the fortnight since the reading making it into something none of them had seen.

It was a floor of glass. That was the only way he had been able to think of it. A great clear floor, flat, without seams, running out level from where they stood to where the light stopped, and on it, under it, shaped out of the same glass and standing up off it in relief, Kaldo.

All of it. The circle of the curtain wall with its towers. The South Gate and the moat bridge. The inner ward raised in the middle, and the keep on it, and the canal ring round it with its little arched bridges like stitches. The lanes going down from the inner ward to the wall in steps. The river along the east side, flat and dark in the glass, and the east bridge, and the dock quarter beyond it, and the works on the far bank with its wheel. The new shed outside the north wall, with the line of a keel inside it. Every roof. As far as he had got.

It was built out of what he had. The tithe map from the first spring, which was the oldest thing in the barony's chest and wrong in a dozen places. His own wall plan, from the sensor's year. And four years of walking. Wherever he had walked, it was sharp: every lane he had been down a hundred times, the high street, the way up to the ward, the square, the water gate, the drains under the east wall, which he had crawled. Wherever he had not walked, it went grey. Not wrong. Blurred, like a drawing somebody had started and smudged with the side of a hand, the shape of a roof and not the roof, the line of a lane and not the doors in it. There were whole corners of the lower ward that were nothing but grey. There was a quarter of the dock quarter he had never once gone into, and it was a fog.

Nobody said anything for some time.

Then Saban walked out onto the glass. He walked as if it might break, and it did not, and he went across it in his boots, over the moat and the wall and down into the town, a giant striding over the roofs, and stopped above the square and looked down at the exchange.

"That's wrong," he said. "The exchange has a lean-to on the back. You've not got the lean-to."

"I've never been round the back."

"There's a lean-to," said Saban. "With a water butt. And a dog." He looked up. "Can I put it in?"

"Put your hand on it," Emeric said, "and think of it as it is. Not as it should be. As it is."

Saban crouched over the exchange. He put out his hand, and touched the glass roof, and frowned. And on the back of the exchange, slowly, as if it were being pushed up out of the floor from underneath, a lean-to grew. Low. Crooked. With a water butt at the corner. There was no dog, because the room did not do dogs, but there was a ring in the wall where a dog would be tied.

Saban stood up and looked at it. Then he looked at Emeric.

"Well," he said, very quietly. "Well, now."



After that it was like a barn raising.

Ferra did it in census order. Emeric had known she would; it was how she knew the town, not by its lanes but by its households, and she started at the top of the inner ward with the first house on the roll and went down it, door by door, in the order she had counted them in the first summer, and wherever a house was grey she put her hand on it and it came up sharp. She was fast. She did not hesitate at a door. She knew how many people had lived behind every one of them on the day she counted, and how many lived there now, and he watched the grey go out of the lower ward a street at a time in front of her like frost going off a window.

Bram corrected her twice.

The first time it was a chimney. She had put a chimney on the cooper's in the wrong place, in the middle of the roof, and Bram leaned over her shoulder and said no, it was at the end, he had rebuilt it in the plague year when the old one came down in the gale, and he put his own big hand on the roof and the chimney moved.

The second time it was a whole house. Ferra had it on the roll as a house, with four people in it, and Bram said it was two, a house and a shed, and the shed had been knocked through into the house by the man who lived there, and the four lived in the shed because the house leaked. Ferra looked at it. Then she looked at her father.

"The roll says a house," she said.

"The roll's wrong," said Bram. "I mended his door."

Ferra did not say anything at once. Then she took her hand off the roof and let him do it.

Imani did the water.

She did not walk the town the way Ferra did. She walked it the way she had walked it in the first year with her father's razor in her pocket and a boy carrying her bag: by the wells. She went from well to well, the north well and Foulard Street's and the two low ones by the rows that had been shut since the second spring, and at every one she crouched and put her hand flat on the glass beside it, and the ground round the wells came up out of the glass darker than the rest. Wet. Where the water lay under the lanes in the winter and came up through the floors of the low houses. Emeric had never been able to see that. He had known it from her map, and from the pipe, and from the drain, but he had never seen the shape of it laid out under the whole town at once, a long dark stain running down from the inner ward to the river like a shadow under the roofs.

And on the edge of the dock quarter she stopped at two houses that Ferra's census had marked empty.

"Not empty," Imani said. "Salt boat. There's a family off the salt boat in that one, came up in the autumn, the man works the boat and the woman's had a baby since. And three men in that one. I've had two of them in the ward with their hands." She looked across the glass at Ferra. "They're not on your roll because they came in by the water and nobody asked them."

Ferra looked at the two houses for some time.

"Then I'll ask them," she said.

Saban was wrong.

He was wrong confidently and at length and with a good deal of pleasure, because he had grown up in Kaldo and left it before the plague, and he went across the lower ward putting things back. A smithy where there was a yard. A bakehouse that had burned in the plague year. Two whole houses on a lane by the east wall that Emeric knew for a fact had been pulled down for the drain, because he had watched them pulled down, and Saban put them back up, stone by stone, with their shutters and their window boxes, and stood looking at them with his hat in his hands and an expression on his face that Emeric had not seen on it before.

"They're gone," Emeric said, as gently as he could. "Those two. We pulled them down for the drain. The second summer."

"I know," said Saban. He did not take his hand off them. "I know they're gone." He looked at them a little longer. "I bought my bread at that one. When I was small enough to be sent."

He took his hand away, and they stayed. Emeric took them down himself, later, when nobody was looking, and left a mark where they had been.

But Saban was right about one thing, and he was more right about it than anyone in the room had been about anything. On the lane behind the north well there was a house that Ferra had brought up sharp and Imani had passed by, and Saban stopped at it on his way back across the town and crouched and frowned and put his hand on the door, and the door changed. It changed colour. It had been grey glass, like everything, and it went a deep dull red, the colour of old brick, with a split in the bottom panel and a nail driven in at a slant to hold the split shut. And a knocker. A ring of black iron, worn bright at the bottom where it struck.

"Red," said Saban. "Ox-blood. The man paints it every spring, out of a pot he's had since before the war, and he's never had enough to do the bottom panel because he's always run out by then." He stood up. "I stood across the lane from that door for a week, once. In the autumn. Watching who went in." He did not say who, or why, and nobody asked him.

Ferra was standing looking at the red door.

"This room," she said, "is exactly as true as the last person who corrected it."

Nobody argued.



Ward walked down into it.

He did it while the others were busy, without saying anything. He went over the curtain wall on the north side and stepped down off the glass into the town, and the town grew round him, or he shrank into it, Emeric could not tell which, until he was standing in the lane by the north well no taller than a man, among the roofs, with the wall going up beside him the height of a wall.

Then grey men came over it.

Emeric had not put them there. He had not known the room would do it. But Ward had stood on that wall in the war with the ladders coming up, and he had stood on it every night since in his head, and he had brought them in with him the way Imani had brought in her jars: a line of men without faces, grey, coming over the north wall where it was lowest, at the place where the walk narrowed behind the second tower, with ladders.

Ward stood in the lane and watched them come. Then he moved his hand, and the garrison came up out of the town to meet them, grey too, but with something about the way they stood that was Kaldo's and not anybody else's: the crestless helmets, the crossbows, Osric's wide stance at the head of the stair. They met on the walk. Emeric watched the attack go wrong, at the narrow place, three ladders at once where the garrison had planned for two, and the walk go, and grey men come down into the lane. Then Ward moved his hand again, and it all went back, the men off the wall and the ladders off it and the garrison down into the town, and he started it again with the third ladder further along, and this time he had a man at the narrow place before it came up.

He did it nine times. Emeric counted. He did not stop him.



Then Emeric did the thing he had been afraid of, because he had to know.

He put himself into the town. Not himself; a grey copy, a man the shape of him, walking down from the keep on a Tuesday morning, the way he walked it every Tuesday: the keep stair, the inner ward, the high street, the exchange, Ferra's trestle, the works across the bridge, the ward. He knew his own week. He knew it better than anything in the town. He set the grey man going and stood back and watched him walk it, and the others stopped what they were doing and watched too.

The grey man went down the high street. He crossed the square. He stopped outside the exchange and went in, and came out, and turned toward the water gate.

"He's late," said Ferra.

"He's what?"

"He's late. You came in this morning at nine. That's how you came in last Tuesday. This Tuesday you came in at ten, because you'd been up the hill with Tess, and you came in by the side door, not the front, because the front was frozen." She was looking at the grey man. "And you didn't go to the water gate after. You went to the keep, for the board."

The grey man walked down the lane to the water gate, perfectly, the way Emeric had walked it a hundred Tuesdays.

"And the cooper's isn't open," said Imani. "He's got the shutters up. He's been in my ward since Thursday with his chest."

The cooper's shutters were down, in the glass. The grey man walked past them.

It had taken them a few minutes. Not longer. Emeric stood and looked at his own week going on in front of him, in perfect order, through a town that had not been doing it for days, and felt something he had not let himself feel about the room since the first night it opened: that it could lie to him, and he would believe it, because it was in his own hand.

"It's a memory," he said. "That's all it is. It's what we've put in it, exactly as we put it in, and it stays like that. It doesn't know what happened this morning. It never will." He let the grey man stop. "It's never an eye. It's never going to show us what's happening. Only what we've told it."

"Then we'll have to keep telling it," said Ferra.

"Every week," said Imani.

"More," said Saban.



Armand had been counting all this while. Emeric had felt him do it, on the thread, every breath, the drain going down out of the old man like sand through a glass, and the others too, fainter, each of them paying for the room by the minute. He had felt it get worse as they worked. Every roof they brought up sharp cost something, and every grey man of Ward's cost a great deal.

"Stop counting," said Ferra.

Armand turned his head.

"You've been counting since you came in," said Ferra. "I can see you doing it. You keep stopping in the middle of a sentence." She had her hands folded in front of her. "If it's that bad, say. If it's not, stop."

Armand looked at her.

"It's not bad," he said. "It's interesting."

And then, because he was who he was, and could not leave a thing that was interesting alone, he did something nobody had thought of doing.

He poured.

Emeric felt it. Not the draw going out of him, the other way: a great slow push of mana coming down the thread from the old man's body by the fire and into the room, deliberately, the way a man pours oil into a lamp that is burning low, and the room took it. It went into the glass. It went into the walls that were not walls. And the drain on every one of them, the steady draw that had been pulling at Emeric since he came in, eased. Not stopped. Halved. He felt Ferra feel it, and Bram, and saw Ward look up from his wall.

"Ah," said Armand. He sounded pleased with himself. "It burns its own. It's not taking it from us to build things. It's taking it to stay lit. If somebody puts in enough to keep it lit, the rest of you only pay for what you build." He looked round at the room with enormous satisfaction. "That's a stone. That's how a stone works. It's a very large stone."

"You're paying for all of us," said Emeric.

"I'm paying for the lamp," said Armand. "You're all paying for your own suppers. It's a sound arrangement. I've lived on worse."



He had brought something in for each of them. He had thought about it for most of the fortnight, at night: what each of them would want in a room where a thing could be made exactly and kept exactly and looked at as long as you liked.

For Bram it was a bearing.

It sat on the bench at the edge of the glass, a ring of steel the size of a man's fist, cut in half so that the inside showed: two rings, one inside the other, and between them a row of balls, perfectly round, perfectly alike, each sitting in a groove, each a little apart from the next in a cage of thin brass. He had drawn it once on a slate for Bram in the first year and Bram had looked at it and said nobody could make balls that round, and Emeric had agreed, and put the slate away.

Bram picked it up. He turned the inner ring with his thumb, and it went round, smooth, without a sound, with the balls turning in their cage, and he turned it again, and then held the outer ring still and spun the inner one hard and watched it go and go and go.

"There's no rub," he said.

"Hardly any. The balls roll. Nothing slides."

"Every shaft in the works rubs." Bram was still watching it turn. "The wheel shaft. The engine's. The lathe. I feed them tallow every morning and they eat it and they wear and every year I'm sleeving another one." He looked up. "How long have you known about this?"

"Since before I came to Kaldo."

Bram looked at him for a long breath, with the bearing still turning in his hand.

"And you let me sleeve shafts."

"You couldn't make the balls," said Emeric. "Nobody can. Not yet. You need a steel that's hard enough and a way to grind it round to a hair, and we've got neither. But you can make the rest, in here, and look at it, and take it apart, and work out where the cage goes and what it wants to be made of." He nodded at the bench. "I'll make you the balls in here. Perfect ones. You'll have the rest worked out by the time we can make them out there."

Bram looked at the bearing. Then he sat down on the floor of the room, on the glass, over the river, with his back to the bench, and began to take it apart.

For Imani it was a table.

A scrubbed table, like the one she had brought into the room herself the first time, at the edge of the glass on the other side, and on it, under a sheet, a body. Not a real one. A man of no particular age, made the way he had made the back of the girl on the boards in the summer of the war, the way Imani had corrected it: everything where it went, every vessel, every nerve. She lifted the sheet and looked at it, and he saw her understand what it was before he told her.

"You can open it," he said. "As often as you like. And when you've done, you put the sheet back and it's whole again, and you can open it again. The same man. Every time."

Imani looked at the body under her hand a while. Then she looked at him across the table.

"The same man," she said.

"Yes."

"I've opened four dead men in my life," said Imani. "And every one of them was dead and I had an hour and a priest at the door. And I've had a hundred I'd have given a year to look inside, alive, and couldn't." She put the sheet back down over the face, carefully, the way she would have done it on the ward. "I'll want a woman too."

"You'll have to correct her."

"I'll correct everything," said Imani. She did not take her hand off the sheet. "Every one you make. You've read about backs. I've held them."

For Ward it was nothing on a bench. Emeric did not give him anything. Ward had already found it, on the north wall, nine times.

"You can drill here," Emeric said. "As long as you like. With whatever you can remember."

Ward looked at the town, at the north wall, at the place where the walk narrowed behind the second tower.

"Yes," he said. That was all.

For Armand it was a slate.

A stone the size of a roof slate, flat and grey and blank, lying on the bench where the twelve watch stones had lain on the night of the frost, and beside it a stylus, and nothing else. Armand looked at it for a while before he understood. Then he picked up the stylus.

"It's a stone," he said. "It's an empty stone. The size of a table."

"You can't cut a stone out there. Your hands shake. In here they don't." Emeric looked at the old man's hand on the stylus, which was steady. "Cut on that. Anything you like. Cut what you know, the old signs, the tower's. Then take them apart and see what's in them. It won't fill and it won't fire. It'll hold the lines."

Armand put the stylus to the slate and drew a line on it, short and straight, and it stayed there, bright, the way the lines stayed in the watch stones.

"That's the bench," he said. He sounded as if he had been hit. "That's the bench in my study in the tower. That's what I wanted, for thirty years. A bench where the stone holds still and my hand holds still and the line stays where I put it." He drew another. "That's the bench."

And for Saban it was the edge of the map.

The glass went on past the town. It went on past the fields and the woods to where the light stopped, and from the north wall to the edge of the light it was empty. Flat clear glass with nothing on it at all.

"I've never been anywhere," said Emeric. "Not really. The capital and the road between. You've been everywhere."

Saban walked out to the north edge of the town and stood at the end of the fields and looked out at the empty glass. Then he crouched down and put his hand on it.

A road came up out of the glass under his hand. It went north. It went past the woods, and over a river at a ford, and up a long slope and down, and on into the empty glass, and as it went other things came up beside it: a village, a crossroads with an inn, a toll bar, a stand of trees by a bridge where a man could stand and see the road both ways. Saban did not stop. He walked beside the road as it grew, and the road went on north, and when it had gone further than Emeric could follow it bent west, and there was another river there, broad, and a town on it with a great house on a hill above it and, at the bridge foot, a long low house with a yard.

The crossing. Wayford's.

Saban stood beside it, a long way off, small, on the glass.

"That's the back door," he said. "The one the bags go in by. And that's the window where they light the lamp." He did not look round. "I'll put Sethe's hall in, next time. And the halls round it where his companies are sitting. I've not been to them all yet." He stood up. "I'll go."



Ferra did not get anything. He had not known what to give her. He had thought about it more than about any of the others and come to the end of the fortnight with nothing, because anything he could make for her in the room she could already make better herself.

She did not seem to mind. She stood by the bench and watched the others with her hands folded in front of her, and when they were all busy, Bram on the floor with his bearing in pieces and Imani at her table and Armand bent over his slate and Ward on the wall and Saban walking north, she came across the glass to where Emeric stood.

"You want something," she said.

"Yes."

"You've wanted it since the reading. You've been waiting till everybody was busy."

"Yes."

"Ask."

He looked at the town under the glass, sharp now from wall to wall where they had been, and the red door by the north well.

"A file," he said. "On everyone in Kaldo. Everyone on your rolls, and everyone who isn't and should be. Not what they owe. Who they are. Who they're married to. Their children, where they go to school, who minds them. Their brothers in other baronies. What they're afraid of." He kept his eyes on the town. "Garrin found Rylan's handle before we'd ever written his son's name down. He knew Finn's name, and Wren's, and Nessa's, and he said all four in a doorway, and we didn't know Rylan had a son who could be taken from school because nobody in this barony ever wrote down who was at school. Garrin did. Somebody in Kaldo told him." He looked at her. "I'm not going to have another man made into a door."

"No," said Imani.

She had come across from her table. She stood with her sleeves pinned and her hands held a little away from her sides, the way she held them when they were wet, and her face was the face she wore at the ward door for men who came to tell her how to run it.

"A file on everyone in Kaldo," she said. "What they're afraid of. Who they love." She looked at Ferra and then at him. "Do you know what that is? It's what Garrin had. It's what he used. You'll have it in a box in the keep, and one day it won't be you holding the box."

"It'll be mine," said Ferra.

Imani turned.

"It'll be mine," Ferra said again. She did not raise her voice. "Not the prince's. Not the keep's. Mine. And I'll keep it on three terms, or I won't keep it." She held up one finger. "Nobody reads it but me and him. Not Ward. Not Saban. Not you. Not the next holder of Kaldo, whoever he is." Another. "The day he dies, I burn it. All of it. Before the body's cold. I'll not wait for anyone to tell me I can." A third. "And it's in my cipher, from the first line. Nothing in it is ever written out plain on any paper, for any reason. If he wants to know a thing in it, he comes to my room and I tell him, and he doesn't take notes."

Imani looked at her.

"And if they take you," she said. "Like they took him."

"Then they'll have a box of pages they can't read," said Ferra, "and me." She put her hand down. "I count slower when I'm frightened. I'd count very slowly."

Imani looked at her for a while longer. Then she looked at Emeric, and it was not a look he liked, and he did not look away from it.

"I don't agree," she said. "I'll say so at the table. In front of all of them. And Ferra can write it down."

"I will," said Ferra.

"Good," said Imani, and went back to her table.

Ward had come down off the north wall at some point and was standing at the edge of the glass, and Ferra went and held out her hand to him, and there were pages in it. Three of them, closely written, in a script that was not a script, small rows of signs that were not letters and not numbers. She had made them in the room while they were talking, the first three pages of the file, the keep's own household, the people who lived on the stair.

Ward took them. He looked at them. He turned them over, and looked at the backs, which were blank, and turned them back, and held them in his big square hands as if they were something he had been asked to carry across a river without getting wet.

"I cannot read this," he said.

"No," said Ferra.

Ward considered the pages.

"Good," he said, and gave them back.



When they came out, Declan had relit the candle twice.

He told them so through the door. Ferra's candle had burned down past its third notch and guttered and gone out, and Declan had come in on tiptoe with his crossbow and found seven people sitting in the dark with their eyes shut and the fire low, and had relit the candle from the fire and gone out again, and done it again an hour later, and had not woken anybody, because nobody had told him to.

Two hours. They had been inside for what had felt like the best part of a day.

Nobody spoke for a while. Bram sat on the end of the bed with his elbows on his knees and his head in his hands. Imani lay back across the foot of the bed with her arm over her eyes. Saban had dropped his hat and not picked it up. Ferra sat at the table looking at her candle as though she did not know what it was for. Armand was asleep in his chair, all at once and very deeply, with his mouth open and his breath slow and even, and Emeric reached across and put two fingers on his wrist and found the beat and the space and the beat, no worse than it had been, and took his fingers away.

Ward was still sitting on the floor under the window with his back against the wall and his legs out. His eyes were open. He was looking at nothing. His face was grey and wet with sweat in the cold room, and his hands, which Emeric had never in fifteen years seen do anything but what Ward told them, were not quite still on his knees.

"Ward."

"Nine times," Ward said. He did not look round. "They came over the north wall nine times. And I lost the walk four of them." He looked at his hands. "I will not sleep tonight."

He did not. Emeric heard him, much later, on the wall walk under the window, going up and down the north stretch alone in the dark until the sky went grey.



When they had all gone down the stair, slowly, in ones and twos, holding the wall, Emeric sat on the rug by the dead fire with his mother's stone in his hand and did not go to bed.

He thought about Rylan.

About a man standing in his own doorway at dusk with a stranger saying his children's names, and going in, and shutting the door, and sitting down at his own table with it, alone. Choosing the board. Choosing six nights in seven. Asking Declan two questions on a cold wall about which stretch went blind. Leaving a letter in Doran's box in a hand that ran downhill. Doing all of it, every step of it, by himself, because Garrin had told him that if he told anyone, the captain or the prince or his priest, the boy would go into the river, and Garrin had people in Kaldo who would tell him.

Rylan had not known whether that was true. He had said so, on the seventh morning, standing in his polished harness in the middle of the floor. He had not been able to find out without it being true.

Nobody in Kaldo would have told Garrin anything. Emeric was nearly sure of it now, with the town lying sharp under the glass in his head, every door of it. Garrin had stood across a lane for a week, the way Saban had stood across from the red door, and watched a boy go to school. That was all it had taken. And Rylan had had nobody to ask, because he had decided there was nobody, and he had never checked.

He would have asked Ward. If he had known that Ward could be asked. If there had been anything in Kaldo, anything at all, that told a man in a doorway at dusk that there was somebody he could go to who would not let his son into the river.

Emeric turned the stone over in his hand.

Then he got up, and put more wood on the fire, and lit a candle from it, and sat down at the table under the window, and began to write out the first page of the next book for Ward. Moving Unseen. And under it, on the same page, before anything else, a line that had not been in any book he had ever read: If a man comes to your door and says your children's names, come to the captain. Whatever he tells you. The captain will not let them into the river.

He looked at it. It was not a thing a book could make true.

He left it in.
