Chapter 109 - Stations

They went down to the lower water in the first week of the autumn, in the steamboat, with the tide.

It was more cargo than she had carried since the ore run. Nine crates of cells lashed in her waist under a tarpaulin, twenty to a crate, every cell the forge had made since the summer, packed in straw with their caps screwed down and their splinters dull, because Orrin would fill them at the other end and not before. A long box of stone rods from the forge, each in its own groove of felt. Bram's tools. Two reading stones and a deciding stone in a small iron case that Emeric kept on his knees. And a second case, smaller, with a lock on it that took Armand's key, which held the stones for the stations.

They had come off the new face, all of them, cut by Nevin in the pattern store over three weeks and read by Armand on three days each. Sixteen of them were entered in the strongroom ledger under a heading in the crabbed hand. The station set. Lent. The other eight, cut from the same face in the same weeks and read on the same days, had no heading at all, because Armand would not give a heading to a thing that was not yet anything. He had brought the ledger down on his own knees. Stones did not leave the strongroom without the book going with them.

He sat in the stern with it and watched the banks go by, and said very little, and when they passed the place where the section had fallen in the thaw he looked at it for as long as it was in sight and then did not look back.

Ward came because the levers were his. Bram came because the housings were, and the engine in her, and he brought his lad, who fired the steamboat on the river and whose page in Ferra's census had nothing on it that anybody could take hold of, to learn a boiler twice the size.

Fletcher was on the beach when they grounded. He was standing at the foot of his stocks with his arms folded, and he had evidently been standing there some time.

The hull stood on the stocks behind him.

Emeric had seen her in drawings for two years and in Fletcher's letters for one, and he had seen the section, and none of it had prepared him for her size. She was iron from keel to sheer, riveted in plates the width of a door, black with the first coat and shining where the tide had caught her, and she stood on her stocks above the high-water mark with her bow toward the bar like something that had come up out of the sea to rest. A hundred feet on the master foot from her stem to her sternpost, and twenty across at her widest. Her frames stood at their stations under the plating, every one of them where the drawing said, heavier than any join needed. Her stack rose a little forward of her middle, and aft of it the engine Bram had sent down in the summer was bedded in her and turned over sweet. Her screw hung at her stern on an iron arm that pivoted at the transom, so that it could be swung down into the water behind her or up and clear of it, a thing Fletcher had built exactly as drawn in the spring and asked about only once, by letter, to be told it was for beaching her. She had no mast, and no step for one, and no sail anywhere on her, which the lower water had found stranger than the iron. At each of her quarters, and at her bow, there was a housing let into the plating the size of a man's head, with nothing in it yet, which the drawing said was for pumps.

And there were the holes. Low in her frames, at the turn of the bilge, sixteen of them, eight to a side, round and clean and each the size of a man's fist, as they had been in the section. The six had drilled them to the drawing, and had been told when they did it what Fletcher had been told, that they were for stonework his highness would fit himself. They had asked nothing more. Stonework was not a shipwright's business, and Fletcher had made it plain that it was not theirs.

She was not quiet. There was a mallet going somewhere inside her, and a saw, and a man singing under his breath the way men do when they have forgotten they are doing it. The oldest of Fletcher's six had come to her rail with a plane in his hand to see who had grounded, a grey man with a burn across the back of one hand that had come from a rivet forge twenty years before, and he looked down at them for some time with no expression at all.

Fletcher did not say anything. He waited, as he had waited through two letters and a bill and a summer.

Emeric walked past him to the hull and put his hand on the plating, where it was cold, over the hole in the nearest frame.

"You asked what I'd done to her," he said. "I told you I'd come down and tell you when there was something true to tell. There is." He did not look up at the rail. "When your men have gone home."

Fletcher looked at him, and up at the old man with the plane, and back. Whatever he made of it, he kept.

"Then you'll see what you've paid for first," he said, and went up the ladder.

He took him through her the way he had built her, from the bow.

Forward, at the foot of a ladder under a scuttle, a wall of iron plate was riveted across the whole of her from side to side, and beyond it, in the narrow of her bow, was a space for her cable and her spare canvas and her paint and nothing else. Fletcher put his hand flat on the plate.

"Your drawing had four of these across her," he said. "I thought you'd drawn them to stiffen her. Then the section came back down the river with its ends shut, and I sat and looked at it for a week before I saw what they were." He took his hand away. "She puts her nose on a rock, the sea has this and no more. She opens a seam by the engine, the sea has the engine and no more. Four walls, five rooms, and she'll swim with any one of them full." He said it the way he said a price he had checked twice. "Forty years I've built hulls that went down with one hole in them. I never thought to build one that wouldn't."

Aft of the wall were the berths for her hands. Eight of them, in two tiers along her sides, each with a board along its edge to keep a sleeping man in when she rolled and a locker under it for his gear, and a table hinged to the partition that folded up flat when nobody was eating off it. There was a hook for every man's coat. The room smelled of new oak and linseed, and it was warm, though nothing in it was lit.

"Eight to work her at sea," Fletcher said. "Fire and deck and a cook, watch and watch. I've given them the bow, because that's where she moves most, and the men who work her ought to know how she moves." He opened a door in the partition behind him. "And they're next the galley. Men who work her ought to be warm."

The galley was the width of her and not much longer than a man. The range in it had come down from the Kaldo foundry on the same barge as the engine: an iron stove with an oven in its belly and a rail round its top so that a pot could not walk off it in a sea, and a copper at its back for hot water, and its flue led aft through the iron wall behind it into the stack's own uptake, so that she had one chimney and not two. Across from it stood a sink lined with lead, and over the sink a small brass pump.

Fletcher worked the handle twice, and nothing came.

"Nothing in the tank yet," he said. "Iron tank, under your feet. Another the same aft. Your drawing said a week of water for everybody she sleeps. I made it ten days, because a week at sea is never a week." He let go of the handle. "Casks go foul. Iron doesn't, if a man washes it out."

Beside the galley, behind a latched door, was the store: lidded bins for flour and meal and biscuit, because the mice at the lower water would swim out to anything that floated, and shelves with a rail along each, and low down against her plating, below where her waterline would come, a locker that was cold to the hand even now, on a dry beach in the sun.

"Meat," said Fletcher. "The sea keeps it. That's the one thing the sea's good for, after floating."

Beyond the second iron wall was the boiler, with its coal in bunkers either side of it, lying between the fire and the sea. Aft of the boiler was the engine, and beside the engine a pair of bilge pumps, real ones, one to be worked by hand and one off the engine, which had been drawn as well, so that the three housings in her plating that were not pumps would have company. Bram went down the ladder into the engine room with his lad behind him and did not come back up.

Aft of the engine, beyond the third wall, was the hold. There was a hatch over it in the deck, and in it nothing at all but an oak rack running across her, bored with rows of holes like a rack for bottles, and on her centreline a bracket over a place where something was meant to swing.

"I've built a good many holds," Fletcher said, looking down into it. "I never built one to be empty."

"Tonight," said Emeric.

Fletcher looked at him and went aft.

The companion aft came down beside the wheel into a room the width of her, with a skylight let into the deck over it, glazed with the glassworks' glass, so that the room was full of the grey light off the bar. A table ran down the middle of it, bolted through the floor, with a fiddle round its edge and a bench either side, and from the beams over it hung four brass cages with lamp stones in them, because Fletcher would have no flame below in a hull full of new oak but the range's. One of the six was on his knees under the table with a brace and bit. He got up when they came in and went out without being told, and Emeric heard him on the ladder, and on the deck, and then not at all.

It was the table he had drawn. He had drawn it the size of the one in the long room, because that was the only size of table he had ever needed, and he stood and looked at it for longer than he meant to.

Off the room, two to either side, were four cabins, each about the size of a horse's stall. Each had two berths, one over the other, with boards, and a locker, and a hook, and a round port in her side the size of a dinner plate, with a hinged iron cover that screwed down over the glass. Fletcher showed him the cover.

"Deadlight," he said. "In a sea you screw it down, and the sea can break on her all it likes. Every port in her's got one."

Across her stern, the width of her, was one more room. Three ports looked out of her transom above the place where the screw's arm pivoted. There was a desk that let down from the bulkhead on two chains, and a locker with a hasp for a padlock, and against the forward wall a bed. It was built in like a berth, with boards to its sides, but it was half as wide again as any berth in her.

"I drew a berth," Emeric said.

"You drew a berth." Fletcher did not apologise for it. "It's a prince's ship. A prince's ship goes where princes go, and wherever she goes somebody's going to be shown round her, and the first thing they'll want to see is where you sleep. I'll not have it said on any coast that a hull off my beach put a king's son on a shelf." He ran his thumb along the board. "The oak was mine. You'll find it on the bill."

Emeric had found it on the bill. The fitting-out had come up the river at the end of the summer on a sheet of its own, oak and brass and lead and six men's summer, twenty Gold Rand and four Silver, and Ferra had paid it without a word and written one in the margin. Rooms.

The last door off the saloon, to starboard, opened on a closet. There was a seat in it of scrubbed oak over an iron pipe, and a brass pump beside the seat, and a basin of lead in the corner with a smaller pump of its own, and a copper tub, short, the kind a man sits in with his knees up, with a plug in its bottom and a pipe under the plug.

Fletcher did not show him this one. He stood in the doorway with his arms folded and let Emeric look.

"The same again forward, for her hands," he said, "less the tub. The pump brings the sea in to wash it down. After that, on every ship afloat, the sea has it. Over the side. It always has." He unfolded one arm and pointed at the floor. "You've drawn me a tank under it. To hold it. And a pump to empty the tank when you choose." He looked at Emeric. "As though she was to lie in a town square."

"She'll lie in rivers," Emeric said. "And in harbours. Where people draw their water."

He had spent his first years in Kaldo on what went into the river above the town and what came up out of the wells below it, and Fletcher had not been there for any of it, and it was not a thing that could be explained in a doorway. Fletcher looked at him for a moment as though he knew that.

"It's built," he said. "As drawn."

On deck again, in the wind, Emeric looked along the length of her. The wheel aft on its pedestal, and beside it an iron post with nothing fitted to it yet. The skylight. The companion. The hatch over the empty hold. The stack. Between the stack and the hatch, turned over on chocks and lashed down, was a boat, clinker-built, the length of three men, which had not been in any drawing he had ever made.

"Every hull I build carries her own boat," Fletcher said, before he could ask. "Whatever else she is."

Then, because it was the question underneath every other one he had not been allowed to ask all summer, he asked the one he could.

"Where's she going," he said, "that wants ten days of water in her and a bed like that?"

Emeric thought about it honestly, because Fletcher had earned that much.

"I don't know yet," he said. "Somewhere a horse can't take me."

He had drawn her a ship before he drew her anything else, and he had done it for a reason he had written nowhere but in the notebook, under the rule the thaw had given him. Every build still works when its stones die. If every stone in her went dark she would still have to be a ship: a steamer that could carry sixteen berths and one bed of people along a coast for ten days on coal and water, and keep them dry and fed and out of each other's way while she did it. The berths and the tanks and the galley were what she would still be for afterward, whatever happened to the rest of her.

Fletcher took the answer about as well as he had ever taken a customer's, and went back to his men.

He told him that evening, when the six had gone up the beach to the cottages and the light was going.

He did it standing at her side on the stocks, with his hand on her, in plain words, the way he had once told Fletcher that iron did not sink if you gave it enough of the sea to push on. He told him about the black stone in the hill at Grayrock that the Crown bought at two Platinum the pound. He told him what it did when a man put his work into it, and what it did when the man let go and drew nothing back, and what it did when a charged stone stood at the other end of the thread instead of a man. He told him about the section, and the two faces, and the rivets going across the water in the thaw like shots, and why the frames at the stations had always been heavier than any join needed. He told him about a man in the Crown's own harness flying circles under a roof beam with nobody pouring anything into him, and about the cells in the crates on the steamboat behind them, and what the holes in her frames were for, and what her empty hold was for. And he told him about the letter under the chancery's seal that had come up the road from Milford in the first week of the autumn and asked, politely, what the barony of Kaldo was doing with that stone.

Fletcher listened to all of it without interrupting, with his arms folded, looking at his hull. When Emeric had finished he went on looking at her for some time.

Then he asked his first question, and it was a shipwright's.

"Where does it sit," he said, "against her weight?"

Emeric waited.

"Your stones. At the turn of the bilge." Fletcher unfolded one arm and pointed along her side at the holes. "That's low. That's below her middle, well below, with the engine in her and the boiler. You push a thing up from under its middle and it wants to go over. Same as a boat with her ballast on deck. Same as a man standing on a barrel." He looked at Emeric. "What stops her rolling over on your stones?"

It was the question. It was the only one that mattered, and Emeric had been waiting two years for somebody to ask it who knew why it mattered.

"The stones at the stations aren't set," he said. "They're fed, all the time, and watched. There's a reading stone on a dish of oil in her hold, and it watches the dish the way you'd watch a plumb, and the moment she leans a hand, a stone that's never tired and never asleep tells the stations on the low side to push and the high side to ease, or to pull. It's a man at the tiller who can feel the whole of her at once." He had seen Ward go over onto his face in midsummer with the push below his middle, and he had seen the deciding stone catch him. "Low's where they have to be, for the frames. The watching is what keeps her up."

"And when the watching stops?"

Ward had brought a lantern down from the steamboat. Emeric took the drawing out of his coat and unrolled it on the stocks in the light of it, and held the corners down with two of Bram's wrenches.

It was the section of her at her middle station, the whole of her from keel to deck, and it had the fed stones drawn low where the holes were. And it had another set drawn in, in a different ink, high up, on the deck beams under her deck, as far above her middle as the fed stones were below it.

"That's the reserve," he said. "If the feed ever stops, for any reason, I set those. All of them, at once, in one working, a hair under her weight. Nothing watches them. Nothing has to. They're above her middle, so she hangs from them the way a lamp hangs from a hook, and a lamp on a hook doesn't roll. And they're a hair under her weight, so she doesn't hang, quite. She comes down. Slowly. Onto whatever's under her."

Fletcher bent over the drawing. He read it the way Bram read drawings, twice, the second time slower, with his finger moving along the deck beams. He looked at the fed stones low down, and at the reserve high up, and at the line Emeric had drawn through her middle where her weight sat, and he did not say anything for so long that the lantern began to gutter and Ward trimmed it.

"That was the only thing I'd have argued with," Fletcher said at last. He straightened up. "Lift under her middle and nothing to catch her if it stopped. I'd have argued with that till you sent me home." He put one finger on the deck beams. "And it isn't there."

"No."

"You drew it high before I asked."

"I learned it on a man," Emeric said. "In the summer. He went over onto his face."

Fletcher looked at him for a moment. Then he rolled up the drawing, carefully, square at the ends, and handed it back, and went up the ladder onto her deck.

He did not say why. He went to her rail in the near dark and put both hands flat on it and stood there, and Emeric, watching from the beach, understood after a while what he was doing. It was what he had done to every hull he had ever built, the first time she went into the water: standing on her and feeling how she lay. She lay on her blocks on a dry beach, and there was nothing to feel. He was trying to feel it anyway. He was standing on a hundred feet of iron and oak that he had put together with his own hands, and trying to imagine her lying on nothing.

He came down after some time.

"My oak," he said. "The berths and the table and that bed. The tanks, when they're full. The range. The coal. There's a deal of weight in her I'd never have put in a hull that was only meant to float."

"The stations were sized for her full," Emeric said. "Tanks full, bunkers full, every berth slept in. That's why the frames are what they are. It was on the first sheet."

"And the bed?"

"The bed wasn't."

Fletcher grunted, which might have been an apology and might not.

Then he stood very still on the dark sand, and Emeric watched him arrive at it.

"The tank," Fletcher said. "In her heads."

"Yes."

"She'll not always be over the sea."

"No."

Fletcher considered the pipe under the scrubbed oak seat, and whatever he could see at the far end of it.

"Some poor devil's roof," he said.

"Somebody's field. Somebody's well."

"That's the one thing in her I thought I understood before you told me," Fletcher said, "and I had it wrong." He turned toward her, black against the last of the sky. "Well. Let's see her do it, then."

Emeric took the other sheet out of his coat.

He had written the trial out fair in Kaldo, as he had written every trial since the thaw, on one sheet, and it had gone to Ferra before anybody else saw it and come back with her initials at the foot. The place: the stocks at the lower water. The hour: low water, at first light, with the beach dry for fifty yards on every side and the yard sent home. The floor: the sand. Who would stand where, and what each of them would do at each count if it went wrong. He gave it to Fletcher, and Fletcher read it by Ward's lantern, and handed it back.

"No," he said.

Emeric waited.

"Not on my beach. There's sixty chimneys at the lower water. The salt house has its pans boiling before first light, and the crabbers go out past my stocks in the dark to be at their pots by dawn. You'll not lift a thing that size here at any hour of the day or night without somebody seeing her do it. And whoever sees her'll tell it wrong, and by the end of the week she'll be a devil." He looked up the beach at the cottages, where the lamps were lit in the windows. "And my men."

"I'd have sent them home."

"You'd have sent them home and the whole of the lower water would have told them by noon." Fletcher folded his arms. "You said a letter. With a seal."

"Yes."

"Asking."

"Yes."

"Then there'll be men down here one day asking too. Men with a seal of their own. They'll ask at the salt house and at the cottages and on the quay, and they'll ask my men, because my men built her." He did not raise his voice. "A man can't be made to tell what he never saw. I'll not have six men who've never been past the bar stood up in front of the Crown's notary and asked what they know about a stone. Not for you. Not for her."

"They built her."

"They built a ship," said Fletcher. "Every rivet in her. That's what they'll have seen, and that's what they can swear to, to anybody, any day of their lives, and every word of it true." He was quiet for a moment. "I'll carry the rest."

It was the cost, and Emeric had known a cost would come, and he had not known it would come in Fletcher's voice, or land on Fletcher. He looked at Ward. Ward looked back at him and did not say anything, which from Ward was an opinion.

"Then where?" Emeric said.

"Where you try any hull." Fletcher nodded at the bar, a black line now against a last grey line of sea. "In the water. Out past the bar and on, until the dunes go down under the edge of the sea from her deck. When you can't see them from her, they can't see her from them. They'll see her smoke, and a smoke's a steamer. Nobody ever called a steamer a devil."

Emeric had thought about the sea. He had put it aside in Kaldo for the reason Fletcher had asked about first. Lift below her middle, over deep water. A ship that went over on her stones on the stocks would come down on sand. A ship that went over on them at sea would go over into the sea, and she was iron, and there would be men inside her.

He said so.

"Then don't lift her off it," Fletcher said. "Not all at once. Lift her out of it." He put a hand flat on her plating, low down, beside the hole. "A hull in the water wants to be upright. That's what I build them for. It's the whole of the trade. Give your stones a notch of her and let the sea keep the rest, and if your stone in the hold watches wrong, the sea'll put her right, and you'll feel it do it, and you take your notch back. Then give it another. By the time there's nothing under her but your stones, you'll know whether it watches true. You'll have watched it."

He was right, and it was better than anything on the sheet, and Emeric turned it over and found the other half of it already waiting there.

"Under way," he said. "With the engine driving her."

Fletcher looked at him.

"Coal's cheap. Cells aren't. Every yard she makes on her screw is a yard the cells don't have to pay for, and once she's moving, a thing that weighs nothing keeps what it's given. And the lighter she rides, the less of her there is in the sea to push aside, so the same steam drives her harder." He looked out at the bar. "And she'll want way on her. Stopped, she'll lie in the swell and roll."

"She'll sit her sea better going." Fletcher said it slowly, turning it over. "And she'll steer, for as long as there's water on her rudder." He was quiet for a while. "Like a swan off a pond. They don't go straight up. They run at it."

"Like a swan off a pond," said Emeric.

He wrote it out fair that night in Fletcher's cottage, one sheet, and it went up the river road with the salt carter on the third morning and came back down with him on the seventh. The place: the sea, a dozen miles off the bar. The floor: the sea. The lift: a notch at a time, under way, the sea keeping whatever the stones did not, no higher than a man, and water under her the whole way. Ferra had initialled it at the foot.

The margin was empty. In the summer she had written her conditions down the side of his sheet, and he had half expected them again, and they were already in it. Fletcher had written them for her.

Folded inside the sheet was a slip torn off the bottom of some other page, not initialled and not part of anything, with three words on it in the same square hand.

Home before dark.

It took them nine days to fit her.

The six finished her joinery by day and went home at the end of it, because Fletcher sent them, and after they had gone the work that was not joinery went in. Bram seated the station stones himself, one to each hole, in housings off the any-size drawing, bolted through the frames where Fletcher had made them heaviest. He ran the rods from them up the inside of the frames, under the ceiling planking, to the deciding stone in its case on the iron wall between the engine and the hold, and from the deciding stone down to the rack in her hold where the cells would sit, a hundred and eighty of them, necks in their rails. He set the dish in her hold on the centreline, gimballed so that it swung, and the reading stone on its bracket over it, looking down. He set the two brass levers on the iron post by her wheel, heavier than Ward's and the same shape, so that a hand that knew the one would know the other. By the second night the hatch over the hold had a padlock on it, and the key lived on Bram's belt. The six saw the crates go aboard, and the long felt box, and asked, and Fletcher said the prince's, and that was the end of it.

Orrin came down on the second day with the carter, grumbling. He filled cells in the hold with the hatch shut over him, from first light until the six went home, because he could not do it anywhere the six might see, and he did it in the only silence anybody in Kaldo had ever known him keep. At dusk he came up out of the hatch grey in the face and went up the beach and talked to the lower water until midnight to make up for it. He said he had never in his life been paid to sit in a dark hole on a beach, and that it was the best posting he had ever had.

Fletcher asked about the screw on the third day, standing under her stern with his hand on the arm. He wanted to know what use it would be to her once there was no water for it to turn in. Emeric told him, and told him why it had to be up and locked before the last of her left the sea and not after, and what the arm had really been for since the spring.

"It's for beaching her as well," Emeric said.

"I'd hope so. I built it strong enough." Fletcher looked at the arm for a while. "You'll not see the moment. It'll be under her, in her wash. But you'll hear it." He tipped his head at the engine-room hatch, where Bram was. "A screw that's near the top of the water starts to draw air down. It races. Your smith'll hear it in his engine before anybody on deck sees it in the wake."

Bram, below, said something that sounded like agreement.

The reserve went in last. Bram seated it high, in housings bolted under the deck beams, eight of them spaced along her on her centreline, above the fed stones, where the drawing said. The stones sat in their cups with their collars screwed down, and Emeric did not touch them.

"They're not set," Fletcher said, looking at them.

"No. They're fitted. They're set if they're ever needed, and not before, because a set stone spends from the moment it's set and there's no way back." Emeric looked along the row of them under the beams. "It's a matched set. One face of the body, read together, fitted together. If they're set, they'll spend together, and if they're spent, they'll be replaced together, every one, never a fresh stone beside a tired one."

"Because of the section."

"Because of the section."

Armand wrote them up that night on the beach at the foot of her ladder, with the ledger open on his knees and Ward holding the lantern. He made a new heading under the station set, in the crabbed hand. The reserve. And under it, after some thought, one word. Fitted.

He did not write set, and he did not write lent.

Emeric did not say the other thing, which was that the reserve would be set by one man, and the man was him, and it was the one place on her where a floor still needed a man to put it there. Under the height of a man, the sea was her reserve. Above it, he was. He had not found a way round that. He had written it in the notebook in the column for things he could not answer, and it sat there, under Who tells it twice, with a line through that one and not through this.

They launched her on the tenth morning, at the top of the spring tide.

Half the lower water came down to watch, because half the lower water had said for two years that she would go to the bottom like a dropped anvil the moment she touched the sea, and wished to be there when she did. The six knocked out the last of the shores. She stood for a moment on the greased ways as if she had not heard, and then she went, slowly and then not slowly, with a long groaning run of timber under her, and her stern went into the lagoon and her bow came after it, and a wave went out from her across the flat water and broke on the far shore, and she floated.

She floated high and light and black, with her tanks and her bunkers still empty and the line of red lead Fletcher had painted along her waterline standing a hand clear of the water. A man on the salt-house quay began to laugh, and could not stop, and nobody told him to.

Fletcher went aboard her from the steamboat and stood at her rail with both hands flat on it, as he had on the stocks, and this time there was something to feel, and he felt it.

They had water in her and coal in her and steam on her by the middle of the morning, and before he would take her anywhere, Fletcher heeled her.

Every hull off his beach was heeled before she went past the bar, and his six had heeled every one of them. They came out to her in the yard's skiff and climbed aboard, and Fletcher hung a plumb bob on a line down the engine-room hatch, over a batten marked off in inches that Bram's lad held square against the ladder. Then he sent the six to her port rail together.

She went over to them, slowly. The bob swung off its mark and hung, and Fletcher read the batten and called the inches, and the oldest of the six chalked them on the hatch coaming. Then to starboard, and she came back through upright and leaned the other way and hung there. Then the six walked back to the middle of her, and she came up under them, quick and stiff, and stood.

Fletcher looked at the chalk for a while.

"Stiff," he said. "Her weight's low. Engine, boiler, tanks, the lot of it down in her. She'll take a sea on her quarter and not mind it." He rubbed the figures out with his thumb, as though they were somebody else's business now. "She's a ship."

Emeric had stood by the hatch the whole time with the medium open, and there had been nothing in it to see: sixteen dense places low in her frames, asleep, and eight above them, asleep. Six men who had set every rivet in her had walked her deck from rail to rail over the top of them and read her lean in inches, and they would go home tonight knowing more about how she lay in the water than anybody alive but Fletcher. It was the whole of what they would ever know about her. Her weight was low. That was what made her stiff in the sea, and it was the same thing that had made Fletcher ask his question on the stocks, and none of the six would ever hear the second half of it.

The six went back to the beach in the skiff and stood in a row, where Fletcher had told them to stand. He had told them the night before that a first trial took the builder and the engine and the owner and nobody else, because if she opened a seam he would rather it was his own feet that got wet, and they had taken it, because it was Fletcher. Behind them the salt-house men stood about, and the women from the drying frames, and behind them again, on his own, well back from the water, Armand stood with his hands at his sides, where anybody could see them. Orrin stood beside him and talked to the oldest of the six about a cow.

Bram's lad swung the arm down. Bram gave her steam. The screw turned, slowly, and the water under her stern went brown with sand and then white, and she moved.

That was all the lower water saw. A black iron hull with her screw down in the water behind her and a thread of smoke going up from her stack, turning in the lagoon under Fletcher's hand and going out through the gap in the bar, as steady as a cart on a road, into the open water beyond, and on.

They stopped her a dozen miles out, on a sea with nothing on it.

Fletcher had brought her out past the last of the crab pots, and on past the lane where the salt boats went east and west along the shore, and on again. He did not watch the sea ahead. He stood at the wheel with his head turned over his shoulder and watched the land go down. The cottages went first, and the stocks. Then the long pale line of the beach went, and the dunes stood on the edge of the sea by themselves, and went too, a little at a time, until the salt-house chimney stood alone out of the water like a stick in a pond, and then it went, and there was nothing astern of them but a smudge of the lower water's smoke on the rim of the world.

"When you can't see them," Fletcher said, and rang down to stop her.

The engine fell quiet. She lost her way slowly and lay rocking in a long low swell out of the south. The light air off the land had died to nothing. Ward went round her rail one way, looking out, and Fletcher went round it the other, and they met at the bow, and neither of them had found a sail.

"Ring for her," said Emeric.

Fletcher rang down for full steam, and Bram gave it to him.

She gathered way. The screw bit, and her stern settled a little under the push of it, and she put her shoulder to the swell and went at it, and the swell came along her side and lifted her and let her down, long and slow, the way the sea had lifted every ship there had ever been.

Ward stood at the post by the wheel with his left hand on the lift lever.

"A notch," Emeric said.

Ward moved it one notch.

In the medium she was a long shape of iron with sixteen dense strange hot places in two rows low down in her frames, waking, and above them another row of eight, cold and quiet, waiting. The cells in their rack in her hold began to thin, all hundred and eighty of them together, the way a lake goes down in a dry month, a little and evenly everywhere.

She came up in the water. Not far. Emeric leaned out over her side and watched the line of red lead come up out of the sea along the whole of her length at once, as though somebody had drawn it up on a string. She went on rolling in the swell as she had rolled all morning, a ship's roll, long and slow, and in her hold the oil in the dish ran out to the low side and back, and the reading stone watched it go.

"Another."

She came up another hand, and the roll changed.

It was still a roll. She still went down to port and came back and went down to starboard. But she came back from each side sooner than the swell meant her to, as though something inside her were leaning against it, and at the top of each roll there was no hang, only a small quick catch and the deck coming level under their feet. Fletcher's face did not change. His hands on the wheel moved very slightly, the way a man's hands move on a rein.

"She's stopped rolling like a ship," he said.

"What's she rolling like?"

Fletcher thought about it for three rolls.

"Like a ship with a man down in her hold holding her up by the keelson," he said. "Who knows his business."

She was going faster. The engine's beat under their feet had not changed, and she was going faster on the same steam, because there was less of her in the sea to push out of the way. The bow wave that had stood out white from her stem lay down along her sides. Her wake flattened.

"Another," said Emeric, and then, "Another."

She sat on the sea now the way a skiff sits, riding over the swell rather than being lifted by it, and the water along her plates went by with a hiss instead of a wash. There was very little of her left in it. Fletcher had begun to hold the wheel differently, loosely, with his fingertips, as though it had become a lighter thing than it was.

Then the engine changed.

It came up from below, through the deck, a quickening, the steady knock of it running up into something ragged and fast, an engine with nothing to push against. At her stern the water behind the screw went white, and whiter, boiling with air.

"There," said Fletcher. "Screw."

Bram had shut her steam off before the word was out. His lad swung the arm, and the screw came up out of the sea still turning, throwing water, and slowed, and stopped, and locked against the transom, clear.

In the same breath Ward moved the right lever off its stop.

The housings at her quarters woke. It was the bellows sound, the long breath Ward's back had made in the foundry at midsummer, but deeper, from two places at once, and the water under her quarters went dark and flat in two long shivering patches. She did not lose her way. She had it from the screw, and nothing was taking it from her now but the last of the sea along her keel, and the housings gave it back as fast as the sea took it.

"Up," said Emeric.

Ward took the lever the last of the way.

The sound of the sea went first. It had been with them all morning, the wash and slap of it along her plates, and it went thin and became a hiss, and the hiss ran aft along her keel from her stem to her sternpost like a blade drawn out of a sheath, and stopped. Spray came off her forefoot in threads, and then in drops, and then there was no spray. There was only the bellows at her quarters, and the wind of her going, and under her the swell running past, long and grey, a hand below her keel, then a foot, then the height of a man.

Emeric was watching Fletcher's hands.

The old man had worked the wheel the whole way out, small movements, feeling the rudder answer him through the ropes, and then there was no water on the rudder and nothing for it to answer, and the spokes went slack under his fingers. His hands stopped. He did not take them off the wheel.

Ward held her at the height of a man, as the sheet said, and she ran. She ran over the top of the swell at the pace of a trotting horse with nothing under her but air, level, and in the medium the sixteen stations took their feed and the cells went down evenly in their rack, and the reserve under the deck beams stayed cold. Ward had said it on the shelf in the thaw, looking at a slice of iron lying twisted in a foot of water. A boat that doesn't need the water. He did not say it now. He stood at the levers with both hands on them and counted under his breath, as he had in the foundry.

"Walk her," Emeric said.

There were three of them to do it, which was not six, and would have to serve. Bram came up out of the engine room with his lad behind him, because the engine had nothing to do now, and Emeric went with them to the port rail, and they stood there together, heavily, and in her hold the oil ran toward them and the reading stone saw it go. Emeric felt the deciding stone answer. The port stations took more. The starboard eased. The deck came back level under their feet. They walked her to starboard, and she came back. They walked her into the bow, and her head went down a hair and came up, and into the stern, and it did the same. Then Emeric told them to run, and they went across her at a run, all three, from one rail to the other, and she leaned under it and caught herself and came back, and the swell ran under her the whole time, a man's height down, and never once touched her.

At three hundred, which was the plan, Emeric said, "Down."

Ward brought the lift back a notch at a time. The sea came up toward them, grey and long and moving. Her keel touched the top of a swell and she skipped off it, once, the way a flat stone skips off a pond, and came down further on, and the sea caught her forefoot and threw a sheet of water back over her bow onto all of them, and she began to skip again, and Ward took the lever past the notch.

The stations went heavy. For a count of three she weighed more than any hull of her size had a right to, and she sat down into the sea the way Ward's heels had gone into the sand at midsummer, with the water coming up her sides in a rush. Then Ward brought the lever back to its stop, and she was a ship, rolling in a swell a dozen miles out, with her screw up and her way coming off her and the sea running off her deck and out through her scuppers.

Nobody said anything for some time.

Fletcher took his hands off the wheel and looked at them. Then he took a lamp stone in its cage off the hook by the companion and went below without a word, and Emeric heard him go along her, frame by frame, through the saloon and past the engine and into the hold and on, down on his knees by the sound of it at every frame, looking along the seams at the bottom of her for a weep. It took him the best part of an hour. They lay stopped while he did it, rolling like a ship.

He came up with his knees wet.

"Tight," he said. Then, "I'll want her out of the water. Tomorrow, at the top of the tide, hauled up the ways onto her stocks. I'll want to see her bottom from the outside, every plate." He looked at the place on the sea where she had come down, which was no longer anywhere. "Nobody ever set a hull down on the sea at a run before. I'll want to see what it did to her."

"And then?"

"Then I'll put her back in on the next springs," Fletcher said, "and you can do what you like with her."

Bram's lad swung the arm down, and the screw went into the water behind her. Fletcher rang for steam, and the engine came up under their feet, and she turned for home.

The six were still on the beach when she came in through the gap, in their row, as if they had not moved all day. Armand had gone up to the cottages. Orrin was sitting on an upturned hull among the salt-house men, talking.

The oldest of the six came down to the edge of the water when they came ashore in her boat. He looked at her lying at her mooring in the lagoon, and at Fletcher, and did not look at Emeric at all.

"How'd she go?"

"Light," said Fletcher.

The man thought about that. He had a way of thinking that did not show on him anywhere.

"Your smoke went out, out there," he said. "Best part of an hour."

"Had her screw up," said Fletcher, "and I went through her bottom with a lamp."

"Tight?"

"Tight."

The man nodded, as though that were a reasonable answer, and looked at her for a while longer, the way he had looked down at Emeric from her rail on the first day, with no expression at all. Then he went up the beach to the cottages and did not look back.

That night Emeric sat in Fletcher's cottage, in the wind off the bar, with the lamp between them, the way he had sat there two years ago with a drawing of a ship that did not sink.

"He watched the smoke," Fletcher said.

"I saw."

"He's worked this beach with me twenty years. He knows how long a man takes to look at a screw." Fletcher turned his cup on the table. "He'll not ask me. He'll wait for me to tell him. He's waited on me twenty years, one thing and another, and I've never once not told him a thing about a hull."

Emeric did not say anything.

"Six men," Fletcher said. "Every rivet in her. They'll stand on the beach and watch her go out and come back, and I'll stand next to them and tell them she went light." He drank. "Every word of it true."

It was very nearly what Emeric had written to the Crown a fortnight since: every word of it true, and none of it an answer. He did not think Fletcher meant it that way. It was only a man telling him what the thing cost.

"Is that a cost you can carry?" Emeric said.

Fletcher looked at him for a moment across the lamp.

"It's not mine," he said. "It's yours. I'm only holding it for you."

Then, unexpectedly, he laughed, a short dry sound like a board creaking.

"I'll send you the bill," he said. "I always do."
