Chapter 207 - The Gallery

They went up through the cloud an hour out of Kaldo.

It lay over the whole north that night, from the hills to wherever it ended, and the K Discovery climbed through it blind for most of a mile, wet grey at the glass and the probe on its pole outside the bow a smear of nothing; and came out of the top of it into stars. Fletcher took her on up. Nobody had to ask him. At a little under four miles the probe began to brighten, and the needles on Vesk's board crept, and then climbed, and Fletcher eased her off and held her there at the Band's foot with Ottoline's chronometer open on his knee.

Emeric opened Abloh's chest on the saloon table, and lifted the felt off the long box, and opened the box, and left the lid up.

He felt it go in before he saw it. The gun's crystal first, quick, the way a dry sponge takes water; then the breach stone beside it, slower, being bigger. The air in the saloon prickled along his arms. Vantic, at the iron box with her book on her knees, looked up once, and looked down again, and turned a page she had not read. And under all of it, in himself, the well he had sat at the bottom of on the cellar floor came up under him like the tide in a dock, cool, and then not cool.

Ward stood over the table with his helm under his arm and watched the crystal. It had been clear as water in the cellar. Now there was a light in it, pale, faint, the colour of an early morning with nothing in it yet; and the light came up the stone from the base, slowly, the way a thermometer comes up in a warm hand, and reached the tip, and stayed.

"Full," said Emeric.

Ward looked at it a while longer. "Then it is full," he said, and put his helm on the table beside it, and went forward to Fletcher.

Fletcher had her back under the floor of the Band in nineteen minutes by the chronometer, and down through the cloud again north of the hills. Tobe wrote the minutes in the log. Nobody bled.



The K Discovery came over Lisle's wood at a quarter past one in the morning, a mile up, dark, with the wind out of the north behind her and the moon behind a long bank of cloud.

Below her the wood was a black fleece with no edges. Beyond it, south, the fork of the roads showed only as a place where the black was a little less black: two pale threads coming together, and between them a darker square, and a single light. The Staple house. One window lit, on the upper floor, at the north end; the long panelled room, shuttered, with the light coming through the cracks of the shutters in thin gold lines. Every other window dark. Smoke still going up thin from two of the stacks, after three days, into the dark.

Emeric stood at the rail in the black suit with his helm under his arm and the gun in its case across his back and the buckler on his left forearm, and spread his sense down.

Thirty-eight. Fewer, now; thirty-five. Three had gone in the three days, Saban's groom said, over the back wall at night, house men who had looked at the smoke and thought about the word transport. Six on the roof by the stacks, cold, bored, dense with sleeplessness. Men in the weighing shed. Men at the main gate, and the knight among them, the lit lamp of his tracery, steady. Men asleep in a long room at the foot of the main stair, a dozen, close together, the soldiers' room. The cluster in the long panelled room, under the light, awake. All of them awake. Nobody in that room had slept for three nights either.

And in the yard behind the house, by the stable gate onto the woods road, the horses, saddled. And the still note by the stable wall.

And under the north end of the house, under the cellars, under everything, the stillness. The hand over the mouth.

He put his helm on.

"Gate," said Ward, at his shoulder, low, with his own helm on, a black shape against the black.

Down on the road below the house, a mile south and a mile down, a single small light went on, and off, and on. Declan, at the edge of the wood on the capital road, with the column behind him in the trees: forty of the garrison in their black, the five masks among them, Osric's big shape in the lead, Tobe's twelve pots in a handcart, all of it set down in the clearing an hour before when the ship touched there for a quarter of a minute on her way. Ready.

Fletcher, at the stick, said nothing at all. Emeric felt the ship turn under his feet, a little, into the wind, and settle.

"Now," said Emeric.



He went over the rail.

The hearts asleep, the cape-wing furled on its battens on his back, nothing. He fell. The ship went away above him into the black, a darker shape against the stars, and then was not there; and the air came up at him cold and roaring, tearing at the edges of the helm, and he counted. One. Two. On the three the watchdog threw the cape, and the battens snapped out to either side of him with a crack like a sail filling, and the wind took the wing under him and his fall became a slide, long and silent and fast, down the dark toward the darker square in the fork of the roads.

Ward was off his left shoulder, fifty yards back, a black wing against a black sky. Rylan off his right.

He could feel them both: Ward slow and even, a wall; Rylan quick, with the half stop in him, the catch in the note that had always been there, as if part of him were always waiting for something. Tonight it was very loud.

Below, on the capital road, the first of Tobe's pots went over the chain.

He saw it before he heard it: a small spark arcing up out of the black edge of the wood and over the chain and the cart behind it and landing in the road at the main gate; and then a second, and a third, and the smoke coming up off them in thick grey rolls, white in the light of the gate lantern, swallowing the lantern. Then the shouting. Men at the gate. Men in the weighing shed coming out. The knight's dense note turning toward the road. And on the roof, the six notes by the stacks all turning, at once, the way a flock turns, toward the gate, toward the smoke and the shouts and the lantern going out; their backs to the north. Their backs to the wood.

He put his heels down at a man's height above the slates and stalled the wing, and dropped.

The slate took him like a drum. He went to one knee on the north slope of the roof behind the ridge, and the cups on his boots took the slate, pop, hold, and he did not slide; and Ward came down beside him a breath later without a sound, and Rylan a breath after Ward, on the far side of the stack.

The six on the roof did not turn round. They were at the south edge, at the parapet, looking down at the smoke.

Ward went along the ridge. Emeric had seen him do it in the yard at Kaldo a thousand times, the knight's way of moving when the knight wanted not to be heard, the weight carried so evenly that a board did not creak; he had never seen it on a roof in the dark. Ward took the first man from behind with an arm across the throat and a hand over his mouth, and held him, and laid him down asleep on the slates behind the parapet, and was at the second before the first had finished falling. Rylan took the third and fourth together, one with the pommel of his sword and one with his fist. The fifth turned round. Emeric was close enough by then; he put two fingers on the man's neck through the gap in his collar, the way Imani had shown him on a dead man in the ward, the place where the blood went to the head; and pressed; and the man's eyes rolled up and he went down soft against the parapet.

The sixth was Ward's. He did not even turn round.

Six men asleep on a roof in the dark, and the smoke rolling up the front of the house from the gate below, and the shouting. It had taken less time than it took to say.

"Gallery," said Ward.



The gallery ran along the top of the house under the roof, the length of the north end, the old way the Staple's founders had built, with a row of small windows set into the slope of the roof to light it; a long low room where, Saban's groom said, the five had walked up and down in the rain two hundred years ago with their guests, admiring the view of their road. The windows had iron catches on the inside. Rylan opened the second one from the end with the point of his knife through the gap in its frame, as he had opened the window of a dyehouse once when he was a boy on the port, he said; and they went in, one after another, feet first, onto bare boards in the dark.

It was black inside. Black, and long, and smelling of dust and old wax and, faintly, from the stair at the far end, smoke.

Emeric stood on the boards with his eyes useless and his sense spread wide, and saw the gallery the way he saw everything now when the light went: as notes. Ward, close, a wall. Rylan, half a step ahead, quick, catching. The stair at the far end, forty feet off, going down, with the cluster of the long panelled room below it and to the left, all awake, all frightened, all very still. Men on the main stair below that, coming up, shouting about the gate.

And in the gallery, with them, twenty feet away, against the wall by the third window, in the dark, a man.

Not asleep. Not frightened, either. Still. A thin hard note, cold, waiting, in the place a man waits who has been told to sit in the dark at the top of a house all night and shoot whatever comes in by the windows; and he had seen them come in, three black shapes against the faint grey of the window glass, and he had not shouted, because he was not the kind of man who shouted. He was raising something. Emeric could feel where the man's hands were and how they moved: up, together, steady, to the shoulder, the way a man brings up a crossbow.

At Rylan. Then not at Rylan. The hands moved a hair left. At the one in the middle. At him.

He had his left arm up before the man's finger closed. He did not decide it. His body had read the man before his head had finished reading him, the way it had read Garrin on a road in the forest years ago; and his arm came up across his chest with the buckler on it, and he pressed into the sign on the flake under the brass ring, hard, all at once, with the little he had.

The bolt hit the flake.

He felt it more than heard it: a blow on the forearm like a hammer on an anvil, that went up his arm into his shoulder and his teeth, and stopped. The bolt did not go through. It hung there, for less than a breath, on the face of the flake in the dark, a hand's breadth from his chest, pressed against something that was not there; and then the flake let go. It went to dust under the brass ring, all at once, with a sound like a sigh, and the bolt dropped to the boards at his feet and rolled.

Ward was already moving. Ward had heard him move; Ward did not have the sense, and did not need it, because Ward had heard the click of a crossbow's nut in the dark at twenty feet a hundred times in forty years. He went down the gallery in three strides and took the man and the crossbow together against the wall, and there was a short flat sound, and the man was on the boards.

"He saw us," said Rylan, very low.

"I saw him first." Emeric looked down at his arm. The buckler was empty under the brass ring; a little grey dust in the bottom of it, like ash in a cup. His forearm ached to the bone. "He picked me."

"He would," said Ward, coming back. "You are the one in the middle." He looked at the empty buckler. "One gone."

"One gone."

"Two left," said Ward, and went to the head of the stair.



The soldiers' room was at the foot of the main stair, where the groom had drawn it; and the men in it were awake now, and coming out of it, a dozen of them, into the hall at the stair foot, with lanterns and swords and the shouting from the gate. Ward stood at the top of the stair in the dark, looking down at the lanterns coming, and took the flash stone out of the pouch on his belt, and pulled its pin, and counted under his breath, and dropped it.

It went down the well of the stair turning over and over, a small dark thing in the lantern light, and landed at the foot among the boots.

Ambrose had wanted to know how loud. Emeric would tell him, afterward. He had his eyes shut and his hands over the ear-holes of his helm and his back to the stair and he still saw it, through his own eyelids and the steel, white, as if the sun had come up inside the house; and the bang was not a bang, it was a blow, a hand of air the size of a door that came up the stair and hit him in the back and rattled every window in the gallery in its frame. Below, in the hall, there was a silence. Then a sound of men falling over things.

When he opened his eyes and looked over the rail, there were seven men on the floor of the hall at the foot of the stair and five more on their knees with their hands over their faces, and the lanterns were rolling about on the flags, and one of them had set a man's coat alight and he did not seem to have noticed.

Rylan went down the stair. He went down it the way he went down the keep stair at Kaldo when the class was waiting at the bottom, fast and light, and at the bottom he did not stop to look at anybody, but kicked the burning lantern away across the flags and went on, through the hall, toward the back of the house, toward the passage that led to the yard. To the stable gate. Emeric felt him go, the quick catching note, the half stop in it very loud now, like a held breath.

"Rylan," he said, after him, down the stair. Not loudly.

Rylan did not turn round. "Alive," he said, without stopping. "I know."

He was gone.



The door of the long panelled room was at the head of the main stair, on the left, oak, with the light coming under it.

It was not locked. Somebody inside had been about to lock it, and had not, because the flash had come up the stair before he could; Emeric felt him behind it, a thin frightened note with its hand on the key. Ward put his shoulder to the door, once, and the key went across the floor inside, and they went in.

The long panelled room was full of people and smoke.

Not much smoke; the fire in the great hearth at the far end had been fed with paper for three days and was still being fed, and the air was thick and grey and stinking with it, and black flakes lay on the table and the chairs and the floor like a fall of dirty snow. The shutters were closed. The lamps were lit. And round the long table where Placit had given him dinner in May, standing, with their backs to the far wall and the fire, were the five, and their sons, and two of their wives, and their clerks, and their servants; twenty people, more, in the grey air, all turned toward the door.

Two black shapes in the doorway, in helms, out of the dark.

Somebody screamed. It was Vennor's wife. Vennor, beside her, did not; Vennor went grey, and sat down on the floor where he stood, very suddenly, as if his legs had been cut.

Hollin moved. Big and soft and fast, faster than Emeric would have believed, for a man who had told him stories about the old king's hunting with his mouth full of beef; he took the nearest servant by the arm, a girl in an apron with a tray of cups she had been carrying when the door broke, and pulled her in front of him, and had a knife at her throat, a little silver fruit knife off the table, before the tray had finished falling. And Placit, at the head of the table, small and old and upright with his close white beard, took the boy beside him by the collar, a pot boy of ten, and pulled him back against his own legs, and put his thin old hand round the boy's throat, and did not need a knife.

"No closer," said Placit. His voice was perfectly steady. "Whoever you are."

The girl in front of Hollin was crying without any sound. The pot boy was looking at the black shapes in the door with his mouth open, as a boy looks at a thing out of a story.

Emeric took the gravity stone out of his belt.

It was small in his glove. Six ounces of Grayrock's stone, with its brass pin. He looked at the girl in front of Hollin, and the boy in Placit's hand, and the twenty people in the grey air round the table, all of them, inside ten yards; every one.

"I'm sorry," he said, to the girl, through the helm. "I'll apologise to you properly for this later." And pulled the pin, and rolled the stone across the floor under the table like a man bowling at a fair.

The rune counted five.

Everybody in the long panelled room went down.

All of them, all at once, as if the floor had been pulled out from under the room and the room had fallen onto it. The five and their sons and their wives and their clerks and the girl and the boy and the servant with his hand on the key, all of them, slammed flat onto the boards by four times their own weight, with a single great sound like a cart of sacks being tipped; and the chairs went over, and the cups, and the black flakes on the table jumped and settled; and Hollin's little silver knife went skittering away across the floor out of a hand that could not hold it up. The girl lay on her back beside him with her eyes wide, pinned, and breathing, and not hurt; the pot boy lay across Placit's legs. Nobody could lift a hand. Nobody could lift a head. The fire in the hearth pressed down flat in its grate as if a lid had been put on it.

Sixty. A slow sixty.

Somebody in the room was still moving.

Emeric felt him come up through the floor of the room before he saw him: the lit lamp. The house knight. He had come up the back stair from the gate at the sound of the flash, in his plate, with his crest that nobody knew, and he came in at the door at the far end of the room by the hearth, into the stone's ten yards; and he did not go down. He went to one knee, and stayed there, and got up again.

Slowly. As if he were climbing out of deep water in his armour. His own mana, all of it, the tracery in him lit like a lamp turned up to the top, pushing back against the pull, the way a knight's body pushed back against anything that came at it from outside; and the stone was a stone, six ounces, and he was a knight, and he stood. He was dragged down at every joint, at every plate, his sword hanging in his hand like a bar of lead; but he stood, and took a step, and another, round the end of the table, through the people lying on the floor, toward the door.

Ward went to meet him.

Ward stayed outside the ten yards until the last step, and then went in; and the stone took him too, a great invisible weight on his shoulders and his arms and his blade, and he went down to one knee the way the knight had, and came up again the way the knight had, slowly, with his tracery lit; and the two of them met by the end of the long table in the grey air over the bodies of the five, two big men in plate moving like men in a dream, slow, enormous, each blow taking as long as a breath, each parry a long heavy shove of steel on steel. Emeric could hear Ward breathing in his helm. He could feel them both, two lamps, burning, pushing.

The knight was strong. He was very strong; he had been chosen for this, and he had come up into the field on his own and stood up in it. But Ward had stood up in a great many fields in forty years, and this was only weight. He let the knight's blade come down, slow, and took it on his own near the hilt, and leaned, and turned it; and the knight's sword went down into the boards point first under its own impossible weight and stuck there, quivering, and Ward put his shoulder into the knight's chest and his foot behind the knight's heel, and let the stone do the rest.

The knight went down full length on his back with a crash that shook the room. The stone held him there. Ward stood over him with his blade at the gap between helm and gorget, swaying, heavy, breathing like a bellows.

"Stay," said Ward.

The knight stayed.

Fifty-eight. Fifty-nine. Sixty.

The weight went off the room all at once, like a held breath let out. Under the table, the gravity stone was a little heap of grey dust on the boards. The fire in the hearth leapt up in its grate. Twenty people lay on the floor of the long panelled room and did not get up, because they had been pinned flat for a slow sixty and their bodies did not yet believe they could.

Emeric went round the table.

He took the girl by the hand and lifted her up and sat her in a chair, and took the pot boy off Placit's legs and sat him in another. Neither of them said anything. The girl was shaking. He took off his left gauntlet and put his bare hand on her shoulder, a moment, and felt her note under it, small and quick and frightened and not hurt; and said through the helm that she was not to be afraid of him, which was a stupid thing to say through a black helm, and she looked at him with enormous eyes and nodded.

Then he counted.

Placit, on the floor, small, his pale eyes open, looking up at the black helm. Hollin, on his back, soft and grey, with his hand open where the silver knife had been. Vennor, on his side, holding his wife's hand. Strade, on his back, very neat, with his hands folded on his chest, as if he had decided to lie there; his eyes resting on the helm without moving at all.

Four.

"Dray," said Emeric.

Strade's eyes moved, for the first time. To the window. To the shutters on the yard side of the room.



Dray had not been in the room. Dray had been on the back stair when the flash went, coming up from the yard, from the stable, where he had gone, Emeric learned afterward, at midnight, to look at the horses, because Dray did not trust anybody with a horse but himself; and he had heard the flash and the shouting and turned round on the back stair and run. Down, out, across the yard. To the horses. To the stable gate onto Lisle's woods road, and the dark, and the north.

Emeric went out of the long room's yard window.

He did not open it; he went through the shutter and the casement together with his shoulder and out onto the top of the yard wall below, which ran along under the long room's windows at the height of a man and a half from the yard, dressed stone, a coping a foot wide; and the cups on his boots took it, pop, hold, and he was standing on the wall in the dark with the yard below him and the gun coming off his back into his hands.

The yard was dark. Smoke from the gate rolled over the front of the house and down into it in grey drifts. And across it, forty yards off, a big square shape was running, heavily, flat out, toward the stable gate, where the horses were stamping and screaming at the smoke and the flash and the shouting. Dray. And at the gate, at the horses' heads, by the stable wall, the still note. Not still now. Moving. Not toward Dray. Toward the gate.

And between the gate and the house, coming out of the dark of the back passage at a dead run, quick, catching, the half stop gone out of his note entirely, as if whatever had been waiting in it for five years had stopped waiting, Rylan.

Emeric put the stock to his cheek.

He had the crystal in the receiver, full, the selector on air. He had the blade of the foresight in the notch of the leaf. He had Dray's running shape at the top of the blade, a dark heavy thing in the drifting smoke, forty yards, crossing. He put the top of the blade on Dray's thigh, the meat at the back of it, above the knee, where a ball would put a man down and not kill him, and breathed out, and squeezed.

There was no bang.

A cough. A short, hard, sharp sound like a man spitting a cherry stone, and a kick in his shoulder, small, and nothing else; no flash, no smoke, no noise that would carry past the wall. Ambrose's flash had been the loudest thing in the county. This was the quietest thing that had ever been done in it.

Dray went down.

He went down the way a man goes down shot through the thigh, which is nothing like the way it looks in a play. The leg was simply not there under him on the next stride. He folded over it onto the cobbles of the yard with his hands out, shrieking, and lay there screaming and holding the back of his thigh in both hands.

The meat of it. Where he had put the blade. Emeric lay on the wall coping with the gun in his hands and his cheek against the stock, and felt the receiver under his cheek as full as it had been at the Band's foot, less one breath; and knew that Dray would live, and that Dray would limp, and did not have time to feel either, because in the stable gate, Rylan had reached Garrin.



Garrin had a knife in his hand. He had had it before Rylan came out of the passage. He threw it.

Not at Rylan's chest, which was steel. At his face, at the eye slit of his helm, from eight feet, underhand, fast, the way a man throws who has thrown a great many knives at a great many things in the dark and does not miss; and Rylan's left arm came up across the slit with the buckler on it, and the knife hit the flake and hung, and the flake sighed and went to dust, and the knife fell on the cobbles between them.

Two gone.

Garrin did not stop to look at it. He had a sword out by the time the knife hit the stones, and came in under Rylan's arm while it was still up, low, at the gap in the plate at the hip, as he had been taught by somebody who knew what plate was and where it was not; and Rylan was not there.

Emeric had watched Rylan fight for nearly eight years. He had never watched this.

Rylan did not fight Garrin. He took him apart. Not fast, though he was fast. Not hard, though when he hit, he hit like a falling wall. He took him apart the way Bram took apart a lock he wanted to understand, piece by piece, with great care, without hurry: the sword first, beaten out of Garrin's hand at the third pass with a blow on the forte that rang across the yard like a bell, and skittering away under the stamping horses. Then the knee, the left one, with the flat of the blade across the side of it, so that it went out sideways with a crack and Garrin went down on it. Then the right arm, when Garrin came up off the knee with a second knife from his boot, at Rylan's throat; Rylan took the wrist in his gauntlet and turned it, slowly, until the knife fell out of the hand and the hand was looking at the wrong side of the arm, and Garrin made a sound Emeric had never heard a man make.

Then Rylan had him on the cobbles, face down, with a knee in his back and the broken arm held up behind him, and Garrin's face in the dung and straw of the stable gate, and the horses screaming over them both.

And Rylan had his sword in his other hand. Its point was at the back of Garrin's neck, at the place under the skull. And it was not moving.

It went on not moving.

Emeric lay on the wall and watched it not move. He could feel Rylan from forty yards, every part of him; the quick note with the half stop gone out of it, burning, not catching any more, all of it pointed down the length of a sword at one place on the back of a man's neck. He could feel what it wanted. He did not move. He did not say anything. He had said it in the long room at Kaldo, once, and Rylan had heard it, and either that was enough or it was not, and nothing Emeric could shout across a dark yard now would make it more.

The point moved.

It came up off Garrin's neck, slowly, a hand's breadth, and stopped there. Then Rylan reversed the sword in his hand and put it back in its sheath, by feel, without looking, the way a man sheaths a sword he has sheathed ten thousand times; and took a length of cord off his belt, and began, very carefully, to tie Garrin's good wrist to his ankle, behind his back, under the stamping horses, in the dung.

"Nessa," said Rylan, to nobody, quite clearly, across the yard. "His Highness asked."

Emeric put his forehead down on the stock of the gun on the cold stone of the wall coping and shut his eyes.



Ward flew Dray back.

That was not in the plan. The plan had Declan's men coming round from the gate when the smoke cleared to take whatever was in the yard. But the smoke had not cleared, and Dray was screaming on the cobbles forty yards from the stable gate with a ball through his thigh and dragging himself toward the horses on his elbows, toward the woods road and the dark; and Ward came out of the long room's broken window behind Emeric onto the yard wall, and looked at him, and stepped off the wall into the air.

He woke the hearts on his suit as he stepped. Emeric heard them, the deep thrum of the gravity stones taking his weight and the air hearts behind them pushing; loud, in the night, and nobody in the yard to hear it but the men who were already shouting. Ward went across the yard at a man's height off the cobbles, low and black and fast, like a great bird going down a field after a rat; and stooped over Dray without landing, and took him by the back of his coat and his belt. The suit could not lift them both. It was made to carry a knight in plate and a little more, and Dray was a great deal more; so Ward did not try to make it. He came down, and kicked off the cobbles with all of a knight's legs, and the hearts took off as much of the weight as they could hold, howling; and the two of them went up thirty feet in one long slow bound over the yard, Ward in his black and Dray hanging from his fist, and came down, and Ward kicked again, and went up again. Three bounds, across the yard and over the wall, like a man on the moon in a picture-book. And at the top of each one, Dray screamed.

"Demons!" Dray screamed. His voice went all over the yard and over the wall and down the hill. "Demons! They fly! God help us, they fly, they fly!"

Ward brought him over the sill on the last bound and set him down, not gently, on the flags of the long room's floor through the broken window, beside the other four.

Nobody in that room believed a word Dray said, then or ever. He went on saying it for the rest of his life, to anybody who would listen. Demons. Black, and silent, and they flew. Nobody believed him at all.



The strongroom was in the north end of the house, on the ground floor, at the end of a stone passage, behind an iron door; and there was smoke coming out from under it.

Emeric could feel the man inside before he got to the door. One note. Alone, behind the iron, with the fire. A man of middle years, soft, ordinary, not dense, not thin; a clerk's note, or a steward's, or a shopkeeper's. Calm. Very calm. Moving about the strongroom in no hurry at all, from the shelves to the fire and back, as if he had a great deal to do and all night to do it in.

He knew the note. He had sat across a table from it at Placit's dinner in May, in the gold-laced coat, with a cup in his hand, asking about a share; and it had laughed. Softly, pleasantly, first, before anybody else at the table, with its clean hands folded on the cloth.

The Staple's chief clerk.

He took the breach stone out of his belt.

The iron door was very good iron. He did not want to know what was behind it so much as he wanted the door not to be there; and he set the stone against it at the lock, on its little cup of Duvrenne pitch so that it would hold to the iron, and pulled the pin, and stepped back round the corner of the passage, and counted.

The air in the stone came out of it all at once.

The door went into the strongroom off its hinges, flat, with a noise like a cannon, and slid across the floor inside and hit the far wall; and the fire in the grate went sideways out of the hearth in a long rush of sparks and burning paper, and settled. Fennick's canvas sausage, in its tin, on Emeric's belt, unlit, would go home to Kaldo that night unused, and Fennick would not speak to anybody for a week.

Emeric went in through the smoke.

The strongroom was a stone box twenty feet square with shelves on three walls from floor to ceiling, and on the shelves, the Staple. Two hundred years of it, in ledgers, in boxes, in bundles tied with tape: most of it gone now, shelf after shelf of it bare, the ash of it in the grate and on the floor in drifts. A few shelves left. On the wall over the hearth, in a frame of black wood under glass, a single sheet of old vellum with a great red seal hanging from it on a cord: the charter. Untouched. Keep the charter.

And in the middle of the floor, in the ash, a man in a good dark coat with his clean hands black to the wrist, kneeling where the door had knocked him down, with a ledger still in his arms. Looking up at the black helm in the smoke with an expression of mild, polite, intelligent interest.

"Ah," said Marrow. "Your Highness."

He did not get up. He did not drop the ledger. He knelt in the ash with it held against his chest, as Ottoline's carter had held an iron box against his chest in the snow.

"You were a great deal drunker at Placit's," said Marrow pleasantly. "I did say so. Strade said so. Placit wouldn't hear it." He looked at the gun on Emeric's back, and the black suit, and the empty buckler. "I'd have liked to see that ink of yours write. I held a bottle of it once, at Placit's table, and never got to use it. Hollin's steward poured his in the river." He smiled. "Clever. Very clever. It nearly had us." He looked at the ledger in his arms. "It nearly had all of it."

Emeric took off his helm.

He did not know why. He stood in the strongroom in the smoke with the helm in his hand and looked down at the man who had planned the boat. Who had sent Garrin to Rylan's door with four names, and a hold in the dark, and a boy of nine in it for days. Who had said, through a quick voice on a deck, that a story reads better than a ransom. Forty Gold, to M. And again, for forty and not a Copper more.

A soft ordinary man of middle years with clean hands, kneeling in ash.

"Who are you?" said Emeric.

"The chief clerk of the Staple at Wayford," said Marrow, "under the charter of the crown." He nodded at the frame on the wall. "That's all I've ever been. Twenty-two years."

"Who do you answer to?"

"The five."

"No," said Emeric.

Marrow looked up at him with his mild polite interested eyes.

"The five answer to you," said Emeric. "Placit wrote it in his own hand. Burn the book, keep the charter. He's sent for you. He's afraid of you." He did not move. "You don't answer to the five. Who do you answer to?"

Marrow smiled. It was a very good smile. It went all the way to his eyes and stopped there, as if it had run into a wall.

"Nobody, Highness," he said.

And he was lying. Emeric knew it. He did not know how he knew it; there was nothing in the man's face, nothing in his voice, nothing in the clean black hands on the ledger. But he had his sense spread through the strongroom, through the smoke and the ash and the man kneeling in it, and the man's note, under the calm, under the smile, had done something when he said nobody. A flinch. Not fear. The other thing. The note of a man who has looked up, for a breath, at somebody standing over him who is not in the room.

Somebody stood above Marrow. Somebody Marrow was more afraid of, kneeling in the ash with a black helm over him and a gun on its back, than he was of anything in this room.

And Marrow would not say. Not now, not at a trial, not at the end of a rope. Emeric could see that in him too. A man who had been very carefully chosen for being a man who would not say.

There was one way to know.

He had a rule. He had written it himself, in his first autumn in Kaldo, in the back of Armand's notebook, the week after he first took Bram and Ferra in: Contact. Consent. Nothing forced. Not ever, and not to save time. No strangers. No enemies. He had said it aloud to every one of the six before he took them in. He had never broken it. He had never once taken anybody into the room who had not said yes.

He knelt down in the ash in front of Marrow and took him by the wrist.

Marrow's mild face changed, for the first time. He tried to pull the wrist away. It did not come.

"What are you..." said Marrow.

Emeric took him in.



It was like going through a wall that did not want to be gone through.

The room was there. The room was always there, behind his eyes, in the stone on his wrist: the long quiet place with its light that came from nowhere, where he had taken Bram and Ferra and Ward and all of them, and they had come willingly, through a door he held open, and stood blinking in the light. Marrow did not come through any door. Emeric pulled him, and Marrow came the way a man comes who is being dragged through a hedge backward, tearing, resisting every inch; and the room did not like it. The room had never had anybody in it who did not want to be there. It went cold. The light in it went thin and hard. And Marrow stood in it, or something stood in it that was Marrow, a soft dark shape that would not hold still, that kept coming apart at the edges like smoke and pulling itself back together, and screaming, without any sound.

Who, said Emeric, in the room. Who do you answer to.

The shape screamed.

Who.

And something came out of it. Not an answer; Marrow did not give an answer. Something came out of him the way water comes out of a cracked jar when it is squeezed, not because the jar wants to give it: a letter. Emeric saw it, in the room, for less than a breath, in the cold thin light. A letter on good paper, folded, with no seal, in a hand he did not know; a hand that was not any hand at all, square and careful and anonymous as print. And a feeling with the letter, that came off Marrow like heat off a stove: the feeling of a man reading a letter from somebody he has never seen and never will, and doing what it says, at once, every time, for twenty years, because the last man who had not done what one of those letters said had been found in the river with stones in his coat.

Somebody above him. Somebody who wrote in a hand like print and never signed.

Then the room threw them both out.



He was on his back in the ash on the strongroom floor with his nose pouring blood into his mouth and the back of his head ringing where it had hit the stone.

Marrow was on his side a yard away, curled round the ledger, with blood coming out of his nose and his ears, and his eyes open and staring at nothing, and his mouth moving. He was alive. Emeric could feel him; a thin, torn, shaking note, like a man who has been beaten. He would live. He would not be the same.

Neither would Emeric.

He knew it before he sat up. There was something in his head that had not been there a moment ago. Not in the room; in him, in his own head, in the place where memories were. A kitchen. Small, low, warm, with a black range and a scrubbed table and a window over a sink with a geranium on the sill in a red pot. He had never stood in it in his life. He could smell it: bread, and coal, and the geranium. And a woman's voice, from the next room, calling a name, a name he did not catch, gently, the way a mother calls a child in from a garden at dusk.

It was not his. He knew it was not his. He could not get it out.

He sat up in the ash, holding his nose, with the kitchen in his head and the woman's voice calling, and looked at Marrow curled on the floor.

Ward was in the doorway. He had been there, Emeric understood, for some time. He stood in the broken door of the strongroom in his black suit with his helm off and his face grey with smoke and the smoke going past him into the passage, and looked at Emeric on the floor, and at Marrow, and at the blood; and said nothing while the smoke went by.

"You forced it," said Ward at last.

"Yes."

"You have a rule."

"Yes."

Ward looked at Marrow on the floor.

"Was it worth it?"

Emeric thought about the letter in a hand like print, and the man in the river with stones in his coat, and the kitchen with the geranium, and the woman calling.

"I don't know," he said. "Ask me in a year."

Ward was quiet. Then he came into the strongroom, through the ash, and held out his hand; and Emeric took it, and Ward pulled him up, and held him a moment by the arm when his legs did not want him.

"The yard," said Ward. "The shot."

"I know."

"You should not have taken it. A running man, at forty yards, crossing, in smoke, with Rylan coming the other way. You could have put that ball in his spine, or his belly, or the horse, or Rylan." Ward's voice was perfectly flat. "It went where you meant. That was the gun, and the Band. It was not you being right."

Emeric did not argue. There was nothing to argue with.

"He'll limp," he said.

"He will limp," said Ward, "and he will live, and he will tell everybody who will listen for the rest of his life that demons fly." He let go of Emeric's arm. "Next time, if there is a next time, you will let the man run, and Declan's men will take him at the gate. That is what the column is for." He looked at the gun on Emeric's back. "That is a fine gun. It is not a reason."

"No."

Ward looked at him a breath longer. Then he turned to Marrow on the floor, and bent, and took the ledger out of the man's arms, gently, the way you take something from a child who is asleep; and Marrow let it go.

Ward did not open it. He held it out to Emeric.

"Ferra will want this," said Ward. "Unburned."



The column came in through the gate at a little before three, when the smoke cleared, with Declan at its head and Osric beside him with his mask pushed up on his forehead and his face black with soot round where it had been. Some of the house men at the gate had put down their swords when they saw what came out of the smoke at them in masks, with eyes of glass and mouths like cans, breathing; and some had not. Four of them were dead on the road by the chain. One of Kaldo's was cut along the ribs, and walking.

The five were in the long panelled room, under guard, on chairs. Dray was on the floor, because he could not sit, with Declan's man binding his thigh and Dray telling him about demons. Garrin was in the stable, in the straw, tied wrist to ankle, with Rylan sitting on an upturned bucket in the stable door, not looking at him, eating an apple he had found in a manger. The knight sat against the wall of the long room where Ward had put him, with his helm off, a young man with fair hair and a broken collarbone, who had not said one word, and who watched Ward wherever Ward went with an expression of great respect.

Marrow lay on a bench in the passage outside the strongroom with a wet cloth on his face. He had not spoken since the room.

The charter hung on the strongroom wall in its frame, under its glass, untouched, with its great red seal.

Overhead, the K Discovery had come back down out of the dark at Declan's lamp and hung three hundred feet over the yard with her stones dim and her panes black, waiting to take up the wounded. Vantic was at her rail with her book, Cade said afterward. She had been there since the flash went, and had not written anything.

Emeric stood in the stone passage at the north end of the house with the unburned ledger in his hands and blood drying on his upper lip and a stranger's kitchen in his head, and did what he had been not doing all night, and let himself feel what was under his feet.

The stillness.

It was right under him now. Not a hundred feet down; ten, perhaps, fifteen, under the flags of the passage, under the cellars. Close. A patch of quiet the size of a long room, and a hand pressed flat over a mouth. And from inside here, from on top of it, with nothing between it and him but stone, he could feel what he had not been able to feel from three hundred feet up in the dark, on the ship.

It was not empty.

It was full of people. He could feel them now, very faint, at the very bottom of his sense, the way you feel the warmth of a hearth through a wall when the fire is nearly out. Many of them. A dozen; more. Close together, in the dark, very still. And every one of their notes so thin, so worn, so faint, that from three hundred feet up he had taken them for nothing at all.

He stood in the passage with the ledger in his hands.

He had meant to send the five to the crown. He had meant it all summer; he had meant it in the yard, on the wall, with his cheek on the stock, and in the strongroom with the helm in his hand. He had said it at the long table, in May: the five go to the crown, on what can be got openly, and the crown hangs them or does not, and either way it is the crown's.

He still meant it. He stood in the passage and made himself go on meaning it.

"Ward," he said.

Ward came down the passage.

"There's a way down," said Emeric. "Under here. A cellar under the cellars. There has to be a door." He did not take his eyes off the flags under his feet. "Find it."
