Chapter 207 - Winter Yards

The Milford farm book came in on the Saturday wagon every week in its tin box, and Ferra read it on the Saturday night, and for the whole of the harvest she read it looking for the lie.

There was always a lie in a first year. Not a lie anybody told. The lie a first year told on its own, because it was a first year and everybody wanted it to be something. She had seen it on the lake farms the autumn after the prince cut them into nine divisions, when the first harvest came in no better than the one before and Farris had stood at the long-room table and said so, in front of everybody, with his hat in his hands, and been right. A rotation did nothing in its first year. The prince had said so himself, at the drum, with a sheaf in his hand. It took four fields and four years to go round once, and until it had gone round once the ground did not know it was in a rotation at all.

So she read the Milford book for the thing somebody would want to be true, and looked under it.

The bench wheat was up. That was true, and she checked it three ways before she let herself write it. It was not the new ground; the new ground on the first bench had gone under turnips and peas and beans at midsummer, off the drill, and would not see wheat for a year. It was the wheat Paxton's own tenants had sown on the lower end of the second bench the autumn before, in the old way, broadcast off the hand, on ground that had been in corn on and off since Paxton's grandfather. Colm's main had reached the head of it in the middle of the summer. The farm book said, in Colm's bad hand, the week it reached: Sluice to the old wheat. F. says why not. And then, all through the dry month, when every field on the flats went brown and the old men said it was the driest they could remember, a line every few days: Old wheat, watered.

It had come in heavier than any wheat on that bench in the tenants' memory. Not by a miracle. By about a third. Ferra read the figure and read it again and worked it against what the same fields had given in each of the four years before, which Paxton's steward had sent at her asking, and it was a third, near enough, and it was all on the watered ground and none of it anywhere else.

The flats were what they had always been. Where the water had not gone, the wheat had come in thin, and the barley thinner, the way it came in thin every dry year. The book said that too, and Ferra was glad it did. A book that only had good figures in it was a book somebody had been kind to.

The rest of what was better was not in the ground. It was in the getting-in.

The reaper had gone out on the second bench behind two horses in collars, and cut in a day what twelve men with sickles cut in three, and the drum had threshed at the siding shed off a belt from the engine house, the same engine that lifted the water, on the days the bench did not want water, and the grain had gone into bins at five steadings and not one barn. There was a line in the farm book in Farris's hand, very small, beside the fifth: Five. Not one. Ferra knew what it meant. So would anybody who had been in Kaldo the winter a man called Sethe burned a barn at Milford in a night and a quarter of the barony's seed with it.

It had rained at the end of the harvest, as it always did, for a week. In other years the rain had caught a third of Milford's corn in the stook and rotted it where it stood, because there were not hands enough to get it in before the weather turned. This year it caught almost none. The reaper had been too fast for it.

Ferra wrote the figure at the foot of the harvest's page. It was not a miracle. It was a harvest a little better than any Milford had had, got in a good deal faster, and lost less of afterward. It was a first year.

It was enough.

The grain went east in closed wagons, on the Milford line, in the first weeks of the autumn, as fast as the drum could fill the sacks: east over the long bank across the flat to the works siding across the water from the island, and nowhere near the Left Bridge.

Ferra sat with her sheet from the autumn before, the sheet with the heap on the left and what wanted it on the right, and did the sum again.

She had done it the autumn the beds were tripled, and every quarter since. It had never come out. There had always been two figures on that page that were both correct and could not both be paid: the dung the fields wanted and the dung the beds wanted, out of one heap that was not big enough for both. And in the winter between, the camp on the flat had eaten through the reserve, as Hollis had said it would, and Saban had bought grain on the coast at the coast's price to see them to the thaw, and Hollis had said at the table that he had written it down.

She did it now with Milford in it.

Milford's grain came in on the right-hand side, against the camp and the city, and covered them, with a margin; not a large one, but a margin, which was a thing she had not written on that side of that page since she ruled it. And because Milford fed the camp, the lake farms did not have to, and because they did not have to, they did not have to be pushed; they could have the dung they had and no more, and be what they were, nine divisions on the edge of a lake growing fresh grain and seed for the island and the works. And because the lake farms did not need more dung, the beds could keep the heap. And because Milford's bone stayed on Milford's fields now, and Milford's yards made their own dung for their own benches, nothing on Milford's side of the book wanted anything out of Kaldo's heap at all.

The two figures stood on the page, left and right. For the first time since she had ruled it, they could both be paid.

She took it to the long-room table on the Monday, and read it out, and read the margin, and said that nobody would be buying grain in the spring.

Hollis had his sheet in front of him, as he always did, with his one good hand flat on it. He listened to her read it. He looked at his own sheet, and then at hers, for some time. Then he picked up his pencil in his good hand and wrote a line at the foot of his own sheet, slowly, the way he wrote everything, and turned it round so the table could see.

"I've written it down," he said.

It was what he said at every table. He had said it about the harvest bounty, and the beds, and the thin year, and the grain bought on the coast. Ferra had begun, privately, a long time ago, to write it at the foot of her own sheets, because it was the most useful sentence anybody at the table said.

She realised, looking at his sheet turned round on the table, that it was the first time she had ever heard him say it about something that had gone right.

She did not say so. Nobody did. Emeric looked at Hollis's line for a while, and then down the table at nothing in particular, and then went on to the next thing.

Paxton did not kill his cattle that autumn.

It was the first autumn in Milford's memory, and in his, that he did not. Every autumn of his life, and his father's, and back as far as anybody in the market town had heard of, Milford had killed before the grass went. Every beast that could not be carried through the winter on the hay the bottoms made was driven to the slaughter yards behind the town in the tenth month and killed and salted and sold, for what the autumn market would give, which was never much, because every barony in the south killed at the same time and every market was full of meat. Then the herd that was left, the breeding cows and the best of the young stock, stood through the winter in the bottoms on the last of the hay, getting thinner, and came out onto the grass in the spring looking like hides on sticks, and began again.

That was the herd. That was what a herd was. He had not known there was any other way for it to be, and nor had anybody he had ever met.

This autumn the slaughter yards killed what they were meant to kill, the beasts the plan had put a mark against in the margin, and no more. And the rest went into the yards.

There were five of them, one at each steading, where the prince's plan had drawn them. Long roofed sheds open on the south side, with stone walls on the other three against the wind off the flats, built all through the summer by the kiln men and the drain men and three of the thirty-one, for a wage, with their debts coming down a Copper at a time in Ferra's book. Beside every yard, the turnip clamp: the turnips off the first bench pulled in the autumn and heaped in long ridges under straw and earth against the frost, as the plan showed, so that a man could open one end with a fork every morning and take out a day's worth. And the clover ricks, the second cut of the summer, stacked and thatched. And straw. Straw everywhere, last year's and this year's, off the benches and the flats, that Milford had always burned in a heap in the autumn because it had no use for it; the engine house burned some of it now, and the paper works at Kaldo took some by the wagon, and the rest went into the yards, deep, bedding, so deep that a beast standing in it stood in straw to the knee.

Paxton walked the yards with Farris on the morning of the first frost.

It came hard, that year, overnight, white on the flats from the town to the horizon and the leg smoking in the cold. He rode out to the first steading at first light with Dunmore, and Farris was already there, standing at the open side of the yard with his hands in his coat, watching the cattle.

They were eating. That was what Paxton saw first. It was the first morning of real winter, the morning in every other year when the herd in the bottoms began to get thin, and they were standing along the manger in the straw in their long row, forty head in this yard, heads down, eating sliced turnip out of the manger as fast as the boy with the fork could put it in, with their breath going up in the cold in a cloud over the whole yard. The yard smelled of turnip and dung and warm cattle. It was warm. He stood in the open side of it and felt the warmth come off them against his face.

He walked down the yard behind their tails, slowly, with Farris beside him, the way he would have walked down a sale ring. He looked at every beast. He had looked at these cattle all his life; he knew them by their backs. They were carrying the weight they had come in off the grass with. Not more, yet. But not less. On the first morning of the frost they were carrying the grass's weight, in the straw to their knees, eating.

At the end of the yard he stopped, and put his hand on the rump of a roan cow he had bred himself, and left it there.

"They'll come out in the spring like this," Farris said. "Or better."

"I know."

"And they'll be in calf, most of them, and the calves'll come strong. And the bullocks that would've gone to the yards this month'll go to market in the spring instead, when nobody else has any, fat."

"I know," said Paxton. "I read the margin."

He had read it at his own table in the spring, standing, with his hand flat on the edge of the board beside the plan, and he had read it every week since in Farris's farm book on the way through to Ferra, which Farris had let him do without asking why. He had known, since the spring, what the yards were for. He had not known what they would look like on the morning of the first frost. He had not known that a yard full of cattle eating in a cold that would have been starving them made a sound, all together, a low steady tearing and chewing and breathing, like a mill, or like a crowd in a church before anybody has begun to speak.

He took his hand off the roan cow. Farris did not say anything else, and he was grateful for it.

Outside the yard the heap had begun.

Every morning the boys forked the straw out of the yard where the cattle had dunged it through the night, and forked it onto the heap beside the clamp, and forked clean straw in. The heap was the height of a man's waist already. By the spring, Farris said, it would be over a man's head, and it would go out onto the first bench, onto the turnip ground, before the barley went in with the clover under it, and the barley would be better for it than anything on the flats. And the yard would be empty again, and the cattle out on the paddocks, and the grass better for the cattle not having stood on it all winter at the ford.

Down on the leg, below the siding, the bone mill was running. It had come west on the wagons in the late summer, a pair of iron rollers off the works' pattern and a stamp, and Colm had built its house by the engine house so that the same belt could turn it, and every day that the engine was not lifting water it was crushing bone. The heaps behind the slaughter yards that had stood for twenty years with the dogs on them were going down, a cartload at a time, and a new heap of pale grey grit was going up by the mill, for the benches.

Paxton rode past it on the way back to the town, and stopped, and looked at it. His father had sold that bone to Hollis's father. He had tried to sell it to Kaldo at three times its worth, to make a point about a law. It was going onto his own wheat.

He rode on without saying anything to Dunmore. Dunmore had the sense not to say anything to him.

He went to Kaldo on the train the week after.

He had never been to Kaldo any other way than on a horse, or behind one. It was a day's ride, a long one, on the old road east over the flat and round the lake; he had ridden it to the long-room table twice in his life, once over a contract and once with his box of rolls, and he had ridden home both times in the dark with the thing he had gone about unanswered, or answered in a way he did not like. He had not meant to go this time either. He had not meant to go at all, until he found himself on the cinder platform at the siding at nine in the morning with Dunmore beside him holding a ticket, because Dunmore had bought one without being asked.

The carriage had a bench and a window. He sat on the bench and looked out of the window. He saw the benches go by, and the first tower with its wind pump turning, and the yards at the steadings with the smoke of the cattle's breath going up over them, and the flats, white still in the hollows where the sun had not reached. He saw the long bank across the flat, and the lake, and the island on it, and the new city behind its wall on the flat with the scaffolds on its gate towers and a great iron frame standing in one of its streets under canvas. He saw all of it in an hour.

An hour. He got down at the works siding with his legs not tired and his coat not wet and his horse, for the first time in his life, not with him, and stood on the platform for a moment and did not know what to do with his hands.

They put him at the long-room table in the afternoon, because he asked for it. Ferra was there with her book, and Hollis with his sheet, and the prince at the head, and Ward in his chair by the door, and Paxton sat down in the chair they gave him, which was the chair he had sat in with his box of rolls, and put his hands on the table.

He did not say anything about the yards, or the harvest, or the bone. Ferra had expected him to. She had her figures open in front of her in case he wanted them. He did not look at them.

"Three years," he said.

The prince looked at him down the length of the table. Ferra watched him understand it: that this was not a question, and not a complaint, and not thanks, though it had some of each in it; that it was a cattleman who had read a margin in the spring and walked a yard at the first frost, telling the man who drew the margin that he had understood how long it would take.

"Three," said the prince.

That was how long the ring in the corner of the first sheet took to come round. Wheat, turnips, barley and clover, and wheat again. Three more years before every field on the benches had been all four, and the ground knew it was in a rotation, and the first year's figures stopped being a first year's and became what the farm was.

Paxton nodded, once. He sat for a while longer, with his hands flat on the table, looking at nothing; and then he said that he had a train at four, and got up, and shook the prince's hand, which he had not done either of the other times he had sat in that chair, and went.

Ferra watched him go down the stair. She thought, not for the first time, that she had no column in any book for the way a man walked out of a room, and that if she had, the figure in it would have been the best on the page.

She wrote the total that night at the foot of the first page of the Milford farm book, the fair copy, the one that lived in the press in the counting room and went to nobody.

The wheat. The barley. The turnips in the clamp and the clover in the ricks. The head in the yards, and the head killed, and the difference. The bone crushed. The wages paid, and the debts come down, a Copper at a time, against thirty-one names. The grain gone east. The margin on the right-hand side of the heap's page, which she carried across in pencil, so that the two books would agree.

Under the total she ruled a line. Under the line she wrote, in the small plain hand she used for things that were only true:

Year one of four.

She blotted it. Then she sat for a moment with the book open, and thought about Milford as the prince had drawn it and she had costed it: the barony's larder now, all four baronies' bread and meat, and the knot where the barony's rails met, lying out on open grass from the siding to the sky with not a wall anywhere on it, and a herd in its yards that anybody could count from the road.

She did not write that down. She closed the book and put it in the press.
