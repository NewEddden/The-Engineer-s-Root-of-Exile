Chapter 80 - Rag and Gall

The rags came in over three weeks at the end of summer, and they came from everywhere, and they smelled.

Ferra put a notice on the church door and another on the gate, and a boy with a handcart went round the island twice a week, and a clerk sat at a table at the foot of the keep stair weighing what came in and paying for it by the pound. Worn shirts. Sheets gone thin as a promise. The ends of worn-out sails from the lower water, which Fletcher sent up in a bale with a note saying he supposed Emeric knew what he was doing, and the bandage linen from Imani's ward, boiled twice and sent over in a basket with a note saying she would like to know what it became. It all went into a shed beside the finishing shop, into tubs of water, and was left to rot.

That was the part nobody liked. The rags had to soften before they could be beaten, and the way to soften them was to let them sit wet and warm until they began, very gently, to go bad. For nine days the shed smelled like a drain in August. Men from the foundry floor walked the long way round it. Bram, who said he had smelled worse at a forge, said it was nothing, and then stopped going in.

On the tenth day Neta cut the rags into strips, and the strips went into the beater.

It was a small thing, as Emeric had wanted it. A long oval tub with a wall down its middle, so that the water could run round it in a loop, and in one side of the loop a wooden roll set with iron blades, turning against a bladed plate in the tub's floor, driven off the wheel by the finishing shop's belt. The rags went round and round and through the blades, and each time they came through they were a little less like cloth and a little more like something else, until by the end of a day the water in the tub was the colour and thickness of thin porridge and there was no cloth left in it at all.

"That's paper," Emeric said.

Bram looked into the tub for a long time. "That's soup."

"It's paper that hasn't been made yet."

The making was a thing he could show but not do. He had drawn the mould: a wooden frame the size of a large sheet, with fine brass wires laid across it close as the strings of a harp and a few heavier wires running the other way to hold them, and a loose wooden rim, the deckle, that sat on top. Jorrin had drawn the wire through a plate, over and over, finer each time, until it was as fine as he could get it. The dipping was Neta's. She took the mould in both hands, dipped it into the vat of pulp, lifted it level, and shook it, once side to side and once front to back, and the water ran out through the wires and left a thin wet mat of fibres lying across them. Then she turned the mould over onto a sheet of woollen felt and pressed, and lifted, and the mat stayed on the felt. A sheet.

She did it wrong eleven times on the first morning. By the afternoon she was doing it right one time in three. By the end of the week she did not think about it.

The wet sheets went between felts in a stack, and the stack went into a cider press borrowed from the keep's orchard, and the water came out of them in a long grey stream. Then they went up into the loft over the finishing shop and hung over cords like washing, and dried there for four days. Then they went, one at a time, through a warm bath of glue boiled out of hide scraps that had come east from Milford on the grain carts, and back up to the loft to dry again.

That was the step that mattered, and it was the step nobody in Randillia did properly. Paper that had not been sized drank ink the way a cloth drinks water. He remembered it. The first drawing he had ever made for Bram, nine years ago, a furnace on a sheet of the barony's best paper, had feathered into a smear in two seconds because the paper had nothing in it to stop the ink going in. A sheet that had been through the glue bath had a skin. Ink lay on it. A line stayed where it was put.

He drew a line across the first dried sheet with the nib of Ferra's pen, which she did not lend him willingly, and the line stayed where it was put.

The ink was simpler and it was also worse, because it involved sulphur.

Kaldo's ore ran high in it. Bram had corrected the whole blast around it nine years ago, before a single pour, with the air coming in at an angle so the melt did not cook uneven. The heaps of spent ore behind the foundry had been sitting in the rain ever since, and the rain had been doing something to them. Emeric took a boy and a spade and a barrel, and dug down into the oldest heap, and found what he was looking for crusted in pale green along the cracks in the stone: a salt of iron that the rain had drawn out of the sulphur, and that would dissolve in water and turn black when it met the right thing.

The right thing was galls. Saban brought back two sacks of them off a dyer on the coast road, and the children of the island brought in a great many more from the oaks along the edge of the Whispering Woods, at a coin a basket, until Ferra stopped the coin. They were hard little brown balls that grew on the oaks where a wasp had laid its eggs, and they were bitter as anything on earth, and when they were crushed and soaked they gave up a pale brown liquor that went black the moment the iron salt went into it.

He made two inks, in two pots, and he was very clear about the difference.

The first was book ink. Full of iron, strong, black as a crow once it had been on the page a day. It would outlast the paper it was written on and the man who wrote it. It was for dip pens and quills and the kind of document that was meant to still be true in a hundred years.

The second was pen ink. Less iron, more water, strained four times through linen and then left to settle and poured off the top, so that there was nothing left in it that could come out of the liquid and sit in a fine channel. It was paler in the pot, and it went black more slowly on the page, and it did not eat a steel nib, and it did not silt a barrel. It was the bottle he had put beside Ferra's pen at the start of the summer, made now in a quantity that would last her a year.

"Two inks," Ferra said, labelling the jars.

"Two inks. Never the other way round."

"I heard you the first time."

She had. Nine days later she was writing up the trial's accounts at the end of a long evening, and the pen ran dry in the middle of a column, and the pen bottle was on the other side of the room and the book well was at her elbow, and she was Ferra and the column was not finished. She topped it up from the well.

He found her at it an hour after that, at her table in the lamplight, with the pen taken to pieces on a folded cloth and a bowl of warm water and a pin. The book ink had done exactly what he had said it would. It had come out of itself in the fine channel of the feed, a black silt no thicker than a hair, and blocked it solid. She was clearing it a grain at a time.

He did not say anything. He had the sense to see that anything he said would be the wrong thing, and he went and sat at the other end of the room and read something until she had finished.

It took her most of the evening. The pen came clean. When it was back together she filled it from the pen bottle, and wrote a line to test it, and then she opened the trial's book to the page headed WHAT WENT WRONG, which until that moment had been blank, and wrote at the top of it, as item one, in the pen, in her own hand:

Pen filled from book well in haste. Feed silted. Cleared in one evening. Pen undamaged. Do not do this.

She did not look at him while she wrote it, and she never did it again.

By the end of the summer the trial had made eleven hundred and forty good sheets and threw out four hundred and some that had come off the mould with thin places or holes or a boy's thumbprint, and it had made enough of each ink to see the six and the keep's clerks through to the spring. Ferra had all of it in the book: the rags in by the pound, the glue in by the pot, the sheets out by the quire, the waste by the quire, and the page of what went wrong, which by then ran to nineteen items, the first of them in her hand and the other eighteen in his.

It was a trial and he kept it a trial. It was not an industry. It had been built to find out what an industry would need, and it had found out, and every line in the book was a line in the plan for the real thing, which he was not building yet.

But the sheets were good. That was the difficulty. They were white and even and they took a line like a drawing board, and there was nothing like them in the south.

He showed them to Vantic in Ferra's room, because anything that was going to become money was going to become a line in her report, and he would rather she saw it early.

She took a sheet and held it to the window, and looked at the light through it. She turned it and felt the edge. She took a second sheet off the stack, from somewhere near the bottom, and compared the two, and then she asked Ferra for the book and read the page of what went wrong, all nineteen items, without comment.

"Four vats," she said. "A proper beater on its own wheel, not a belt off somebody else's. A drying loft three times the size of that one, and a glue house. Say thirty hands in the first year. You'd clear the build in three years if the south takes the paper, and nine if it doesn't." She put the sheet down. "The mills at Linmouth went the nine. They made better paper than anybody within a month's ride, and the only buyers who'd pay for better were a month's carriage away, and the carriage ate the difference."

She said it the way she said everything, as a figure. Nobody in the room had ever heard of Linmouth.

Emeric asked where it was.

"North," Vantic said, and handed the book back to Ferra. "A long way north."

Ferra took the book, and wrote something in the back of it, small, and did not look up.

"Four families," he said, at the table, the next morning.

He had laid it out for them the way Ferra had laid the year out at midwinter, as a number and then the things underneath it. Wayford made paper. It had made paper for as long as anybody could remember, badly, out of whatever rags came through the junction, and sold it up and down the south because there was nothing else. There were two families at the vats there, the Pells and the Keelings. There was an ink house, the Ambrys, three generations old. And at Milford there was a family called Hesketh who made coarse paper for wrapping and a little for writing, and who had sold most of it to Paxton's factor for twenty years.

As of the Crown's judgment every one of them was his, in exactly the way the ore was his and the tolls at the junction were his. He was responsible for them. He had not asked to be, and it made no difference.

"And when the real works run," he said, "they're finished."

Nobody argued. They had all held the sheets.

"Then don't build it," Saban said. "Or build it and sell north, where you won't be in their market."

"The carriage eats it. Vantic says so and she's right." He turned his hand over on the table. "I'm going to build it. I'm going to go to them now, before there's a works or a vat or a single sheet for sale, with these in my hand, and tell them what's coming and what they can have instead. A man told the terms in advance is choosing. A man told afterwards is being handled."

"What can they have instead?" Imani said.

"Whatever they'll take." He had it written out. "Come into the works as masters of their own floors, on wages, with their names on the floor and not on the door. Or sell to the works: hide glue from Milford, the iron salt from Grayrock's spoil, rags from anywhere. Or license the process from me and make the good paper themselves, in their own shops, and pay me something for the telling. Or turn it all down and keep making what they make, and go on selling it for as long as anybody buys it."

"And you'll sell yours cheaper," Saban said.

"I won't sell mine at all. Not at first." He put a sheet down on the table between them. "Every clerk in four baronies goes over to this on one order. Every writ, every tally, every receipt, every letter out of this keep. It's the paper my government is written on. That's all. Anybody who deals with my government gets his copy of the contract on it, because a contract is a thing two men each keep one of. He'll find that convenient. Then he'll find it usual. Then he'll stop noticing, and he'll start buying it for his own books because it's what his books are already full of." He looked round the table. "Nobody will be told to use it."

There was a short silence.

"So in ten years," Ferra said, "there's one paper in the south, and it's ours, and nobody was ever made to use it."

She said it flatly, without any weight on any of it, the way she would have read out the total at the foot of a column, and then she went back to her book. Nobody picked it up. Emeric did not either. He had heard it described more accurately than he had described it to himself, and he let it sit.

Saban's objection, when it came, was commercial and correct. Buying out four small families would cost less than a single season of fighting them, and he had never seen a man lose money by being generous to people who were about to be poor anyway. Ferra had the number for what it would cost, and it was less than Emeric had feared and more than he had hoped.

It was Bram who said the thing that landed.

"A man who's been master of his own vat for thirty years," Bram said, "you give him a wage and his name on the floor, and he'll take your money, and he'll thank you for it, and he'll go home and look at his own vat standing cold." He did not look at Emeric. He looked at his own hands. "He's lost his door. That's what you're buying. You're not wrong to buy it. Just know what it is."

Bram had not worked his own forge in two years. Nobody at the table said so.

He went to Milford first, because it was a day west and because he wanted to learn how the words sounded out loud before he said them to anybody who would hate him.

The Heskeths were three generations under one roof at the edge of the town, where the stream came down off the plains, and old Hesketh listened to the whole of it standing up, turning one of Neta's sheets over and over in his big cattleman's hands. His paper was coarse and grey and good for wrapping. He had never pretended otherwise. When Emeric was done he said that he had been selling wrapping to Paxton's factor for twenty years and that Dunmore had been telling him for ten of them that something better would come along one day. He took the wage. He took it the same afternoon, and asked whether his grandson could come to Kaldo and learn the dipping from whoever had made the sheet in his hand, and Emeric said her name was Neta and that she would be glad of him, which he hoped was true.

Then he rode north, with Ward, for Wayford.

It was the first time since the reading that he had been more than a day from the keep, and the first time the seventh clause of the Standing Order had been anything but ink. Armand held Kaldo. The old man had come down to the Left Bridge to see them off with a sheet of paper in his hand, one of the new ones, on which he had written a list of the things he intended to do and not do in the barony's name while its holder was away, and he had asked Emeric to read it, and Emeric had declined. He had given Armand the barony. He was not going to give it to him and then check his list.

Five days of road. The relay posts were going up along it now, one every hard ride, squat stone towers on the high ground with a trench round each for the footings and a man already living in the nearest cottage to learn the lamps. Half of them had stones in. The rest were footings and holes. The Wayford leg would take the year, as he had said it would, and on the fourth day Ward, who had been silent for most of a morning, pointed at a half-built post on a ridge and said it would make a good place to put an archer, and Emeric said yes, and they rode on.

Wayford did not want him.

He had known it would not, and he found that knowing it was not the same as riding into it. The town had lost its baron in a public judgment nearly two years ago and its independence in the same breath, and it had lost its easy money to a clean audit a season after that, and nobody in the square looked at him. They looked at Ward. They looked at the horses. They looked at the ground in front of the horses, and then they went on with what they were doing, and the space around the two of them stayed exactly one step wider than the street needed.

The clerk met them at the counting house. His name was Pennock, and he had been the most junior man in Sethe's employ nearly two years ago, and was now the man through whom four baronies' worth of tolls passed at the junction. He was thin and neat and very quiet, and he had the sheets of the four families' rate declarations for the last six years laid out on a table before Emeric had got his gloves off.

"You'll want to know which of them will listen," Pennock said.

Emeric had not said why he had come. He looked at the table, and then at the clerk.

"How do you know which of them will listen?"

"They've declared to this office every quarter for six years," Pennock said. "I've read every declaration. Nobody's ever asked me about any of them." He put a finger on the first stack. "The Ambrys declare honestly and pay late, because the old man's proud and the son does the books and is frightened of him. They'll take a wage if the son is in the room when you offer it and the old man isn't. The Keelings declare low and pay on time and have one vat and four children, and they'll take anything that feeds the children. The Pells." He moved his finger. "The Pells declare exactly, to the sheet, every quarter, and pay on the day. They've never been late and they've never been wrong. Jonas Pell makes the best paper in the south and knows it, and he won't take your offer, whatever it is."

"Why not?"

Pennock considered the question with every appearance of care.

"Because you're you, Your Highness," he said. "And he's from here."

Emeric took the Ambrys first, with the son in the room and the old man out of it. The son took the wage, and the old man, told afterwards, came to the counting house and shouted at Pennock for a quarter of an hour, and then signed. The Keelings took it in the time it took to read it to them. Their eldest girl asked whether she could learn the dipping too.

Jonas Pell listened to the whole of it standing at his vat, with his arms wet to the elbow.

He was sixty or near it, lean and bent at the shoulders from forty years over the same tub, and he let Emeric talk all the way to the end without once interrupting. Then he held out a hand for one of the trial sheets, and Emeric gave it to him, and Pell held it up to the window.

He looked at it for a long time. Then he put it down on the edge of his vat, and took a sheet of his own off the stack behind him, and laid it beside the other.

Emeric could see the difference. So, he knew, could Pell. Pell's sheet was more even. Its surface was finer, the fibres laid closer and flatter, with a thinness at the edges that Neta, after a summer, could not yet get anywhere near. It was a more beautiful sheet of paper. It was also soft, unsized, and would drink any ink put on it, and Pell knew that as well.

"Your girl shakes it too hard," Pell said. "Front to back. She'll get the edges thin if she stops fighting the water."

"I'll tell her."

"Do." He picked up his own sheet again and put it back on the stack. "No."

That was all. He did not argue, or explain, or say anything about the town or the baron or the judgment. He turned back to his vat and dipped his mould and lifted it and shook it, once side to side and once front to back, gently, not fighting the water, and turned the sheet out onto his felt, and did not look round again until Emeric had gone.

Jonas Pell made thirty more sheets that afternoon and did not remember any of them.

He had known the prince would come. Not this prince and not about this, but somebody, about something, sooner or later, because that was what happened to a town after its lord was put to death in front of it. Men came up the road with papers. The papers were always reasonable. That was the worst of them.

The sheet the boy had brought was good. He was not a fool and he would not pretend otherwise, not even here, alone in his own shop with the light going. It was sized. It was white. It would take a line and hold it. The forming was poor, a girl's first summer, too much hand in it, but the girl would learn, and they would build a hall with four vats in it and a wheel of its own, and in five years the forming would be as good as his and the sizing would be better, and in ten nobody would remember that there had been a Pell vat in Wayford at all.

He knew that. He had known it the moment he held the sheet up to the window. He had said no anyway, and he would say it again, and he found when he tried that he could not have given a reason that would have satisfied anybody but himself. It was not the money. The money had been fair. It was not even the town, although the town was in it.

It was that the prince had stood in his shop and told him, very courteously, what was going to happen to him, and had offered to let him choose which way it happened.

His father had made paper at this vat. His father's father had cut the channel that fed it. There would be buyers for Pell paper for a while yet, men who did not like where the new paper came from and would not want their accounts written on it. There were always men like that, after a judgment. He would sell to them. He would sell to anybody who did not buy from Kaldo, for as long as there were any, and when there were none he would stop.

He dipped the mould, and lifted it, and shook it, gently, and did not fight the water.

That night, in a room at the inn above the junction that was a good deal colder than it needed to be, Emeric sat at the window with the trial book open on his knee and the four families written out on a single page. Three had taken the terms. One had not.

He had expected one. He had even, if he was honest, expected it would be Pell, from the moment Pennock put a finger on the stack. He had not expected to mind as much as he did. Pell's paper was better. Not all of it and not for long, but in the one place a craftsman would look first, it was better, and the man who made it would sooner go under than stand on a floor with his name over it, and Emeric had just ridden five days to offer him exactly that.

He thought about Bram and the door. He thought about Pennock, who had read six years of declarations for nobody and had known all four answers before the question was asked, and who had been shouted at for a quarter of an hour that afternoon by a man who would never have dared shout at Sethe's clerk, and who had taken it with a face like a closed ledger. Emeric thought him an admirable clerk and a colourless man, and filed him accordingly, and turned the page.

Paper, then. Ink for the paper. The six and the clerks first, then four baronies, then whoever came after them without being told. He could see the whole of it from where he sat, the vats and the loft and the glue house, the sheets going out by the cartload on the Wayford road, the cartloads coming back as coin. And at the end of all of it, the thing that made it worth building, which was not the paper at all.

"And then a printer," he said, to the cold room and the window and the dark street full of people who did not want him, and nobody heard it, and he did not say it again.
