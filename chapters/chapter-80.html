Chapter 80 - Twin Mail

The House's answer came down the south road in the second week of January, in the House's rider's satchel, in a cover so plain that it was very nearly an argument for something.

Vantic opened it at the trestle in the back room of the exchange, read it once, and laid it on the trestle facing Ferra, which was as close as Vantic ever came to handing a thing across.

It was one line, under the House's form and the date. The cost of sending every report of the barony to the crown twice, by two roads, did not exist on the day of signing; it is a new cost; it falls within the limit.

Ferra read it. She read it again. She did not say anything, and her face did not do anything, and she put it in her book with the other rulings, in its place, by its date, and went on with the column she had been adding when the rider came in.

Emeric, at the window, had been braced for something. He did not know what. A word, at least. He watched her add the column to the bottom and draw the line under it, and then he understood that she had known what the House would say since the morning she wrote the question, and had asked it anyway, so that the answer would be in the book.

"Now we know," Ferra said, to the column.



She laid the second sheet on the trestle on the Thursday.

It was in the House's form again, with the House's line across the top and the date, and under it, in her small plain hand, the decision already made, as she had laid the aqueduct down in November. Vantic read it, and this time, before she reached for her pen, she read it again.

The covenant gives the House the right to see anything that touches its security. The barony's reports to the crown on its revenues, its works and its war touch the security. The barony has owed the House a copy of each such report since the day of signing, and has not until now sent one. It will now send one, on the day each report is sent to the crown. The carriage of a copy owed under the covenant is a running cost of the covenant, and falls outside the limit.

Under that, the clause. Under the clause, a line: The barony regrets the omission.

Vantic looked at the line about the omission for some time.

"This isn't sending every report twice," she said.

"No," said Ferra. "It's sending every report once. To the crown. And the House's copy, which the House is owed, to the House." She did not look up from the week's page. "The House ruled on the first thing. Nobody's asked it about the second."

"It's the same paper."

"It's the same words," said Ferra. "It's a different duty."

Emeric, at the window, did not breathe. Vantic sat with the sheet in front of her and her pen in her hand and looked at it as she looked at all of Ferra's sheets, as one craftsman looks at another's joint, feeling along it for the gap. He watched her find that there was not one. The covenant said what it said. A borrower who offered the lender more than the lender had demanded, in the lender's own terms, under the lender's own clause, was a borrower who could not be refused without the House refusing its own right.

Vantic initialed it at the foot. She did not hurry. She did not take under a minute this time either. She blotted it and put it in her case beside the House's line of the week before, the two of them together, as if she wanted the partners to read them in that order.

"The House will receive its copies," she said. And then, without any change in her voice at all: "It will be very grateful for the barony's diligence."

Ferra wrote the date in her book. The corner of her mouth went in, very slightly, and came back out.



The rest of it was boring, and Emeric made it boring on purpose.

He sat down at Ferra's table in Bram's kitchen that night with a sheet of the grey paper and wrote at the top of it what Keller had said in a cold room and then struck out of the record, and under each of the four words, what Kaldo would do about it. He had worked it out on the road home, seven days of it, in the saddle and in the yard of every posting house, getting up out of the straw. It was not clever. Clever was what he did not want. Clever could be argued with.

Dated, against something outside the barony's own house.

Every report to the crown would go out of Kaldo the same way it always had, from Hale's hand to the post house on the north road, in the bag, on the day. But this time it would not go into the bag until the post house's gate clerk had weighed it, and looked at the seal, and written on a slip the weight and the seal and the day and the hour, and signed it. A receipt for a letter, from a man who belonged to the crown's post and not to Kaldo, and had never heard of Keller, and would give a receipt to anyone who paid him a copper for it.

"He'll want two coppers," said Ferra.

"Then he gets two."

Paired.

The same day, the same words. Hale would write each report out twice, as he had written the war three times, and Ferra would read the second against the first, word for word, line for line, and initial the foot of the second to say she had. The first went north by the post. The second went to the House's coast house as the borrower's copy, the copy the House was owed. Not by the post. Never by the post.

Attested, by people who did not know what they were attesting to.

That was the House. The House's clerks at the coast house would receive the borrower's copy, and write in their own book that they had received it, and on what day, and from whose hand, and send a line back by the carrier to say so, because that was what the House's clerks did with everything, and had done for a hundred years, for borrowers who were not trying to prove anything to anyone. They would not know there was a case. They would not know there was a hole at Wayford. They would only know that Kaldo was very diligent, and they would write it down.

A year.

He wrote that one down and did not write anything under it.



Bram cut the die in the second week.

The barony's seal was the old baron's seal with the old baron's device on it, a tower on a hill, cut into a brass matrix so worn that the tower had gone soft at the corners and the hill had nearly gone. Bram took one look at it and said he would not put that on a letter to a horse trader, and cut a new one from the start, on the anvil, at night, with the punches he used for the hinges and three he made for the purpose, and it was the same device, a tower on a hill, crisp as a new coin.

Except at the shoulder of the tower, on the left, where the wall met the roof. There Bram put a nick.

It was very small. It was a cut no wider than a hair, made with the corner of a chisel, at an angle a man would have to look for, and it showed in the wax as a tiny flaw in the line of the roof, like a crack in a real tower. Anyone who saw the seal would see a tower on a hill. Anyone who knew to look would see the crack.

"A forger copies what he sees," Emeric said, with the new die in his palm under the lamp. "He takes an impression off a good seal and casts a copy from it. If he's careful, he gets the crack too."

"Then he's careful," Bram said. "Most aren't."

"Most aren't. And if he is, he's got the crack from somewhere. He's had a real seal in his hands to cast from. Which means he's had one of our letters in his hands, open, long enough to take a cast." Emeric turned the die over. "Either way it tells us something."

Bram looked at the die, and at Emeric, and then at the forge, where nothing was happening.

"You're making it a trap," he said.

"I'm making it a seal."

"You're making it a seal that's a trap." Bram took the die back, as if it might have been insulted. "I don't mind. I'd like to know it, that's all. A man likes to know what he's made."



Ferra did the fold.

He had meant to do it himself. He had half remembered something from the other life, a way of folding a letter on itself and cutting a slit through all the layers and passing a strip of the same paper through the slit and sealing the strip down, so that the letter could not be opened without tearing the strip, and the strip could not be put back. He had half remembered it, and drawn it badly, and showed it to Ferra across the table.

She looked at the drawing for a while. Then she pushed it aside and took a clean sheet and folded it, three times, in a way he had not drawn, and turned the last fold back under itself, and cut the slit with Bram's smallest knife, and passed the strip through, and laid the seal across the strip and the fold together, so that the wax held both.

"Open it," she said.

He tried. There was no way in that did not tear the strip, or crack the wax across the fold, or both. He tried the way a man would try who had a whole night and a candle and a thin knife and wanted to read it and put it back, and the paper told on him every time, in a crease or a tear or a lift of the wax.

"Where did you learn that?"

"My mother," said Ferra. "For the tithe returns." She took the ruined sheet back from him and looked at what he had done to it, as she looked at a column that had been added wrong. "You fold the wrong way first. Everyone does."

No magic. Not a rune, not a stone, not a trace of mana on any of it, from the gate clerk's two coppers to the strip through the fold. He had made sure of that the way he would have made sure of a part he meant to put in front of an inspector. A rune could be read by a mage, and argued by another mage, and a court full of men who could not tell one from another would believe whichever mage had the better coat. A gate clerk's slip with a date on it, and a nick in a tower, and a strip of paper through a slit, could be held up in a room and looked at by anyone with eyes.



The first pair was ready in the last week of January: the year's last quarter account, which had to go to the crown anyway, written out twice in Hale's hand and checked against itself in Ferra's, folded her way, sealed with Bram's tower.

Ferra put the two of them side by side on the trestle and looked at them.

"Every one of these is bait now," she said.

He had known she would see it. He had not known she would say it.

"Every report we send north," Ferra said, "goes into that room at Wayford, and we want it to. We want it pulled. We want it to vanish, with a receipt for it in our hand and its twin in the House's book. And every one that vanishes is a report the crown doesn't get. The account of the quarter. The census. The aqueduct. Whatever we need the crown to know, this year, it doesn't know, because we're using it to catch a man." She did not say it as a complaint. She said it the way she said a cost. "That's what it's paid for with. The crown hearing nothing. For a year."

"A year," Emeric said.

"Then it's a year." She picked up the first of the pair, the crown's, and turned it over to check the seal, and put it down again with the crack in the tower facing up. "I'll keep the book."



Vantic asked him on the stair.

He had gone up to the east tower with the House's copy of the quarter account himself, because it was the first, and it seemed wrong to send the first up with a boy. She took it at the door. She looked at the fold, and the strip, and the tower in the wax, for long enough that he knew she had seen the crack, and then she looked at him.

"What is the barony buying with the second copy?" she said.

Nine words. He counted them afterward, on the stair, going down, because he could not help counting things and because it was the shortest question anyone had asked him that winter, and the most expensive.

He could have lied. He thought about it for the length of the pause, honestly, the way he would have thought about any tool. A diligent borrower. A careful clerk. A new seal because the old one was worn. All true, and none of it the answer, and she would know that as soon as he said it, and write it down, and after that every sheet Ferra laid on the trestle would be read as a sheet that might be lying, and the most useful arrangement he had in Kaldo would be over.

So he told her.

All of it. Standing in the doorway at the top of the stair with the cold coming up behind him. The register and the twenty-one. The house by the ferry approach at Wayford with the smoking stove. Saban's map and Keller's corridor and Keller's room. Dated, paired, attested, a year. That the House's copies were the twins, and that the House's clerks writing them down was the attestation, and that the House was the disinterested party because the House did not know what it was attesting to, and now she did.

She did not write while he talked.

That was the thing he noticed, and went on noticing all the way down the stair. Every time he had ever spoken to her, she had had a pen in her hand, and she had written, the date and the hour and four words, while he was still speaking. This time she put the pen down on the table behind her, without looking at it, and stood in her own doorway with her hands folded in front of her, and listened to the whole of it, and did not write a thing.

When he had finished, she was quiet for a little.

"You understand what you've told me," she said.

"Yes."

"That the House is being used."

"Yes."

"That the barony is spending money on carriers and copies and gate clerks, every month, for a year, to buy something that may never be worth anything at all."

"Yes."

She looked at him a while longer. Then she turned, and picked up her pen, and drew her book toward her, and wrote. He could read it upside down from the doorway. He always could.

The date. The hour. A cost, carried against no asset.

It was the worst category in her book. He knew that, because she had told him so once, in September, when he had asked her what the House hated most. Not a loss. A loss was a figure. A cost against no asset was money going out of a door into a dark room, with no way of knowing whether anything would ever come back out.

She blotted it, and closed the book, and put the pen down on top of it.

"Good night, your highness," she said.

"You're not going to stop it."

"It isn't mine to stop." She looked at the House's copy of the quarter account, on her table, with its fold and its strip and its cracked tower. "The barony's diligence has been noted."

He went down. On the last turn of the stair, the narrow one, going sideways, he heard her door close, very quietly, and not all the way.
