Chapter 168 - Green While It Snows

In the first week of December the room began to give him time.

It had always given him time. That was the first thing he had known about it, the first summer, on the floor of his chamber with his mother's stone in his hand: that a furnace rebuilt three times in the room took fifty-four seconds outside it, and that the clock on the wall did not care what had happened in between. He had used it for drawings and books and stones for six years. He had never once used it for the thing it was best at, because he had not known until the autumn that it was the thing.

Ward had said it in the yard. You come back late. Half a breath, every time you go out. Somebody will find it. And Ward was right, and it was the hybrid's oldest trouble, older than Kaldo: a man with the faculty open and his body open too, who sent his mana out as a spell and then had to bring it back in to put it in his legs, or the other way round, and in the switch, every time, there was a half breath when it was in neither. Armand had told him once, in the long room, that every hybrid in the tower's books had died of that half breath in the end, one way or another. That the ones the books called great were only the ones who had made it shorter.

So he made it shorter in the room.

Every night, from the first of December, after the house was abed, on the floor of his chamber with the stone in his hand. Not drawings. Not books. In the room he stood on the floor of it, which was nothing, a sourceless grey light and the soft-edged walls and the shelves, and he cast. A flame off his palm, small, the size of a candle's, and as it closed, in the same breath, the push down into his legs, as if to jump; and then out again, the flame; and in again, the push. Out, in. Out, in. The way a man practises a turn with a blade a thousand times until his feet do it without him.

And the room ran slow. That was the gift of it. A night in the room was an hour outside, or less; and in the room, in the long slow grey time, he could feel the switch. Feel it happen, every time, the way he could not in the yard where it was over before he knew it had begun: the mana going out of his arms into the flame, and the arms going empty, and the long cold moment of nothing, and then the push coming back into the legs from wherever it had been. He could feel the nothing. He could feel how long it was.

The first night it was very long. As long, in the room's time, as it took to say his own name.

By the tenth night it was as long as it took to say Ward.



Ward came at him in the yard on the twelfth and Emeric did not walk into anything, and did not lose to himself, and on the fourth exchange he did a thing he had never done.

He did it without deciding to, which was the point. Ward came in low, a long cut at the knee, and Emeric took his breath of the slow time on the lean of it, the way he had learned in August; and in the breath he was already in the air. Not a dodge. A jump, straight up, Rylan's way, the push into his legs; and at the top of it, over Ward's blade as it went under him, where any knight would have turned and come down where the jump put him, he did not turn. He stopped. The Stop, a breath, hanging; and then the other way. Not his weight back. More than his weight. Heavy, all at once, from the top of the jump, so that he did not come down. He dropped. Faster than a fall, as a stone dropped from a tower is not as fast as a stone thrown down from it, onto the place on the cobbles behind Ward's left shoulder, with the blade coming down with him.

He landed like a hammer on an anvil. The cobbles under his boots cracked, three of them, with a sound like a plate dropped on a flagged floor; and Ward's wave went through his body top to bottom the way it had on the Milford bench, everywhere at once, and his knees took it, and he was standing, with the flat of his practice blade laid against the back of Ward's neck.

Ward did not turn round.

He stood with his own blade still out in front of him at the height of the knee he had cut at, and Emeric's on his neck, and was quite still. Then he lowered his blade.

"Don't," said Ward, "do that on a man."

"Why not?"

"Because I felt the stones go under you from here," said Ward, "and I'm a knight, with a knight's bones. A man without them, you'd have put through the cobbles." He turned round. He looked at the three cracked stones and then at Emeric's face. "A hammer," he said. "From the top. That's what that was."

"Yes."

"Keep it for things that aren't men." He went back to his mark. "Again. And the switch was shorter. I saw it." He did not say anything else about it, then or afterward, but he came at Emeric four more times that night instead of two, and on the last of them Emeric felt him smiling inside the even wall of his note, very carefully, the way Ward smiled at nothing at all.



The glasshouse was finished the week before the snow.

It stood on the warm side of the works, against the long south wall of the ice works where the copper fins of the cold stones came out through the brick: Ivo's glass, every pane of it, the best sheet glass the glassworks had ever pulled, in a frame of iron that Bram's men had made and painted white, sixty feet long and twenty high, with a pitched glass roof and a stove at each end that it did not need. It did not need them because of the wall. The cold stones inside the ice works threw their heat out through the copper fins into whatever was on the other side of the wall, and all autumn that had been the lane, where the fins stood too hot to touch and the lane's puddles never froze. Now it was the glasshouse. The heat that the ice works pulled out of the trays of water to make its ice went through the wall and into the glasshouse's back, day and night, and the glasshouse was warm.

That had been Tess's idea, in October. She had put her hand on the fins in the lane and taken it away and said that it was a waste. That a room in Kaldo was being made cold by throwing its warm into a lane, and another room in Kaldo was going to be made warm by burning coal; and if you put the second room on the other side of the first room's wall, you did not have to burn the coal. Emeric had been about to say the same thing. He had shut his mouth.

Hollis had planted it. Not because he was asked; because he heard there was going to be a glass house that was warm in the winter, and turned up at the door the day the last pane went in with a barrow of seed trays and a look that dared anybody to tell him what to put in them. Salad greens in long beds down the middle. Beans up strings on the back wall. Herbs, Maren's, in boxes. A row of Imani's: the plants for the fevers and the gripes and the coughs that she had always had to send to the coast for, dried, at a price. And at the end, in a tub, a small dark-leafed tree the coast boat had brought up in September as a curiosity, in a pot of soil from somewhere south, with three hard green fruits on it the size of a thumb, which Hollis said would never ripen in Kaldo in a hundred years, and watered every morning.

It snowed on the eighteenth of December. It snowed in the night and all the next day, thick and soft and windless, the way it snowed in Kaldo perhaps one winter in three; and by the evening of the nineteenth the town was white to the eaves, and the river was black between white banks, and the glasshouse at the works stood in the snow like a lantern, green inside, every pane of it steamed at the edges and clear in the middle, and the snow on its roof sliding off in slow soft slabs as fast as it fell, because the roof was warm.

He asked them both to meet him there at eight.



He had not decided how to begin, and so he began badly.

They came together. He had not known they would. He had sent the same note to each, in his own hand, the same words, and they had come down the hill from the keep through the snow together, in their cloaks, with their hoods up and snow on their shoulders, Ferra with her book under her arm because she did not go anywhere without it and Imani with her hands in her sleeves; and they stopped in the glasshouse door, side by side, and looked at it. At the green. At the beans on their strings and the long beds of salad and the herbs in their boxes and the little dark tree in its tub at the end, all of it warm and wet and growing and smelling of earth, with the snow coming down past the glass on every side of it in the lamplight.

"Oh," said Ferra.

Imani said nothing. She took her hands out of her sleeves and walked down the long bed to her own row, her fevers and gripes, and put her fingers on a leaf, and smelled them.

He had put a bench at the far end, by the little tree, and a lamp. He sat them on it. He did not sit. He stood in front of them in the green warm air with the snow coming down past the glass behind him and found he had nothing ready at all, not one word, after a month of trying.

"You made terms," he said at last. "In the spring. In Ferra's room. Four of them." He looked at his hands. "The second was that there would be no secret one of you had that the other didn't."

"We remember," said Ferra.

"So I'm telling you both. Now. Together. Because there's something I haven't told either of you, and if I told one of you first, I'd have broken it." He stopped. "I've broken it already, by not telling either of you for a year and a half. I know that. I'd rather break it that way than the other."

Imani had sat down on the bench beside Ferra. She looked up at him with her hands in her lap.

"Then tell it," she said.

He told it.



He told it the way he would have told a column, because it was the only way he knew how to tell anything that mattered: from the beginning, in order, and leaving nothing out because it was hard.

That there had been another world. Not a story, not a dream, not a country across the sea; a world, with a sky like this one and a sun like this one and people like these, and no magic in it at all, not a stone, not a grain, not a breath of it, so that the people in it had had to do everything with their hands and their heads and their machines, for longer than anybody here had been writing anything down; and that they had got very far. Further than he could easily tell. That he had lived in it. Not as Emeric. As a man called Marcus, who had been born in it and grown up in it and gone to its schools and learned its trade, which was making machines, and been good at it, and been nobody in particular.

That Marcus had been paid, in his last year, to let a company of physicians put things into his head that he had never learned. Whole trades. Medicine; Imani looked up. Metals, and the chemistry of the dyehouse and the acid works, and electricity, and the writing of instructions for machines. Whole, as if he had studied them for twenty years; and that he had kept them, and brought them here. That every book Marcus had ever read, he could still read, a page at a time, in the room. That this was where the soap had come from, and the wells, and the furnace and the wire and the lamps and the rails and the engine. Not from a lost book. From a man who had died.

Because Marcus had died. He told them that too. On a street, in the other world, in the summer, getting a small girl in a yellow coat out of the way of a cart that moved without a horse, faster than any cart here; and he had got her clear and not himself. And then he had woken in a cold corridor in the capital with a king's sentence in his ears, in a body he had been living in for twenty-two years and had never once seen from the inside, with two lives in his head and no way to tell anybody about either of them.

And the other thing. That he was not only a mage. That the body he had woken in had been born with both doors open, the mage's and the knight's, which was supposed to be a thing that did not happen, or happened once in a generation and killed the child or was killed out of it; and that it had not killed him, and he did not know why, except that his mother had been a tower mage and his father was what Ward had once called a knight, and that Ward had stopped short of saying the rest. That the warmth on the downs had been that. The four strides at Grayrock. The knee off the wall walk.

He told it until he had come to the end of it. The snow came down past the glass. The lamp on the bench burned down a finger's width.

When he had finished he stood in front of them and did not know what to do with his hands, and put them in his coat, and took them out again.



Ferra asked her questions first. In order.

She did not open her book. He saw that before anything else, and it frightened him more than anything she could have said; she sat on the bench with the book shut on her knees and her two hands flat on its cover, and did not open it, and did not take out her pen, and asked.

How long had the other world been writing things down? Longer than here, much. How many people were in it? More than he could make her believe. Were they all like him? No. Most of them did not make machines; most of them did what people here did, the farming and the selling and the raising of children. What did he owe them, the physicians who had put the trades into his head? He did not know; he had found the papers frightening, and had been keeping a file on them, and had died before he finished it. Did anybody else here know? Nobody. Not Ward. Not Armand. Not anybody, until tonight, in this glasshouse. Had he told anybody else, ever, in either world, everything? No.

"Was it you," said Ferra, "who looked at me in my father's yard, the first time? With the soap?"

He thought about it honestly.

"Both of us," he said. "I think. I don't know where one stops."

Ferra looked down at the book on her knees. She did not open it. She sat with her hands flat on it, and he understood, watching her, that she was not going to write any of it down, tonight or ever, in any book, in any cipher; that it was the first thing in her life she had ever decided would never be in a column; and that she had decided it while he was talking.

Imani had not asked anything. She sat beside Ferra with her hands in her lap and her eyes on his face, the whole time, the way she looked at a man telling her where it hurt.

"What did it feel like," she said. "Dying."

He opened his mouth.

Imani stood up. She came across the space between the bench and where he stood, under the little dark tree with its three green fruits, and took his right wrist in her fingers. Two fingers on the inside under the bone, her thumb on the back, the way she had taken it on the slip in the summer and at the foot of the keep stair on Lantern Night. She did not look at him. She looked at nothing, the way she looked when she was counting.

"Go on," said Imani.

So he told her. With her fingers on his pulse. That it had not hurt. That there had been a noise he did not hear so much as arrive after. That he had seemed to stand a little way above the street and look down at himself, and that it had felt like minutes and had been, he thought, four or five breaths, and that he had been curious about the difference, which had seemed to him even at the time like a ridiculous thing to be curious about. That he had found the woman whose child it was very sad, and the man in the cart very sad, and himself only interested. That he had thought about who would find out, and in what order, and had been sorry for them. And then nothing, for a long time or no time, and then the corridor, and the cold, and the king's voice.

Imani counted. He felt her count. She did not lose it.

"Ninety," she said, when he stopped. She did not let go of his wrist. "You've told it all as if it were a column, and your heart's going at ninety." She looked at him then. "That's the most honest thing you've said tonight."



Then Imani said a thing he had not thought of.

"The lantern," she said. "Both years. The third name. On the inside. Facing the flame." She was still holding his wrist. "That was him."

He had not known she had seen it. He had written it small, by the hoop, where it would face the fire and not the town, and she had said only same one, last spring, on the wall, and not asked.

"Yes," he said.

"What was it?"

"Marcus."

Imani let go of his wrist.

And Ferra, on the bench, put her book down beside her, on the bench, carefully, the way she put it down only when she meant to leave it, and stood up, and came to him; and Imani did not step back to make room for her, and Ferra did not ask her to. They came from either side. He did not know afterward which of them moved first and he did not think either of them did; they put their arms round him at the same time, from either side, under the little dark tree, in the warm green air with the snow coming down past the glass on every side and the lamp burning down on the bench, and held him.

They held him. Longer than anybody had held him in either of his lives. He stood between them with his arms at his sides and then not at his sides, and the snow came down past the glass, and nobody said anything at all.

"You were someone else," said Ferra at last, into his shoulder.

"I still am," he said. "A bit."

Imani laughed. Not much; a breath, against his other shoulder, the laugh she had laughed on the Grayrock wagon going down the hill.

"So are we all," she said. "A bit. After the plague." She did not let go. "Two of you, then. That's still even."

Ferra made a sound into his coat that might have been a laugh too.

They did not let go until the lamp on the bench began to gutter. And when they did, Ferra picked her book up off the bench and put it under her arm without opening it, and Imani put her hands back in her sleeves, and the three of them went out of the green into the white and up the hill through the snow to the keep, not talking, Ferra on one side and Imani on the other, with the glasshouse behind them on the warm side of the works shining like a lantern that nobody had let go.
