Chapter 168 - The Deep Ground

The seam turned down at the far end of the fourth gallery, where it had always turned down, and where the old men had stopped following it when Hask's grandfather was young.

Bryda took him to it with a lamp on the second morning, with two of her crew behind carrying the box between them on poles, and neither of the two looked at the box once all the way down. The gallery ran level for half a mile under the hill and then pinched, and the roof came down, and the grey band in the rock that was the whole reason Grayrock existed dipped away from the floor at an angle like a stair going into the dark, and the rock around it went from the soft brown stuff they cut with picks to something blue and close-grained and ringing that turned a pick's point in a morning.

"Here," Bryda said. She put her hand flat on the blue rock. "Hask's father drove a heading into this for a year and made eleven yards. You can see the marks. Then he stopped."

Emeric could see the marks. Eleven yards of them, a hundred years of them, a heading the width of a man's shoulders going into the hard rock beside the seam, every inch of it chipped by hand, and at the end of it a face of blue stone scored with the last season's pick-work and nothing beyond it.

He had stood here before. It had been in his first years, when he had come up the river on a borrowed barge to look at the only thing his barony had that anybody wanted, and Hask had brought him down this gallery and showed him this face and said the seam went on under it and down, richer than anything above, and they could not reach it. And somebody on the barge had said, a crystal charge would do it. A fire crystal set in a hole and overloaded. The high mages' way, for rock.

He had said no. He had said it before he knew enough to say why, and then he had learned why, and been glad. A fire crystal per shot, and a fire crystal was not cheap. A crystal let go on purpose in a shaft, in the dark, with the gallery full of men and air, and whatever it did when it let go done in a space the width of a man's shoulders. And after it, at the face, for a week or longer, a dead patch in the rock where no stone would work: the lamps out, the pump stones on the sump dead, the hoist at the shaft head gone slack, and men carrying water up ladders in buckets while they waited for the medium to come back. The old men who had tried it, somewhere in the north, had lost more than they had gained, and he had known that, and said no.

"Powder doesn't do any of that," he said.

"You said." Bryda looked at the box. "It's dirt, you said."

"It's dirt."

"And it breaks this?"

"We'll see," he said.

Her two men drilled the holes. They had been drilling for a week, since his letter came, with long steels and double-jacks, one man holding and turning the steel and the other swinging, in a rhythm Emeric could hear through the gallery floor from the shaft foot, and they had put four holes into the blue rock at the face, each an arm deep, angled in toward each other the way he had drawn it. It had been the hardest week's work they had ever done and they had not asked what the holes were for.

He charged them himself. He had thought about letting Bryda's men do it and decided that for the first time it would be his hands, and his hands only, and then not again. He put the paper cartridges in, one after another, pushed home with a wooden rod, never iron, and the fuse after them, and packed the mouth of each hole with clay. The fuse was a tube of paper rolled round a line of powder mixed wet with gum and dried on a string, and he had timed it on the lower field in Kaldo forty times, a yard of it at a time, with Hobb counting and writing down what he counted. A yard burned in a count of ninety, give or take four. He had cut four yards for each hole.

"Everyone out," he said.

"They're out," Bryda said. "Everybody but us. I counted them up the ladder myself and Ilsa counted them again at the top."

"Then you."

She looked at him. "And you?"

"I light it and I walk. Six minutes. I've walked this gallery in two."

She did not argue. She took the lamp that was not his and went back along the gallery, and he heard her boots on the rungs of the ladder at the gallery's end, and then nothing.

He stood at the face alone with the lamp.

He was aware, standing there, of the rock. Of the sense of it. The whole hill was a stone the size of a hill, and the grey seam ran through it like a vein, and he could feel the seam from here, dense and heavy and silent, the same way he could feel the racks in hull two's keel from the slip, going down away from him into the dark. It went down a long way. Further than he had thought, the first time. Further than Hask's father could have gone in a hundred years of chipping.

He lit the four fuses from the lamp, one after the other, and watched each one catch and spit and start to crawl, and then he picked up the lamp and walked.

He did not run. He had told himself he would not run. He walked the half mile back along the gallery at the pace of a man walking to his breakfast, and counted, and climbed the ladder at the end, and went along the upper level to the shaft foot, where Bryda was standing with her arms folded in the light from above, and stood beside her. He had counted two hundred and eighty.

They waited.

It was not loud, when it came. He had expected it to be loud. It was a thump, far off and down, felt in the soles of the feet more than heard, and then a long soft rushing roll through the rock that went on for longer than the thump had, and then a breath of warm air that came up out of the gallery and past them to the shaft and smelled of rotten eggs and burned paper and broken stone.

Then nothing.

Bryda stood with her arms folded.

"That's it?" she said.

"That's it."

"I thought," she said, and stopped. "I don't know what I thought."

"We wait an hour," he said. "For the air."

They went in after two, because he would not go in after one, and Bryda went in first because she said it was her mine and she was damned if she would walk behind a prince into it.

The gallery was full of dust still, fine and grey, hanging in the lamp's light, and the smell was worse near the face and made their eyes run. The heading was not where it had been. Where the face had been there was a heap of broken blue stone across the floor, sharp-edged, some of it in pieces the size of a fist and some the size of a chest, and beyond the heap the heading went on. Two yards. Nearly three. In one night of drilling and a count of two hundred and eighty, three yards of rock that had cost Hask's father a season a yard.

Bryda climbed onto the heap with the lamp held up, and stood there, and did not say anything for a long time.

Then she said, "Come here."

He climbed up beside her.

The blast had split the blue rock along a line he had not known was there, a seam of its own, a crack running up the new face at a slant, and in the crack, where the lamp's light went into it, something glittered. Not the grey of the stone seam, which was dull, and drank light. This was bright. Heavy, square-faced, silvery grey crystals packed into the crack like teeth, catching the lamp in a hundred small flat faces, and more of it below them, going down.

Emeric knew it before he touched it. He touched it anyway. It was cold and heavy and it came away in his hand in a little cube with square corners, and he weighed it in his palm, and it was heavier than it had any right to be.

"That's your stuff," Bryda said. "The lump you had in your pocket. The stuff I've been keeping."

"Yes."

"There's more of it here than I've kept in two years."

"There's more of it here," Emeric said, "than I've ever seen."

She took the cube out of his hand and held it to the lamp and turned it, the flat faces flashing, and he watched her look at it. He had watched her look at the same stuff in the spoil carts for two years, lump by lump, as she put it aside under his orders in a bin by the tally house, and she had always looked at it the way she looked at anything that was his and not the mine's, as a bother she was paid to keep. She was not looking at it that way now. She was looking at it the way she looked at the grey seam. The way her crews had looked at the grey seam for a hundred years, since before the covenant, when a pound of it was worth a year's wage in the capital and a man could see the year in it.

"Lead," Emeric said. "It's lead ore. There's silver in it, some. Not a great deal."

"Lead's cheap."

"Not in Kaldo," he said. "Not for what I want it for."

"And what's that?"

He thought about Nevin's type, and the lead chamber, and the third thing, and said, "More than I can use," and Bryda turned and looked at him in the lamp's light with the cube in her hand.

"What does it cost?" she said. "The dirt. To break this."

He told her. He had worked it out on the boat. The saltpeter off the beds at what the handcart and the old men and Colm's two hands cost Ferra, and the sulphur off Rowena's boats at what Ferra paid for it by the ton, and the charcoal at nothing because the foundry made it anyway, and the paper, and the fuse. Four holes. It came to less than the wages of the two men who had drilled them. It came to less than one week of Hask's father's heading.

She stood on the heap with the lamp and the cube and did the sum herself, the way Ferra would have done it, and he watched her get to the end of it.

"Can it be sold?" she said.

"No."

"Not ever?"

"Not yet."

She looked at the cube for a while longer, and then put it in her pocket, and got down off the heap, and held the lamp up for him to get down.

"I'll want more holes, then," she said. "Before you go."

Oswin Fenner came up the river on the fourth day, on the steamboat, with two clerks and a weigher and a black book, and asked for nothing but water for the horses and the loan of a table, as he had said he would.

He was older. Emeric had seen him twice before, once at the Western Gate on his first visit and once, three years ago, at the pithead, standing narrow and grey in the chancery coat watching Hask draw the sealed shaft in the dust with a stick. He was narrower now. His coat was the same coat or its twin. He had the same way of standing in a room as if he were measuring it and had nearly finished.

He greeted Emeric correctly, and Ferra correctly, and Bryda by name, which surprised her, and Ilsa, whom he had never met, by name too, which surprised her more. He did not say how he knew it.

The spring raising was two days. The stone came up the shaft in the cages on the first day, the whole winter's cutting of it, in the canvas sacks the pithead women sewed, and was carried to the weigh house and emptied on the floor under the lamp in grey heaps, and Ilsa weighed it on the great beam, a sack at a time, and called the weight, and Hobb wrote it, and Fenner's weigher stood at Ilsa's shoulder and watched the beam and wrote it too. On the second day the sold portion went into the Crown's chest and Fenner's weigher weighed it again, alone, and Fenner watched him, and the chest was locked with Fenner's key and Ferra's, and sealed with Fenner's seal and Kaldo's.

Fenner did not speak much on either day. He watched. He watched Ilsa at the beam, and the pithead women, and the cages, and Bryda at the shaft head calling the lifts, and he watched the spoil carts going out to the heaps, and once, on the first afternoon, Emeric saw him stand for a long time at the mouth of the upper level looking at the fresh blue spoil on the top of the heap, sharp-edged, a colour none of the other spoil was. He did not ask about it. He wrote something in the black book, and went back to the weigh house.

On the evening of the second day he asked for the table.

They gave him the tally-house table, which was the biggest in Grayrock, and he sat at one end of it with his book open and his clerks at his shoulders, and Ferra sat at the other end with hers, and Emeric sat at the side with Bryda, and Ilsa and Hobb stood by the door. The lamp was lit. There was a jug of water on the table and nobody touched it.

Fenner turned the pages of his book to one he had marked with a strip of ribbon, and read it for a moment, and then he began to speak, not loudly, in the tone of a man reading out an inventory.

"Stone raised at Grayrock," he said, "since my last attendance, three years and a season ago, by the barony's own weigh-sheets as returned to the chancery, and by my own weigher's figure for this raising, which agrees with Miss Renner's to within a pound." He gave the figure. It was a large figure. Emeric had seen it in Ferra's book in her hand and it was the same figure.

"Stone sold out of the barony in the same period, under the covenant, by the Crown's own records of the first purchase taken and the Crown's share paid, which have never once been short and which I commend to my lady for their exactness." He gave that figure too. It was also the same as Ferra's.

He paused.

"The difference," he said, and gave it.

It was the number in Ferra's margin. Emeric had not seen her margin. He did not need to have seen it. He watched her face and saw that it was the same number to the pound, and that she had known it would be, and that knowing it had not made it any easier to hear said aloud across a table in the chancery's voice.

"Stone raised and not sold," Fenner said. "In the barony. I have no figure for where."

Nobody said anything.

Fenner turned a page. "I have read the covenant," he said. "I read it again on the boat. I should be glad of my lady's reading of it, for my return."

Ferra put her hand flat on her book.

"The covenant," she said, "gives the Crown the right of first purchase on stone of Grayrock sold out of the barony, at the declared price, and a share of the price on every pound so sold. It binds stone that is sold. It says nothing of stone that is not." She did not look at her book. She had it by heart. "Stone kept in the barony for the barony's own use is not sold. It is not offered. It does not leave. No first purchase arises on it, because no sale arises. It is entered in the barony's book by the pound as raised, as kept, and as used, and my lord's assessor is welcome to the book."

"The barony's own use," Fenner said.

"Yes."

He did not ask what the use was.

Emeric watched him not ask. It was the same thing he had watched Enno do at the board in the yard, and the thing Ferra said Vantic had done at the long-room table in the winter, and he was beginning to know the shape of it. Fenner sat with his pen above the page and his narrow grey face perfectly still and did not ask the one question that anybody at the table would have asked, and he did not ask it because he did not need the answer to write his return, and because a question asked and answered would be in the barony's book as well as his, and a question not asked would be in nobody's.

"That is the covenant," Fenner said. "My lady's reading is correct. I should have read it the same." He wrote a line. "No first purchase arises on stone not sold. I shall so return."

"Thank you," Ferra said.

He wrote another line under the first. Emeric could not read it from where he sat, and did not try to. He did not need to. He had watched Fenner's pen, and it had written a number. The same number. The difference. In a black book, in a chancery hand, under a line that said the barony had done nothing wrong.

Then Fenner blotted the page, and closed the book, and put his hand on the cover of it, and looked across the table at Ferra, for the first time that evening, directly.

"I shall be at the autumn raising too," he said. "If my lady will have me."

"My lord's assessor is always welcome," Ferra said.

He went down the river in the morning on the steamboat with his clerks and his weigher and the Crown's chest, and Emeric and Ferra stood on the Grayrock quay and watched the boat go round the bend under the hill.

Fenner's returns went to the chancery. They always had. They went to the chancery's desk for the covenants, and from that desk up to the man who had chosen Oswin Fenner out of all the chancery's clerks to be Kaldo's assessor, when the covenant was sealed and Kaldo was a ruin by a lake with a stone mine on a hill that nobody else wanted. Emeric knew whose desk that was. He had known for a long time.

He did not say it. Ferra did not ask him.

"He knew my name," Ilsa said, behind them. "He'd never met me."

"He'd read your weigh-sheets," Ferra said. "Your name's at the bottom of every one of them since last spring."

"He read them?"

"He reads everything," Ferra said. "That's what he's for."

Bryda had four more holes drilled by the time the boat had gone, and Emeric charged them, and walked the gallery, and waited, and went back in with her after two hours, and the heading went on another three yards into the blue rock, with the lead in its crack going down beside it the whole way, bright in the lamplight.

He charged the next four holes with Bryda's two drillers watching him do it, and told them every step as he did it, and made them tell it back to him. The ones after that they charged, and he watched.

Ferra went back to Kaldo on the second boat with Hobb and Ilsa and the weigh-sheets. Emeric saw her aboard at dusk. She had her book under her arm, and she stood at the rail as the boat pulled out, and she did not open the book. She did not need to. She had the number in it in pencil in a margin at home, and she had it now in a narrow grey man's black book in a chancery hand, going down the river two days ahead of her, and she knew exactly what it was, to the pound.
