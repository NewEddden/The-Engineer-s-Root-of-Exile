Chapter 370 - The Sharing

The word session had moved to the bay house kitchen when she did, and it had brought its habits with it. Armand had the chair by the range, because there was a range. The table had Ferra's word book on one end of it, open, weighted with the salt cellar. The pool door stood open to let the tide in, so to speak, and on the twenty-seventh of October at a little after four in the afternoon it was letting in a good deal of cold, and nobody had shut it, because Ilenne liked it open and it was her kitchen.

It was a Monday. Monday was Emeric's.

He had come with a question. He'd had it since Thursday, when Ferra had come back from her own session with three new words in the book and had read them out at supper the way she read out the day's takings. A word for husband and wife, which was one word with a colour that told you which; a word for the binding, the marriage at a door, before witnesses, in the water; and a third word that Ferra had spelled with a great many of her small marks, because it had given her trouble. She'd asked Ilenne what that one meant, and Ilenne had thought about it, and said, in her careful Kaldo, "Once. Until one dies."

"Until one dies?" Ferra had said. "Not until they choose? Not until one of them asks?"

And Ilenne had looked at her with the expression she kept for questions that had no sense in them, and said, "No. Once."

Ferra had written it down. She'd also written, in the narrow column, ask E. to ask why, and underlined it, and so here he was.

"Forty minutes," said Armand, and opened the watch.

Emeric put his hands out on the table, palms up, and Ilenne put hers in them, and he went in.



It was easy now. It had been easy since the summer, easier than with anyone he'd ever delved, Ferra and Imani included. The door between them opened almost before he'd touched it, the way a door does when somebody on the other side has heard you on the step and is already reaching for the handle. He'd noticed it in July and wondered about it and put it away. It had got easier every week since.

Inside, she was what she always was: a long cool place, very deep, with light coming down through it in slow bars, the way it came down through the water in the bay. Her words were near the top, where she'd been keeping them for him, laid out like things on a market stall. He didn't want the words today.

He asked. Not in words; you didn't, in here, not after the first few weeks. He held up what Ferra had brought home on Thursday: once, and until one dies, and Ferra's face asking why not until they choose. And under it, his own: why does it hold? What holds it?

She was quiet a while, in the way she was quiet in here, which was like the water going still.

Then she showed him a stone.



It was in a woman's hands. The hands were long and grey-white and older than Ilenne's, with the same shape to them, the same long fingers, and they held the stone between them in front of a breast that rose and fell slowly. It was a control stone. He knew it at once, the way you know a face in a crowd: matte grey, about the size of a hen's egg, with lines in it, and very old, so old that its surface had gone smooth as river glass from being held. There was light in it. Not much. A faint slow glow, going in and out with the woman's breathing, as if it were breathing too.

The memory was a child's. He could tell by the angle; he was looking up at the stone from below, from somewhere low down and close, a lap or a bed, and the hands were enormous. He was very small and very sleepy and the woman holding the stone was her mother, and the stone was the most beautiful thing in the world, and it was not to be touched.

My mother's, she gave him. And my father's. Their stone. From when they were bound.

And then she gave him the rest. She gave it to him the way she'd given him the word for the sun on the first night, as a picture, not a word; but it was a long picture this time, and it went down a long way, and some of it wasn't hers. Some of it she had been given, by the woman with the stone, a long time ago, in the dark, because a child had asked why her mother's stone glowed and her friend's mother's didn't.

Two people in the water. A man and a woman, young, the woman unmistakably the one with the long hands, the man broader and darker at the throat, both of them in the deep pale that he knew now was the bow, the deepest courtesy. They were holding the stone between them, each with both hands, the four hands over it, and their foreheads nearly touching above it. Others were round them, at a distance, in a ring, in the water: witnesses. And the two of them opened.

He felt it as she'd been given it. It was a delve. It was the same thing he was doing now, the door opening between two minds, except that it went both ways at once through the stone held between them. Neither of them went in and the other waited. They both went in, together, and met in the middle.

And each of them gave the other something.

Not a word. A piece. A sliver of the man went across into the woman, small and real, something of what he was and what he remembered and how he saw: the smell of a particular cold current, his own mother's voice, a way of holding a spear. And a sliver of the woman went into the man the same way, at the same moment, across the same stone. And neither piece was cut off from home. That was the thing. Emeric saw it so clearly that if he'd had a body in here he would have sat down. The piece of the man that lived in the woman was still tied to the man, through the piece of the woman that lived in him, going the other way. A loop. Closed. Each held a piece of the other, and each piece could still find its way home through the other, so neither of them had lost anything at all. The man still had all of himself. He had it in two places now.

And when it was done they let go of the stone and it was only a stone again. It had been the door, not the store. It didn't keep anything. They kept it anyway, after, the way you'd keep a ring.

So they feel each other, she gave him. A picture of the woman somewhere far away, turning her head, knowing without being told that the man was hurt; and of the man, somewhere else, stopping in the water with his hand to his chest. Always. Not thoughts. Only near, far, well, hurt. And they carry. A little. Each of each.

And then the other half.

The man was gone. He saw that in her mother's face, as Ilenne had seen it, from much nearer the height of a woman now, not a child: the long grey hands holding the old stone again, and the stone dark. No glow in it. And her mother not crying, sitting quite still in the water with the dark stone in her hands, and holding herself, very carefully, the way a person holds a cup that's too full.

He understood what she was holding.

When one of them died, the living one's piece came home. It had somewhere to go. It snapped back to its owner the way a mind does when its body is gone. But the dead one's piece, the sliver of the man that had lived in the woman since the day in the water, had nowhere to go home to. Its home was gone. So it stayed. Her mother carried a piece of her father now, and would carry it as long as she lived. Faint. A smell of a cold current. A voice. A way of holding a spear.

Once, Ilenne gave him. Until one dies. Because you carry. You can't carry two.

It wasn't a law. He could feel that it wasn't. Nobody had ever written it down. It was older than writing, and it had stayed after most people stopped holding the stone at all, the way the shape of an old road stays in the fields after the road's gone: a custom, about a thing nobody did much any more, that everybody kept.



He was nearly out when he felt the room.

He hadn't been looking for it. He'd been coming back up through her, slowly, the way you come back up from a long dive, and the light was getting brighter, and the words were up ahead of him on their stall where she'd left them; and off to one side, in the cool deep place she was, there was something shut.

A door. Not a door, nothing in here was a door, but that was the nearest thing. A place she had closed. It wasn't hidden. She wasn't trying to stop him seeing that it was there. It was the opposite; she'd put it where he'd pass it, on purpose, so that he would know it was there and know that it was shut. He could feel its edges. He could feel, faintly, the shape of what was on the other side the way you can feel the shape of furniture in a dark room by the air it moves. Something with a great many people in it. A house. Fear, old, and anger, newer, and a cold underneath both of them that he didn't like. Something to do with the word Ferra had brought home on Thursday. Something to do with binding.

He could have opened it. He was, he supposed, strong enough. He'd have had to push, and pushing in a delve did harm both ways; a forced read came back on the one who forced it like a slammed door on the hand. But he'd done harder things.

He didn't. He didn't even put his hand near it. He went past it, the way you go past a closed door in somebody else's house, and on up into the light, and he felt her feel him go past, and something in her eased.

It was hers. He knew what that was. She'd stood at a mess table in June with her palm flat on the boards and said My life, and nobody had argued. This was the same. Whatever was behind that door had chased her into the thin water with a fork in her back, and she was going home to face it in the spring on her own legs, and she wasn't going to hand it to Kaldo to deal with. It was her war. She'd tell them when she chose to, or she wouldn't.

"Time," said Armand.



He came out of it into the bay house kitchen with the pool door open and the cold coming in and Ilenne's hands still in his, and she was looking at him across the table with her head on one side, a little pale at the throat, the courtesy.

"Thank you," she said, in Kaldo. She didn't say for what. She didn't need to.

"Thank you," he said, and meant the stone.

Armand had shut the watch and was watching them both over it, with the expression he wore when he knew something had happened and wasn't going to be told. Ferra's word book lay on the end of the table with the salt cellar on it and ask E. to ask why underlined in the narrow column, and he picked up the pencil, and under it, in his own hand, he wrote: Asked. Will tell you tonight.

Ilenne read it upside down, and the corner of her mouth went in.

He went out of the bay house gate onto the shingle in the last of the light, with Armand going slowly up the shore road ahead of him toward the tram, and stopped by the garden wall with his hand on the cold stone of it and stood there for a while, because something had come up behind the rest of it and caught him on the step.

One way.

A piece sent across, and nothing sent back.

It came to him whole, the way things did, the way the light comes on in a room. He had just been shown how two people held each other without losing anything, by the loop being closed. Turn it round. A delver splits a sliver of himself off and puts it somewhere and takes nothing back in return. It's cut loose. It doesn't answer to him any more; it has no road home. Which means it can be told to do a thing, and it will go on doing it without him. A standing order. A script. It will sit in a stone and wait, and when the moment comes it will do what it was told, in another mind, and nobody will ever be able to call it back. Not even the one who made it.

And he'd be short that piece forever.

He thought of a man awake in a stone in the dark under Ostrel, a man who'd once brought an apple to his reading and forgotten to eat it. He thought of how the pope had come to own that man's body, the night he wanted it, from a stone a thousand miles from where he stood. He'd known that he could. He hadn't known how. He knew now. He knew why his cardinals did what they were told, and why he could end any one of them he liked, and what it had cost him every time. A piece of himself gone with every order, two thousand years of them, given away down a road with no way back.

No wonder there was so little of him left.

He stood at the wall a while longer. The tide was coming in over the shingle in long flat lines, grey and cold, and up the road Armand had got to the tram stop and was waiting with his hands in his cardigan pockets, not looking back.

He noticed, standing there, that he knew how to do it.

It wasn't a decision. It was only a fact, like knowing how a lock was cut after you'd seen the key. He'd been shown one end of a thing and he could see the other end of it, and he knew, quite exactly, step by step, the way he knew a lathe, how a man would set a one-sided leash on somebody in a delve. What it would want. What it would cost. He noticed that he knew. He didn't do anything with it.

Then he took his hand off the wall and went up the road after Armand.



He told them that night, at the castle, in the bedroom with the door shut and the lamp turned low, Ferra on one side of the bed with her knees up and her book on them and Imani on the other side lying on her front with her chin on her folded arms. He told them all of it. The stone in the long grey hands. The two in the water with the four hands over it and their foreheads nearly touching. The loop. The man gone, and the dark stone, and the mother holding herself like a full cup. Once, until one dies. Because you carry. You can't carry two.

He didn't tell them about the shut room. That wasn't his.

And then he told them the other end.

They were quiet a long time after.

"So that's how," said Imani at last, into her arms. "Augustine."

"That's how."

"And all the rest of them. The cardinals." She turned her head on her arms and looked at him. "He can just..."

"Any time he likes. If he's got the stone."

"And every one he makes," said Ferra slowly, "he loses a piece." She had her pen in her hand and hadn't written anything. "For good. That's what you're saying. It doesn't come back."

"It's got nowhere to come back to."

"Two thousand years," said Ferra.

"Yes."

Ferra looked at the page for a while. Then she wrote, very small, on a line on its own: One way costs the maker. She looked at it. She didn't write anything else. She shut the book on her finger.

"Nobody else," she said. "Not the table. Not Saban. Not Armand, even, not yet." She looked at Imani, and Imani nodded against her arms. "The Church doesn't know that we know how he does it. I'd like to keep it that way as long as I can keep anything."

"Nobody else," said Emeric.

Imani had turned over onto her back and was looking at the ceiling with her hands folded on her stomach.

"Her mother," she said. "She still carries him. Years on. A smell of a current." She was quiet a moment. "That's the most frightening thing I've ever heard, and I'd do it tomorrow."

Nobody said anything to that. Ferra didn't look up from her closed book. Emeric didn't look at either of them. He lay back between the two of them with his hands behind his head and looked at the ceiling too, where the lamp threw a soft ring, and none of the three of them said the next thing, and all three of them, he was fairly sure, heard it anyway.

After a while Ferra put the book on the floor and turned the lamp down the rest of the way.

They were asleep inside a quarter of an hour, both of them, the way they always went, all at once and all the way, Ferra with her face against his shoulder and Imani with one arm flung across his chest and her knee against his hip, warm right through, the pair of them knotted round him in the dark like roots round a stone.

He lay awake.

He lay awake a long time, with their weight on him and their heat, and the ceiling going from grey to black, and his head too full to let go of anything. He thought about four hands over a stone in the water. He thought about a dark stone in a widow's lap. He thought about a man in a stone under Ostrel. He thought about a door in a deep cool place, shut, and how easy it would have been, and how he hadn't. And under all of it, quietly, like the tide coming in over the shingle, the thing he'd noticed at the garden wall and hadn't done anything with.

He knew how it was made. He knew exactly.

He lay there and didn't do anything with it, and at some point toward morning, without meaning to, slept.

At breakfast they were both as fresh as paint. Imani put two sugars in his coffee without being asked, and said "Poor thing," with great tenderness, and this time he didn't argue.
