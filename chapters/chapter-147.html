Chapter 147 - Just More Up

The suit could not have been made on the forge as it was built. Bram worked that out before Emeric did, which Bram did not let him forget.

It was the rails. The forge's head moved on rails and lead screws, round the chamber, so that it could work a piece from any side; and the rails and screws had been made in the autumn, by hand, in Bram's shop, before there was a master gauge or a true plate or a true screw in the whole of the south. They were good. They were the best Bram had ever made. They were also the reason the cube had been an inch to a breath and not to a hair, and for a cube a breath was nothing. For a breastplate made in one piece with no seam, that had to sit against a man's chest and a stone heart and a harness and a gauntlet's stick all at once and fit every one of them, a breath was a great deal.

So in the fortnight after the bands, Bram made the forge new rails.

He made them on the lathe, from the forge's own lead screw and the forge's own plates, the way he had learned. New lead screws cut from the master. New rails scraped flat against the true plates, red lead and scraper, three plates in turn. The only parts the forge made for itself were the small ones, the parts that fitted its chamber: the nuts that rode the screws, the bushes the rails ran in, the collars that took the thrust of the screw at its ends. Everything else came off the lathe and the bench, by hand, from the masters. On the night of the twenty-first of March Bram and Emeric took the old rails out, and put the new ones in, and ran the cube again.

It came out to a hair.

Bram put it on the tally room's shelf next to the first cube, and the first cube next to the old iron inch, three steps in a row, and stood and looked at them with his hands in his apron.

"Gets better," he said, "and it's no bigger."

"That's the idea."

"I know it's the idea." Bram turned away from the shelf. "I just didn't think it'd be my lathe doing it."



The suit came out of the forge in the second week of April, a piece at a night, on the new rails, and nobody who saw it ever forgot the first sight of it.

It was black. Blackened steel, like the chair, like the knights' harness, with a soft dull sheen to it where the light caught the curves, and not a seam anywhere. The breastplate was one piece. The backplate was one piece. Every plate of the arms and legs was one piece, shaped to the knight it would go on, and where the plates met they met in joints so close that a fingernail would not go between them. There were no rivet heads. There were no hammer marks. There were no smith's marks at all. It looked, laid out on the bench in the shed piece by piece under the lamps, like something that had grown.

And there was nothing in it that had not been in the chair first.

That was the thing Emeric had wanted, and he had it. There was one heart, in a housing in the back, under iron, with its cells in it, glass-lined; one, because the forge could not yet make cells fast enough for two in every suit, and this was one suit, to be flown in turn. There were gravity nodes, small, under smoked glass, at the shoulders and the hips and the heels, that lifted the plate and not the man inside it, so that the man rode in his own armour on the harness straps the way he rode in a saddle. There were air stones at the calves and the shins and the small of the back, behind louvred glass, and in the palms of the gauntlets, which would only fire on an empty hand, so that a knight who drew his sword gave up his hands' push to do it. There was a thumb stick in the grip of the left gauntlet: push for where you wanted to go, twist to turn, let go and it fired against the turn and held you. There was a lift lever at the belt, on the right, with a notch in it between Lighten and Lift that you had to push through on purpose. There was a bellows watchdog. There was an armed and safe toggle. There was a chute, flat, down the spine, silk, with no stone anywhere near it.

Every one of those had failed once already, in the works, at night, with Emeric in a chair a finger's width off the floor and Ward counting. Every one had been fixed. Ward looked at the suit laid out on the bench, piece by piece, and Emeric watched him look, and watched him count. Twist and counter-burst: failure one. Notch: two. Heater shielded and failing off: three. Watchdog and toggle: four. Cooling loop: five. Heavy graded: six. Ducts low and guarded: seven.

"It's the chair," said Ward.

"It's the chair."

Ward was quiet. Then he said, "Who's fitting it?"

Emeric had not decided. He opened his mouth to say he would, and stopped, because there was somebody in Kaldo who knew the stick better than he did, and who had moved his ripcord an inch and a half to where a hand actually was, and who had written in his manual's margin that he measured where things ought to be.

Ward saw him stop. He saw who Emeric was thinking of. Something went across his flat face that in any other man would have been dread.



Sorrel flew first.

She had not been asked to. Since the night of the lanterns she had been in the paper store every morning, measuring the biggest sheets the mill could make and asking Tess how big a bag of paper could get before it would not hold its own shape, and Tess had been answering her, and the two of them had been very quiet about it in a way Emeric had decided not to notice yet. In the afternoons she had been in Abloh's loft with the cape.

That had been Emeric's drawing, and Abloh's cloth, and Sorrel's everything else. The suit's last answer, if every stone in it died at once: a cloak, a hooded cloak of the kind a knight wore over his harness, that was not only a cloak. Inside its lining ran thin battens of sprung cane, folded flat along the cloak's length like the ribs of a closed fan, with cords from their ends to the knight's wrists and heels. Furled, it was a cloak. If the watchdog's bellows went slack, springs at the shoulders threw it open, and the battens snapped out straight, and the cords pulled it taut between the arms and the legs, and the knight was no longer falling. He was gliding. A great black wing with a man in the middle of it.

Emeric had drawn it the way he had seen them drawn in the other life. Abloh had sewn it the way he had sewn the chair's chute. And Sorrel had taken it to pieces three times, and bent every batten to the fourth of his eleven sheets, and changed their run, and moved the cords on the heels, and cut the trailing edge back by a hand's width and then let it out again by half that, and argued with Abloh about every stitch, and Abloh had argued back, and by April they were the best friends either of them had ever had and could not be left alone in a room without shouting.

Bram had offered her the works yard wall. It was twenty-two feet on the inside, where the yard had been dug down for the furnace, and his men had shovelled a sand pile at the foot of it without being asked, rather more of it than anybody could have needed. Sorrel came out of the loft, and looked at the wall, and at the sand, and laughed at both of them.

"I'd be in that before the wing knew it was a wing," she said. "It wants to be going fast. Faster than you can run. And the only thing that makes you go that fast for nothing is falling." She patted the wall, kindly, as if it had tried its best. "I need a long way down, Master Bram. Then I need water at the bottom of it, because this doesn't stop. It only stops falling."

Bram looked at his sand pile. "And then?"

"And then I need you lot to stay at home, so nobody sees me break my other leg."

They did not stay at home. On the eighth they went up the lake road in the works cart, a few miles north to the Mere, and Sorrel climbed the goat path to the top of the cliff beside the falls, where the water came out of its cut in the rock and went down the better part of three hundred feet into the lake. She wore the cloak over a quilted jacket with cork sewn into its lining, and a pair of Rylan's breeches. Two of Rylan's men were out on the Mere below her in a boat. Half the works stood along the shingle at the foot of the cliff, looking up, and Bram stood among them with his hands in his apron and the look of a man whose sand had been insulted.

Emeric had gone up with her. She stood at the edge in the spray off the falls with her limp and her cropped hair and the cloak hanging off her shoulders, and looked down.

"You don't have to," he said.

"I know I don't have to," said Sorrel. "That's what makes it fun." She did not look round. "And if you catch me with anything on the way down, I'll know, and I'll never speak to you again."

She pulled the cords at her wrists. The cloak opened behind her with a crack like a sail taking wind, and the battens snapped out, and she was standing at the top of the cliff with a black wing ten feet across spread behind her arms; and she did not jump. She leaned. Forward, very slowly, until she was past the point where anybody could have stood, and then she was off the edge, and falling.

She fell a long way. She fell further than the works yard wall was high, head down, with the wing open behind her doing nothing that anybody on the shingle could see, and somebody on the shingle made a noise; and Emeric, at the top, had his hand half up before he put it down again. Then the wing bit. The cane stiffened along its length and the trailing edge of the cloak went flat and taut, and her fall bent, the way a skipped stone's path bends when it meets the water. She came out of the dive in a long curve and went out over the Mere, silent, lower all the time but going out a great deal faster than she was coming down, the way a paper plane goes out off a wall on a still day. Halfway across she turned, a little, by dropping one hand, and came round along the shore toward the boat.

A man's height over the water she put her heels down and her arms up, nose too high, going too slow, on purpose this time. The wing reared and stalled and let go of her, and she dropped into the Mere feet first beside the boat with a smack like a plank thrown flat, and came up in her cork with the wing spread on the water round her, laughing.

Nobody on the shore had cheered. Then Bram's men, who had shovelled the sand for nothing, began to clap, slowly, and the rest came in.

Emeric was on the shingle when the boat brought her in. He had come down the goat path in a way it had not been made to be come down. He offered her a hand out of the boat, and she took it, and came up the shingle with weed in her hair and her weight on her good leg and the wing hanging off her, sodden, like a broken umbrella.

"No push," she said. Her eyes were very bright. "No stone. No mage. Nothing pouring into anything. A wing." She shook his hand, hard, with the Mere running off hers. "They'll never believe it in the tower."

"Good," said Emeric.



The chute failed on the tenth, which was what it was for.

They tested it with sand. A sack of sand the weight of a man in plate, with a dummy harness on it, and the chute packed down its spine and the cape over the chute, exactly as a knight would wear them; and the whole of it hauled up on a block to the top gantry of the shed, under the roof, high above the half-built hull of the ship, and dropped, with the ripcord on a string. Ward pulled the string. Ward liked pulling the string.

The cape threw. The chute threw. The chute went up, and came out of its pack, and went straight up into the cape, which was already open above it, and wrapped itself round the battens in a long silk knot, and stayed there. The sack fell the whole height of the shed with a great black silk-and-cane bundle streaming off its back and hit the sand at the bottom with a sound like a dropped side of beef.

Abloh looked at the bundle on the sand for a while with his hands on his hips.

"Well," he said. "It's the cape's fault."

"It's the chute's fault," said Sorrel.

"It's the order," said Tess, from her chair in the door of the shed, where she had come down on the motor to watch. She had her data book open on the drafting board. "The cape goes first. The chute's under it. So the chute comes out into the cape. It's always going to come out into the cape." She did not look up from the book. "Throw the chute below the cape. Not up through it. Down and out. Then it's in clear air."

Abloh repacked it to throw below. It took him a day and a new set of springs. On the thirteenth they hauled the sack up to the gantry again, and Ward pulled the string, and the cape threw, and the chute came out below it, down and out and sideways, into clear air under the black wing, and opened with a whump like a door slammed in a wind; and the sack came down under it, slowly, swinging, and settled onto the sand without a sound.

Tess wrote it in the data book. Abloh said it was the cape's fault the first time and he would hear nothing else about it.



Tess fitted the knights. She fitted them in the shed, at night, with the doors shut and Ward's own men on the doors and the ship's long frame over their heads in the lamplight, and she was merciless.

She fitted the harness first, on each man, standing, and made him walk, and made him kneel, and had it off him and on him again, and moved the buckles, and moved the straps. She fitted the gauntlet. She set the stick's spring for each hand, as she had set her own, by feel, with the knight's thumb on it and hers over his, and wound it stiffer for Osric, whose thumb was strong and clumsy, and softer for Rylan, whose thumb was not. Then she put each of them into the suit, one at a time, in a sling from the gantry so that the suit's heels hung a hand above the sand, and drew the lift lever to Lighten with her own hand, and stepped them through it.

Rylan was good. Rylan was very good. He had been good in the chair in the works, when Emeric let him try it, and he was better in the suit, because he had spent a year on the sand in the yard learning Close Quarters, and he knew already, in his body, how to put weight where it was needed and take it off where it was not. He went up into Lift on his first try and hung there, a man's height over the sand, steady, turning slowly on the stick, and came down light on his heels, and Tess wrote a number in her book and did not say anything, which from Tess was a song.

Declan was good. Osric was not bad.

Ward was hopeless.

He was hopeless in a way Emeric had not known Ward could be hopeless at anything. He was a man who had spent his whole life making his body do exactly what he told it, and the suit did not care what his body did. It cared what his thumb did. And his thumb, which had held a sword since he was a boy, did not understand that it was supposed to push a little and not a lot. He pushed a lot. He went sideways across the shed in a long ungainly slide at a man's height and fetched up against the ship's frame with a clang that brought Fletcher out of his office in his nightshirt. He let go of the stick, as he had been told, and the counter-burst fired, and he spun round once and stopped facing the wrong way. He drew the lift lever and missed the notch and went up three feet too far and hung there, very close to the gantry, looking down at the sand with an expression of deep personal betrayal.

Tess sat in her chair below him with her data book on her drafting board and her pencil in her hand.

"Again," she said.

Ward came down. Ward went up again. Ward went sideways again, the other way this time, and fetched up against the other side of the frame.

"You're pushing," said Tess. "Stop pushing. Lean. Your thumb's not a sword. It's a, it's a thought. Think left."

"I am thinking left."

"You're thinking left very hard," said Tess. "Think it softly." She wrote something in the book. "Again."

Ward came down. He stood on the sand in the black suit with the helmet under his arm and his grey hair stuck to his forehead and looked at her across the shed. A girl in a chair, with a pencil. He had been patient with her about the crossbow for a month. She was not being patient with him at all.

"How," said Ward, very evenly, "do you do it?"

Tess looked up from the book.

"It's my chair," she said. "Just more up."

Somebody by the doors laughed and turned it into a cough. Ward did not laugh. He looked at Tess a little longer, and then he put the helmet back on, and nodded to her, and she drew the lever for him, and he went up again; and this time he only went a little sideways, and fetched up against nothing, and hung there, a man's height over the sand, turning very slowly, with his body rigid in the harness as if he were standing to attention in the sky.

When he came down he took the helmet off and stood holding it.

"It pulls me over," he said. To Emeric, not to Tess. "Backwards. In the hover. All the time. Like a pack that's too heavy." He put his free hand behind him to the heart's housing in the small of the back. "It's all here. All the weight. Nothing in the front to set against it." He looked at the suit. "One's not enough. Not for a man in plate. It wants another one. Front. So it balances."

Emeric looked at the housing, and at Ward, and at the number Tess was already writing beside Ward's name in her book.

"Two hearts," he said.

"Two hearts," said Ward. "Breast and back." He tucked the helmet under his arm. "Then I'll fly it."

Tess wrote that in the book too. She underlined it.



The matrix went into the ship the same week, in the same shed, under the same lamps, and almost nobody knew it had happened.

The stones had been coming out of the forge one a night since February, in among everything else, the way the run list said: the lift stones that would hold the ship up, each one cut from inside to a shape and a pattern in the delve that nobody but Emeric could read. They went into cases along the hull's long frame, under the line where the deck would be, a row of them down each side. Fletcher had made the cases. Ivo had made their liners: glass, thick, smooth, because glass held mana and the ship was going to be a long way up. Bram had made the iron round the glass. Every case had a lid that screwed down on a gasket of Abloh's oiled silk.

Emeric put them in at night with Fletcher holding the lamp. One at a time. A stone into its liner, the liner into its case, the case lid down and screwed and sealed, and then the next. Fletcher held the lamp and watched every one go in and said nothing. Fletcher had been told that the long legs under the hull were for beaching, and that the cases were for ballast stone, and he had never asked. He watched the prince put grey stones into glass-lined iron cases under the deck line of his hull, one after another, very carefully, at night, and he went on holding the lamp, and his face in its light was the face of a man who has known for some time that he has been told less than all of it, and has decided that he will be told the rest in the end, and can wait.

When the last case on the port side was sealed, Fletcher lowered the lamp.

"Ballast," he said.

"Ballast."

Fletcher looked along the row of cases in the dark under the frame. Then he turned the lamp up again and went round to the starboard side and held it there, without being asked, for the next one.



On the twentieth of April Tess went up the hill.

She came out of the powerhouse at the end of the shift, at six, with the evening light coming long and gold down the slope from the ward, and stopped at the foot of the hill, and looked up it.

It was the hill Emeric had pushed her up with his shirt stuck to his back, two springs ago, with Imani walking beside them doing nothing; the hill that Osric had pushed her up every evening since, by the pegged ash handles Bram had fitted to the old chair, which she had never once touched except to find out where they were. It was steep at the bottom and steeper at the corner by the well and the camber at the corner ran the wrong way, toward the wall of the corner house. Nobody had ever got the old chair up it without a man behind it.

Osric was at the powerhouse gate. He had come down to fetch her, as he did every evening. He was standing by the gatepost with his hands at his sides, and he did not move toward her, and he did not say anything. He had been told.

Imani was at the foot of the hill. She had come down from the ward for no reason anybody had given, with no basket, in her shawl.

Tess drew the lever to Lighten. Not past the notch; Lighten, the street mode, wheels on the cobbles, the stones carrying most of the chair's weight and hers, so that the chair rolled at a touch. She touched the stick forward. The right hub hummed. And the chair went up the hill.

It went slowly. It went up the steep bottom of the hill on its wheels, on the motor, lightened, as easily as a wheelbarrow on the flat, with Tess sitting very upright in it with her hand on the stick and her eyes on the top of the hill. People came to their doors. The lower ward had been watching that chair go up that hill behind a man every evening since the autumn, and it came to its doors now and watched it go up with nobody behind it.

Imani walked beside her.

She walked on the left side, the side away from the drop, a little behind the chair's front wheel, the way she had walked beside it the first time, two springs ago, when Emeric had been pushing and she had been doing nothing. She did not touch the chair. She did not offer to push. She did not say anything at all. She walked beside it up the hill at exactly the pace of the chair, with her hands folded under her shawl, and looked straight ahead, the way you walk beside a friend up a road you have both walked before.

At the corner by the well, where the camber ran the wrong way, the chair started to slide toward the wall of the corner house. Tess saw it. She did not stop. She leaned on the stick, a little, and the air ring under the base fired low along the cobbles on the downhill side, a breath, no more, enough to hold the chair straight against the camber, and nobody at any door saw anything but a girl in a very good chair taking a bad corner very well. Imani did not look at the chair. She did not look at the wall. She walked round the corner beside it.

Emeric watched from the powerhouse door, at the bottom of the hill, with Osric beside him at the gate.

He watched them go up together, the black chair and the woman in the shawl beside it, getting smaller up the long slope of the hill toward the ward, in the gold evening light, until they came to the top. At the top Tess stopped the chair and turned it round, on its wheels, and looked back down the hill at the powerhouse, and the works, and the river, and the whole of the lower ward on its doorsteps.

She did not wave. She looked for a while. Then she turned the chair round again, and went in at the ward's gate, and Imani went in after her.

Osric, at the gatepost, let out a long breath.

"Well," he said. "That's me, then."

"You've still got the drafting board," said Emeric.

Osric thought about it. "I have," he said, and cheered up considerably, and went up the hill after them to carry it.
