Chapter 147 - Letters North

Keller's answer came on the twelfth day, by the Crown's rider, and it was not angry.

Emeric had expected anger. He had spent the ride home down the Wayford road with the column, five days of it in the saddle at a column's pace with Marrow in a closed cart behind him and the three hired men walking in irons behind the cart, composing the answer to Keller's anger in his head, and he had got it very good. He had got it good enough that he was a little sorry, opening the packet at his table in the keep with Ferra across from him, not to be able to use it.

It was a page and a half in Keller's own hand, which was small and quick and leaned forward, and it said that the Crown's justice acknowledged receipt of His Highness's letter concerning the Staple at Wayford. It said that the barony's high justice was not in question and that the Crown's justice had not written to question it. It said that a sentence of death carried out on five persons of rank by a baron's own authority, without writ, was without precedent in the present reign, and that the Crown's justice would be asked about it, in the chancery and elsewhere, by persons who had a right to ask, and that when he was asked he would wish to be able to answer from the record.

It asked for the record.

All of it, it said. The summons and its grounds. The evidence on which the summons was issued. The evidence found in the Staple, and in what order, and by whom, and in whose presence. The testimony given in the square, as nearly word for word as the barony's clerks had it. The book, or a fair copy of it, attested. I ask for it not because I doubt what you have done, Keller wrote, near the end, in a line that leaned further forward than the rest, but because I do not, and I should like to be able to show why.

Emeric read it twice and put it down.

"He wants the grounds," Ferra said.

"He wants the grounds."

She did not say anything else. She did not need to. They both knew what the grounds were. The grounds were a room at the back of an ink house in Wayford with two printers on trestles and a grey stone in a socket, and fifteen knots in fifteen chimney pots and on one weathervane, and five bins, and a book of hands, and a line in Placit's own writing about a man called Marrow that nobody but Placit and Marrow and a printer had ever seen until a clerk of seventeen flagged it in red.

He wrote the record over four nights, and it was the worst thing he did that autumn.

He had thought the square would be the worst. He had stood on the counting house steps and watched Vennor on the end of the rope for as long as it took and not looked away, and he had thought, standing there, that there was nothing ahead of him in the whole business that would be as bad as that. He had been wrong. The square had been terrible, and it had been true. Every part of it had been exactly what it was. The record was a lie with no false statement in it, and he had to build it one true sentence at a time.

He began it with the tenth, because the tenth was clean. The charter of the Staple reserved a tenth of every duty through the beam to the lord of Wayford. The tenth had been paid to Sethe for eleven years and entered nowhere. Since the judgment it had been paid to nobody. Lord Hollin had handed a part of it to the present lord at his own table and called it the usual; the purse was in the barony's strongbox, entered as received. On those grounds the barony had examined what it could of the Staple's affairs over the summer, and had found figures it could not account for, and a tally under the heading Held against rent that matched the wording of the tenancy law, and had reason to believe that persons were being held by the houses of the Staple unlawfully, and had summoned the five.

Every word of it was true. He read it over, at the table in the keep at the second hour of the morning, with the lamp and the pen and Ferra's book of the Wayford summer open beside him for the dates, and it read like the truth, and there was a hole in the middle of it exactly the shape of a bottle of ink.

Had reason to believe. That was the hole. He looked at it for a long time. Behind had reason to believe there were fifteen knots and a root and two printers and a summer of other people's letters, a laundry list and a sick child and a lord writing twice a week to a woman in Milford who was not his wife, and Wenna asking Ferra whether she minded it, and Ferra not answering. None of it could go in. Not because it was false, but because it was true, and if it went into a record on Keller's desk it went into the chancery, and from the chancery it went everywhere, and the next man to find a way to hear a pen at a quarter of a mile would not be him.

So he wrote round it. He wrote the summons and the column and the gate and the gallery. He wrote the hall and the stone, and the shot, as a shot, from a height, with a weapon of the barony's, without saying what the weapon was, because Keller would not ask and would not want to be told. He wrote the strongroom and the book-man and the grate. He wrote the cellar, in order, in the plain words Ferra had cut out of the notice, and it took him most of one night to write it and he did not stop once. He wrote the testimony from Ferra's notes and Hobb's, word for word where they had it, Hew's and Colley's and Jem's and Perrin's and Maud's, and the three hired men's, and Strade's correction of her own figure.

He did not write the anger. He did not write that either.

On the fourth night he read it all through, and it was accurate in every line and incomplete at its heart, and he signed it, and Ferra read it the next morning and attested the fair copy of the book, and it went north by the Crown's rider sealed with the barony's seal, and he went up onto the keep wall afterward and stood there a long time in the wind off the lake.

He had never before in his life sent a man he respected a thing he knew to be less than the whole truth and let him think it was the whole. He had sent Keller exactly that. Keller would read it, and believe it, and stand up in the chancery with it and defend him from it, and never know.

His father answered in one line.

It came by the ordinary post, not by Ward's road, under the Crown's seal, in a clerk's hand, and it said that His Majesty had been informed of the proceedings at Wayford and was satisfied that the barony's justice had been exercised within the barony's rights. That was all. There was no summons. There was no request for anything. There was no second line.

He read it and put it face down on the table.

Ward was in the room. Ward had been in the room when it came, and had seen the seal, and had not asked. He was at the window, with his back to it, doing something to the strap of a gauntlet.

Emeric could have asked him. He knew exactly how. A word, in the evening, in the gatehouse, at the third bell, when there was nobody else there: write to him. Ward would have written by the road in the cipher only he and Kassim knew, and the letter would have gone north by the one carrier, and Kassim would have read it aloud to his father in whatever room his father kept for such things, and his father would have heard what Ward had to say about the square and the cellar and the eleven on their benches. And what Ward had to say about him. And perhaps his father would have written back, two lines, or three.

He did not ask. He turned the letter face down and left it there, and Ward finished with the gauntlet and went out, and neither of them mentioned it.

Saban brought Aldane's letter on a supper night, and put it on the table after the dishes had gone, in front of Emeric, without a word, because he had decided on the way up the stair that there was nothing to say about it that the letter did not say.

It was a copy. A clerk in the capital had made it for Saban's man, for a Silver, from the broadsheet, which had been pasted up on the chancery steps and on the doors of three churches and read aloud, Saban's man said, at the fountain in the upper market by a crier in Aldane's own colours, twice a day for three days, to anybody who stopped.

It was a letter of condolence. It was addressed to the widow of Aubrey Placit, lately warden of the Staple at Wayford, and to the widow of Mattias Vennor, both of them daughters of houses that had sat at Aldane's table for three generations, and it was written in Aldane's name in the high style, and it grieved. It grieved for two gentlemen of old family, taken from their homes by armed men in the night and hanged in a public square within two days of their taking, without trial before any court of the Crown, without writ, without counsel, on the word of a single young lord on his own steps. It grieved that such a thing could be done in Randillia under the present reign. It did not say what they had been hanged for. It did not mention the Staple's cellar, or the eleven, or the nineteen, or the book. It said that Aldane would not forget them, and that his house was the widows' house for as long as they had need of it, and that there were debts of honour in the world, and that they would be paid.

It did not name him. It did not need to.

Emeric read it twice. Then he passed it to Ferra, who read it once and passed it on, and it went round the table, Armand and Bram and Imani, and Vantic, because it was a supper night and she was there and there was no reason she should not see a thing that had been pasted on the chancery steps. Nobody said anything while it went round. When it came back to Saban he folded it and put it in his coat.

"Debts of honour," said Bram.

"It was read at the fountain," Saban said. "Twice a day. For three days."

"That's not a letter," said Ward. "That's a muster."

The capital's other answers came in over the next fortnight, by Saban's men and the ordinary post and, once, by a letter under Maeve's own seal, and Emeric read them all at the table in the keep and did not like any of them, for different reasons.

Corbin wrote to praise him.

It was a short letter and a warm one, in Corbin's own big careless hand, and it said that his brother had done at Wayford what their father ought to have done in half the baronies in the south years ago, and that it was time somebody in the family remembered that a lord's justice was a sword and not a ledger, and that he was glad it had been Emeric. He said that there were men at court saying otherwise, and that he had told some of them what he thought of them. He said he would be glad to shake his brother's hand.

Emeric read it three times, looking for the thing underneath it, and could not find it, which worried him more than if he had.

Maeve had refused twice.

He did not hear it from Maeve. He heard it from Saban, whose man in the capital had it from a clerk in the chancery, and then, a week later, from Maeve herself, in four lines under her seal, because she had heard that he had heard. There had been a letter got up in the court after the broadsheet, to the king, from a number of persons of rank, asking that the Crown's justice examine the proceedings at Wayford and consider whether the barony of Kaldo had exceeded its rights, and it had been brought to Maeve to add her name, and she had declined. And a week after that there had been another, got up by Corbin's friends, thanking the king for the barony's firmness at Wayford and asking that its example be commended to the southern lords, and it had been brought to Maeve to add her name, and she had declined that too.

I have signed neither, her four lines said, and shall sign neither, and have told both parties so. I do not know what you found in that cellar. I should like to, one day, from you. Until then I have no opinion I am entitled to. M.

He sat with that one for a long time. It was the only letter from the capital that autumn that did not want anything from him.

Paxton came in person.

He came down the Milford road in the second week of autumn with two servants and a clerk and a box of papers, and asked at the Western Gate for an hour of His Highness's time on a matter of the barony, and was given it, in the long room, with Ferra at the table and Dace at the side table with a pen, because Dace had kept Paxton's factor's accounts for four years and knew every figure Paxton was about to use before he used it.

Paxton was the steward of Milford under the barony of Kaldo, which he had been since the judgment, and he was a quiet man and had always been a quiet man, and he was quiet now. He laid the box on the table and opened it, and it was the Milford tenancy rolls.

"I have one hundred and forty tenants on the barony's holdings in Milford," he said. "Thirty-one of them owe. Some a little, most a good deal. Some have owed for years." He put his hand on the rolls. "Under the law as it stood until a month ago, a tenant who owed could be held on the holding until he paid. Not in a cellar. On his own ground. Working it. He could not leave for Kaldo or the coast or anywhere else with what he owed on his back, because if he could, he would, and the holding would stand empty, and the debt would walk away with him and never come back. That is the law. It has been the law since before either of us was born. I have used it for twenty years. I have used it gently. I have never in twenty years held a man a day longer than the debt, or put a Copper on it he did not owe." He looked at Emeric. "Your notice made it void. In Milford. Not only in Wayford. In every barony held by the lord of Kaldo, it says. I have thirty-one men who may walk off my rolls tomorrow with their debts, and some of them will, and I cannot stop them, and I am not a criminal."

"No," said Emeric. "You're not."

Paxton waited.

"The five used the same law," Emeric said. "Word for word. Held against rent. It was in their books as a heading, in those words. They made the debts, but they didn't need to make the law. It was lying there. It had been lying there since before either of us was born, and they picked it up." He looked at the rolls. "I know you've used it gently. Dace has told me. I believe it. But the law can't tell a gentle man from those five. It can only tell a man who owes from a man who doesn't. And the man who owes can't leave. That's the whole of it, and that's the thing that let them do what they did, and it doesn't stop at Wayford's boundary stone."

"So I am to lose thirty-one debts because of five men in Wayford."

"You're to collect them another way." Emeric nodded at Dace. "Against the man's goods. Against his wages, if he works for somebody else. The debt stands. Every Copper of it. He can't walk away from it. He can only walk away from you." He paused. "If he walks to Kaldo, Dace will find him on the census inside a month and send you his wage-stop, and he'll pay you out of what he earns here, a little every week, the same as he'd pay you if he stayed. Slower, perhaps. But he'll pay. And he won't be held."

Paxton looked at him for a long time.

"You won't give way," he said.

"No."

Paxton nodded slowly. He closed the box. He did not argue any further, because he was a quiet man and he had said what he came to say and had been answered, and there was nothing to be gained by saying it twice. At the door he stopped.

"There will be other stewards," he said, "who will not come in person."

"I know."

"And lords outside your baronies, who will read your notice and not like it, and will wonder what it means for them when their own men hear about it."

"I know that too."

Paxton looked at him a moment longer, and went down the stair with his box.

They were already coming.

The first family came up the south road on foot on the last day of Paxton's visit, a man and a woman and three children, with a handcart, and stopped at the Western Gate, and the man had a sheet of Kaldo's paper folded in his coat, a copy of the notice, soft with handling, torn at one corner where it had been pulled off a post. They were from a holding of Hollin's on the Tollet road. The man had owed four years' rent he did not think he had ever really owed. They had been held against it, and now they were not, and he had put his children in the handcart and walked south for six days, because he had heard that the prince in Kaldo did not hold anybody.

The gate sent them to the census. Dace, who had come home from Wayford with Wenna and the rest of the office a week before, put their names on the roll in his slow neat hand and found the man a place on the rag floor at the paper works at a Copper and a half a day, and the woman a place in the laundry, and wrote in the margin beside the man's name, because it was the rule now, debt to Hollin's estate, four years, to be stopped at a Copper a week against the executors' claim.

Three more families came the next week. Ferra began a new page in her book for them. She did not head it anything yet.

The net came down under the fifth condition in the last week of the month, because the Staple matter was closed, and the fifth condition said that when it closed the net came down, entirely.

Ferra read the condition aloud at the long table, from the fund's own schedule, the way she had read it the night the seven signed it, and nobody at the table said anything, and she wrote the date against it.

Ward and Osric and Declan and Rylan went north in the ship over three nights in the dark of the moon and took the knots down, all fifteen, the seven that listened and the eight that relayed, one chimney pot and one vane and one dovecote at a time, and brought them home in a sack, and Bram opened the casings on his bench and took the stones out and gave the lamp stones to the mine and the forge beads to the forge, and put the empty brass halves in a box on a shelf. The ram knot came out of the rust and pigeon lime of the Staple's vane collar last, on the third night, with Rylan lying along the ridge where he had lain in the summer, over a building that was dark now, and empty, with its big doors chained and a notice of the barony's on them, and no lamp in the upper window at all.

The office in the Ambry back room was closed. The printers came south in their crates. Wenna and Dace burned the last of the pages that bore on no crime on the first of the month, as the rules said, in the ink house's copper, and entered the count and nothing else, and copied the line for Ferra's box. The book of hands went into the strongroom at Kaldo, sealed. The bins went back to the cooper.

The root came south in the green silk, in Emeric's own case.

He did not decide what to do with it. He put it in the strongbox beside the chipped stone, its sister, the one that had never been bound, and looked at the two of them lying there side by side in the silk for a while, and shut the box.

Ferra wrote the last line of the net in the fund's own book. Taken down, entire, under the fifth condition. Fifteen knots, one root, two printers. Pages bearing on no crime: burned, all. Pages bearing on crime: in the record. And under it, smaller, because she wrote figures: Nineteen Gold, six Silver, four Copper for the net. One Platinum, five Gold for the stones. It found the Staple.

The fifty-three full stones from the rack in the cellar came south on the column's last cart, in their straw crate, and Ferra entered them at nothing, because she did not know what else to enter them at.

Emeric looked at them in the strongroom, in their straw, the ordinary cut, a little bigger than a lamp stone, humming to the sense with what was in them. They were full stones like any other full stones. Anybody who bought one would never know.

He knew. He could not put one in a lamp. He could not put one in a suit. He stood over the crate for a long time and could not think of a single thing in Kaldo he could bear to have running on what was in them.

In the end he went to Imani.

"They'll want slow induction," he said. "The ones who are left. Armand will want it for them. Low rate, no forcing, every day. It won't give them anything back. I don't think it can." He looked at the crate, which he had had carried up to the ward's store and set down by the door. "But it might stop them losing any more. And if it's going to be done with anything, it should be done with these."

Imani looked at the crate, and at him.

"It's theirs," she said.

"It's theirs."

She did not thank him. She had the crate put in the back of the store behind the lime, and wrote it in her register, and went back to the ward.

Vantic said it at supper, on a night when the whole table was there, and she said it to Armand.

It was a warm night for the season and the window in the long room was open, and the talk had come round, as it did most supper nights now, to what the works were doing: the steel, which was rolling whole every heat now with Colm's grit in the pot and which Bram talked about the way other men talked about their children; the paper, and the new straw sheet; the first cargo that had gone west to Emberfell the week before on Rowena's slow boat, which had been reams and printed primers and the well standard and a set of Bram's measures and one of Orrin's schoolteachers, a young woman from the mill road who had never been further than Milford and had gone down to the quay with her box looking as though she might be sick, and had gone aboard anyway. And the type, which Bram had started to cast in the forge shop for the press Emberfell would need, because a printer that ran off a root needed a man who could delve and Emberfell had none, and which had stopped after nine days because the type wanted lead and there was no lead in Kaldo that was not already spoken for, and Bryda Renner at Grayrock was keeping every lump of the grey ore she dug out of the old workings and would not say why.

Armand had been describing the printer to Vantic, because she had asked. How it ran a page with nobody deciding each stroke. How the root held the page. How the whole of it had been built, in the end, out of a thing that Emeric had first made to hold a sequence for a gate.

Vantic listened to all of it with her chin on her hand. When he had finished she laughed, a short surprised laugh, the kind that comes out before a person has decided whether to.

"My brother would lose his mind," she said, "if he knew how many wonders there are here."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Then Armand said something courteous about brothers, and Bram said something about lead, and the talk went on.

Emeric sat very still in his chair.

He had not known she had a brother. He had known her for more than a year. He had read the Ducatis loan and its clauses and its schedule of repayment a dozen times; he had read every figure she had ever handed Ferra, the lamp-oil figure and the rest; he had watched her at this table on supper nights and on the ship's deck on the way to Emberfell with her hand white on the rail, and he knew her handwriting and her bank and her terms and her way of laughing when a thing surprised her. He did not know she had a brother. He did not know anything about her at all that was not in a ledger.

And it was not the first time.

He sat there with the talk going on round him and counted, the way he counted anything. Rowena's brother at Emberfell, who he had not known was there until he was standing in the man's hall; he had read Emberfell's trade figures for a year and the port's dues and the sulphur tonnage and had not once asked who stood behind the woman who sent them. The Staple, whose books he had read all summer, page by page, figure by figure, intake and held and yield and kept, and had not once in all that summer asked what a figure of four in a quarter might be counting, until he was standing in a whitewashed room with the answer on the benches round him. And now her.

Three times. Every time the same. He had read the ledger and not the person, and every time the person had been standing in front of him the whole while.

He named it to himself, sitting there, in plain words, the way he would have named a fault in a plate, so that he would know it again. Then he looked across the table at Saban, and Saban, who had been watching him and not Vantic, looked back.

After supper, on the stair, in the lamplight, with Ward a few steps above, he said to Saban, "Her brother."

"I heard," said Saban.

"I should have asked a year ago."

"Yes." Saban did not soften it. "I'll start in the morning."

Ward went to Imani in the second week of autumn, on an afternoon, when the ward was quiet.

She was in the little room at the end of it where she kept her register and her instruments, and the door was open, and the survivors' beds were down the ward beyond it with the lamp between them, and Ward stood in the doorway and did not come in until she looked up and told him to.

He sat down on the stool across from her. He did not say anything for a while, and she did not help him. She had learned a long time ago that men like Ward came to her with something and had to be let get it out in their own time or they would take it away again.

"I want to ask you something," he said. "As a question for you. Not for anybody else."

"Ask it."

"In the Staple. In the cellar. The night we found them." He looked at his hands. "Something happened. I don't know what. I'm not going to tell you what, because I don't know, and because it wasn't mine. But I was in the room when it happened, and I felt it." He was quiet a moment. "I felt it in my teeth."

Imani looked at him.

"In your teeth," she said.

"In the roots of them. All of them. Like a cold drink, but it didn't stop. And in my plate. In the rivets." He rubbed his jaw with the heel of his hand, as though it were still there. "The men in the hall felt it too. Upstairs. They told me in the morning. They thought it was the building settling. It wasn't the building."

"Is it still there?"

"No. It stopped. It's been gone since." He looked up at her. "I want to know if that's a thing. If you've ever heard of it. A thing a man feels in his teeth. Whether it does anything to him."

Imani did not answer at once. She looked at him for a while, the way she looked at a man in the ward who was telling her the truth and not all of it. Then she opened her register at the back, at a clean page, and dipped her pen, and wrote.

She wrote the date, and his name. And under it she did not write what she thought, or what she supposed, or what it might be. She wrote what he had said, in his own words, exactly, the way she wrote a patient's own account of his own pain, because a patient's own words were evidence and her guesses were not.

"I felt it in my teeth."

She blotted it. She did not close the register.

"I haven't heard of it," she said. "If I do, I'll tell you."

Ward nodded, and got up, and went out through the ward past the beds, and she sat in the little room with the register open on the table in front of her at the page, and the line in his words at the top of it, and looked at it for a long time.
