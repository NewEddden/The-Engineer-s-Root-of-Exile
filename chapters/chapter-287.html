Chapter 287 - Hoppers

He took the board to Wicket in the second week of January, on foot, along the reed path to Fletcher's village, in the rain.

He went alone. He had thought about taking Fletcher, who had known Wicket all his life, and decided not to; and about taking Arthur, who had counted the notches, and decided not to that either. It was not a thing for a crowd. He went in an old coat with the board and the knife wrapped in a piece of oiled sailcloth under his arm, the two hours of it, along the raised path through the reeds where the north-east arm came down to the sea, with the rain coming in off the marsh in long grey curtains and the wildfowl going up off the channels on either side as he passed.

Wicket's house was the last in the village, at the water's edge, on stilts, with its boat drawn up under it on the mud: a small grey house of tarred boards with smoke coming out of its pipe and a net hung over its rail to dry, uselessly, in the rain. Wicket was in his door. He had seen Emeric coming along the path for half a mile, the way everybody in Fletcher's village saw everything coming along the path for half a mile, and had come out to stand in his door in his old jersey with his pipe in his hand and wait.

He did not say Highness. He said, "You'll be wet," and stood aside.

The house was one room, warm, with a stove in the middle and a bed in one corner and a chair by the stove, and on every wall, hung up on pegs, the whole of a fisherman's life: nets and floats and lines and oilskins and a lantern and a row of knives. Wicket gave him the chair. He sat on the bed's end himself, with his pipe, and looked at the sailcloth bundle on Emeric's knee, and said nothing.

Emeric unwrapped it.

He had thought on the path about what he would say. He had thought about it for three weeks, since the dune. He found when he unwrapped the sailcloth that he did not need to say anything at all; because Wicket was looking at the knife.

The old man took it out of the cloth in his two hands, and turned it over. A fisherman's knife, its blade a sliver, its handle wrapped round and round with tarred twine; and Wicket turned it over, and looked at the twine, close, where it was finished off at the handle's end with a knot. A particular knot, small and hard, that Emeric did not know the name of.

"Our father's," said Wicket. "That knot. He did all his knives so. He showed us both." He touched it with his thumb. "I do mine so." He nodded at the wall, at the row of knives on their pegs, and Emeric looked, and every one of them was finished off at the handle with the same small hard knot.

Then he picked up the board.

He held it a long time. He did not count the notches. He looked at them, down one edge and up the other and across the face, row on row, getting less neat as they went; and his thumb went along the last row, slowly, to the end, to the last scratch, short and shallow, that was not like the others.

He sat with his thumb on it.

"Where," he said, at last.

"Where you said. South-east. Past the bar. The drowned land." Emeric did not look away from him. "Our ship came down there, three weeks ago. On her range trial, before the Long Table. Something in the water pulled her in. The same as took him, I think. It's quiet now." He was careful. "There's a hut. On the dunes behind the reef. His boat's in it, what's left of her; her stern board's still there, with his mark on it. The fish on the hook. There's a fire ring, and a heap of shells. He ate shellfish. There's a stream. And there are graves, on the dune behind the hut. Eight. Somebody buried them. Some of them were before him, a long way before. One of them might be him. I don't know. I didn't dig."

Wicket said nothing for a long time.

"Seven months," he said, looking at the board. "Near enough."

"Near enough."

"He'd have known. That it'd not let him back out. He was a better boatman than me." The old man looked at the last scratch. "He'd have tried it in the first week. And then he'd have stopped trying and started counting." His thumb did not move. "Did he..." He stopped.

Emeric waited.

"Did he suffer," said Wicket. "Out there."

Emeric thought about how to answer that honestly, because nothing else would do.

"He had a roof," he said. "And a fire, and fresh water, and enough to eat. The shell heap's as high as my waist. He built the hut out of his boat so he'd be dry. He kept count, every day, neatly, for a long time." He looked at the board. "The rows get untidy toward the end. The last one's only a scratch. I think he was ill, at the end, or very cold. I don't know what it was. I'm sorry. I don't know more than that."

Wicket looked at the board a while longer.

"That's more than I had," he said.

He did not ask anything else. He did not ask how a ship had come down on a place that was not there one tide and was gone the next, or what had pulled it, or why the duke had come himself on foot through the rain, or whether anybody else would be going out there now. He did not ask if he could go. Emeric had thought, on the path, that he would ask that; and had had an answer ready, which was that it was the duke's ground now, and nobody was to go, and it was a hard answer to give an old man; and Wicket did not ask. He had never in his life wanted to go there. He had wanted to know.

He got up off the bed's end, slowly, and went to the wall, and took down a peg, and put the knife on it, beside the others, in the row, the twine knot outward. Then he stood the board on the shelf over the stove, against the wall, where he would see it from the chair.

"Thank you," he said, with his back to Emeric. "Highness."

Emeric went home along the reed path in the rain.



The rest of the winter was the island's, and nobody in Kaldo but a few dozen people knew it.

The six sat over it at the table in the wine store for three nights in January and argued; and Fletcher came to the third night, which no shipwright had ever done before, because Ned had told him about the harbour, and sat at the table's end turning his hat in his hands and said almost nothing, and when Ferra asked him at the end of it what he thought, he said that he had been building ships in a covered shed on a lake for eight years because he had nowhere else to build them, and that he would like to build them somewhere the sky could see them for once in his life and nobody else could. In February a works crew went out in the K Endeavor, twelve sworn men and their stores, and came back a month later burned brown by a wind off the sea, saying it was the best land they had ever stood on. In March they went back with forty. The castaways' cairns were rebuilt, stone by stone, by the first crew, the eight of them on the dune's slope where they had lain, the oldest of them, for longer than anyone could say; and a ninth, small, at the end of the row, with no one under it, for the board that was on Wicket's shelf. The crew did that without being told.

And in the castle and the yard and the works the year went on as Kaldo's years did now, too fast to watch. The K Amara's frames went up on her slip, rib by grey rib, until from the yard's fence she was a great silver skeleton four hundred feet long against the sky, and the town's children stopped calling her the market and started calling her the whale. The twins' hulls were plated. Every house in the town and the port and the garden suburb had the new current in it by March, and a switch by its door. The trucks came out of the vehicle works at the rate of one a week. Nothing waited.

And in the north, on the plains beyond Wayford, it did not rain.



Linus saw them first, from the K Discovery, on the ninth of May.

He was flying the long survey north to the Wayford line's end and the plains beyond, low, as he did every spring, drawing the land; and at a little after noon, a hundred miles north-west of Wayford, over the dry country where the great farms gave way to the grazing and the grazing gave way to nothing much, he put his glass down, and picked it up again, and called Emeric to the window.

"There," he said. "What's that."

The plain under them was the colour of a dry biscuit. It had not rained since February, anywhere north of the Wayford line; the farmers' letters to Ferra's office had been saying so since March, every week, with a rising note in them that Ferra had begun underlining. The grass was short and pale. The winter wheat in the fields was thin and yellow at the tips. And across one of the fields below them, from its far hedge to its near one, the ground was moving.

Not the wheat. The ground. A band of it, dark, a quarter of a mile wide and longer than he could see, going across the field below them slowly, like a shadow of a cloud on a day with no clouds; except that it was not a shadow, because where it had been the wheat was not there any more.

Emeric took the glass.

Hoppers. Thousands of them. Hundreds of thousands. A band of them, on the march; young ones, wingless still, each no longer than a man's finger, packed so close that the ground under them was black with them, all going the same way at the same slow walking pace, a great dark river of them across the dry field, climbing every stalk of wheat in their path and leaving it bare. Behind them the field was brown stubble. Ahead of them it was still green.

He had seen pictures of it. Not here; on the other side of everything, in a book about a plain in another world, two hundred years before he was born, where the sky had gone dark at noon for a week in summer and the people under it had lost everything they had. He had read that book at fourteen, for a school prize, and forgotten it, and it came back to him now in the K Discovery's window at four hundred feet over a dry field a hundred miles north of Wayford, every page.

"Locusts," he said.

Linus looked at him.

"Young ones. Hoppers. They hatched out of the ground in the dry, a few weeks ago, out of eggs laid last year, and they're walking because they can't fly yet." He gave Linus back the glass. "In six weeks they'll have wings."

"And then what?"

"And then they go wherever the wind does," said Emeric. "And eat everything when they get there."

They flew on north for two hours, low, and counted bands. Linus counted them on a sheet with a pencil, one line for every band, and marked each on his map with a small black cross where it was and an arrow for the way it was walking. By three o'clock there were forty-one crosses on the map, scattered over a hundred miles of dry plain, all the arrows pointing south-east. By four, sixty.

The wireless went home that night from the K Discovery's set, in Ferra's cipher, a long one; and at the end of it, uncoded, because Emeric had leaned over the operator's shoulder the way Fennick had in December, two words.

Not one of them was for the table. They were for the line room, where he knew she would be, because the wireless came in there and she always came down to read the night's slips herself.

Two words. A pair of initials, at the end of a long cipher about locusts.

The reply came back in forty minutes. It was from the line room, uncoded, the operator's hand; and it said, D.F. and D.I. And under that, in a different hand, because the operator had evidently been made to hand over the pencil: Count them home.

He knew whose hand that was. He did not need the initial.

And under that, in a third hand, square and small, the operator having evidently been made to hand the pencil over a second time: Sleep. That is not a request.



He came home the next day and laid it out at the table.

It took him an hour. He had been thinking about it for twenty hours, since the window; he had been thinking about it, he found, since he was fourteen and read the prize book, without knowing he had. He laid Linus's map on the table with its sixty black crosses and its sixty arrows, and stood over it, and the six sat round it and looked, and he told them what was coming.

Not here. Not yet. On the plains, in the north, where it had not rained. But the plains were the kingdom's bread; and the wind, in high summer, blew south-east, toward everything.

"Four things," he said. "Only four. That's the whole of it. If we do all four we'll lose a part of a harvest and nobody in the four baronies will go hungry, and fewer people in the kingdom than would have. If we do three, we'll lose more. If we do two, people will die." He held up his hand, and counted on it. "One. Watch early. We've already done it. Linus saw them six weeks before they'll fly; nobody in the kingdom has ever seen a swarm coming six weeks off before. We go on watching. Every ship we've got that can fly low and slow flies the plains every day from now to August, and counts, and the wireless and the telegraph bring it home, and Linus puts every band on the map."

He folded a finger.

"Two. Count everything. Every band. Every field they eat. Every bushel of grain in every barn in the four baronies and in the kingdom, if we can get it. Every mouth." He looked at Ferra. "If we don't know what we've got and where it is, we can't send it where it's needed. And somebody will be selling it to the people who need it at ten times its price while we're finding out."

Ferra was already writing.

"Three. Kill them before they fly. That's the one that matters. While they're walking, a man with a spade can kill a thousand an hour. A ditch across their road kills a hundred thousand: they walk into it and can't climb out, and you bury them. Turn the ducks and the hens out on them. Dig up the egg beds in the autumn, where they've laid, so there's nothing to hatch next spring. Once they've got wings, nothing kills them that we can afford. Once they've got wings, it's too late."

He folded another.

"And four. Move food faster than hunger." He put his hand flat on the map. "They'll eat the north. Some of it. A lot of it. We can't stop all of them; nobody can. So the grain has to go to the places they've eaten, before the people there run out. Faster than the people there run out. By rail. By ship. From Milford's barns, and Grayrock's, and anywhere else that has it." He looked round the table. "And it has to go at a fair price, or it'll go to the people who can pay, and the people who can't will walk down the Wayford line looking for it."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"How do you know all this," said Ward, at last. He did not say it as a challenge. He said it the way he said everything, flatly, as a thing he wanted to know.

"I read about it," said Emeric. "A long time ago. In an old book, about a plain where it happened, and what the people there did, and what they should have done." He looked at the map. "They did two of the four. A great many people died."

Ward looked at him a moment longer, and then at the map, and did not ask anything else.

"The leaflet," said Ferra.

"The leaflet." Emeric looked at her. "One sheet. Both sides. Plain words, and pictures for the ones who can't read, which is most of them. What a hopper looks like. How to dig a ditch across its road. How to turn the poultry out. Where the egg beds are and how to dig them in autumn. What to do and when. On the Milford Book's presses, as many as they'll print, and to every parish in the kingdom, not only ours." He paused. "Every parish. Every priest's door. Every market cross."

"The crown will take that badly," said Saban.

"The crown will take a famine worse," said Emeric. "And so will I."



The leaflet went out of the Milford Book's presses at Milford on the fourteenth of May, ten thousand of it, and by the end of the month a hundred thousand: one grey sheet, both sides, Kaldo paper, Kaldo ink.

Ferra wrote it, in two nights, in words a carter could follow, and crossed out every word he could not. Ivo drew the pictures: a hopper, life-sized, so that a man could hold the sheet up to the thing in his field and know it; a ditch, with the hoppers walking into it like men going over a cliff; a woman with a spade; a flock of ducks. A boy from Sela's school, sent to the printing works with Ferra's draft, read it aloud to the compositors in a strong clear voice to see if it made sense to somebody who was twelve, and it did. It went out on the Wayford line in sacks, and on the Grayrock and Milford lines, and in the K Discovery and the public lift hull to every town that had a field to land them in, and on foot from the railheads, carried by anybody who would carry it, to every parish in the kingdom; and at the bottom of the back of it, in small plain letters, it said who had printed it, which was the duchy of Kaldo, and that it was free.

He had the next part afterward from Imani, and from her notebook, in the order she wrote it down.

In Milford, in the cheese cellar dug into the hill behind her dairy, where she had been growing moulds on bread and cheese rind and broth for thirty years because her mother had and her mother's mother, Hester Mull put a hopper in a jar.

She had been asked to. Imani had gone to Milford on the fifteenth on the morning train with a box of jars and two hundred live hoppers in a cage, brought south from the plains in the K Discovery's hold in sacking, to the great displeasure of the K Discovery's crew; and had sat in Hester's cellar at Hester's table, by lamplight, among the shelves of Hester's dishes and crocks and cheeses furred white and blue and green, and said that she did not believe a word of what she was about to ask and would like it tried anyway.

Because Emeric had said that somewhere, on the other side of everything, men killed locusts with a mould.

He had not said it to Imani in those words. He had said it to the table: that there was a kind of mould, in the ground, that grew on insects and killed them, and nothing else; and that if Kaldo had such a mould anywhere, anybody would know of it, it would be the mould wife of Milford, who had found the cowpox and the bread mould for Imani's ward and had forty kinds in her cellar nobody had names for. And Imani had looked at him across the table with her eyebrows up and said that if he wanted her to believe a mould would kill a locust he could bring her the locust; and he had brought her two hundred.

Hester put one hopper in each of forty jars.

That was Imani's way: forty, not one. In twenty of the jars, Hester dusted the hopper with a pinch of something green from a crock on her top shelf that she had scraped off a dead beetle in her garden two summers ago and kept growing because she liked the colour; and in the other twenty, she dusted them with a pinch of plain flour, so that they would be handled the same and dusted the same and want for nothing the others had, except the green. Imani labelled every jar. She wrote down the time. They put the forty jars on the cellar's shelf in a row, and fed them every morning on fresh wheat, and waited.

On the fourth day, three of the hoppers in the green jars were dead, and none in the flour.

On the sixth, eleven.

On the ninth, all twenty of the green, every one, lying on their backs in their jars with their legs folded and a fine pale green fur coming out of the joints of their bodies like mould on bread; and in the twenty flour jars, nineteen hoppers alive and eating, and one dead of nothing anybody could see, which Imani wrote down as well, because that was what you did.

She put the green crock and her notebook and the forty jars in a box and brought them back to Kaldo on the train, and put the box on the table in the wine store in front of Emeric, and opened her notebook, and turned it round so that he could read it, and folded her arms.

"It kills them," she said.

"I know."

"It doesn't kill anything else. She's had it in her garden for two summers. Her hens have eaten it. Her cat sleeps on it. I put it on a rat for a week." Imani looked at the box. "Nothing. It's for them. For hoppers and beetles and things of that kind. Nothing with a backbone." She looked at him. "She calls it her green rot. She's been growing it on the top shelf because she likes the colour."

"Can she grow a lot of it?"

"She's growing a lot of it," said Imani. "She started on the second day, when the first three died. She didn't wait for me. She said if I wanted to wait till I believed it I could, and she'd have the cellar full by the time I did." She paused, and something that was very nearly a smile went across her face. "She was right. She has. She's using the dairy's cheese vats. She says the cows can wait."

"Imani."

"I know," said Imani. "I know. I'll write it up properly. Controls and all. So when the lords say it's a witch's brew we've got it in writing." She unfolded her arms and put her finger on the last line of her notebook, where she had written in her square hand, the night before, on the train: Hester was right. I was wrong. Mark it. "There. Marked."



The crown's answer came south on the twentieth, in print.

It was a proclamation, on the crown's own stiff white paper, under the great seal, posted at every market cross from the capital to the coast; and Saban's man in the chancery had sent a copy down the Wayford line in his own bag the day it was struck, with one line of his own in pencil on its back, which Saban read out at the table before he passed it round.

"His own hand. Prince Corbin's. He sat up three nights with the clerks."

The six read it in silence.

It was good. That was the first thing Emeric thought, reading it, and he went on thinking it all the way to the end: it was good. Five measures, in the crown's language, plain enough for a market cross. A bounty, paid by the crown at every parish church, for every bushel of dead hoppers brought in and every pound of their eggs: a copper for a bushel, two for a pound. Fire: every lord in the north to burn a line across the hoppers' road, a field wide, and drive them into it. The grain: every barn's grain north of the Wayford line to be counted by the crown's clerks and sold at a price the crown would fix, so that no man could hold it back and sell it dear. A day of prayer and fasting in every church in the kingdom, at Midsummer, in the Light's name, with the Church. And the roads: closed, north of Wayford, to anybody without a parish paper, so that the hungry should not wander and carry the trouble south.

"He's not stupid," said Armand, when he had read it twice.

"No," said Emeric. "He's not stupid at all."

"The bounty's good," said Ward.

"The bounty's very good." Emeric put his finger on it. "It pays the hungry to do the work the crown can't. Every parish becomes a crew. Every child with a sack. It'll kill more hoppers than every lord's fire line together." He moved his finger down the sheet. "The fire's half good. It'll kill hoppers. It'll burn farms that could have been saved, and it won't stop anything with wings. The grain price is half good. It'll stop the worst of the hoarding. And then every farmer who's got grain will bury it in a pit in his orchard and sell it at night, at three times the price, to anybody who comes with coin." He tapped the prayer day. "That one costs nothing and it'll comfort people and I won't say a word against it. Except that it'll give every pulpit in the kingdom a day to say what it thinks the swarm is for." He came to the last. "And the roads."

"The roads are bad," said Imani.

"The roads are bad. The hungry will walk anyway, papers or no papers. They'll walk down the Wayford line, and the line runs to us. And the crown's men at the road blocks will turn them back into the country the hoppers have eaten." He looked at the sheet a long time. "He's right that the crown has to act. He's right about the bounty. He's wrong about the roads the way a sensible man of his age would be wrong about them. That's all." He put the sheet down. "Nobody in his family has ever had to think about a famine. He's thought about it for three nights, with clerks. It's a good answer for three nights."

"You've thought about it for twenty years," said Ferra, not looking up from her book.

"I've read about it for twenty years," said Emeric. "That's a different thing."



He tried the fire himself, on the twenty-seventh, on a band in a fallow field forty miles north of Wayford, because he wanted to know.

The band was crossing a strip of bare fallow between two fields of wheat: the hoppers walking across the brown earth in their dark river, half a mile wide, toward the green on the far side; and nothing on the fallow but earth and the hoppers on it, nothing that could catch, no hedge, no house, no stack, for a quarter of a mile in every direction. He had chosen it from the air for that. Linus had flown him to it in the K Discovery and set him down at its edge, and Linus and the crew stood a long way back with buckets anyway, because Linus did not trust fire and never had.

Fire, and air. The firestorm. He had never done it. He had read about it in Armand's old book of combinations, years ago, and done each half a thousand times, the fire and the air, alone, and never put them together; because the book said that a firestorm was the most dangerous thing two mages could make between them, and that one mage making it alone had better be very sure of the ground he was making it on.

He was very sure of the ground.

He stood at the fallow's edge in the wind, with the band coming toward him across the earth a hundred yards off, and put up his left hand, and drew the fire: not a bolt, not a jet, a sheet, low and long, laid along the ground across the band's front like a hedge of flame a quarter of a mile wide. And with his right hand, behind it, the air: not a push, a feed, a long steady breath of it under and into the fire from behind, so that the fire took the air and roared and went up, and forward, and rolled.

It rolled across the fallow like a wave.

He felt it go out of him, the whole cost of it, all at once, like a door opening in a cold house; and the heat came back off it into his face so that he had to shut his eyes against it; and when he opened them the wave had gone across the band's front and fifty yards into it, rolling, yellow and orange and roaring, eating the air and the hoppers together, and behind it the fallow was black and smoking and there was nothing walking on it.

He held it for a count of thirty. Then he let it go, and it fell on itself and went out in the bare earth, and the smoke went up straight and grey into the dry sky.

Linus and the crew came up from the ship with their buckets and stood at the edge of the black and looked at it.

"Well," said Linus, after a while, impressed in spite of himself.

Emeric looked at it too.

A strip of black earth, a quarter of a mile long and fifty yards deep, smoking. A great many dead hoppers in it, curled and burned. He could not have said how many. Ten thousand. Fifty. And beyond the black, on the far side of the fallow, the rest of the band, the other quarter of a mile of it, still walking, on into the green wheat on the far side, as though nothing had happened; because for them nothing had.

And his arms ached to the shoulder, and his head ached behind the eyes, and there was a dull hollow in his chest where a great deal of what he had carried that morning had gone.

He had burned fifty yards of one band out of sixty, with everything he had, for a count of thirty. On bare ground, where nothing else could catch. And there were not enough mages in the kingdom to do it to the rest, and not enough bare ground, and if there had been, they would have been empty by noon.

Hester's green rot, in a cheese vat in Milford, had killed twenty out of twenty for the price of a pinch.

"The mould's better," he said.

Linus looked at him sideways.

"You're disappointed," he said.

"No," said Emeric, and found, a little to his surprise, that he was telling the truth. "I'm glad. I wanted to know what it would do. Now I know." He looked at the black strip, and the band going on into the wheat beyond it. "It'll do that. It won't do this." He nodded at the plain, at Linus's map in its case under Linus's arm with its sixty crosses on it. "Nothing that comes out of one man's hands will do this. That's the point of it." He turned to go back to the ship. "Write down that it cost me most of a morning to burn fifty yards. Somebody'll want to try it with a hundred mages one day, and they ought to know."



The count came home from the capital on the twenty-ninth.

It came the way news from the capital always came to Kaldo first, by Ward's road: in cipher, in Harrold's hand, down Kassim's line, by men who did not know what they carried. Ward read it alone in the wine store, at the table, at night, with the lamp; and then he went up the stair to the castle and found Emeric in the study, and Ferra with him, and put the sheet on the desk between them without a word, and stood back.

It was a list.

The Proving class. The sixty-one who had ridden north from the knights' base at first snow in November, under Abloh's tents, with Ward saying each of their names at the gate; to the capital, for the rite season, first snow to the Ascent, the floor. Harrold had sent their names. All sixty-one. And beside each name, in his own small upright hand, one word.

Stood. Or Fell.

Emeric read it down. He did not count; he could not, at first. He read the names. Some of them he knew: lords' sons from the north and the west who had come to the base in July in their good boots and their fathers' coats and stood in the yard while Ward looked at them; the crown's men in their grey, posted south by Harrold's seal; the miller's son from Milford; the smith's boy from Wayford, who had wept the first day on the cold water and never again. The girl from Grayrock. Stood.

Harrold had written the lords' sons the way the court wrote them, with the house after the name: Fairhythe; Cinderhale; a barony held of Dunholt; a barony held of Tarrowmede; the Narrow March, twice. The Cinderhale boy in his grandfather's coat. Fell.

Ferra counted.

She did it with her pen, down the column, a small mark beside each Stood; and wrote the total at the bottom, and then counted again, as she always did; and the second total was the same as the first.

"Twenty-two," she said.

Nobody said anything for a while.

"Of sixty-one," said Ferra.

"Of sixty-one," said Ward.

"On the floor it's one in ten." Ferra did not look up from the column. "Nine in ten die. That's the rite. That's what it's always been. If they'd gone in from their fathers' houses without the Proving, six of them would have stood." She drew a line under the total, very straight. "Twenty-two stood."

"Thirty-nine fell," said Ward.

He said it flatly, as he said everything. But Emeric was looking at him, and saw his hand on the back of Ferra's chair, and saw that it was holding the chair's back quite hard.

"I said their names," said Ward. "At the gate. Every one. In November." He looked at the list. "I'll say them again."

He did. On the thirtieth, at the base, in the yard, at dawn, in front of the new class that had come in that month, and the knights, and the base's people, and Emeric, who went because Ward had not asked him to and he knew that meant Ward wanted him there. Ward stood in the middle of the yard on the trodden earth with Harrold's sheet in his hand and read it. Every name. The twenty-two first, one after another, slowly, and after each, the word: stood. And then the thirty-nine. Slowly. Every one. And after each: fell.

It took a long time. Nobody in the yard moved.

When it was done Ward folded the sheet and put it inside his coat, and stood there in the yard a moment longer with his hands at his sides; and then he looked at the new class, in their rows, the forty-eight of them who had come in that spring, lords' sons and crown's men and a girl from Milford and a boy from the port, standing in the yard in the dawn looking at him.

"Twenty-two," he said. "Without the Proving it would have been six. Next year it'll be more. That's why you're here." He looked along their rows. "Not to be made knights. Nobody here can make you a knight. To go to the floor with half of it built already, so that the floor doesn't kill you." He was quiet. "I'd rather none of you went to the floor at all. I'd rather the floor was shut. It isn't my floor to shut." He looked at them a moment more. "Cold water at six."

He walked off across the yard.

Ferra wrote it in her book that night, at the study table, one line, in her small exact hand, under the date: Ascent, yr 12. The Proving. 61 to the floor. 22 stood. Then, after a while, under it, smaller: Earned. Not promised. And then she sat with her pen over the page a long time, and did not write anything else.

The second gravity hall at the base was ordered the next morning. Ferra's office ordered it; Bram signed it, from his works, where he was back on his feet with his arm out of its sling and shouting at everybody to make up for the month; and Ward did not say anything about it at all, which was how Emeric knew how much he wanted it.



The bands kept walking.

All through June. Linus's map in the drawing office grew crosses the way a field grows weeds: a hundred by the first of June, a hundred and sixty by the tenth, two hundred and twelve by the twentieth; all of them on the northern plains, all of them walking south-east, all of them eating. The K Discovery flew the plains every day. The Hod flew them twice a week, between charge runs. The public lift hull, on the Milford route, carried leaflets and brought back reports from every town she touched; and the reports came into Ferra's office by wire and by wireless and by the morning train, and Ferra's clerks put them on the map, and the map grew darker.

And the leaflet worked, where it was read. That was the thing that kept Emeric sleeping at night, a little. Where the leaflet went, people dug. Linus flew over a parish forty miles north of Wayford on the twelfth of June and saw a ditch across a hopper band's road a mile long, dug by every man and woman and child in the parish in three days with spades, and the band walking into it and the parish standing along its far edge with shovels, burying them as fast as they came; and in the next parish, which had not had a leaflet, because the carrier had been turned back at a crown road block for not having a parish paper, the wheat was gone.

And Hester's green rot went into vats.

Not cheese vats any longer. Bram's vats. He built them at the works at a run, in a week, with his arm out of its sling: a row of twelve great copper vats in a shed at Milford beside Hester's dairy, each the size of a cart, with lids and steam coils to keep them warm and paddles to stir them, and a boy at each paddle; and Hester went up and down the row from dawn to dark in her apron and her boots with a lamp and a long spoon, lifting every lid, smelling, tasting, not letting anybody else touch the mould but the boys she had chosen, and saying that the cows could wait. By the twentieth of June the vats were full and green, and the Motion Shop was building the booms.

"To spray it," said Tess, when Emeric came to the shop to see. She had two of them on the bench: long arms of dural tube, light, the length of a ship's beam, with a row of nozzles along them and a pump at their roots. "Off the ships. Over the bands. Low. You mix the green in water and it comes out of these in a mist, and it falls on them." She looked at him. "And on the egg beds, after. Where they've laid. Before the eggs can hatch next spring."

"Off the ships," said Emeric.

"Off every ship you've got that can fly low and slow," said Tess, "and keep still in a wind."

Wren, under the bench, with a lamp in her teeth, took the lamp out to say, "The Hod," and put it back in.

"The Hod," Tess agreed. "She's slow. She's steady. And she's got two hulls to hang a boom off either side of." She looked at the booms on her bench. "I'll have four by the first of July. Six if Dov stops arguing with the pump."

"I'm not arguing with the pump," said Dov, from the far bench. "The pump's arguing with me."



The first band grew wings on the twenty-sixth of June.

Linus saw that too, from the K Discovery, on the plains north of Wayford: a band he had marked on the ninth of May, one of the first, gone from the field it had been walking across the day before. Not walking. Gone. And above the field, in the hot white noon, a haze, like smoke, like a heat shimmer, very high, moving.

He put the glass to it and looked a long time. And then he went to the wireless himself, and sent it, not in cipher, because there was no need of cipher for this; and it reached the line room in Kaldo at a quarter past one, and the operator took it down and read it and went white, and got up from his set and ran up the hill to the castle with it in his hand.

It said: THEY FLY.

Emeric was in the air when it came. He was in the Hod, over the plains, a hundred miles north, with Ned at the helm and Tess's first two booms hung off her hulls on either side and two hundred gallons of Hester's green in her tanks, on her first spray run. He heard it on the Hod's own set, the new one, with its valves packed in felt that Cade had built in January so that it would never again crack on a jolt; and he went to the Hod's after window and looked north, and there, a long way off, high, against the white sky, was the haze. Moving.

He stood at the window and watched it a long time.

That night the Hod lay over in a field near Wayford, and the set was on, and at ten o'clock it came alive in his ear, in Ferra's cipher, from the line room.

It was a short message. It said what was in the vats at Milford and how many booms Tess had and when the trains would run north with Milford's grain. It said that the crown's road block at Coldwell had turned back six hundred people that day.

And then, at the end, uncoded, in the operator's hand, which meant somebody had been leaning over his shoulder again:

D.F.: How many days.

D.I.: How tired.

He sat in the Hod's house in the dark with the earpiece on and the pencil in his hand for a long time.

Then he sent back, uncoded, three words; and he knew, as he sent them, that the operator in the line room would take them down and hand them to the two women standing behind him without reading them, because the operator had been in the line room for two years now and knew better.

Many. Very. Yours.

And the set was quiet; and then, a minute later, it came alive again, one line, both hands, the operator having evidently been made to hand over the pencil twice in quick succession:

Ten o'clock. Every night. Until you're home.
