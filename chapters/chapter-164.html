Chapter 164 - Cold Stones

Maren had asked whether there was any way of making a room colder than the cold store in the summer without ice, and Emeric had said there might be, and on the ninth of September he brought her a stone in a box and said that this was it.

She looked at it in its box on her kitchen table. It was a fire stone. Red-orange, the size of a hen's egg, faintly warm, exactly like the stones in the bread oven and the stones under the soup and every other fire stone in Kaldo; and she knew a fire stone when she saw one, and said so.

"It's a fire stone," said Maren. "I asked for cold."

"It's a fire stone set to pull," said Emeric.

That was all a cold stone was. He had known it for two years and never used it, because there had been nothing worth using it on. A fire stone pushed heat out of itself into whatever was round it: the oven, the pan, the water in the bath house. Set it the other way, from inside, in the room, and it pulled. It drew heat out of everything in its field and threw it out past the field's far edge, into a plate of copper set there for the purpose, and never into itself. The field went cold. The plate went hot. The stone stayed exactly as it was, as a pump stays exactly as it is while the water goes through it.

"So it's not making cold," said Maren slowly. She had picked up the box and was turning it, looking at the copper plate fixed to one end and the stone at the other.

"Nothing makes cold. Imani says so, and she's right. It moves the warm somewhere else." He touched the copper plate. "Here. Out of your room and into this, and this goes through the wall, outside, where the warm can go wherever it likes."

Maren put the box down.

"Show me," she said.



He showed her in the cold store under the north wall that afternoon. Bram's men had cut a hole through the store's back wall the size of a man's hand, and the copper plate went into the hole with the stone on the inside of the wall on a shelf and the plate's far end out in the lane, in the sun, with its fins standing out like the gills of a fish. Vesk charged it. Emeric touched it, through the room, the way he had touched it the night he cut it, to wake it.

It took a quarter of an hour. Maren stood in the cold store with her arms folded and her breath going in and out, and after a quarter of an hour her breath began to show. A little cloud, in the light of the lamp. She looked at it. After half an hour the crocks of butter on the shelves, which had been soft, were hard. After an hour there was frost on the inside of the lid of the milk churn by the door, a white fur of it, and Maren put out her finger and touched the frost, and took her finger away and looked at the wet spot on it, and did not say anything for so long that Emeric went outside to look at the copper fins in the lane, which were too hot to touch.

When he came back she was standing in the middle of the store with a crock of cream in her hands.

"Honey," she said. "And cream. And eggs. And you stir it, while it freezes, so it doesn't go to a lump. My grandmother made it at midwinter, once a year, with the snow off the roof and salt in a tub round the bowl, because salt on snow makes it colder than snow, and you stir till your arm drops off." She looked at the crock. "Once a year. If it snowed. For the lord's table."

She looked at the stone on its shelf.

"In September," said Maren.



The hospital's cold room went in first, because Imani asked first, and because Imani's asking came with a list.

She had been keeping things on ice for six years: the ether, which went off in the heat; the broth for the fevers; the milk for the babies whose mothers had none; the matter Pell was growing in her hundred dishes of broth in the corner of the cutting room, which Imani did not want growing any faster than it had to; and the dead, in the summer, when the ground was too hard and the families too slow. The ice came from the old ice house below the castle, cut off the canal ring in the winter in blocks and packed in straw, and by August it was gone. Every August it was gone. And every August the ether went off, and the broth soured, and a baby or two at the breast-shelter got the summer flux from milk that had turned, and the dead waited in the warmth.

Three stones, in the hospital's cellar, with their plates through the north wall. Bram's men lined the room with cork from the coast and plastered it, and put a double door on it with straw between the doors, because Emeric said a cold room was a bucket with a hole in it if you let the warm in faster than the stone could throw it out. Ivo's new thermometer went on the wall by the inner door, on its card, and the first morning Imani went down to read it, it said that the room was colder than the canal ring in January.

She stood in front of it for some while. Then she went upstairs and wrote a new rule on the cutting room's slate, under the others: ether in the cold room; milk in the cold room; nothing warm through the inner door without the outer one shut first. And under that, in smaller letters, as if it had come to her after: the dead may wait.

Then the barony's stores. The long cellar under the counting house where Ferra kept the grain from the farms, and the salt fish from the coast boat, and the barrels of the castle's beer; six stones, and its plates through the cellar wall into the moat ring. The butchers' row in the lower ward, where the slaughter yards' meat hung through the summer and went green by the third day. The dairy that took the north farms' milk, two stones. The salt fish man by the water stair, one, which he paid for himself, in a month, out of the fish he did not throw away.

The ice works was last, and it was the strangest.

It was the old ice house below the castle, the brick dome sunk in the hillside where the canal ice went every winter, and Bram's men cut a long room into the hill behind it and lined it with cork and put a row of eight stones down its back wall with their plates through into a chimney, and filled the long room with shallow iron trays of the canal ring's water off the new sand filters, a hand deep. And in the morning the water in the trays was ice. Clear and hard, a hand thick, in slabs the size of a table top, and the men knocked it out of the trays with mallets and slid it down a wooden chute into the ice house in the dark where it had always gone, and filled the trays again; and by the end of September the old ice house below the castle was full to the dome, in September, for the first time since it was built.

The town came to look. It came to look at the chute, and the men with mallets, and the slabs of ice sliding down into the dark in a month when there had never been ice in Kaldo, and it stood in the lane outside with its arms folded, the way it had stood outside the works the first night the furnace was lit, and the way it had stood under the first arc lamp on the wall, and said nothing for some while. Then a child said that it was cold, and put her hand on a slab of ice coming off the chute, and took it away very fast, and laughed; and then all the children were doing it, and the men at the chute let them.



Maren's barrow came out on the twentieth, market day.

It was a barrow. An ordinary handcart from the castle's yard, with two wheels and two handles, that Bram's apprentice had fitted with a box: a tin box inside a wooden one, with cork between, and in the bottom of the tin box, under a grating, a cold stone with its copper plate standing up out of the box's lid at one end like a little chimney with fins. And in the tin box, on the grating, three tubs.

Honey. Cream from the north farms' dairy, and the castle's eggs, and the castle's honey, stirred in a tub inside a bigger tub of ice and salt in Maren's kitchen all morning by two kitchen boys taking turns until their arms dropped off, exactly as her grandmother had done it once a year for the lord's table, and set in the cold box on the barrow to stay hard.

She wheeled it to the market square herself at ten o'clock and stood behind it with a ladle and a pile of little horn cups that the turner had made her by the hundred, and a slate on a nail on the barrow's front that said, in Maren's hand: ICED CREAM. HONEY. A COPPER.

The square did not know what it was. The square looked at the slate, and at the barrow, and at Maren, and went on buying its onions.

Then a boy bought one. He had a copper from somewhere and nobody to tell him not to, and he gave it to Maren, and she gave him a horn cup with a scoop of something pale and soft and gold in it and a wooden spoon, and he put the spoon in his mouth.

The square watched his face.

By eleven the queue went right across the square and down the lane beside the church. By noon Maren had sent the kitchen boys running back to the castle twice for more tubs, and the second time they came back with Hale, who had been sent down to see what the noise was and had bought two and was eating one on the way back up the hill with an expression Emeric had never seen on him in six years. By two the tubs were empty, all of them, scraped to the tin; and Maren stood behind her barrow in the empty square with a pile of copper in her apron so heavy that she had to hold the apron's corners up with both hands, and looked down at it.

Ferra counted it at six. She counted it twice.

"More than the soap works," she said, at supper. "This week. All week. The soap works, which has sold in four baronies and on the coast for five years." She looked down the table at Maren, who had come up from the kitchen to hear it and was standing in the door with her arms folded and flour on them. "In a day."

"It was a market day," said Maren.

"It was a market day," Ferra agreed. She wrote something in the book. "I'll want to know what the cream costs, and the honey, and the eggs, and the cups, and the boys' time. And I'll want a second barrow." She looked up. "And a third for Grayrock, on the line, on Grayrock's market day."

Maren looked at her.

"Beet," she said. "Next year. Not honey. Your Milford farmers grow the beet for the cattle, and there's sugar in it, he says." She jerked her chin at Emeric. "He says you can boil it out. White, like the coast sugar, the lords' sugar, at a copper a pound. Then I'll want six barrows." She went back down to the kitchen.



The second water tower went up that month too, on the hill above the lower ward, beside the first; and behind it, at the intake on the river where the town's water came in, the settling tanks.

That was Imani's, and had been since the summer she first put the Seers' Table's drawings of the canal ring's water beside her own book of the summer flux and saw that the bad weeks in the book were the weeks the drawings were thickest. The water came into Kaldo from the river above the town, up the beam pump, and it was good water, as river water went. But in the spring floods and the autumn rains it came down brown, and in the brown there were things the Seers' Table drew, and the tower gave them to the whole town.

So now the water came in from the river into a long shallow tank of brick, and stood there a day, and the brown went to the bottom; and then it went over a weir into a bed of sand and gravel the size of a barn floor, a yard deep, fine sand on top and gravel under, and down through the sand slowly, the way rain goes down through a field, and came out clear at the bottom into a culvert to the pump. Slow sand filters, Emeric told the table. On Earth a city had put its whole water through beds like that for a hundred years and the summer flux had gone out of it like a tide. The top of the sand grew a skin, in a week, brown and slimy, and the men who tended it were horrified and wanted to scrape it off; and Emeric told them not to, because the skin was the filter, and Pell came down with the microscope and drew it, and the skin was full of small things eating the other small things, which pleased the Seers' Table more than anything they had drawn all year.

The two towers and the tanks and the filter bed were piped together by three different gangs. Bram's men did the towers. The levy did the tanks. A crew of the dock quarter's best men, who had done the hospital's mains, did the pipes between. And on the morning they were joined, it turned out that the dock quarter's pipe threads did not go into Bram's fittings, and Bram's flanges had their bolt holes in a different place from the levy's, and for two days nobody in the lower ward had any water at all.

The forge did it on the twenty-second, at night, in the cellar, under the castle's renovation.

That had been the forge's cover for a year: the castle renovation. Since the summer the cellar was dug out, anybody in Kaldo who asked what Bram's men were doing in the keep's undercroft at all hours had been told that the prince was having the castle's water and drains renewed, which the castle certainly needed, and nobody had asked again. Nothing had been renewed. Now it would be. Master gauges for pipe threads, a separate thread from the Kaldo thread, tapered, so that a pipe screwed tighter the further in it went and sealed itself, one master for each size of pipe Kaldo made. And a flange template, a disc of steel with the bolt holes drilled in it where every flange in four baronies would have its bolt holes from then on, for each size. They went into the tally room under the glass with the others.

The first job done to them was the castle.

Bram's men pulled out every pipe in the keep, which was not many, and put in new ones, cut to the masters and tapped to the masters and flanged to the template, from the cistern on the roof to the kitchen and the hospital's old wing and the new bath on the second floor that Hale had been asking for since the first winter. It took three weeks. For three weeks the keep was full of men with pipes, banging, and dust, and Hale's face, and at the end of it the keep had hot water on the second floor out of a tap for the first time since it was built.

And the cover had become true. If anybody in Kaldo ever asked again what Bram's men had been doing in the keep's undercroft all year, they would be told the castle's water and drains had been renewed, and taken up the stair to turn the tap.



He found Maren at the cold store at the end of the month, on the last evening of September, standing in the open door with her arms folded, looking in.

The frost was on the inside of the churn lid again. The butter was hard. There was a row of tubs on the top shelf now with cloths over them, and when he came up beside her she did not look round.

"My grandmother," said Maren, "would have given her eyes for this." She looked at the stone on its shelf, red-orange and faintly warm, pulling. "She made it once a year. For a lord who never came down to the kitchen to say it was good." She was quiet. "There were children in that square on Thursday who'd never in their lives had a thing just because it was nice. Not bread. Not a pie for a feast day. A thing you eat because it's cold and sweet and it's Thursday."

She unfolded her arms and went in and took a tub down off the top shelf and put it in his hands, with a spoon, and stood and watched him eat it in the doorway of the cold store with the last of the light going over the north wall, until he had finished, and then took the empty tub back.

"Beet," she said. "Next year." And went back up the hill to her kitchen.
