Chapter 164 - Downstream

"That's a liver," said Tamsin, "and that's a liver," and put her finger on the plate, and then on the thing on the table, and looked up at Imani with her face gone pale and stubborn. "They're not the same."

"They're the same organ."

"They're not the same colour."

"The plate hasn't got a colour," said Imani. "That's the trouble with the plate."

It was the second week of the late autumn and the long ward's back room was cold, because the fire in it was for boiling and not for warmth, and the twelve of them stood round the table in their aprons with their hands red from the washing. On the table under the lamp there was a pig, opened, because Imani could get a pig from the butcher on the island for four Silver and could not get anything else. Beside the pig, pinned flat on a board, there was a plate from the printer: a drawing of the inside of a man, the organs in their places, each one outlined in black and named in small letters beside it in Orrin's hand as the stone had printed it. The prince had drawn it for her in the spring from what she told him and what he knew. It was very good. It was the best drawing of the inside of a man that had ever existed in Kaldo, or, as far as Imani knew, anywhere south of the capital.

It was grey.

Everything in it was the same grey, or white, or black. The liver was a shape outlined in black with a name beside it. The spleen was a shape outlined in black with a name beside it. The bowel was a coil of black lines. And the thing on the table in front of Tamsin was a pig's liver, dark red, almost purple, glossy, with a pale mottled patch on one lobe where the pig had had something wrong with it, and Tamsin could not tell from the plate whether the pale patch was the liver or the illness, because the plate had no pale and no dark. It had lines.

"When you open a man," said Imani, "the first thing you'll see is colour. Before shape. Before anything. Healthy is one colour and dying is another and you've got about as long as it takes to breathe to tell which, and you tell it by colour, and I can't teach you colour off a sheet of grey paper."

"Then how did you learn it?" said Orm, at the end of the table.

"By opening a great many people," said Imani, "most of whom were dead, and one or two of whom I killed learning." She did not look at him. She put her knife on the pale patch on the pig's liver. "This is sick. This is well. Look at the edge between them. Look at it until you'd know it with your eyes shut. That's the lesson."

They looked.

There were twelve of them. They had come to her in the spring, the first of them, because the prince had said at the table that Kaldo would have physicians of its own, trained, and that they would not be trained the way she had been trained, by being lucky and stubborn and in the right place when a man was dying. They would be trained from books.

She had not believed him about the books. She believed him now, a little. There were the plates, eleven of them off the printer, the bones and the muscles and the organs and the vessels, all in black and grey. There were her own notes, which the prince had had printed off her hand, sixty pages on wounds and fevers and water and the well standard and the dose. There was the rule, printed and pinned up in the washing room: boil the water, wash the hands, burn the rags. And there was the camp.

The camp was their training ground. She had not chosen it for that. It had chosen itself, because it was where the sick were. Five thousand people on the thorn ground at the north edge of the flat and along the foundry bank, men from four baronies and their families, living in tents and lean-tos and turf huts with a pump for every two hundred and a drain the prince had cut before he let anybody pitch, and sickness came to them the way it came to any camp. Cuts from the quarry. Crushed hands from the rail spur. A man with his leg under a stone. Fevers. Children with coughs. Women with babies coming.

She took the twelve through it every morning in two sixes. They went with their bags and their boiled water and their book, and they saw everybody who would be seen, and they wrote every one of them down in the ward register: the name, the tent, the complaint, what was done. At the end of every day they brought the register back to the long ward and Tamsin, who had the neatest hand and the worst temper, entered it fair.

They sewed. That was what they did best. She had taught them to sew the way she had been taught once, in forty minutes, in a room that was not a room, with the prince's voice in her ear and her own hands moving. She could not teach them that way. She taught them the long way, on pigskin, on the pig, on each other's aprons, for weeks, until Orm could close a quarry cut on a big man's forearm so neatly that the big man looked at it afterward and said Orm had missed his trade and should have been a tailor.

Tamsin found it in the register.

She found it on a wet evening in the third week of the late autumn, entering the day's pages, and she did not say anything at first. She sat at the end of the washing room table with the register open and the day's loose sheets beside it, and she turned back a page, and back, and back again, to the start of the autumn, and she took a sheet of scrap and started writing numbers on it. Imani, washing at the copper, saw her do it and did not interrupt. When a student started writing numbers on scrap without being asked, you let her finish.

Tamsin finished. She brought the scrap over to the copper and held it out.

"The lower water," she said.

Imani dried her hands and took it.

It was a list of weeks, down the left, from the first of the autumn to the week just gone. And beside each week, a number. It was the number of people from the lower-water hamlets who had come to the ward or been seen in their houses by the students on the Thursday round. Not the camp. The lower water: the three hamlets on the marsh edge below the flat where the river came out to the salt, the eel traps and the lighter moorings and the fishermen's cottages and the new quay where the sulphur came in. The hamlets Imani had first walked in the fifth year with Ferra's second census, and found three hundred and seventy-six people nobody had counted, and dug new wells for, to the standard, in the gravel above the marsh.

The numbers were small at the top. Two. One. Three. Two.

Then they were not small. Seven. Eleven. Nine. Sixteen. Twenty-three.

"It's the flux," said Tamsin. "Nearly all of it. The running flux, and the fever with it. I've marked which." She pointed. "It's not in the camp. The camp's got the ordinary, a case a week, a case every two. It's the lower water." She looked at Imani. "And three children last week. Dead. Orm saw two of them on the Thursday round. He wrote them. I didn't add it up till now."

Imani looked at the scrap for a long time.

She knew what it was. She had known it on the second line, before she had read to the end. She had seen it in the first year on the island, in the north well lanes, before the prince came and before they boiled the water. She had seen it in the capital at the bottom of the slope of the lower city every summer of her girlhood. It was the thing the prince had fought first when he came to Kaldo, before the soap, before the foundry, before anything: water that had something in it that came out of people and went into people and killed them, and the only thing that stopped it was keeping the one away from the other.

She knew what it was. She did not know why it was there.

"Well done," she said to Tamsin, and Tamsin went red and then angry at herself for going red.

She went down to the lower water the next morning with Orm and two of the others, in the rain.

She walked the three hamlets. She went into the houses where the sick were and saw them. She saw a woman lying on a pallet by a cold hearth with her eyes gone into her head, and her husband sitting beside her with a cup of water, trying to make her drink, and the water in the cup was from the well. She saw two children in one bed. She saw the eel-trap woman, Tollin's mother, whose son was in the sailors' ground above Rowena's harbour, standing in her doorway with her arms folded, well, looking at Imani with a face that said she had buried enough this year.

She walked the wells. There were four of them, on the gravel shelf above the marsh, where the ground was highest and driest in the hamlets, dug six years ago to the standard: lined with stone, capped, with a lip and a lid and a pump, sixty paces from any privy, uphill of every privy. She had sited every one of them herself. She had walked the ground and dug the test pits and chosen where they went. They were good wells.

She drew water from each and looked at it and smelled it and put it in a clean bottle to take back. It looked clean. It smelled of nothing.

And she stood on the gravel shelf in the rain with the bottle in her hand and looked north, uphill, up the long gentle slope of the flat toward the camp, a mile off, the smoke going up from five thousand cook fires into the wet grey air. And she knew. And she could not see it.

She needed it on one sheet.

That was the whole of the problem, and it sat on her table in the washing room that night in three pieces. There was the Tarrants' survey of the flat, which Ferra had lent her from the South box: sheet after sheet of heights and falls and chain lines, the whole of the flat measured to a hand. There was the prince's drawing of the camp's drains, the ones he had cut in the summer before he let anybody pitch a tent: a main trench down the east side of the thorn ground, and branches off it, and a pit at the bottom, and from the pit an overflow ditch going away down the flat, to the marsh, temporary, until the beds, in his hand, in pencil. And there were her own old sheets from the fifth year, the lower-water wells, sited and drawn, with the gravel shelf and the marsh and the hamlets.

Three sheets. Three different men's measures, three different scales, drawn at three different times, for three different reasons. Nobody had ever laid them on top of one another. Nobody had ever had a reason to.

She tried. She sat at the table with the lamp and a straightedge and tried to trace the camp's overflow ditch off the prince's sheet onto the Tarrants', and got it wrong, because his was drawn by eye and theirs by chain, and tried again. She tried to find her wells on the Tarrants' survey and could not, because the Tarrants had stopped at the edge of the flat where the prince told them to, and the gravel shelf was below it. She had three pieces of paper and the answer was in the gap between them.

She put her hand on her band.

She had not meant to. She had not done it alone before. She had been in the prince's room above Kaldo with the others, at the long table in the air, every week since the seating. She had never once tried to go in without him. She did not believe she could. Bram could; Bram had done it out of a boiler, and she had been glad for him and had thought, privately, that it was the kind of thing that happened to Bram.

She did not try to see the room. She did not think about the room at all. She thought about the flat.

She thought about it the way she had thought about the wound in the man's thigh in the tent after the battle, years ago, with the prince's voice in her ear: not as a picture, as a thing she had to get her hands into. The slope of the flat, from the ridge down to the marsh. She had walked it that morning. She knew how it fell under her feet. She had read the Tarrants' sheets so many times in the last hour that she knew the heights by heart, the long gentle fall from north to south, a hand in every twenty paces, steeper at the marsh edge. She laid the heights down one by one in her mind, the way you lay a dressing, and the ground came up under them, grey, wet, as it had been that morning.

She put in the camp. The thorn ground, the tents. The prince's main drain down the east side. The branches. The pit.

She put in the overflow ditch from the pit, going south, to the marsh, temporary.

She put in her wells. The gravel shelf, the four of them, where she had sited them, sixty paces uphill of every privy in the hamlets.

And she was standing on the flat.

She was standing on it in the rain, on grey ground, with the camp's smoke going up behind her and the marsh ahead, and it was not a picture. She could feel the ground under her feet. She could feel the rain. She could see the overflow ditch going away from her down the slope in a straight line, and she walked along it, and she walked a long way, and nothing hurried her.

She knew where she was. She did not stop to think about it. She had needed a place where three sheets were one sheet, and she had made it out of what she knew, and it had opened round her because she needed it to. The way the sewing had come into her hands in forty minutes in a tent, when there was a man bleeding on the table and she had had no time to learn it any slower.

It was hers. There was a door behind her somewhere. She did not look for it.

She walked the ditch.

It went down the flat from the camp's pit, south, following the fall of the ground, the way water goes, straight, because the prince had cut it straight. It went down to the edge of the flat where the Tarrants' survey stopped. And it went on. It went over the edge of the flat and down the steeper ground to the marsh, and at the marsh it did not stop, because a ditch does not stop, it only spreads, and it spread into the reed beds at the top of the marsh in a long fan of grey water.

At the top of the marsh.

Above the gravel shelf.

She stood at the end of the ditch, at the top of the fan, and turned round, and looked down the slope, and there were her wells. All four of them. On the gravel shelf, below her, sixty paces uphill of every privy in the hamlets, and a quarter of a mile downhill of five thousand people's waste, spreading into the gravel from the top of the marsh in the wet, and going down through the gravel the way water goes down through gravel. Into her wells.

She had sited them uphill of every privy in the hamlets. Nobody had ever put five thousand people uphill of the hamlets. There had been nothing uphill of the hamlets, when she sited them, but thorn and sheep.

The outfall sat above the lower water.

She came out at the washing room table with the lamp burned down an inch and her hand on her band and Orm standing in the doorway staring at her.

"You were gone," he said. "I said your name. You didn't."

"How long?"

"I don't know. I didn't count." He looked frightened. "Should I have fetched somebody?"

"No." She took her hand off the band. It was shaking, a little, which she noticed, and noted, and put away. "Go to bed, Orm."

He went.

She sat at the table and drew it. On one sheet, fresh, with the straightedge, off what she had walked. The flat and its fall. The camp. The pit. The ditch, straight down to the marsh. The fan at the top of the marsh. The gravel shelf. The four wells. She drew it plainly, in ink, the way she would have drawn a wound for Tamsin, and when it was done she looked at it, and it was the simplest drawing she had ever made, and anybody in Kaldo who looked at it would understand it in the time it took to look.

Then she wrote beside it the numbers from Tamsin's scrap. The weeks and the sick. And under them, the dead: three children last week, and, when she had been back through Orm's round sheets and the parish notes Doran had sent up at her asking, eleven in all since the start of the autumn. Seven of them children.

She wanted to open one of them.

She asked Doran the next day. She asked him in the vestry, with his book open on the lectern between them at the page of the dead, where the eleven were, and she asked him plainly, the way she asked for anything. The eel-trap man's youngest had died in the night, a boy of six, and the family had no money for the burying and had asked the parish, and the parish would bury him. Imani asked for the boy first. For one day. To open, on the table in the back room, with the twelve round it, and show them the bowel of a child who had drunk from a well that had the flux in it, so that every one of them would know what the flux did to the inside of a body and never forget it.

Doran heard her out. He did not interrupt.

"I can't bury him," he said, "if you open him."

"You buried the dead from the war. Some of them were opened. By swords."

"By swords," said Doran. "By the war. Not on a table, by a knife, with twelve students round him, for a lesson." He did not raise his voice. He never did. "The Church has never let an opened body into consecrated ground, Imani. Not in this parish, not in the capital. A body is laid in the ground whole to wait for the Light. You know that. You were raised to it."

"The boy won't know."

"His mother will."

She did not have an answer to that. She stood in the vestry with the parish book between them and did not have an answer, and Doran looked at her with his old tired kind face, and did not have one either.

"Ask her," he said. "If she says yes, I'll not bury him in the churchyard. I'll bury him outside the wall, by the lake, where we put the ones we don't know, and I'll say the words. That's all I can do." He closed the book. "I don't think she'll say yes."

She did not ask. She went down to the lower water and stood outside the eel-trap man's cottage in the rain and looked at the door and thought about the woman inside who had buried a son in the sailors' ground in the summer and was burying another now, and she did not knock.

She went back to the long ward and took the twelve through the pig again, and the grey plate, and drew them the bowel of a child from memory on a slate, and told them what colour it would be. They learned less than they should have. She wrote that down too, in the register, under the day's date. Asked for the eel-trap boy to open. Not asked of the family. Taught from a drawing. They learned less than they should.

It rained all that week.

It rained on the camp and on the flat and on the lower water and on the island, and on the long ward. And on the long ward's roof, which was old slate on old laths over a span that had been built to keep grain dry and had never quite managed it, and on the four places along the ridge and the valleys where the rain came in.

It came in on the fourth night in the end room. It came in in the corner by the south window, where Senna slept, who did not know the year and asked every night whether the lamp would be put out soon. It came through the laths in a slow bright line and dripped on the blanket over Senna's feet, and Senna did not wake, and the girl on the night watch found it at the second bell and moved Senna's bed three feet to the left and put down a pail.

Imani found the pail in the morning.

She stood in the end room and looked at it for a while, three-quarters full, and at the wet stain on the floor where the bed had been, and up at the dark patch on the laths, and at Senna asleep in her new place three feet to the left with her face to the wall. She did not say anything. She emptied the pail herself, in the yard, and brought it back and put it down under the drip again, and went to wash.

She took the sheet to the long-room table that evening.

They were all there. It was the last table before the winter. The prince at the head, with the survey sheets for the first quarter's mains in front of him, and Ferra with her book, and Ward, and Bram, and Saban, and Armand in his chair at the end with his hands folded. She came up the stair with her canvas roll under her arm and her coat still wet from the lower water, and she did not take her coat off, and she did not go to her place at the table.

She went to the head of it.

She unrolled the canvas and took out the sheet and laid it on the table in front of the prince, on top of his mains, and weighted its corners with his own inkwell and a book. The flat. The camp. The pit. The ditch, straight down to the marsh. The fan. The gravel shelf. The four wells. Tamsin's numbers in the margin, the weeks and the sick, and under them the dead. Eleven. Seven children.

She did not say anything. She had nothing to add to the sheet.

The table was silent. Ferra had stopped writing. Saban had stopped looking at whatever Saban had been looking at. Bram had leaned forward to see the drawing upside down, and Imani watched him read the ditch and the fan and the wells and understand it in about the time it took to breathe, the way anybody would, and sit back.

The prince looked at the sheet.

She watched him look. She watched him find the camp's pit, and the ditch, which he had cut. She watched him read the pencil note she had copied off his own drawing in her own hand beside it: to the marsh, temporary, until the beds. She watched him follow it down to the fan at the top of the marsh, and see the gravel shelf below it, and the wells.

She watched him see what it was.

He did not look up at her. He did not say anything either. He sat with his hands flat on the table on either side of the sheet, not on it, and looked at the numbers in the margin, and she saw his face go still in a way she had seen once before, a long time ago, in the tent after the battle, when a man he had tried to save had died under his hands.

There was a chair at her place. Ferra had moved her own book an inch to make room beside it. Imani did not go to it. She stood at the head of the table beside him, in her wet coat, with her hands at her sides, and she did not sit down.
