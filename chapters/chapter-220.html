Chapter 220 - White Flag

Ward took the needle first.

Nobody had asked him to. Corin's pairs had come up from the ship's clearing on the ninth of February in a borrowed cart with their crocks packed in sawdust and snow, and set up a table in the officers' hut at the stove end, and Corin had looked down the long room at three hundred men of the levy in their coats and their grey faces and their winter beards, all of them watching him, and had gone visibly a little smaller inside his own coat; and Ward had come in out of the cold, and looked at the table, and taken off his gauntlet and his coat and rolled up his left sleeve, and sat down on the stool in front of the boy without a word.

"It's quick, Captain," said Corin.

"Then be quick."

Corin dipped the split wire and pressed it into the captain's upper arm, five times, fast, the way Rue had taught the pairs on a willow stick, and the girl beside him with the book wrote the day and the arm and the number of the crock it had come from. Ward looked at the place. A bead of something clear stood on it and did not run.

"That is all?"

"That's all. It'll come up in a blister in a week. Don't scratch it."

Ward rolled his sleeve down over it and stood up and put his coat on and went out, and the hut breathed out behind him, and the first man of the third company got up off the bench and came and sat on the stool.

It was Osric. He had his sleeve up already and his face set like a man about to have a tooth pulled.

"How deep does it go?"

"It doesn't go anywhere, Sergeant," said Corin. "It's a scratch."

"That's what the farrier said to the horse." Osric looked at the split wire in the boy's fingers, and then away at the stove, and then back, as if he could not stop himself. "Go on, then. Don't tell me when."

Corin did not tell him when. It was over before Osric had finished deciding not to watch, and he looked down at his arm with real indignation, as though something had been taken from him that he had paid for.

"That was it?"

"That was it."

"I've had worse off a bramble." He got up. He looked down at the girl with the book, who was about fourteen and writing his name in a hand very like Ferra's, and he read it upside down as she wrote it, Emeric saw, his lips moving very slightly; and then he caught Emeric watching from the door and became very interested in his own sleeve.

"Next," said Corin, who was getting his voice back. "Next. Sit there. Sleeve up."

They did three hundred and twelve that day, levy and garrison and cooks, and Imani did none of it. She stood at the back of the hut with her arms folded and watched every arm, and twice she came forward and took a split wire off a boy who was dipping it too shallow, and showed him, and gave it back.

"They don't need me," she said to Emeric afterward, on the bank, with the snow going grey along the top of it in the first thaw. "That's what I wanted to see."

"You came three hundred and fifty miles to watch them not need you?"

"I came to see if they'd do it right when I was standing behind them," said Imani, "because next week I won't be." She looked north up the meadow, where the stream had broken its ice and was running brown between white banks. "Where's the barn?"



The barn stood behind the earthwork where it had stood since November, a long stone thing with a slate roof and a door at each end, and Ward had kept a guard on both doors all winter and a fire in the middle on the threshing floor, and nobody but the guard and Imani's surgeon had gone in.

The thirty-one had come through the speckled winter whole. Imani had seen to that in the first week of the camp's closing: their own water, their own cook, their own privy pit outside the north door, and nobody from the huts within the stakes. None of them had taken it. They had played dice and written letters home that Ward read and sent, and the man who kept the guard's roll said they had complained more about the beer than the levy had about anything in three months. They sat on their benches along the walls now and watched the pair set up a table by the fire. The north had had the speckled death since before Christmas, and they had had letters.

The first man up was Lord Aurel Sallow.

He came down the barn from his corner with his limp, which was the ankle, and sat on the stool in front of Corin and put out his arm without being asked, and said nothing at all while it was done. Then he looked at the bead on his skin, and at the boy, and at Imani standing behind the boy, and at Emeric in the doorway, and gave Emeric a nod. It was a small nod, the depth of a man acknowledging a debt he had not yet worked out how to pay, and he went back to his corner.

The second was a slim young man of twenty with fair hair grown long over the winter and a face that had not slept well in a barn. He sat down on the stool and held out his arm and looked past Corin's shoulder at the door, at Emeric, steadily, the whole time it was being done; and when it was done he did not get up.

"I said I'd remember," said Bastian Sallow.

"I hoped you would."

"I remember all of it." He pulled his sleeve down. "My sister's in your keep. My father's in your barn. You've got a boy scratching my arm with a bit of wire." He stood. "I'll remember that as well."

"Remember the wire," said Imani, before Emeric could say anything. She did not look up from Corin's book. "It's the only part of this that'll keep you alive. Next."

Bastian looked at her. Then he went back to his father's corner and sat down beside him, not close.

The big grey-haired knight came last. He had kept his arm in the belt sling into December and out of it since, and he had kept, the guard said, to the end of the barn by the south door, where he could see the stream ground through the gap under it. He held out his good arm and had it done, and then looked up at Emeric from the stool.

"Highness."

"Sir Tancred."

The knight's eyebrows went up a little. He had not given his name to anybody on the night of the field.

"Declan wrote it down," said Emeric. "When the heralds came for the count."

"Did he." Tancred considered that. Then, in exactly the same voice: "My lord's grandchildren. Two of them. Before Christmas. Your Highness will have heard."

"I heard."

"The letter came at the turn of the year. His lady wrote it." He looked at the bead of matter on his arm. "Mine have it in the village now. My wife writes the doors are chalked down half the street." He was quiet. "The boy's still in your church at Wayford."

"In the vault. In lead. Aster had it made."

"I know. I asked the guard." Tancred stood up slowly, as a big man stands who has been sitting on a bench for three months. "I said I'd take him home, when the ransom was paid. I've been counting the days to the court since the month came in. Nobody's named one."

"No," said Emeric. "Nobody has."



Saban came up from the bridge on the eleventh with a man in a cart.

He was a soft-faced man of fifty in a good brown coat gone bad at the cuffs, with a dealer's satchel he held in his lap with both hands. He got down at the earthwork and looked at the stream ground beyond the bank, with the snow going off it in patches and the dark shapes showing through, and then at his own boots, and did not look up again until Saban put him in the officers' hut by the stove with a cup of something hot and shut the door on him.

"His name's Hartwell," said Saban. "He deals in wool on the upper river. Mostly honest. Sold four hundred blankets at Wayford market in the last week of November at four Copper apiece, and the other two hundred in the two market towns north of Lisle on his way home, at the same." He took off his gloves. "He's frightened of three things, in this order: His Grace, me, and you. He's right about the order."

"Where did you find him?"

"In his own kitchen. Sitting up with his wife, who's had it and lived. Not from his blankets. From the market." Saban sat down at the table in the hut's cold end, where Ward and Rylan already were, and took a folded sheet from inside his coat and laid it down on the boards and did not unfold it. "He didn't run. He'd have liked to. He didn't know where to."

"And that?"

"That," said Saban, "is the ledger."

He unfolded it.

It was a copy, in a clerk's hand Emeric did not know, very small and very careful, of two facing pages out of a book, ruled in red, with the dates down the left margin in the northern way, the day before the month. The clerk had copied even the blots. Down the right-hand side, in Saban's own round trading hand, someone had added a few figures in pencil, and Emeric did not have to ask whose.

"There's a clerk in His Grace's quartermaster's office at the wool town," said Saban, "who is paid a Silver a month to write letters for a man who can't, and who was paid somewhat more than a Silver to sit up one night in December with a lamp and this book. The under-quartermaster's book. One man's, in one town, who buys bread and boots and blankets for a host and writes down what he pays, because if he didn't he'd be hanged." He put his finger on the first page. "Read it."

Emeric read it.

The fourteenth of October. To the parish of the upper town, for the bedding and wearing linen of the house of the dyer, deceased, by the parish's sale for the burials: three Gold. The fourteenth of October, the same day, under it. From store, army blankets, grey, striped, worn, four hundred and fifty, struck off the store's count as surplus to the host. And under that, the next day. To the baling shed, the above two lots, to be baled together in the usual way. And under that, the sixteenth. To Hartwell, wool, of the upper river, on account, for carriage south and sale at Wayford market and on the road: twenty Gold.

Under it all, a line ruled in red, and nothing more about blankets on either page.

Emeric read it twice. Then he looked at the pencil figures down the margin in Saban's hand. Four hundred and fifty blankets from the store, and a hundred and fifty of Hartwell's own that he had put in to make the wagon worth driving: six hundred, sold at four Copper. Twenty-four Gold the wagon had taken, in all, in three markets. Twenty Gold paid to the man before he left, to carry it.

"A quartermaster who pays twenty Gold to have twenty-four Gold of blankets sold for him," said Saban, "isn't buying blankets. He's buying a road." He took his finger away. "And he wrote it down. That's the part I like least. He wrote it down as if it were bread."

"Baled together," said Ward.

"In the usual way." Saban looked at him. "A dead house's sheets wouldn't fill a cart, Captain. Six hundred blankets fill a wagon. You put the one in among the other, a sheet or a shift in every bale, and a man who sells them at four Copper sells you the sheet with every one, and doesn't know it." He nodded at the door, at the hut's other end, where Hartwell sat with his cup. "He says he didn't know it. I believe him about half. He knew the price was wrong. A man in his trade knows when he's being paid to go somewhere and not to sell something. He didn't ask what was in the bales because he didn't want to know." He was quiet. "So he sold it to his own people as well, on the road home, at the same price, and his own wife had it by Christmas. That's what not wanting to know costs, in wool."

"He sent it," said Rylan.

It was what he had said on the ninth of December, in this hut, by this stove. He said it the same way. He had not joked once all winter about the blankets.

"Somebody did," said Saban, "and there it is in a book, by the day, with the price." He laid his hand flat beside the copy. "And now look at what isn't there."

They looked.

"His Grace's name," said Saban. "His order. His seal. A letter from him to anybody saying do it. There's a dyer's sheets bought by the under-quartermaster of one town's store, and baled in the usual way with surplus, and sent south with a dealer. That's what this page proves. It proves a purchase and a sale." He took his hand away. "A good advocate would say the man was clearing his store and was careless with the parish's linen. A better one would say a clerk was paid by Kaldo to write a false page. And there'd be no answering him, because one was." He looked at Emeric. "I told you that you wouldn't like it either way."

Emeric did not say anything for a while.

He thought of the court in the capital, which had not named a day; and of the chancery where the duchy's patent had lain since the grant, and where the crown's mediation had been offered with no date in it, and where everything Kaldo had sent for three years had gone in at the top and come out at the bottom slower and smaller, if at all. Corbin's court. Corbin's chancery. And the duke whose under-quartermaster had written this page was Corbin's mother's brother.

"If I take it to court," he said.

"They bury it," said Saban. "And to bury it properly they'll want to know where it came from. They'll find the clerk. Then whoever paid the clerk, then my man, then everybody my man drinks with." He spread his hands, very slightly. "I've just had the winter I've had. I'm not saying I wouldn't. I'm telling you the price."

"And if I don't use it at all?"

"Then you've a page in your strongbox and His Grace has a second host in the spring," said Ward. "The houses went home to bury their children. They will come back out when the burying is done, if he calls them and nobody gives them a reason not to." He did not look at the page. "He is calling them now. A man for every son. Two for every knight."

Emeric looked down at the copy for a breath longer. Then he folded it up again along Saban's creases.

"Not the court," he said. "His cousin."

Saban did not say anything. Rylan sat up a little.

"Harlow's father," said Emeric. "His house took it from these blankets. His grandchildren. His nurse, his groom. He sent his men home without leave before Christmas, and every house that went with him went because he went. He's the one man in the north whose answer to the spring call matters." He put the folded copy on the table between them. "And he's the one man in the north this page isn't evidence to. It's news. He doesn't need a court to tell him what his cousin's under-quartermaster bought. He'll know the man. He'll know the town. He'll know the baling shed."

"And who carries it?" said Ward. "Kaldo's herald? He will burn it in front of the man and say it is a forgery."

"Not Kaldo's herald," said Emeric. "His own knight."



Tancred heard it standing, in the snow outside the barn's south door, with the guard twenty yards off at the stakes and the stream going past behind him loud with thaw.

He did not interrupt. He listened to all of it, to the parole, and the cart, and the white flag, and the vaccinators who would go north with him under it; and to what was folded in the leather case Emeric held out to him, and what it said, and what it did not say. When Emeric had finished he did not take the case.

"You'd let me go on my word," he said.

"Knights have been let go on their word to raise their ransom since there were knights. You'll come back for the court or send it. I don't think you'd do otherwise."

"No." Tancred looked at the case. "And that. You want me to carry that to my lord."

"I want your lord to have it from a man he trusts. He won't trust it from me."

"He'd be right not to." Tancred said it without heat, as a fact about the weather. He looked north, up the meadow, toward the far hill foot where the host had stood. "His Grace is my lord's cousin. I've served my lord twenty-two years and I've sat at His Grace's table more times than I can count, and drunk his wine, and heard him talk." He was quiet. "I'll not lie to you, Highness. I'd sooner carry a snake."

"I know."

"If it's false..."

"Then your lord will know it's false. He knows the man who wrote it. You told me yourself he knows the wool town." Emeric held the case out a little further. "I'm not asking you to believe it. I'm asking you to put it in his hand with Harlow and let him read it himself."

Tancred looked at him a long breath more. Then he put out his good hand and took the case, and held it as though it were heavier than it was.

"The boy first," he said. "I'll give him the boy first. Then this." He put it inside his coat. "He'll want to bury his son before he reads anything."



Ward's objection came that night, in writing, on two sheets of the school's paper in his small square hand. He brought it to the officers' hut himself and put it on the table in front of Emeric and stood there while he read it, which was Ward's way of saying that he expected an answer and not a receipt.

It was in three points. That His Highness should not himself go under the flag into a country at war with him, where every man would know his face by its eyes and a single crossbow in a single window would end the house of Kaldo in an afternoon. That the flag protected a herald and a healer by the custom of war, and that no custom anywhere protected a prince who had killed a hundred and eighty-eight knights, and that the north knew the number. That if His Highness went regardless he should go no further than the first town, and turn back at first light the next day, and that the captain of his household asked that this be kept.

Emeric read it through twice and took up the pen, and wrote under it, as Ferra had taught them all to write under a thing they did not strike through: Agreed, the third point. Not the first two. E.

Ward read that. He said nothing for a breath.

"Why go at all?"

"Because I'm asking a man whose son I killed to break with his own blood on the strength of a page I can't prove," said Emeric. "I can't ask that from three hundred and fifty miles away and send a boy with a wire to do the hard part." He gave the sheets back. "And because if I sit behind an earthwork while sixteen-year-olds walk into Aldane's towns with a flag on a stick, I've no business telling anybody in the north what kind of man I am."

Ward folded the sheets.

"The first town," he said. "First light the next day. I will be at your shoulder, and I will choose the road, and the inn, and the window you stand in."

"Yes."

Ward put the sheets in his coat. "It goes to the Chancellor in the morning. By the long wire."

It went. Ferra's answer came back up the long wire at noon the next day, in the camp operator's pencil, three lines, which Ward read and then gave to Emeric without comment.

Received and entered, the Objections folder, second sheet. The Holder's line entered under it. Third point kept. Come back at first light, as written, or I'll know why. F.



His Grace of Aldane let them in.

The answer came down the north road on the thirteenth with a man in Aldane's brown under a white flag of his own, who rode up to the stakes at the head of the stream ground and handed down a sealed paper without getting off his horse and rode away again without waiting to be offered anything. It was not from Aldane. It was from Aldane's steward, in a clerk's hand, and it said, in nine lines, that the vaccinators of the house of Kaldo might pass by the post road through the lands of His Grace to the towns named in the schedule below, with a cart and their stores and an escort not exceeding six unarmed men, under the flag, for forty days; and that His Grace's officers would afford them lodging and nothing else; and that any man of the party found off the post road would be held as a spy.

There was no thanks in it anywhere. Imani read it twice, looking for some.

"He'd sooner watch them die," she said.

"No," said Saban, who had read it before her. "If he'd sooner watch them die he'd have said no. He'd sooner they died than said thank you. That's a different thing." He gave her the paper. "And cheaper."



They went north on the fourteenth under the flag, ten of them and a coffin.

The coffin went first, on a cart of Aster's with a black cloth over it and a pair of greys between the shafts, and Tancred rode beside it in his own harness, which Ward had given back to him out of the barn's store, without his sword. Behind it the vaccinators' cart, with the crocks in their sawdust and Corin and his writer and one more pair on top of the crates in blankets, and Imani on the box with the reins. Then Emeric and Ward, in plain coats with no plate under them, because six unarmed men meant six unarmed men; and two of the garrison, one of them on the coffin cart's box; and Hartwell's written account in a case on Ward's saddle, signed and witnessed in Sethe's counting room, with Hartwell himself left behind in Saban's keeping at Wayford, frightened and fed.

The white flag was a sheet on a pole. Rylan had wanted to paint something on it. Ward had said no.

They went by the post road north, two days of it, through the last of Lisle's land and the country beyond, flat and wet and bare, with the hedges black and the fields grey and the farms shut up. Nobody stopped them. At the posting houses the ostlers looked at the coffin and then at the flag and then at Emeric's face and his eyes, and went and got the horses without being asked, and did not take the coin when Ward held it out, and Ward put it on the mounting block and left it. At one of them a woman came out of the kitchen door with a child on her hip and watched the vaccinators' cart go by until it was round the bend, and did not say anything.

On the second evening they crossed out of Lisle's land at a stone on the verge cut with a duke's arms, worn nearly smooth; and Tancred, beside the coffin, put his hand on the cart's side as they passed it and kept it there for a hundred yards.



The first of the two market towns stood on a low rise above a river crossing, a church and a market hall and two streets of timber houses and a wall that had been a wall once and was now mostly gardens, and it smelled of smoke and lime before they could see it.

The doors were chalked. Emeric saw that from the bridge: a white cross on the door of every house where the speckled death had been, rough, done with a lump of lime by somebody going down the street with a bucket, as it had been done in Kaldo's lower ward in the plague years before he came. Half the doors on the first street had them. More than half on the second. There was a long new mound in the churchyard behind the church with no stones on it yet, and a man digging at the end of it in the half dark.

They had been expected. Aldane's officer met them at the market hall, a thin grey bailiff with a chain of office and the schedule in his hand, and took them through the town to the church without saying more than he had to, which was not much; and the priest was waiting on the step with a lantern, an old man with his own face pitted from the speckled year as Sela's was, and when he saw the cart with the crocks he put the lantern down on the step and went down to it before anybody had spoken and put both his hands flat on the crates, and stood there with his head down.

Imani got down off the box.

"Father," she said. "How many have you had?"

"Ninety-one," said the priest, without lifting his head. "Since the first of January. Thirty-four buried." He straightened up. "I've three in the church tonight. I've nowhere else that's warm."

"Then we'll start with whoever's nursing them," said Imani. "Do you have a woman who's had it? A midwife? Anybody who can hold a child still?" She was already pulling her bag down. "Corin. The table in the porch, not inside. Open air. Get the lamps lit."

They did a hundred and forty that night in the porch of the church by lamplight, with the line going down the steps and across the churchyard past the long mound, and the bailiff standing at the gate with his chain and his schedule watching it, and saying nothing, and not going home.



Emeric found Imani at the well at nine.

It stood at the bottom of the lower street, where the street ran down toward the river crossing: a stone ring with a winch and a bucket and a worn step, and a channel cut in the cobbles beside it a foot wide that took everything the lower street threw out of its doors and carried it, slowly, past the well and on down to the river. Where the channel turned past the well's step it had worn a hollow, and the hollow was full, and there was ice on it, dirty, and under the ice something brown that moved.

Imani stood looking at it with her lantern. She had her sleeves still rolled and her hands red from the vinegar and her coat open over her ward dress in the cold, and she did not turn round when he came down the street.

"A foot," she said. "I measured it. With my hand."

He came and stood beside her.

"It's been there a hundred years," said Imani. "The priest says so. His father drew water here. They had the bloody flux in the summer before the speckled death came, a dozen children, and he buried them, and nobody thought about the well because nobody thinks about a well. It's always been there." She moved the lantern. "Whose job is it? I asked him. Whose job is the well? He said the town's. I asked the bailiff whose job the town is. He said His Grace's. I asked him when His Grace was last here. He said he didn't know. He thought perhaps the year His Grace's father died." She looked at the hollow. "So it's nobody's."

"Yes."

"You stopped it in four towns in five weeks," she said. "We did. With a cow and a page and a box of wire and a lot of children who've been taught to ask everybody everything. And here they're digging a trench in the churchyard because there's nobody whose job it is to ask." She turned round at last and looked at him. "Why? Why does a duke's town die of what we stopped? It's not that we're cleverer. Corin's not cleverer than a boy here. It's not money. That well would cost a mason two days." She held his eye. "Tell me why."

He looked at the well for a while.

"In Sethe's room," he said. "In August. You and Ferra and me. I said, what if there weren't any."

"Lords." Imani did not move. "You said it and went to sleep. I said tomorrow you could take the kingdom apart." She was quiet. "You didn't. You went to war."

"I told Ferra the rest. Under the apple tree."

"I know. She told me you'd told her something and she'd opened a column for it and she'd not let me see the column." There was the faintest dry edge in it. "Term two. She told me there was a secret. She didn't tell me the secret. She said it was yours to tell, and she'd not have it come to me second-hand." She shifted the lantern to her other hand. "Which is term two bent as far as it'll go without breaking, and she knows it, and so do you. It's been bent since August." She looked at him. "So tell me."

So he told her.

He told it as he had told it to Ferra, lying on his back in the orchard with the apples coming down on his head, except that he was standing in the dark at the bottom of a duke's town beside a fouled well, which made it harder to say and easier to mean. Not a war; not a law. Thirty years, perhaps, and done so slowly that nobody could point to the day it happened. Schools first, until most people could read. Then the offices of a barony, and of a duchy, given to whoever could do them, on proof, the way Ferra's clerks were taken and Ward's knights and Imani's nurses: not by blood, not by who your grandfather had been, by what you could show you could do. Then, when there were enough people who could read and enough offices run by people who had earned them, the lords would find that nobody needed them for anything and the work was all being done without them, and the whole thing would have changed under them like a riverbed.

"That's what Ferra has," he said. "That's all of what I told her."

"And what's the rest?"

He had said half of it once, in Sethe's room, half asleep, without knowing it was the half that mattered.

"That they could choose," he said. "Not only earn the offices. Choose who holds them. The people in the town. Every few years, put their names in for who runs the water and the roads and the schools, and if he runs them badly, put him out and put somebody else in. Not the lord. Not the duke. Them." He looked at the well. "Then it's somebody's job. Because it's theirs."

Imani did not say anything for a while. A dog barked somewhere up the hill and stopped. Behind them, up the street, he could hear the line in the church porch, a child crying, Corin's voice saying next.

"No," she said.

He waited.

"Not no, it's wrong." She put the lantern down on the well's step. "No, not like that. Not yet. Nine years ago, Emeric, ninety-three people in Kaldo could read, and some of those only their names. I was there. I wrote half my petitions for women who made their mark at the bottom. You put that in front of people who can't read and tell them to choose, and they'll choose whoever spoke to them last. Whoever bought the beer. Whoever stood on a cart in the market and told them the healer's water was poisoned and the prince was a demon and he'd keep them safe from both." She looked at him in the lantern light. "That's the Staple. That's six men in a strongroom deciding who eats. You'd just be letting them win the vote instead of buying the debt. It's the Staple with more steps."

"Yes," said Emeric.

She stopped. She had been ready for an argument.

"Yes," he said again. "That's why it's last. That's why the school's first and the vote's at the very end, thirty years off, when most of the people in the market can read the man on the cart's broadsheet and tell him where it's wrong. You're right. If it came now it would be the Staple. That's why it doesn't come now."

Imani looked at him for a long breath. Then she looked down at the well.

"Then you've got it in the wrong order," she said.

"How?"

"School first." She shook her head. "Nobody learns sick. Nobody learns hungry. I've had children in Sela's room with the flux, Emeric, sitting at the table with their slates because their mothers sent them for the bread at the door, and they can't hold the chalk. You can't teach a child who's going to be dead of a well before he's ten." She put her foot on the well's step, by the channel, by the hollow full of ice. "Water first. Food first. A ward with a bed in it for anybody, not just for those who can pay a Copper. Then the school. Then your offices. Then your choosing." She looked up. "That's the order. You don't get readers out of graves."

He did not answer at once, because there was nothing to answer. It was the floor under the whole thing, and he had been building the walls without looking at it. Kaldo had done it in that order by accident, because she had been standing in the way with her lists in the first year and he had done what she said: the wells before the school, the bread at the school door before the letters. He had thought of the bread as a way to get the children to the slates. She had thought of the slates as a way to keep them coming back for the bread.

"Water first," he said.

"And it's everybody's. Not a prince's gift. Not a duke's when he remembers. Written down somewhere that the well is the town's to have clean, and somebody's job to keep it clean, and that job doesn't go to a lord's cousin." She picked up the lantern again. "Write that down first. Before your schools. Before anything."

"I'll tell Ferra."

"I'll tell Ferra," said Imani. "You tell her the rest. I'll tell her this part." She looked at him sideways. "Term two. Now we've both got it. You can stop carrying it about like a stone in your shoe." And then, after a breath, less dry: "Thank you for telling me at a well. It's the right place for it."

She turned and went back up the street toward the church, toward the line and the lamps, and he stood a little longer by the well in the dark with the channel running past his boots, and then he went up after her and held the lamp for the next hundred.



At first light Tancred's cart went on north up the post road with the coffin and the vaccinators' cart behind it, and Corin on the box beside the other pair with Imani's list in his coat and her last instructions in his ears, which had taken a quarter of an hour.

Emeric watched it go from the inn's yard. Tancred did not look back. Corin did, once, and lifted his hand.

"He's seventeen," said Imani, beside him.

"He's taller than me."

"That isn't the same thing." She watched until the carts were over the rise. "He'll do it right. I watched him all yesterday." She pulled her coat shut. "First light. Ward's watching you."

Ward was. He was in the yard with the horses already saddled, and he was looking at Emeric across the cobbles the way he looked at a stone that had not yet decided whether to fall.

They went south.



Kaldo had a number on its doors when he got home.

He saw the first one from the ship, coming down over the north gate on the evening of the eighteenth, and did not understand what it was; a small white square on a dark door at the end of the first lane inside the wall, catching the low sun. Then another on the next door. Then the whole lane was white squares, one to a door, and the next lane, and the one after that.

Linus had done it while he was in the north. He had walked every lane in the snow with Ottoline's census cards and his own town map, and given every lane that had never had a name the name its people already called it, which he found out by asking; and he had numbered every door from the end nearest the keep, odd on the left and even on the right, which he had worked out for himself in an afternoon. The foundry made the plates of iron sheet with a white glaze fired on from the glassworks frit and the number in black. Ferra said nobody had asked for them and everybody had them, and the post was already twice as fast.

"Hob Lantry's wife came to the counting room yesterday," said Ferra, at the slip, with her book. "With a letter." She held up a paper so he could see the outside. It said, in a carter's square careful hand, Mistress Lantry, Coopers Row, Number 14, Kaldo. "From Hob. From Wayford. The first letter in Kaldo addressed to a number." She turned it so that the light was on it. "The post boy took it straight to the door. He'd never been down Cooper's Row in his life. She wanted to know if it was right. If it meant anything that it came to the number and not to her name." She folded it. "I told her it meant the town knew where she lived."

"What did she say?"

"She said it was about time somebody did." Ferra put the letter back in her book. "She wants a plate for the back door as well. I've told her no."



He went round the town the next day, because he had been away a week.

The refuse round had begun while he was away: two carts going down every lane at dawn on the day chalked on the end plate, with a bell, and an iron bin with a lid at every number. The carts went over the bridge to the old timber yard below the works, where four women and a boy sorted it, ash to the brickfield, bones to the glue pot, iron to the foundry, rags to the paper mill. Ferra's rag figure for the mill had gone up by a third in a fortnight. The mill's manager had written to ask where the rags had been all these years, and she had written back that they had been in the lanes.

The night classes had begun in the works' long shop the week before: fifty men, two nights a week, at the benches after the presses stopped, with the steamfitters who had laid the line to the ward in December teaching pipe and Tess's line men teaching wire. Emeric stood at the back of the shop for half an hour on the twentieth and watched a big slow man from the dock quarter thread a joint and get it wrong and have it taken off him by a steamfitter half his size, and shown, and given back, and get it right; and nobody in the room looked round at the prince, because they were busy.

The steam had gone into the hospital. Bram's men had carried a branch off the ward's line in the frost, up through the hospital's back wall and along the old wards under the windows, as they had done in Imani's garden, and the old wards were warm all night for the first time since the hospital was built, and the stoves were out. The night porter said he did not know what to do with his evenings now he did not carry coal. Imani, home from the north, walked the length of the women's ward with her hand on the pipe under the windows, the way she had walked the isolation ward in January, and said what she had said then.

"No fuel through the door." She took her hand off the pipe. "Good."

And Paxton wrote from Milford that the thaw had come to the bay, and the second granary's footings were in, and the masons were back on the first with the roof going on; and Hollis had come down from his strip to say that if the prince wanted a twentieth of the barony's grain in each of these, the prince had better tell the farmers there were twenty of them, because the farmers thought there was one and would want to know why their corn was going up a hill.



Doran found him in the church on the evening of the twenty-first.

He had gone in because it was empty and warm and because he had not been inside it since Wat was buried, and he was sitting at the back on the bench by the door where the poor sat, with his coat open and his hands between his knees, when Doran came out of the vestry with a letter in his hand and saw him and stopped.

"Highness."

"Father."

Doran looked at him for a breath, and at the letter, and then came and sat down on the bench beside him, which he had never done, with a priest's care for his knees.

"I've a letter from the priest at Grayrock," he said. "I was going to bring it up the hill in the morning." He did not unfold it. "There's a family in the pit rows. The second row, under the tip. They wouldn't have the needle, when the pair came in January. The mother wouldn't. She said there was no need." He was quiet. "She said the prince was blessed, and his blessing was on the rows, because he'd come down himself in the night and carried lime with a man there. She said God wouldn't let it into a house the prince had walked past." He looked at the altar. "Their youngest took it on the fourth of February. A girl of three. She died on the fifteenth."

Emeric did not say anything.

"The others had the needle the day after she took it. The mother brought them herself. They're well." Doran turned the letter over in his hands. "The priest at Grayrock asks me what he should tell her. He says she asked him if it was because she hadn't believed enough."

The church was very quiet. Somebody had left a candle burning in front of the side altar, and it was the only one.

"I don't want it," said Emeric.

"I know."

"I don't want them saying it. I'm not blessed. Nothing in the rows that night was me. It was Pell's page and Hester's cow and Rue's hand and a box of wire Bram's girl stamped by the thousand. I held a bucket." He heard his own voice go flat and hard, and could not stop it. "I didn't walk past that house. I didn't know it was there. And now a child of three's dead because her mother thought I had."

"Yes," said Doran.

He did not say anything else for a while. Then he unfolded the letter and looked at it, and folded it again.

"They're saying it in the capital," he said. "A priest at the cathedral in the capital writes to me every quarter. An old friend. Kaldo's children stopped the speckled death. The prince sent the cure north into his enemy's towns in the middle of a war. The prince walked into the cursed shaft at Grayrock and came out with the stone that nobody could bring up." He looked at Emeric. "You can't stop it. I've tried, in a small way, from the pulpit, for a month. You can't take a thing out of people's mouths once it's there. They'll say it whether you want it or not."

"Then what do I do with it?"

"Carry it," said Doran. "Without lying. That's all." He put the letter in his cassock. "They're not wrong to trust you. They've watched you for nine years. They've watched the water come and the bread at the school door and the lamps on the wall and the needle. They trust you because you've earned it, house by house." He was quiet. "They're only wrong about why. They think it's God in you. It's a lot of people's work and a great deal of yours. That's the part you tell them. Every time. Not that they're wrong to trust you. That they're wrong about whose hands it was." He stood up, carefully, with his hand on the bench end. "And you go and see the mother. Not to say you're sorry. To tell her it was the needle, and it wasn't her faith, and it wasn't yours either. She'll not believe me. She might believe you."

Emeric looked up at him.

"Does it get lighter?"

"No," said Doran. "You get used to the shape of it." He went back up the aisle toward the vestry, and stopped by the side altar and pinched out the candle with his fingers, and went in.



Saban's news came down the long wire on the twenty-sixth, at night, and it came in full, because Saban said there was no shorter way to put it.

Tancred had reached his lord's house on the nineteenth with the coffin. Harlow was buried on the twentieth in the chapel beside his sister's two children, who had been put there before Christmas, and the nurse and the groom in the churchyard outside the chapel wall. The vaccinators had done the house the same day, and the village, and were gone on north to the next town on the schedule. And on the evening of the twentieth, after the burying, Tancred had given his lord the leather case.

His lord had read it in the chapel. Saban's clerk did not know what had been said. Nobody had been in the chapel but the old man and his knight and the three new graves.

On the twenty-second Harlow's father had written to the heads of the four houses that had sent their men home with his before Christmas. Saban's clerk had seen one of the letters, because it had gone through the hands of a rope dealer's cousin's wife's brother on its way, which was how Saban's north worked now that he had stopped trusting the parts of it he could not price. It said that he would not answer His Grace's call in the spring, nor in the summer, nor at any time; and it said why; and it named the under-quartermaster, and the wool town, and the baling shed, and the dyer's house, and the date. It did not name His Grace. It did not need to.

On the twenty-fourth the four houses had written back. All four had said they would not answer either.

"There's your second host," said Saban, on the wire. "Not gone home to bury its children. Gone home and staying there." There was a crackle, a long one. "His Grace has had his steward write to every house on the northern roll this week, my man says, telling them the spring call stands. Not one has answered yet. They're waiting to see what the old man does. And the old man's done it." Another pause. "He'll go to court now. He has to. He's nothing else to go to. And a man who's lost his host goes to court angry and empty-handed, which is the best way to meet him."

"And the clerk who copied the page?"

"Gone west to his sister's, with a year's wages and a new coat. My man saw to it." Saban was quiet. "I don't say it was cheap."

"Ward's said what's to be done about Dunmore," said Emeric.

"I heard." Saban's voice did not change. "Keep him. Write to him as before, every fortnight, and tell him nothing that's true. A door you know is open is worth more than one you don't." A breath on the line. "I'd not have thought of it. I'd have done something with him I'd have been sorry for." Another. "Ward's a better merchant than I am. Don't tell him I said so."



The levy came home on the last nights of February.

The ship brought them down out of the north in the dark, forty at a time, as she had taken them up in November, to the first ridge, where they got down onto the frozen road under the stars and formed up by companies and walked the last miles in to the South Gate as the light came. A company of the garrison stayed at the Wayford end with the thirty knights still in the barn, Lord Sallow and his son among them, and would stay until the court sat, whenever it sat. Everybody else came home.

The town was on the road. Nobody had told it the hour, and it was there anyway, all down the last half mile from the bend to the gate, in the grey cold, in shawls and coats and the children on shoulders: the river street and the lower ward and the north well and the dock quarter, and Wenna Hallam at the front of the river street's women with her net bag on her arm and her face set; and they did not cheer, at first, because nobody had told them what you did. They watched the companies come round the bend. They looked for faces.

Then somebody found one, and shouted a name, and after that they cheered.

Emeric stood on the wall over the gate and watched them come in under him. The third company came third. Osric was at its head, on foot, enormous, with his helm under his arm and a beard he had grown since Christmas, red as a fox, and Arthur behind him with the company's colour; and Sela was at the gate passage's mouth with her grandchildren, and when Arthur went by she did not call out to him, or reach for him, or do anything at all but look at him, all the way along, until he had gone in under the arch.

And at the side of the gate passage, against the wall, out of the crowd's way, there was a chair with wheels.

Tess was in it with a shawl round her and her hands in her lap. She did not wave. She did not call out either. When Osric came level with her he did not stop, because a sergeant at the head of a company does not stop; but he looked down, and she held up a single folded sheet of the school's paper, between two fingers, without a word, as a clerk holds out a receipt; and Osric took it as he went by, in stride, and put it inside his breastplate where he had carried Ward's order on the stream ground, and went on in under the arch.

Nobody saw it but Emeric. He was fairly sure nobody else had been meant to.

Tess wheeled herself round and went back up the high street toward the works, at a walking pace, wheels down, before the last company was in.
