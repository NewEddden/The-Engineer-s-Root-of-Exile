Chapter 220 - The Hallway

The bit went through on the fourteenth night, a little after the second hour, at a hundred and forty feet, and the rope went slack in Declan's hands.

Emeric was at the windlass with him. He had been at the windlass most of the night, turning his half of it while Declan turned the other, because Rylan's shoulder had gone on the ninth night and Bram would not let him back on it, and because there was nothing else on the island for Emeric to do while the bit went down but turn and count. Wind it up, and the bar came up the casing light as a broom, the stone in its middle set to nothing. Let it go, and the stone went heavy on the drop, and the bar went down the pipe like the hammer of God and hit the bottom of the hole with a thud that came up through the soles of his boots. Wind it up. Let it go. A foot a night in the rock, some nights. Four, when the rock was soft. Fourteen nights, on Ferra's calendar, since the autumn.

On the fourteenth, the thud did not come. The bar went down and down and did not stop, and the rope ran out over the sheave hissing, and Declan grabbed at it with both hands and caught it, and it went slack in his grip.

"It's through," said Bram, from the lip of the pit, very quietly. "Hands off. Everybody up."

Nobody argued. They had drilled it every night for a fortnight, on Bram's orders, until it was dull: when the bit goes through, everybody up the ladder, now, before anything comes up the pipe. Declan let go of the rope. Emeric let go of the crank. They went up the ladder out of the pit one after another, Declan and Emeric and Osric, who had been on the pump for the sand in the bottom, and at the top Ward took each of them by the arm and pulled them clear and pushed them back from the lip, and then stood on the lip himself and looked down into it.

The pipe breathed.

That was the only word for it. It stood up out of the floor of the pit to the height of a man's knee, the iron casing they had driven down through the sand in the first week, with the rope going down into it over the sheave at the tripod's head. And out of it, round the rope, came a long soft sigh, as of something that had held its breath a very long time and had just let it go. And the smell came up with it.

It was the smell of the pools, the one they had been scrubbing off in the yard's wash house since the spring: rotten eggs and hot iron and the sweet wrong thing under both. But it was ten times what the pools had ever been. It came up out of the pit like water out of a spring, and Emeric felt it in the back of his throat and his eyes ran, and Osric beside him swore and pulled his collar up over his mouth.

"Back," said Bram. He did not raise his voice. "Back from the lip. All of you. Further."

They went back. Bram did not. He knelt at the lip of the pit with his hand on the cage at his side, where the bird was, the little yellow finch from the ward's window that Imani had given him in the summer and told him to bring back alive, and he watched the pipe.

"Heavy air," he said. "It's what they had in the Grayrock workings, the old men. Two airs. One that kills you standing up and one that kills you on the floor. This is the floor one. It sits in a hole like water." He did not look round. "The fan."

Rylan had the fan. He put his good hand on the box of air stones by the lip and they woke, and the canvas trunk that ran from the box down into the pit filled and went taut, and the air in the pit began to move, out of it and up and away across the dune in the dark, and the smell went with it, and thinned.

And then the oil came.

It came up the casing on its own. Nobody pumped it. There was nothing on the well to pump it with yet. It came up the pipe pushed by the gas under it, first as a sound, a gulping, like a drain in reverse, and then as a black bubble at the pipe's lip that swelled and broke and ran down the outside of the casing onto the sand at the bottom of the pit. Then another. Then it was not bubbles. It was a slow black pulse, over the lip and down, over the lip and down, like a heart.

"Cap," said Bram.

He had the valve on a sling from the tripod, the cast cap with its plug cock that he had made in the fitting shop in the first week of the drilling and brought down to the island every night since, wrapped in sacking, against this. He went down the ladder into the pit with it, with the fan roaring softly at his back and his collar over his mouth and the cage in his other hand. At the bottom he set the cage on the sand by his foot. The finch hopped on its perch. Bram looked at it for a moment. Then he cut the rope, and let the bar go down the hole for good, and set the cap over the pipe's lip with the oil pulsing up round his hands, and turned it down on its thread, one turn, two, four, six, with the island rubber in the seat under it, until it would not turn. Then he closed the cock.

The pulse stopped. The sigh stopped. The pit was quiet, except for the fan.

Bram stood in the bottom of the pit with his hands black to the wrists and looked at the bird. It hopped. It turned its head and looked at him with one eye, and hopped again.

"Right," said Bram, and picked up the cage, and came up the ladder.

They closed it up in the last three hours of the dark, because the dark was all they had.

The rails went over the pit first, on the ledges Bram had cut for them at its lip in the first week, side by side, close, and the planks over the rails, and the tarred sailcloth over the planks, weighted at its edges. The vent pipe went up through the sailcloth at the high side, with its sand screen on it, and stopped a hand below where the crest would be. Then Ward and Osric set the wind box upwind of the pit on its frame, and Emeric woke its stones, low, as low as they would go, and the air came out of it in a long soft even breath across the sand pile they had heaped all week, and the sand began to move.

It moved like smoke off a roof. It lifted off the pile in a thin stream and went across the dark and fell on the sailcloth, grain by grain, and lay there, and more came after it. It took the whole of the three hours to put a hand's depth over the pit. It would take four more nights to put a dune.

At the end of it, with the first grey showing in the east over the Kesrath shore and the ship coming down out of the band for them, Ferra came down the line.

She had never come down the line before. She had stayed in the deckhouse every night with the log, as she always had. Tonight she came down on Ward's tethered carry, with her book inside her coat, and stood at the edge of the pit, which was not a pit any more but a low hump of sand with a sand-screened pipe sticking a hand out of its top, and looked at it for a long time.

"That's the first thing we've left," she said.

Nobody answered her. Ward was beside her. Emeric was across the hump from them with the wind box's stones going cold under his hand.

"No marks on anything," Ferra said. "Nothing with our name on it. Nothing that can be left behind. That was the rule. I wrote it." She looked at the pipe. "And now there's iron in the ground here, and rails, and a valve Bram cast in our foundry, and it's going to be here after we've gone home, and next year, and in ten years." She did not look round. "I'm not saying it's wrong. I'm saying it's the first one."

"It is," said Emeric.

"Then there's a rule for it." She took the book out of her coat. "Say it now. While it's the first."

He said it. He said it standing on the hump of sand in the grey, with the ship's shadow coming down out of the dark over them and the smell of the pools on everybody: that the well would never be left full. That before any gap longer than a season, for any reason, the last night would drain every tank and close the valve and put pitch down the pipe on top of it, so that if Kaldo ever stopped coming, whatever was found here would be found empty and shut. That nobody would ever go below the lip alone, or without the fan, or without the bird. That there would be no flame at the well, ever.

Ferra wrote it down. She did not write it in the log. She wrote it on the ruled page at the back, the one with Nights at the top, that went in the box.

Nobody slept on the way home.

They were all too tired to sleep, which was a thing that happened, and they were all still smelling of it, and the hose in the ship's belly was the only water there was. The knights stripped and washed in the cold and sat wrapped in blankets on the benches in the hold with their eyes open. Bram sat by the finch's cage with his black hands in his lap, not washing them, looking at nothing. Ferra went back to the deckhouse with the log.

Emeric went up on deck.

It was very cold up there. It was always very cold in the band, thirty degrees colder than the ground, colder than any winter night in Kaldo, and in the winter it was colder still, and the stars were very close and very hard. The ship went south and west over the black sea at the speed she always went, which was faster than anything else in the world, and made no sound but the air in her veins. He stood at the rail aft with his blanket round him, and after a while Ward came up the ladder and stood beside him with his, as Ward always did, a little behind, on the left.

There were lamp stones on the deck, in their cages on the rail posts, turned low for the band. Four of them, down the length of her, dim, steady.

They stood there for a long time.

He had carried the list for a season. He had carried it up the capital road and down it, and through the harvest, and through the autumn, and through fourteen nights of the windlass, and he had not asked. He had told himself there would be a right time, and that it was not at a table, and that it was not in front of anybody. He looked at Ward now in the starlight, a big grey shape in a blanket at the rail, with his face turned toward the sea, and understood that there was never going to be a better time than three miles up in the dark with nobody awake but the two of them and a well behind them in the sand. He did not know why that was. It was.

"What happened," he said, "between my mother and Aldane?"

Ward did not move.

He did not turn his head. He stood at the rail with the blanket round him and his face to the sea, and the ship went on through the dark, and it was so long before he said anything that Emeric thought he would not. Then he let his breath out, slowly, in a long white cloud that the ship's speed took away behind them, and said, "Yes."

He said it as if Emeric had asked something else. As if he had been asked whether he would, and had decided.

He told it the way he told a road. Flat, in order, with nothing added, the way he had reported every road Emeric had ever sent him down.

"You were six," Ward said. "Near seven. It was the winter before. You had a study. A room on the east side, off the long passage, with a table in it and a slate on the table and a window, and your mother taught you in it herself, every morning, before anybody else did. Your letters, first. Then the other things." He did not say what the other things were. "She didn't wear her own clothes for it. She wore a plain dress. Grey wool, like a nurse. She said a boy couldn't learn anything from a woman in a crown, he'd spend the whole morning looking at the crown. So she sat at your slate in grey wool with her hair tied up, every morning, and nobody in the household who didn't know her face would have taken her for anything but a nurse."

The ship went over a cold pocket in the air and dipped, a little, and came up again.

"I was on her door. Hers and yours. The two of us, that winter, and the captain. I was at the far end of the passage that morning, by the stair, where I could see both ways. She came out of the study to send for something. Chalk, I think. She came out into the passage and shut the door behind her so you wouldn't be disturbed, and turned to go to the stair to call down, and Aldane came round the corner from the east court."

"He was at the council," said Emeric.

"He was at the council. He'd been at the council's dinner the night before and he had not been to bed. He was younger, and he was drunk, and he was the duke of Aldane, with a sister on the throne and a line older than the crown, and he had never in his life been told no by anybody in that house." Ward's voice did not change at all. "He saw a woman in grey coming out of a nursery. He took her for a household woman. A nurse. Nobody." He was quiet for a moment. "He put his hands on her. He had her by the arm and against the wall before I'd taken two steps. He said what a man like that says to a woman he thinks belongs to the house. I won't say it to you."

Emeric did not say anything.

One of the lamp stones on the rail post nearest them brightened. It came up from its dim band-glow to the full white of a lamp on the ground, and hung there, and then dimmed again, slowly, as if something had leaned on it and let go. Emeric saw it at the edge of his eye and did not look at it. He was looking at Ward.

Ward's eyes went to the lamp. They went to it, and stayed on it for as long as it took to see it, and then came back to the sea. He did not stop talking.

"I drew," he said. "I was running and I drew. I was going to kill him. I had it in my hand and I was going to put it through him in the passage and hang for it, and I'd decided that before I'd taken the third step." He said it the way he would have said the distance to a ford. "I didn't get there. She had it done before I got there."

"Done."

"Her hands," said Ward. "She took his wrist off her arm. I don't know how. I've done it since, the way I saw her do it, and I still don't know how she did it that fast. She took his wrist and turned under it, and his arm went up behind him, and she put him down. On the floor. On his face, on the boards of the passage outside your door, with his arm up his back and her knee in it. In a breath. He didn't make a sound. He didn't have one to make." Ward's breath went out white again. "She didn't make one either. She bent down to his ear and said something to him. Quietly. I don't know what. I was there by then, and I had the sword over him, over the back of his neck, and he turned his head on the boards and looked up at me. He looked up at me past the blade. And then she said, 'Put it up, Ward.'"

The lamp on the rail post brightened again, and held, and dimmed. The one beyond it did the same, a breath later, as if it had caught something off the first.

"I put it up," said Ward.

"And she let him up. She stood back and let him get up off the floor, and he got up, and he stood in the passage and looked at her, and I watched him know who she was. She told him, I think, on the floor. I watched it come into his face. That the woman who'd put him on his face in front of a guard was the king's wife. The third queen. The one the old blood called nobody's daughter." Ward was quiet for a moment. "He went. He didn't say anything. He went back round the corner the way he'd come. And she turned to me, and she said, 'This didn't happen.' Like that. 'This didn't happen, Ward. Not to the king, not to the captain, not to anybody. You'll swear it.' And I swore it. And she went back into the study and shut the door, and sat down at your slate, and I heard her through the door asking you what came after m."

He stopped. The ship went on through the dark.

"Where was I?" said Emeric.

"Inside. At your table, with the door shut. You didn't hear any of it. There wasn't anything to hear." Ward looked at him then, for the first time since he had begun. "You don't remember it. You don't remember any of that winter. I know. I've watched you not remember it for twenty years."

"Armand was there," Ward said, after a while. "At the other end of the passage, coming for your lesson in the stones. He saw it. He didn't move. I asked him, after, why he didn't move, and he said because she didn't need him to, and he was right, and I've never forgiven him for being right about it." Something that was nearly a smile went across his face in the dark and was gone. "That's the three of us. Me and Armand and Aldane. Nobody else ever knew."

"And the king."

"Not till after." Ward turned back to the sea. "I kept it. I swore to her and I kept it, the whole of that winter and the spring after. Then she was dead." He said it flatly, the way he had said everything. "And I was the man who carried you home from the channel, and I told the king. Everything. The passage and the floor and the sword over his neck, and what she said to me, and what I thought it meant. I broke my oath to her the week she died, because I thought she'd have wanted the man who put his hands on her looked at, and nobody else could tell him."

"What did he do?"

"He looked." Ward's voice did not change. "He had it looked into properly. Kassim did it, and two others I'd trust. Where Aldane was when the carriages were hit. Where his men were. Who he'd paid that year, and what for. And it all came back clean. He was in the north, at his own hall, with forty people at his table who'd swear to it and did. His men were where they should be. His money was where it should be. There was nothing." He was quiet. "The king cleared him. On the evidence. He told me so himself, in the mews, the way he tells things, and he told me the evidence was good, and it was. I read it. I couldn't find a hole in it. I've been looking for twenty years."

"And you don't believe it."

"I believe the evidence," said Ward. "I don't believe him." He turned his head and looked at Emeric, and his face in the starlight was the face he had worn on the floor of the king's hall, two paces behind the stool, when the duke of Aldane had looked at him for two breaths and looked away. "A man who hates like that doesn't need to be there. He doesn't need his men to be there. He needs to know the road she'll take and who'd be glad to wait on it. He needs to be in the north with forty people at his table. That's what I'd do, if I hated like that. I'd make sure I was somewhere else." He looked back at the sea. "He looked at me in the hall this summer. He looked at me and he looked away. He knew my face. He's known it for twenty years. It's the last face he saw over a blade on the floor of that passage, and he looked away from it first." Ward's breath went out white. "You said it was contempt for a guard."

"I was wrong."

"You were wrong," Ward agreed. "It's not contempt. It's the other thing."

All four lamps on the rail posts brightened together, and held, and dimmed, slowly, the way a man's chest goes down when he lets his breath out. Ward watched them do it. He did not say anything about it. He waited until they had gone back to their dim band-glow, and then he said, "That's all of it," and was quiet.

Emeric stood at the rail for a long time after that. Ward stood beside him.

He found that he was thinking about a banquet. Not his mother's. His own, the last one, the night before the exile, in a hall with the tables along the wall: a young man eleven cups into a night with no floor in it, and a woman at the high table with a delegation's business in her hands, and the things the young man had said to her at a volume that reached the musicians, about her father, about his mines. She had not been nobody. She had been a king's envoy and she had gone quiet, and the young man had gone on talking. He thought about the old blood, and what they said to a woman they thought they could, and about his own mouth, which he had inherited and not chosen, and he did not finish the thought. He put it down before it finished arriving. He had been doing that for a long time and he was very good at it.

The Kesrath shore had gone behind them. Ahead, low on the edge of the world, there was a smudge of grey that would be the coast of Randillia in an hour.

"Ward," he said.

"Your grace."

"Don't." He did not look round. "Thank you."

Ward did not answer that. After a moment he put out one hand, and rested it on the rail next to Emeric's, not touching it, the way he had stood at a thousand doors, and left it there until the coast came up out of the dark.

He asked Armand the next day, in the afternoon, in the old man's study at the keep, with Armand at his table under the window and a sheet of his readings in front of him.

He did not tell Armand what Ward had said. He asked only whether Armand had been in the east passage, one morning in the winter before his mother died, when the duke of Aldane came round the corner from the east court.

Armand put his pen down. He looked at Emeric over his spectacles for a long moment, and then took them off, and folded them, and put them on the sheet.

"Yes," he said. "It's true. All of it, I expect. I was at the far end, coming for your lesson." He looked at his folded spectacles. "I didn't move. Ward's never forgiven me for it. I didn't move because she didn't need me to. I've known a great many mages in my life, and a great many knights, and I have never once seen anybody put a man on the floor as quickly as your mother put that one, or as quietly, or look so little as if she had done anything at all afterwards." He picked up the spectacles again. "She could have killed him. I could see that from where I stood. It would have been easier than what she did. She chose the floor."

Emeric stood by the bench and said nothing.

He had known his mother for seven years and remembered none of them. He had a stone she had put in his hand, and a slate with a few letters on it in her writing, and a room in his head he had recognised the first time he stood in it without knowing why. He had a name, and no face. He had never once, in all the years since the wall came down across the front of his memory, heard anybody say what she had been like, in a passage, on an ordinary morning, when a man put his hands on her.

She chose the floor.

"Thank you," he said, and went out, and Armand put his spectacles back on and bent to his readings, and did not watch him go.
