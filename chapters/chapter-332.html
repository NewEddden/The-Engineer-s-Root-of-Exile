Chapter 332 - The First Queen

Imani let him up on the morning of the twenty-first, which was the sixth day, and made it clear before she did it that she was letting him and he was not getting.

"Sit first," she said. "On the edge. Feet on the floor. Don't do anything clever."

He sat. The deck plates of the K Amara's after cabin were cold through his socks, and his legs, which had carried him across the fountain square in one knight's step a week ago, felt like a pair he had been lent by a man who wanted them back. He put his left hand on his knee and his right hand, in its splint and its long clean figure of eight, where she told him, and the cabin went once round him slowly and came back.

"It went round," he said.

"It'll go round for two days. Let it." She crouched and took the right hand in both of hers, the way she turned a jar to read the label, and put her thumb on the strap over the two knuckles and shut her eyes. He felt her go in, like somebody walking through his house with a lamp, looking at the joists.

"Straight," she said. "Knitting. Twice as quick as a man's should, which is why I'm letting you sit up, and not a reason to do anything else." She did not let go of the hand. "You took my splint off."

"You took it off."

"Because you asked me. And I told you what would happen if you hit anything with it. And then you went down into a square and hit an old man and the ground with it, in that order." She laid the hand back on his knee as if it were something of hers she had lent him and did not expect back in the same state. "Next time you want a thing off, I'll take the hand off with it and save us both the evening."

"That's fair."

"It's not fair. It's medicine. Stand up. Slowly."

He stood, and caught the bunk's rail, and stood.

The stone was in the breast pocket of his shirt, buttoned in. It had been in his fist the first two days, while he was under; when he came up on the third it had been the first thing he knew, before his name, a small warm weight in his palm like an egg somebody had given him to hold. Since then his left hand had gone to the pocket every so often by itself, and found the shape through the cloth, and come away. Imani had watched it do that a good many times. She had not once asked.

She did not ask now. "No ladders. No stairs without Ward behind you. If you feel sick, sit down wherever you are, I don't care if it's the middle of the apron. And you eat at noon."

"Yes."

"Don't say yes like that. I know that yes." She stopped in the door and looked back at him in his socks, holding the rail. "You look terrible," she said, more kindly. "It's a great improvement."

The mana was coming back the way water comes back into a well after a dry month, an inch a day, from the bottom. He could feel the bottom. He had never been able to feel it before, and he did not like it.

Ward was in the passage when he came out, and had very likely been there some time. He looked at Emeric's socks, and went into the cabin without a word and came out with the boots, and knelt down in the passage with his ribs still strapped and did the laces, both, while Emeric held the wall; because Emeric could not do laces with one hand, and they both knew it, and neither of them was going to say anything about it ever.

There were nineteen steps up to the after apron. At the ninth he stopped with his good hand flat on the rail and his eyes shut and his whole body deciding whether to be sick, and Ward stood one step below him and did not touch him; and at the twelfth he went on.

The capital lay a mile under the apron, the rings of it going up the hill in the grey morning, the pale scar on the summit, the castle on the east shoulder with its gardens stepping down to the fountain square, small as a coin from here; somebody had swept it, and the cookshop's wall was still black on one side. The K Endeavor, hull three, sat lower and nearer over the outer ring with her hospital lamps lit along her belly in daylight because Imani kept them lit, and he could feel the notes in her, a whole ward of them, Mira's among them, sore and bored.

And north, up the river, past the long bridge and the woods, where the falls came down white out of the hills, there was a house he could not see from here; and in it, at the end of the finest thread he had ever laid, a great cold note that had not moved in six days.

"There's a hull coming up from the south," said Ward. "The lift hull. Armand's aboard. He heard on the set on the eighteenth that you'd come up out of it, and he was on the hull by eleven that night."

"Who's holding the town?"

"Ferra. And your brother. And the South Gate's got its lever." He paused. "She said to say she's staying. She said she'd like it put in the book as hers."

Emeric looked at the city a while longer.

"It was hers," he said.

"I know," said Ward. "So does she. She said that too."



The lift hull came up alongside a little after two and lay off the after apron at a boat's length while her crew put a plank across. She was the old capital lift hull that had flown the king's standard over Kaldo town on the night of the thirtieth so that the king could be somewhere else; the standard was rolled now and lashed in its locker under canvas. Old Edrin was not at her casting seat. He had said that night would be his last flight, and he had meant it. A woman from the yard sat in his place with her hands on the rail, pouring nothing into stones that did not need it, which was all the casting seat had ever been for.

Armand came across the plank as if it were a street.

He had been an old man on a stick the first time Emeric watched him cross anything. He came over the gap now in his long tower coat with a Clothing Works cardigan buttoned under it and his round spectacles on, which had nothing in them but plain glass, and stepped down onto the apron with the plank still moving: a big heavy-shouldered young man with white curls cut short and dark at the roots, at whom the apron knights by the rail still looked twice. He looked at Emeric, and stopped.

"You look appalling," said Armand, with great satisfaction. "Good. I was afraid I'd have come seven hundred miles to find you sitting up in bed eating soup and making jokes. Ferra sends her compliments. She's staying."

"Ward said."

"She said Ward would say. She said I was to say it as well, so that it would have been said twice, by two people, and you couldn't claim afterward you'd misheard." He looked at him over the plain glass. "Bram sent you nothing, and asked me to tell you so."

Emeric laughed, and it hurt, and he stopped.

Armand did not laugh. He was looking at Emeric's left hand, which had gone to the pocket of his shirt while they talked and was resting on the cloth over the small hard shape.

"I've come for your mother," he said.

The hand closed a little on the pocket, through the cloth, the way it had once closed on a grey stone in a banqueting hall at the sound of a sentence, before he told it to.

"Not to take," said Armand, more quietly. "I'd like to hold her. If you'll let me."

They went in out of the wind to the small chart room under the tower, and Ward shut the door and stood outside it. Emeric undid the button left-handed, badly, and took the stone out and held it on his open palm under the lamp. Small, grey-white, no bigger than a walnut; far down inside it the little warm light, the colour of a lamp seen through a hand.

Armand looked at it a long time without touching it. Then he held out both hands together, cupped, the big square young hands that had been spotted and shaking two years ago, and Emeric tipped the stone into them; and Armand shut his eyes.

Emeric could feel him listen, to a note he had last heard through a door at the top of a tower stair that he had once stood outside for a quarter of an hour with his hand up, and not knocked on.

His face did nothing for a long while. Then the corner of his mouth went in.

"She's flat," said Armand.

Emeric looked at him.

"Half a tone under. The whole of every tune. She always was." He did not open his eyes. "I told her once that a mage who could hear a stone's note to a hair and couldn't sing one to save her life was a standing insult to the tower. She said she'd take it up with the tower." His hands did not move. "She did, too. In writing."

After a while he opened his eyes, and kept the stone in one hand, and with the other brought a box out of his coat: small, square, oiled oak, a lid on two tiny brass hinges and a brass hook to shut it, and inside, grey flannel folded into a nest exactly the size of a walnut. There was one man in Kaldo who cut a corner joint like that, and he did not make boxes.

"Bram," said Armand. "The night I left, in about an hour, in the cart shed. I asked him for something to carry a stone in and he didn't ask which stone. He measured my hand instead." He turned the box so the lamp went along the corners. "When I said thank you he said he'd sent you nothing."

"I'd like to keep her."

"You'd like to keep her in your shirt," said Armand, "where you'll put your hand to her forty times a day in front of the whole ship, until every apron knight aboard knows there's something in that pocket you'd rather die than drop." He said it without unkindness, as a sum. "She's been in somebody's fist for twenty-eight years. One man's, and now yours. I'd like her out of fists for a while. I'll not let her out of my sight. That's all I'm offering, and I'm aware it isn't much."

It was a great deal, from him. Emeric looked at the box, and at his own left hand on the table, empty for the first time in six days and not knowing what to do with itself.

"All right," he said.

Armand laid her in the flannel as if she might wake, and put the hook over, and buttoned the box inside his coat on the left side; and laid his hand flat on the outside of the coat for a breath, the way Emeric's had been going to his pocket, and caught himself doing it, and took the hand away. Neither of them said a word about that either.

He did not let it out of his sight all the rest of that day: at the mess table, eating one-handed with the other flat on his coat; in the chair in Emeric's cabin at night, the box on his knee, reading Emeric's copy of Ferra's war column by the bunk lamp and making small noises at her figures.

"There's a thread on Augustine," Emeric said, from the bunk, at about eleven.

"I know. I felt it when I came aboard. It goes out of the top of your head like a kite string." Armand turned a page. "Where is he?"

"The house above the falls. He went up the gardens on the fifteenth and over the crown wall and out by the north lanes, and he was in that house by dark. He hasn't moved since."

"And you've not touched it. But you'd like to." Armand put the column down beside the box, and his young face was not wry at all. "Don't go down the stair yet."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were. You'd have gone tonight, if Imani had let you sit up long enough to be stupid. Down a line you laid yourself, after a man who's had ninety years to furnish his own cellar, with your cup empty to the bottom." He waited. "You can feel the bottom, can't you. For the first time."

Emeric did not answer that.

"Then don't go down the stair," said Armand, and found his place in the column again. "Not yet. Not after him. He'll keep. Men like that keep for years. It's the one thing they're good at."



The note had come down the gardens at dusk that day in the hand of a grey-haired man in good dark wool, with a lamp, who walked down through the open gate at the top of the fountain square as if it were his own front path and asked the knights on the basin's lip for Sir Osric by name, which nobody in the capital should have known; and bowed to Osric exactly as far as a steward bows to a knight he has not been introduced to, and went back up the terraces without looking round.

Saban brought it up on the evening boat, before supper, and laid it on the chart table in front of Emeric, still sealed, and sat down and blew his nose.

"Her steward," he said. "Mabry. Thirty-one years in her household and never been bought. I tried once. He sent the money back with a note about my spelling."

A plain oval of dark blue wax. Emeric broke it with his thumb and opened the sheet left-handed, and Ward and Saban and his father and Armand, who had set the box by Emeric's elbow, all read it upside down at once, which was how anybody at that table had ever read anything.

Six lines, in a hand like ruled iron.

The first queen will receive Prince Emeric at the treasury door at ten o'clock tomorrow morning, on a matter between his house and hers. He will bring one man. He will not bring the king. He will be given what he came for, if he agrees to what is asked; and if he does not, he will be given nothing and will not be asked again.

Under it, smaller: S.

"She's left out the duchy," said Saban.

"She's left out the escheat," said Harrold, from the bench under the chart rack, where he had sat every evening since the twentieth with his collar up. "She won't write a title her husband's council voted away, and she won't write a vote her husband didn't sign. So she writes neither, and calls you what you were born." He was quiet. "She always did write a good note."

"One man," said Ward.

"You."

"She didn't ask for me," said Harrold.

"No."

"Good," said his father, and said nothing more for a while.

Saban unfolded his own slips. Everybody at the table knew where Emeric's brother was; Saban said it anyway, because he said things in order.

"The chancery," said Saban. "On its square in the lower town, by the drapers' street. He walked out of his father's house on the second by the gardens' stair, when the sheet put the castle inside the ring, and down through the town to the chancery, because a lieutenant of the realm has to be somewhere and the chancery's where the seal is. Nineteen days. The rooms over the council stair; he sleeps in the one with the long window. Thirty knights of his own household on the doors and the yard and the roof." The knight in the basin had been the thirty-first, and Saban did not look at Ward. "Nobody goes in but the clerks at eight and nobody comes out but the clerks at six, because nobody's told them to stop. And a boy for bread twice a day from the bakehouse on the corner, who's mine."

"The companies."

"In their barracks since the seventeenth, cooking their own, with one of Ward's knights on every door and a man with a set in every gate. The north road's watched from the air. His uncle's in the north with every horse he owns and hasn't moved, and if he moves I'll know the day before." Saban squared his slips. "Your brother can't leave the chancery without walking through Kaldo, and he can't leave the city without flying. He knows both. He's sitting very still in the corner, writing letters nobody carries."

"And we don't go in," said Ward. It had been said at that table every evening since the sixteenth, and would be said again.

"We don't go in."

Keller had put it in three close pages from Kaldo on the eighteenth, which came down to one line: if the king's son drags the crown's lieutenant out of the crown's chancery by his collar, the verdict that lieutenant sits on becomes true. It had to come apart the way it had gone together, in council, by law; which meant Grier, and waiting. Emeric had his own reason, older than Keller's, which he did not put in any letter.

When the slips were done his father said, from the bench, without looking at anybody, "What did Augustine say about her?"

"That he asked her for the crystal, and she wouldn't give it him. That she's the only person in the city who ever refused him anything to his face."

His father thought about that.

"Twice," he said. "He's counting the one he minded."

He got up off the bench slowly and stood with his hand on the chart rack, looking down at his son.

"Don't thank her," said Harrold.

"You said she'd take it as an insult."

"She would." He went to the door. "Don't."



Fletcher put the K Discovery down on the castle's upper court at a quarter to ten on the twenty-second, on flagstones nobody had swept for three weeks, and Emeric walked down her ramp into his father's house with Ward behind him, and had to stop at the bottom and stand.

The palace was empty the way only a very large house can be. Every shutter on the south front was still closed against a cathedral that was not there any more, and the long gallery was dark but for thin bright lines along the floor at the shutters' edges, and the clocks had stopped. He passed four, and each said something different, and none of them went.

The treasury stair went down out of the east range's undercroft in a square well, the steps worn hollow, a rope along the wall on iron rings instead of a rail. Forty-one steps. At the twentieth Emeric sat down with his back against the rope and his strapped hand in his lap and his eyes shut, and Ward stood a step above him.

"You can say it," said Emeric. "That she can wait a week."

"She said she wouldn't ask again. I read it. I believe her." Ward looked at the wall a while longer. "You've got twenty-one to go."

The treasury door was at the end of a short vaulted passage at the bottom: black iron, banded, as wide as a cart, with a smaller door cut in it for men; and set into the iron in a ring all round its edge, the tower's watch stones, etched and filled with silver a hundred years ago, every one of them sour and cold and awake in his sense like a row of dogs on chains. The steward Mabry stood at the small door with a ring of keys on a chain at his belt. He bowed exactly as far as he had bowed to Osric, and opened it, and stood aside.

And she was in the room beyond, standing.

It was the counting room, long and low and vaulted, with clerks' desks down both sides under green-shaded lamps and a great old double-locked press at the far end; warm, because somebody had lit the stove for her. She stood beside the press with her hands folded in front of her, in a dark blue gown buttoned to the throat with a little silver at the cuffs, and her hair, wheat gone pale and silvering at the temples, piled so high and so exactly that it was a circlet on its own; and she wore a slim plain circlet besides. She was a tall woman, and stood the way a tall woman stands who has been told all her life to make herself less so and has declined.

The grey-blue eyes went over him once, boots to strapped hand to face, and rested there; and he knew the look, because it had rested on him exactly so at every feast of his boyhood, on the rare days it rested on him at all.

"You're not well," said Sabina.

"No."

"Then sit down before you fall down. I won't have it said in this city that I made Amara's son stand in my presence until he dropped." She did not move to bring him a chair. Mabry brought a clerk's high stool from the nearest desk and set it behind him without a sound. "Your man can stand by the door."

Emeric sat. Ward stood by the door. She did not sit. She stood by the press, a woman of nearly sixty who looked a good deal less, with her son's colouring and none of her son's need to move, and looked at him for a while, and he let her.

"You have your father's way of sitting," she said at last. "Badly, as if the chair were a horse he'd been told to watch. I'll say what I have, and then what I want, and then you'll answer. I'd be obliged if you didn't interrupt. I've had a week to arrange it, and I'd like it said in the order I arranged it."

"Go on."

She took a key off her own belt, not Mabry's ring, a small plain one on a black cord, and opened the press's upper half and took out a box of plain treasury deal, iron-cornered, the size of a loaf, and set it on the nearest desk under the green lamp and opened it.

Inside, in two rows, lay twelve small parcels of white paper, each folded square and tied with the treasury's red tape. On every one a clerk had written the same three words in the same brown ink, in a hand forty years old.

Of no value.

"Your mother's," said Sabina. "The rest of them. They were hers, so they were the crown's, so they were his; and he kept them in this press for twenty-eight years and wouldn't let me touch them. I pressed him to be rid of them every year. He let two go to that man of yours, and kept these where I could see them every quarter day and never once throw them in the river." One finger on the box's edge. "Twelve. Mabry's counted them. You'll count them yourself, I expect. You look like a man who counts."

He had already counted them. He did not say so.

"And the nine," she said.

He looked up.

"The nine my son gave you at your farewell. Those came out of this press too, out of the same drawer, written down as relics of no use to anybody, which the tower had said for a hundred years. The night before your farewell I took them out and gave them to my son and told him to give them to you." Her voice did not change at all. "I wanted them out of the house. And I wanted you to carry a box of her dead stones into exile in front of two hundred people and know what they were. The gift was spite. Mine. He only carried it. At that age he'd have carried anything I gave him, if he thought it would land."

Your mother gave you one of these once. His brother's voice, dropping at arm's length so that only he heard it, and his own hand going to his pocket.

"He knew she'd given me one," he said.

"Everybody in this treasury knew the set was ten and the drawer was nine. Clerks talk." She closed the lid, not quite all the way. "The nine are the crown's, as much as these. Not his to give, nor mine. The crown could ask for them back. I'm telling you the crown won't. They're in this. That's all."

"And the rest," said Emeric.

"And the rest. Your father's man will have told you about my laundry. I know which woman it is, by the way; I've known since August. I let her keep her place, because she does the altar linen well and it suited me that somebody in your father's pay should see me say no on that stair." She let that sit exactly as long as she meant it to. "Your mother is in this palace. Nobody alive knows where but eight men of my household who'll die before they say, and Mabry, and me. The cardinal looked for her for five months. If you agree to what I ask, I'll take you to her this morning and you can carry her out tonight. If you don't, you'll look for her the way he did, and find what he found."

"Which was?"

"My brother's cellar door," said Sabina. "Shut."

She folded her hands again.

"That's what I have. Now what I want." She did not raise her voice; by every account she never had. "My son, alive, unharmed and not disgraced past what he's earned."

That was the whole of it: one man, and three conditions, the last weighed to a grain. She did not ask him to spare her son. She did not say please, or anything at all about being his mother. She stood by the press and waited for the price to be accepted, the way a woman waits who has set a fair price and would rather lose the sale than haggle.

"His father put him in the council chair at fourteen," she said, when he had not answered. "For a year, while he was out hunting the men who'd paid for that carriage. Every week. Nobody thanked him. Then his father came home and went up your stair." It was a line in a ledger. "I'm not pleading it. I'm telling you what you're buying."

Emeric looked at the box on the desk, the lid not quite shut, the corner of a white parcel showing in the gap.

He had never been going to kill his brother. Not at the table under the wine racks the night Saban counted the chests going up into every tower in the capital with a man asleep beside each; not in the square with the stone in his hand and Augustine saying, low, ask your brother; not once in thirteen years of being handed reasons by people who loved him and wanted it done. He did not know whether that was a virtue. He thought it might only be the shape he was.

She did not know it. And he could tell her, and it would be true: you needn't give me anything; I was never going to touch him. And she would understand that she had come down forty-one stairs for nothing, and that Amara's son had handed her her son for nothing, as a kindness; and she would never forgive him for it as long as she lived. It was the one gift in the world he could give her that she would not take.

So he did not tell her. He sat on the clerk's stool and looked at the box a long time, as a man looks at a price he had not meant to pay, and let her watch him look; and when he raised his eyes he let her see that it had cost him something, and did not let her see what.

"What has he earned?" he said.

She was quiet. Then, for the first time, she looked past him to the door, where Ward stood.

"Your man can wait on the stair."

Ward looked at Emeric. Emeric nodded once, and Ward went out through the small door, and Mabry after him without being told, and shut it. Through the iron Emeric felt Ward stop two steps up the stair, and stand.

When she spoke it was at the same pitch as everything else she had said, and he understood after a word or two that it was costing her more than anything else in the room.

"I've known for twenty-eight years where my son went the morning your mother died," said Sabina. "I've never asked him what he said there. I'm not going to ask him now, and you won't either, for what I'm giving you."

The stove ticked.

He did not ask her where. Twenty-eight years of chapel at seven, and her son across the table at dinner, and never once the question; she had kept it the way she kept her keys, and never once taken it out to look at.

Ask your brother what the Light showed him. Somebody in that yard sent him the road.

He had put both those sentences away, one in the square and one in Kaldo in the summer, and not looked at either; and now there was a third, and the three of them lay side by side in him in the warm low room like coins on a counter. He could feel them wanting to be added. He did not add them. She had asked for one thing more than the three conditions, and it was this, and he could give it to her.

"No," he said.

"Say it."

"I won't ask him."

She looked at him a while longer, and nodded once, as a clerk nods at a figure that has come out right.

"Then we're agreed."

"Nearly." The room had begun to go round a little at the edges again, and he waited for it to stop. "He can't stay here. Neither can you. When the council's done what it's going to do, he comes south, to Kaldo, under watch." He kept his eyes on hers. "You too."

That got through. Not far: a very little, at the corner of the firm unsmiling mouth.

"Kaldo," said the first queen.

"You'd rather the north?"

"My brother's house in the north is a large cold hall full of men who'll have taken the wrong side by spring." She said it as she would have said the price of wool. "And the cardinal has been refused to his face once in this city, by me, on a stair, in front of a crypt keeper. He'll not forget which face. Or if he does, somebody he sends won't." She considered him. "Your town has walls."

"It has walls. You'd walk where you liked inside them, and be watched while you did it."

"Good," said Sabina. "I'd think less of you if you'd said anything else." She laid her hands flat on the top of the press, and he saw for the first time that they were not quite steady, and that she had kept them folded so that he would not see it. "I'll take it. He'll take it, when I've told him. I'll tell him what I've bought him. I won't tell him what it cost." She lifted the hands off the press again. "He sent for me on the sixteenth. I didn't go. I'll go tonight."

"Does he know you're here?"

"Nobody knows I'm here but Mabry and your man and you. That's how I've kept anything in this house for thirty years." She locked the press's upper half and put the black cord back at her belt. "Come and see her. Can you manage another stair?"

"Yes."

"You can't," said Sabina. "But you'll do it anyway. Your father was exactly the same."



The lowest room was nine steps further down, behind the press, which swung out on its own hinges when Mabry turned two keys in it that were both on the first queen's ring and neither on his. A narrow stair went down into the hill behind it, and a passage, and a door; and beyond the door, the old coin room, where a dead king's clipped silver had sat in iron chests for a hundred years because nobody could think what else to do with it.

She told him on the stair, with the lamp in her own hand and Mabry behind with a second and Ward behind Mabry.

"The dray was real," she said. "Your laundress saw it. Eight men brought her up out of the crypt at one in the morning in a laundry's worth of canvas and straw and put her on a dray in the chapel yard with its lamps hooded, and the dray went out of the chapel yard. That's all the laundress saw; she'd gone back in for the rest of the canvas." The lamp went down a step. "The dray went three hundred yards. Round the east range to the treasury yard, and down the bullion ramp. Eight men brought her down this stair from the ramp's foot, and stopped twice." Another step. "Then they put the old altar stone from the crypt chapel on the same dray in the same canvas. It's very nearly the same length, and heavier. At three o'clock the dray went out of the north gate with its lamps lit, slowly, so that anybody who cared to could follow it, to my brother's house in the upper town; and eight men carried a long heavy thing in canvas down into his cellar with great care, and the cardinal's man on the corner watched them do it." The last step. "It's still there. My brother has never been down to look."

"And the cardinal never searched it."

"My brother wouldn't let a priest into his cellar if the Light itself were down there, and the cardinal knew it. He spent five months looking for a way into my brother's house that didn't go through my brother, and there isn't one." She gave the lamp to Mabry at the door. "He never once thought to look under his own feet. He blessed the clerks in the room above us twice a year. I watched him do it in September."

Mabry opened the door.

A low square room, very cold and very dry, walls and vault one piece of the hill's grey rock with the pick marks still in it. Iron chests round three walls with their lids down and the dust on them undisturbed. And in the middle of the floor, on two trestles out of somebody's kitchen, a long shape under heavy laundry canvas, with straw coming out at its ends in tufts.

Emeric stood in the doorway.

He had thought, coming down, that he would feel her. He could feel the box in Armand's coat from here, if he let himself, faint and warm a mile up the sky. He reached for this, and there was nothing. Not quiet. Nothing: glass and what was in it, and no more note than a chair has, or a coat on a peg. That was how she had kept it. There was nothing in this room for a delver to find, and the only delver who had looked had walked over it twice a year and found clerks.

He went across and put his good hand flat on the canvas where the glass would be. Under it the crystal was hard and smooth and colder than the room. He did not lift the canvas. He had seen her once already, through his father's eyes, in the overlay. It was not the place. There would be a place, and it was not a coin room under a hill with the first queen in the door.

When he took his hand away and turned round, Sabina was looking, not at him, but at the long shape on the trestles, with an expression he could not read and did not think she would have wanted him to.

"Your father used to stand," she said. "In the crypt. Every year."

"I know."

"I never once went in." She turned to go. "Not in July either. Mabry did it. I held the lamp."



They brought her out at ten that night, by the ramp she had gone down.

The K Endeavor came in over the castle's training yard a little after nine with her running lamps out, and lay over it at a roof's height, very big and very quiet, with her belly doors open under her on their chains and her hold lit dimly inside. Osric's team came down out of her belly in pairs, eight of them in black crestless plate, and landed in the treasury yard without a sound worth hearing; and after them, on its own stones, the anchor team's cradle, a long low sledge of ash planks with a lift stone set into each corner, that hull four's anchor teams had used to carry a tower's chest down on the sixth.

Imani had come across from her ward at seven to look at him, and had told him he could stand at the top of the ramp or lie on a bench in the K Endeavor's hold, and was offering no third thing; so he stood at the top of the ramp with Ward, who had the treasury's deal box under one arm, and Imani beside them with her bag. The first queen stood a little apart along the yard wall in a dark cloak with the hood back, holding a lamp. Nobody else in the yard had a light.

He listened to them come up through the hill under his feet, a close bright knot of notes stopping at the turns. At the ramp's foot Osric set the stones; Emeric felt him do it, a good clean setting, nothing wasted, the way Osric did everything now; and the cradle with its long shape in canvas rose a hand's breadth off the flagstones and hung there, light as a door on good hinges.

Then they brought it up the ramp, and it took four hands.

That was all it needed, on the stones. Arthur had the head, both hands flat on the cradle's end, walking backward up the ramp with his visor up and his face set and white in the lamplight. Bren had the foot on the left, one hand. Mira had the foot on the right, one hand, her left. Imani had told her to carry nothing heavy for a month, and Mira had asked at the ramp's foot, in a voice that carried, whether this was heavy; and Imani had said nothing, which was as near as Imani came to yes. Osric walked beside it with his hand an inch from the canvas, and the whole of it came up out of the hill at a walk, as slowly as a cart going up to a church.

Emeric had seen Arthur's face like that once before, in a lamplit yard on the Isle, at the door of the last shed, with a voice inside it saying his name. He did not say so. Nobody in this yard but Ward and Osric and the three had been in that one, and none of them said a word; and Arthur walked backward up the ramp with his two hands on the cradle and did not take his eyes off the canvas the whole way.

At the top, where the ramp's last slab had settled and the yard's had not, there was a lip of stone a hand high.

"Mind the step at the top," said the first queen from the wall, quite clearly. "The eight of them very nearly dropped her on it."

Arthur minded the step. The cradle went over it with an inch to spare, and nobody's hands moved.

In the open yard under the hull's belly Osric said, "Up," and the team rose round it on their held stones, eight black shapes with the cradle in the middle, a man's height, a roof's height, and in at the lit square of the doors; and the doors came up on their chains and shut, and the light went out of the yard.

The first queen stood by the wall a while longer, looking up at the dark underside of the hull with the lamp's light on her face from below. Then she lifted the lamp's glass with her other hand and blew it out, there in the yard, before she had turned to go, so that she would have to go up the dark stair without it.

"Good night," she said, to nobody in particular, and went in.

Emeric did not thank her. His father had been right, and it was very nearly the hardest thing he had done all day.



The cradle lay at the far end of the K Endeavor's hospital hold, past the last cot, in the space Imani kept clear for the bad ones, its stones let down and lashed and the canvas still over it. Imani stood by it with her hand flat on the canvas and her eyes shut.

"Held," she said, opening them. "Exactly as she went in. All of it." She did not take her hand away. "Nobody opens this. Not here. Not till we're home, and the dome's ready, and I've everything I need on a table beside her and the people I want in the room. I don't know what happens when it's opened. I'd like to find out with my hands washed."

"Nobody opens it."

"Good. You're grey again. Sit on that cot."

He sat on it. It belonged to a one-armed private of the Lieutenant's fourth company, who was asleep, and did not wake.

Armand came across from the K Amara on hull four's boat at a quarter to eleven, and walked down the hold between the cots with his coat buttoned and his hand flat on the outside of it, and stopped at the cradle's foot. He did not touch the canvas. He stood in the dim warm hold and looked at the long shape for a long time, and Emeric did not try to feel what was in him, and did not need to.

Then he came and stood by the cot.

"Well," he said. "That's both of her. You'll want to tell Ferra."

The K Endeavor's set lived in a little iron room at the hold's forward end behind a curtain, the codebook chained to the desk. The clerk was a square freckled girl from the Grayrock school, called up to the line room for the war, with her hair pushed up under a knitted cap, and she stood when he came in and sat when he told her to.

He wrote it on her pad left-handed, the way a man writes with a stick in sand, large and slow, in capitals. She did not read it until he gave it her.

"For the Chancellor," he said. "Her own hand at the other end, if she's there."

"She's always there at eleven, Your Grace. She waits for the night slips." The girl read it, and her face did nothing, because she had been taught by Ferra; and she turned to the book and began to code it, group by group, with her lips moving.

"Armand," said Emeric.

Armand was in the curtain's gap. He did not ask what for. He undid the hook, and Emeric put his left hand in among the grey flannel and took the stone out, and held it.

Warm. Small. Half a tone under.

The clerk read the groups into the horn in the flat voice they all learned, five letters and a pause; and at the far end of seven hundred miles of dark the line room's set clicked twice, which was Kaldo, and a voice that was not Ferra's said received and began to read them back, five letters and a pause. Emeric sat with his mother in his hand and listened to his sentence go away across the kingdom in pieces that meant nothing to anybody between here and there.

Tell F. I have my mother's mind in my hand and her body in the hold.

The read-back stopped.

It stopped in the middle of a group, at the third letter of five, and did not go on. The set hissed. Somebody at the far end laid something down on a desk, small and wooden, a pencil; he heard it across seven hundred miles. Then nothing. The clerk looked at the horn, and at her own pad, and very briefly at Emeric, and back at the horn.

Then the set clicked once and a different voice came on, reading groups: precise, unhurried, a little dry, a voice he would have known across the whole of the sky with his eyes shut. The clerk wrote them down and worked them out off the book, her pencil going along under each group, and Emeric watched the clear come, word by word, upside down.

RECEIVED. HALSE HAS STOPPED WRITING. HE WILL GO ON IN A MINUTE. W IS IN THE LOFT AND I CAN SEE THE LOFT FROM HERE. BRING THEM BOTH HOME. I WANT THEM ON THE MANIFEST. F.

She held the sheet out without a word, and he read it the right way up, and then again.

Then he gave the stone back to Armand, who laid it in its flannel and put the hook over and buttoned the box back into his coat on the left side.

"Ask her," Emeric said to the clerk, "to read me the last line twice."

The clerk looked at him.

"Go on," he said.

She coded it and sent it, and in a little while the set clicked once and the same voice came on and read the last groups again, slowly, twice; and the clerk did not trouble to write them down the second time, and nobody needed her to.

Out along the hold the sleepers breathed in their cots under the dimmed lamps, and past the last of them the long shape lay on the deck plates under its canvas with the straw coming out at its ends. Below the hull, in a palace with every clock stopped, a woman who had blown out her own lamp was going down through the dark toward the town and the chancery on its square, to tell her son what she had bought him. And up the river, in a house above the falls, at the end of a thread so fine that nobody but a delver would have felt it, and perhaps not one, a great cold note sat where it had sat for a week, and did not move; and Emeric did not go down the stair.
