Chapter 260 - The Read

Old Edrin touched the clasp of his band eleven times between the field below the north gate and the first ridge, and Emeric counted every one, and said nothing about any of them.

It was a new band. Armand had put it on him in the counting room in August with his own hands, the way he had put on the first, and it looked the same in every way, the plain dark metal, the blue beads and the one grey. Old Edrin had come home from the capital without the first one, down in the cabin while one of Fletcher's yard boys sat in his seat, and the men who brought the hull home said he had hardly spoken from the mooring to the north gate.

This morning he had walked out to the hull by himself, upright, at a quarter past five, and climbed down into the casting seat without help, and put his hands on the brass plate; and then taken the left one off again to touch the clasp.

"It's on," said Emeric.

"I know it's on." Old Edrin put the hand back. "I'm seeing that it's on. There's a difference, at my age."

Ferra was on the grass at the foot of the gangway with her book, as she had been for every departure since the first, in a grey shawl against the early cold. He read it upside down from the deck. VAREK. THE STRAIT. 2 SEP, 5.15. And under it, after a breath with the pencil lifted, a second line and a ring round it: BACK SUN 5, AFTERNOON.

"Sunday," he said.

"I heard you the first time. I'm writing it so you hear it." She looked up at him. "Afternoon. If it's evening I'll want a reason."

"There's a deserter at the other end."

"That's a reason for Friday." She shut the book. "It isn't one for Sunday."

Imani came down from the ward at twenty past with her hands red from the basin, and did not come aboard. She gave Old Edrin a jar of broth over the rail and told him the dose, which was all of it, by noon. She gave Emeric a long look from his boots to his hair, as if she were taking a reading to compare with another one later.

"Sunday," she said.

"Afternoon. Ferra's written it."

"I know what Ferra's written." She stepped back from the hull's side. "I'll have something on."

She went back up the field without looking round. He watched her go, and turned, and Saban was watching him do it, and Ward on the bench by the cabin was watching nothing whatever with great attention.

"Something on," said Saban.

"A pot. She means a pot."

"I'm sure she does, Grace."

Old Edrin lifted her off at half past five, with the sun not yet over the river and Ward's ring of men stepping back with their ropes, and at about two hundred feet began to die.

He did it less well than he had the year before. It was all there, the shoulders going down, the breath coming high and hard with the little catch at the top, the grey in the long face; a man in the field below would have seen what he expected. But the year before there had been somebody underneath it enjoying himself, and there was not now. There was an old man pretending to be tired on top of an old man who was tired, and the two did not quite lie flat on each other.



The day went north under them at the pace capital hulls went, a horse's gallop kept up from dawn, which was a long way in a day and nothing at all against the distance.

It was nine hundred miles to the strait, near enough, further than the capital and by no road. They went north by the sun, over the river flats and then a long grazing country where the sheep ran from the hull's shadow in grey waves and the children did not; and once a woman at a well waved a dishcloth, and Old Edrin lifted two fingers off the plate in a caster's weary thanks, and Emeric was glad of it out of all proportion.

Saban sat forward of the cabin with his case on his knees, reading through what he had on the fort, which was not much. A crown fort on the near side of the strait, the last on Randillia's north coast, rebuilt in the war; a signal mast; a garrison the crown's books called eighty and Saban's man in the capital called something less; and a general who had asked for it, some years ago, out of the palace yards, and had not asked for anything since.

"Asked," said Emeric.

"Asked. At his age, for a rock with the wind off the Isle coming in at the windows. Nobody in the capital could tell my man why." Saban turned a page. "He writes to the war rooms every quarter. Every quarter there's a line at the foot about the Isle's lost men, and every quarter it goes into the same press as the last. My man has seen the press. It's a big press."

Ward, on the bench by the cabin, said nothing; but he turned his head a little, the way he did when a name came by that he had known before Kaldo.

"You know him," said Saban.

"I know how he stands," said Ward, and turned his head back.

At noon Old Edrin drank all of the broth, because he had been told to; and in the afternoon he did not touch the clasp for nearly an hour, and Emeric noticed that too, and said nothing about that either.

They crossed the Wayford line in the last of the light, where it ran straight across a heath on its low bank with the telegraph poles beside it. A lamp was coming south along it with a string of lit windows behind, Haskell or Kemp; and they all watched it go under them, even Ward, until the heath was dark.



They did not come down.

They held her a thousand feet up all night with the lamps out, as they had on the night home from the capital, and for the same reason: a hull that came down at dusk was a hull that men could wait for in a field. Nobody could see a hull with her lamps out, and nobody looked up.

Old Edrin stopped dying as soon as there was nothing to die for, ate most of a pie out of Maren's basket, and fell asleep in the casting seat with a blanket over his knees and his fingers still on the levers under the plate.

Emeric sat in the bows with his back to the stem post and his sense out thin over the country: sheep on a hillside like small warm coals, a fox, a farmhouse with two notes in it awake and close together. Ward was on the gangway's head, awake. Saban had gone into the cabin and not taken his boots off.

At about midnight Old Edrin woke, all at once, as old men do, and looked about him, and found the hull where he had left her; and touched the clasp.

"Twelve," said Emeric.

Old Edrin looked at him for some time over the plate in the dark.

"You've been counting."

"Imani would have. She's not here. Somebody had to."

The old man made a sound through his nose that might have been a laugh, and settled the blanket, and did not go back to sleep.



It was Emeric who asked. He had wanted to for a year and had never had an old air mage awake and alone at midnight with nothing to do.

"Before the stern stones," he said. "On the old boats. What moved them?"

"A boy." Old Edrin did not have to think about it. "The caster held her up; that's the work, that's what they paid for. And in the bows sat a lad with a gift for air, and he moved her, because a caster's got nothing left over for going anywhere." He turned his long face toward the bows. "Three years I sat where you're sitting. On a cushion. The cheapest thing aboard after the rope."

"And you pushed her."

"Did I?" The old man seemed to find this very funny. "Go on, then. Pull your stern lever off and push her. Nobody's looking. I'll hold her up." He put his hands back on the brass, though there was nothing for them to do there. "I'd like to see a duke push a ship."

Emeric went aft and reached past the old man's knee to the second lever under the plate, the one that let the stern stone drive her, and drew it back to the stop. She went on slower and slower, until she hung over the dark country with nothing pushing her at all, turning very slowly, the way a boat turns on a mooring.

He went back to the stern and put his hand out over the rail.

Air was not his. It cost him three times what it cost Sorrel, and Sorrel had told him on the keep's leads, laughing, that the wind was not a thing to be pushed. He gathered a good round body of air behind the stern anyway, and put it against her, hard, the way a man puts his shoulder to a cart.

The hull went sideways and round in a long sick slew, her stern swinging out as if somebody had taken hold of it, and Saban's case came off the bench inside the cabin with a crash, and Saban said a word through the door that Emeric had not known he owned. Ward had a hand on his hilt before the slew was half done. Then she hung broadside, rocking, and the air Emeric had put into her was gone and had taken a good deal of him with it.

Old Edrin was laughing. He laughed with his whole long body, silently, with his head back and his eyes shut and his hands still on the plate, until he coughed, and nobody thumped him, so he thumped himself.

"Everybody young pushes," he said, when he could. "Every lad they ever sat in the bows. The first day, he pushes her, and she goes round, because nothing likes being shoved. Not a boat. Not a horse. Not a man." He wiped his eyes. "Shove a thing from behind and it goes where it likes, and where it likes is sideways."

"Then what?"

"You don't push a ship." The old man lifted one long hand off the plate. "You dig a hole in front of her and let her fall in."

He did it without any fuss at all. A little light came off the back of his hand, faint and pale and steady; and forty feet ahead of the bow Emeric felt with his sense a patch of the night thin out. It did not blow. The air there went away from itself, outward and forward, the way water goes from a stone dropped in a pond, and the air round the hull went into the place where it had been, and took her with it. She came round on her own, bow first, as if she had smelled the hole and wanted it, and slid into it and through it and on, smooth as a sledge on snow.

It cost the old man nothing Emeric could feel. A breath. Less than the broth.

"There," said Old Edrin. "She wants to go where you're going now. You've only to keep the hole ahead of her." He put his hand back on the plate. "Dig it."



The first hole Emeric dug, he dug too near and too deep. The hull put her nose down into it like a horse into a ditch, and everything loose aboard went forward a foot, Emeric included, and Saban's case came off the bench a second time.

"Less," said Old Edrin. "You're not burying her."

The second he dug too far off. Nothing happened at all. Somewhere out in the dark a hundred yards ahead a patch of night went thin and filled again, and a startled owl in a wood below said what it thought.

"More."

The third he dug where the old man's had been, forty feet off the bow, shallow, and let it go, and she went forward into it, a little lumpily, but straight. He dug the next before she reached the end of the first, and the next, and the hull went on over a black heath and a river with starlight on it in a long string of small soft falls that he could feel through the planks under his boots, each one a fraction of the cost of the push.

He had read about it once, in another life, in a book about wings: that the air went thin over a wing and the rest of the air did the work. He had read it at a desk, as a sum. He had never felt it in his feet.

"You've the knack," said Old Edrin. "Not much of it. But you've the knack." He shut his eyes. "Took me a week. I was seventeen and I hated the man who taught me."

"Who taught you?"

"The lad before me. He was nineteen." The old man smiled with his eyes shut. "He went into the tower that winter, and came out a High mage, and died at forty of being one. I've outlived everybody who ever told me what to do." He was quiet. "Except Armand. Armand'll see me out. He's said so."

Emeric put the stern lever back on, and the stone took her again, smooth and even and dull, and the night went on going north under them.

A hole, and the air to fill it, and whatever was in the way carried along. A hull had stones to hold her up; but a thing that could hold itself up could be carried the same way. He did not take that out and look at it, on a public ship, with a man at the seat who did not know what he could do with his own weight. He put it away with the other things he was keeping for later, and noticed that the place was getting full.



Old Edrin touched the clasp again at about two.

"Thirteen," said Emeric.

"I was going for a pie," said Old Edrin.

He said it to the plate. Saban's door had opened at some point in the last hour; Saban was sitting in it, on the sill, with his coat buttoned and his hat on his knee, and did not move.

"Eel, at the foot of the tier master's lane. Every night at nine for a year, and somebody knew it." The long face did not change. "Halfway down, a wind went through the lane. Nothing moved in it. Not a shutter."

Emeric waited.

"And my air stopped." Old Edrin lifted his hand off the plate and looked at it. "Sixty years I've had it in me the way a man has his hearing. Asleep, I've had it. Fevered. And it stopped between one step and the next, as if somebody had shut a door on it, and every lamp in the lane went out, and I thought, I'm dying, really dying, in a lane, after all that." He put the hand back. "Then I was sitting against the wall in the dark with my cuff buttoned, and my wrist was bare."

Nobody said anything.

"Fifty years I was nobody," said Old Edrin. "Nobody wanted a thing off me since I was twenty. And the first time in my life I'm carrying something worth the robbing, I let it go for an eel pie." He looked straight ahead over the bow. "I'd like you to know I know that."

"It wasn't the leaf they wanted," said Saban, from the sill.

"I know what they wanted."

"Then you know it wasn't you that let it go. They'd made their dead patch before you came into the lane. They'd have had it off you in the mooring's own yard." Saban turned his hat on his knee. "I'd the lane from my man an hour after, and not an hour before. If anybody let it go, I did. Put that in your head beside the pie."

Old Edrin did not answer that.

Emeric sat with his own wrist on his knee and the bead against the bone, and thought about what was in it, and in his head behind it, and in a few other heads in Kaldo asleep now. He had drawn a thing in August because of that lane, and it had gone to dust on the long table, and the no had been right.

"Fourteen," he said, when the old man's hand went to the clasp again.

"Stop that," said Old Edrin. But the hand came away.



The strait came up out of the morning a little before eleven on the Friday, grey under a grey sky, with a long low swell running down it from the west and white along every rock.

The fort stood on the last rock on Randillia's side: a square tower of dark stone with a signal mast on top, a curtain wall round a yard, and below the wall a shelf of turf above the shingle where the garrison drilled and dried its washing. A man on it waved a flag at them. A long way off across the water, so low and pale that Emeric took it at first for a bank of fog, lay the Isle.

Old Edrin brought her down onto the drying field in a long series of small careful corrections, each one, to the garrison lining the wall, the last drop of a man's strength, and set her down so gently that the shirts on the lines did not stir. Then he let his head go down, and two of the garrison came running with a chair.

He let them. But at the gangway's foot he turned his long face back, and Emeric saw him look along the wall, at the gate, at the lane down to the shingle: at where a man could stand, and where he could not. Then he let his head go down again.

Varek was at the gate.

He was in the same plain grey coat with the faded knot on the collar, greyer at the jaw again and leaner in the face, as if the wind off the strait took something off him every winter and did not give it back. He watched Emeric come up the field with his hands behind him, and Emeric felt himself read from the boots up, and did not mind it. He had sat on a rail in a drill yard through a whole winter of mornings at eight years old while this man watched other men march, and had never once been asked what he was doing there.

"General."

"Your Grace." Varek looked at the hull on his drying field, and at the old man being carried in under the gate. "That's a long road for one old caster."

"He's very good."

"He'll want to be." Varek did not say anything else about it. He looked at Ward instead, behind Emeric's shoulder, and something went across his face that Emeric could not read at all. "Captain."

"General," said Ward.

"You kept him alive."

"He did a good deal of it himself," said Ward. "Toward the end."

Varek nodded once, a report received, and looked at Emeric again, at the breadth of him, and at the coat, which was not Varek's. Varek's was in a press at home, mended at both cuffs, and tight across the back since the year before last.

"You've grown out of it," said Varek.

"Two years ago. I kept it."

"Hm." Varek turned to the gate. "He's in the tower. He's eaten, and he's been told you're coming, and he's said the same thing to me four times since breakfast. Come and hear him say it."



The deserter had a room on the tower's second floor, with a window, a bed, a stool, a table with a jug of water on it and a door that was locked from the outside. Varek unlocked it himself.

"He's not in a cell," he said, with the key in his hand. "He asked for a cell. The first week. He said he'd feel safer." He opened the door. "I've cells. I've never put a man in one who asked."

The man was sitting on the bed. He stood up when they came in, quickly, and then did not seem to know what to do with having stood, and stood there.

He was younger than Emeric had expected; not thirty, perhaps, with a soldier's cropped hair growing out unevenly and a soldier's shoulders gone a little thin, and big raw hands that he held in front of him, the right one round the fingers of the left. He wore a Randillian garrison shirt that did not fit him. His face was broad and plain and had been weathered by a wind like this one all his life, and his eyes went from Varek to Emeric to Ward in the door, and stayed on Ward, as most people's did.

"This is the man I wrote to," said Varek. "From the south."

"There are men there," said the deserter at once. His voice was thick with the Duvrenne burr, the long vowels, the soft ends of words. "Randillian men. On the Isle. Your men. From the war. They're kept. I saw them." He swallowed. "I've said it. I'll say it to anybody. I saw them."

"I know you did," said Emeric.

The man looked at him. He had been braced for something else.

"Sit down," said Emeric, and sat down himself, on the stool, so that he was lower than the bed; and after a while, with his eyes going to Varek, the man sat down again too.



He talked for an hour.

He talked freely; that was the strange thing, after the letter. He had come across the strait in March in a fisherman's skiff he had taken from a beach on the Duvrenne side, alone, at night, without knowing how to sail it, and walked up to this gate at dawn and told the first man on it what he had just told Emeric. He had told it every day since. He wanted it told. And every time he reached for the where and the how, it came apart in his hands.

"There was a fence," he said. "And the sheds. Long. Long sheds." He held his big hands apart, then wider, then dropped them. "I stood a door. At night. There was a lamp over the door and you stood under it." He frowned. "And the field. They went out to the field in the morning. With picks. And baskets." His hand went round the fingers of the other one again. "It shone. The field. When the sun was on it you couldn't look."

"Where was it?"

"On the Isle."

"Which end?"

"Ours." He corrected himself. "Theirs. Kesrath's." He looked at the floor. "They took us over at night. In the boats, with the lamps covered. And the same going home. You didn't see where. You weren't meant to see where. And you didn't ask, because the ones who asked..." He stopped. "I'm not a clever man. They didn't pick clever men for it."

"Who were the men in the sheds?"

"Randillians." He said it with relief, because it was the part he was sure of. "From the war. Some of them old. One talked to me once, through the planks, and he wasn't supposed to." The big hands tightened. "He asked me what year it was."

Varek, by the window, turned his head and looked out at the strait.

It went on like that. A pit at the end of the field, where the ones who died went; he did not want to talk about the pit, and Emeric did not make him. A smell when the wind was off the land, bad eggs and something sweet, and barrels going down to the boats at night marked for water that the guards were told never to drink. Rain, once, and the men standing out in it with their faces up and their mouths open, and nobody stopping them. He gave it all in pieces, each bright and whole and lying by itself, the way a man gives a dream at breakfast; and when Emeric asked him which way the sheds lay from the field, his face went slowly blank, and his hand went round his fingers, and he said that there were men there, Randillian men, and he had seen them.

"He's not lying," said Varek, at the door afterward, quietly, with his hand on the key. "I've had men lie to me for forty years. He's not got enough in him to lie with."

"No."

"Then you've heard what I have. It's all there and none of it's anywhere." Varek turned the key over in his fingers. "I've had two of my men sit with him with a chart of the Isle every evening for a month. He looks at it like a cow at a gate."

"He wasn't shown it from above," said Emeric. "He was shown it from a door. At night. He doesn't have a chart in him. He has a door."

Varek looked at him.

"I'd like the room," said Emeric. "Alone with him. An hour. Perhaps two."

The general stood in the passage with the key in his hand and looked at him for a long breath, the way he had looked at him in the posting-house yard ten years ago; and Emeric stood still for it, and found, a little to his own surprise, that there was nothing in how he was standing that he needed to hide from this man.

"You'll not hurt him," said Varek.

"No."

"I know you'll not." The old man held the key out. "I'm saying it so it's been said. He walked across that water to tell us, and he'll walk out of here whole, whatever you get or don't get out of him. I'll be on the stair." He put the key in Emeric's hand and closed Emeric's fingers on it with his own, which were dry and cold and very strong. "If he shouts, I come in. If he doesn't, I don't."

Ward, at the stairhead, met the general's eye: two old posts in the same fence. Then Varek went down three steps and sat on the fourth, with his back against the wall and his hands on his knees, and Ward stayed where he was.



He asked first.

He had been thinking since Wayford how to ask a man for a thing the man had no word for. He could not say room, or door, or stone. So he said what it would be like from where the man sat, which was the only part the man needed in order to say no.

"You remember more than you can say," he said. "Everybody does. It's all in there, the field and the sheds and the door, but it's in pieces, and you can't get it out in order. That's not stupid. That's how memory is kept, in everybody." He moved the stool a little closer, not much. "I can help with that. I can see what you remember, if you let me. As if I were standing behind you, looking over your shoulder at the same thing you're looking at. You'll be awake. You'll know I'm there. You'll be in this room, on this bed, the whole time."

The man's hand had gone round his fingers.

"Is it the Church's?"

"No."

"Will it hurt?"

"It mustn't. If it does, I've done it wrong, and I'll stop." Emeric held the man's eyes. "And I'll only look where you take me. The Isle. The camp. Nothing else. If I see anything else, your home, your mother, anything, I'll look away from it, and I'll not keep it." He waited. "If you want me out, say so, and I'm out. Or don't say it. Just push, the way you'd push a man off your arm, and I'll feel it, and I'm out."

The man looked at him a long time.

"The general," he said. "He knows what you're doing?"

"He knows I'll not hurt you. He's on the stair."

That seemed to be the thing that mattered. The man looked at the door, and at Emeric, and down at his own hands; and then, slowly, he took the right one off the fingers of the left and held it out, palm up, on his knee, the way a man holds out a hand to a surgeon to have a splinter looked at, because he did not know what else he was supposed to hold out.

"Yes," he said. "If it'll find them."

Emeric did not take the hand. He put two fingers on the inside of the wrist, lightly, where Imani put hers; and felt the man's pulse under them, quick and hard, a frightened man's, and waited until it came down a little.

"Shut your eyes," he said. "It's easier."



The room was where it always was.

The long table, and the shelves, and everything he had ever read lying open on them; the slow clean light. He went in through the bead at his wrist and stood in it, and then did the thing he had done once at Marrow by force, and once in Imani's ward by accident, and once since by her gift: he reached past it, along his own mana into the man's note under his fingers, and asked.

He felt the man give. It was clumsy. The man did not know how and could not have said what he was doing; he only did what Emeric had asked him to do on the bed, which was to look at the Isle; and because he was looking at it, it was there to be seen. Not the slide of the accident in Imani's ward. Not the door forced at Marrow. The man put out what he was looking at the way a child holds up a drawing, and Emeric took it, and laid it on the table.

A door. A lamp over it, in a tin cage, swinging a little in a wind. Planks. The planks of a long wall going away on either side into the dark.

That was all, at first. The man had been asked about the Isle and had gone to the place he had stood longest, which was a door at night.

"What do you see?" said Emeric. He said it on the bed and in the room at once; the room was slow, so that it came to the man as a voice out of a long way off, unhurried, the voice Imani used at the foot of a bed. "Under the lamp. Look down."

Boots. The man's own, Kesrath issue, cracked across the toe. And the ground they stood on, which was not sand and not earth: grey grit, sharp, crunching. And in it, catching the lamp, a hundred small flat lights.

Shard. Dead shard, the grey flakes the beaches were made of, that every soldier who had ever served on the Isle took home in his pocket for a charm; the camp stood on it.

"What do you hear?"

The sea. Close. Not surf on a beach; a slap and suck against rock, below and to the left. Men breathing behind the planks, a great many of them, asleep. Somebody coughing, the same cough, over and over, at the far end. A dog, up the slope.

Emeric laid it all down. He did not hurry. He felt the man's pulse under his fingers on the bed, a long way off, and it was slower now, steadier, the pulse of a man telling a thing carefully, and he let that set the pace.

"Where does the sun set? When you come off the door in the morning and stand the day watch, where does it go down?"

The man did not know. And then he did. Not as a fact: as an evening. The day watch at the fence, his back to the sea, and the sun going down in front of him, a long way off, over a long low country of sand going away into haze, and the shard field between him and it all gold and burning so that he had to look down at his boots.

So the sea was behind the camp and the land ran away west from it, and the field lay to the west, between the camp and the sunset. Emeric laid it down, and turned it, so that its west lay along the room's west.

"What's above you? Behind. On the skyline."

The man turned his head in his memory, at the door, at night, and looked up; and there, high up on the black of a hill against the stars, was a yellow lamp. One. Steady. Very high.

Emeric knew it.

He knew it the way a man knows his own window from the street. He had seen it from two miles up, falling, on his first night down to the Isle in the suit; and again in February from the forward port with Ward beside him; and for a long time from the rock at the gully's lip: Kesrath's tower on its headland, at the island's north-eastern end. He took his own memory of it off his own shelf and laid it on the table under the man's. They did not match; they were the same lamp seen from two sides of a hill. But a lamp and a hill seen from two sides tell a man where he is standing, if he has the other side, and Emeric had the other side.

The camp lay at the headland's foot, under the tower, with the sea at its back.

"Was there water? Fresh?"

A cistern, the man thought. And barrels. Barrels came down the hill from the old walls on a cart on rails, iron rails, pushed by hand, past the end of the sheds, and down to the landing; and the guards were not to drink from those barrels, ever, and a guard who had, once, for a dare, had been sick for two days. The smell came off them. Eggs and sweetness.

Emeric knew that smell; he had had it on his hands to the wrist at a hatch in a dune. He took the cluster of small lights off his shelf that he had seen from the forward port in February, low at the headland's foot inside the old fort's walls, where Zale's chandler had said Kesrath's well was, and laid them over the man's walls on the slope above the sheds, with the barrels coming down out of them; and they sat on each other like two halves of one drawing.

"How many lamps at night? Look along. Count them, if you can."

The man could not count them. He had never counted them; he had stood under one. But he had looked along them every night for a season, bored and cold, and what a man has looked at, he has, whether he counted it or not.

So Emeric counted them himself.

The man's lamp, over his door. Another door, and another, along the shed going away to the right, each with its lamp and its man. A gap, and a second shed. A second row behind, beyond a lane. The fence beyond, dark, and at its corners, high on legs above it, four lamps brighter than the rest, each with the shape of a man beside it. Four platforms. A low square building apart, with barred windows. A house beyond it with lit windows. And down the slope to the left, below the rock, a single lamp on a pole over black water, and the shapes of boats.

The landing. A cove, small, under the headland's flank, out of the swell.

He laid it all down: the landing as the man had seen it on the night he came, from the boat, with the lamps covered and a sailor whispering the stroke, in round the end of a reef and hard over under the rock; the fence, and the four platforms, and the sheds in their two rows, the barred house, the warden's house, and the pit at the field's end that the man would not look at; and under all of it his own island from two miles up, the headland, the lamp, the long pale shard field running west under the tower, glittering, as far as the eye went.

And the room held the camp.

Not a picture. A place. He could walk round it on the table in the slow light. He had flown over it twice and seen a cluster of lights, and a man who could not read had stood in it for a season and seen a door. Between them, it was a place.



It was in the cove that he went wrong.

Not far. He was laying down the landing, the reef and the two black arms, and the man's memory of it ran on past the landing the way water runs on past a sluice: the boat going out again, at the end of the man's season, and the lamps covered, and the strait in the dark, and the boat coming in to a quay with a town's lights behind it, and a street, and a door with a blue lintel, and a woman in the door with a child on her hip...

He stopped.

He stood still in the room and looked away from it, as he had said he would; turned his back on the quay and the street and the blue lintel, and let them go by him, and did not put out a hand to them, though he could have had the woman's face by turning his head. It was not his. He had told the man so on the bed, and the man had held out his hand because of it.

He had kept a stranger's kitchen in his head once for eight weeks, taken by force from a man who had not given it, and had not been able to put it down until it faded on its own, at its own price. He did not take the blue lintel. He let it go past, back into the man's keeping, and the man never knew it had been near.

And because he stood in the room with his back turned, what was in front of him was the sheds.

At the faces. The man had seen them at dawn every morning for a season, coming out of the doors under the lamps in a long grey shuffling line toward the field with their picks and their baskets, and they were all there, every face he had looked at and not looked at; and Emeric was going along them, quickly, in the slow light, one after another, before he knew he had begun.

Looking for one he could not have known if he found it. A boy when the crown came choosing men, who would not be a boy now, and would be grey, and ruined in the hands. Sela's face, perhaps. Arthur's jaw. Tam's.

He stopped that too.

He had told the man the camp, and the faces were in the camp; but he knew what he was doing, going along them like that, and it was not for the map. He came back to the table and stood at it and did not look at the line of grey men again.

"Thank you," he said, on the bed and in the room. "That's enough. I'm coming out now. Slowly."

He came out slowly. He let go of the man's note the way you let go of a hand, from the fingertips; and was on the stool in the tower room with the grey light of the strait at the window and two fingers on a wrist, and the pulse under them was sixty-two and steady.

The man opened his eyes.

He looked at the window, and at his own hand on his knee, and at Emeric, like a man who has been asleep in a chair and is not sure for how long.

"There was a reef," he said slowly. "At the landing. You come in round the end of it. Hard over under the rock." He looked at Emeric. "I'd forgot the reef."

"You hadn't. You'd put it down."

"I'd put it down." The man looked at his hand. Then, in a different voice, the voice of somebody who has been carrying a thing a long way and has just been told he can set it on the ground: "Did you see them?"

"I saw them."

"Randillian men."

"I saw them," said Emeric again, and that seemed to be all the man had needed for six months; because he put both his big hands over his face, and sat on the bed with them there, and did not make any sound at all, for a long time.

Emeric sat on the stool and waited, with his head beginning to ache behind the eyes, a dull plain ache, the price of laying his own island under another man's, and nothing worse; and outside on the stair, he could feel through the wall, the general had not moved from the fourth step.



He drew it that evening, in Varek's own room at the top of the tower, at Varek's own table, with the strait going dark in the window and a lamp at his elbow.

He had a pen in his coat, as he always did, and a bottle of ink, the plain grey; Kaldo's, out of the mill, the ink every clerk in four baronies used now and nobody north of Wayford did. He did not think about it. He uncorked it and drew.

The headland first, and the tower on it. The cove under the flank, the reef across its mouth, the line a boat must take round its end. The fence and its four platforms. Two rows of long sheds, the barred house, the warden's house. The old walls on the slope with the well in them, and the little rail coming down to the landing. The pit at the field's far edge, marked with a cross and nothing written beside it. And running west under the tower, the shard fields, hatched, as far as the paper went. He wrote the distances he could stand behind and left blank the ones he could not, inside a ruled border, the way Linus did.

Across the top, in capitals, he named it. KESRATH'S END. THE SHARD FIELDS UNDER THE TOWER. A CAMP.

Varek stood at his shoulder the whole time and did not say anything. When it was done he picked it up by its edges and took it to the lamp and read it the way he had read Emeric coming up the drying field: from the bottom up, slowly, missing nothing.

"He gave you this," he said.

"He had it. He didn't know he had it in order."

"And you put it in order." Varek did not ask how. He looked at the cove a long time, and the reef, and the four small squares at the fence's corners. "Four," he said. "I'd have guessed two." He put his finger near the cross by the field's edge and did not touch it. "And that."

"That too."

The general put the page down on the table, flat, and laid his two hands on either side of it, as Ferra laid hers on a book she had decided about.

"Ten years," he said. "Every quarter for ten years I've written to the war rooms that there were men on that island and nobody had found the bodies. This goes up on Monday by rider, with my name on the bottom and a line to say I'd hang my own coat on it." He looked at the page. "They'll read this one."

"And then?"

"And then the war rooms will argue about it for a year, and then they'll want a fleet. You don't put men ashore on Kesrath's end with two boats." He said it as a man says the weather. "And they'll see a fleet from that tower a day before it's in the strait."

Emeric looked at the cross at the field's edge.

He had seen it already, at the table in the room, before Varek said it. A fleet in the strait meant a tower that saw it coming. A tower that saw it coming had a day, perhaps two, and hundreds of men in sheds who were proof of a thing Kesrath's king swore had never happened, and a pit already dug. The crown would land at an empty camp. Or not empty. Kesrath was not a careless crown.

And there was a name on a toll ticket in this man's coat.

He did not say don't send it. He had no right to, and the old man had been waiting ten years to send exactly this. He stood at the table and knew, with the lamp hissing between them, that the K Discovery could be off that headland in the dark inside two months, and that he was going to be. He had known it on the stool in the tower room with the faces going past him. He had perhaps known it at Wayford, with the letter in his hand.

Varek was looking at him.

He looked for a long time, the way he had looked at the posting-house yard and at the rail, and whatever he read in Emeric's face, he did not put a name to it. He folded the page once, along the line of the fence, and put it inside his coat, on the left, in the pocket where Emeric supposed the toll ticket still was.

"I'll not ask," said Varek. "Whatever that is. I never have." He buttoned the coat. "Don't promise her."

"I haven't."

"Good." The old man turned to the window and the dark strait. "Go and eat. My cook does a fish stew that'll take the paint off a door. Your captain's had two bowls already and said nothing about either, which from him is a compliment."



They lifted off the drying field at six on the Saturday with the shirts back out on their lines, Old Edrin dying with all his old relish for the benefit of the whole garrison along the wall; and Varek on the gate with his hands behind him, who did not wave, because he never had.

The deserter was on the wall too. Varek had put him on the strength the night before, as a cook's man, with a garrison shirt that fitted. He stood at the end of the wall in it with his big hands at his sides, and watched the hull go up, and lifted one of them, once, and put it down.

Saban waited until the coast was gone behind them and the old man asleep at the plate in the afternoon sun. Then he came forward and sat down on the bench beside Emeric in the bows, and took his hat off, and looked at it.

"Grey ink," he said.

"It's the ink I carry."

"I know it's the ink you carry. It's the ink I sell." Saban turned the hat. "There's a page of it in a general's coat this morning, going to the war rooms on Monday, that anybody who's been to Wayford can tell across a room. And a general who's had one Kaldo page can have a second, and a pot of the black to write his answers in, as a gift. A knot on the chimney of that tower and we'd hear the war rooms think, four times a year, in his own hand."

"No."

"I'm only saying what it is."

"Not on Varek."

Saban turned the hat once more, and stopped.

"No," he agreed. "Not on him." He looked out at the country going by, a long brown moor with a drover's road across it and a line of cattle on the road. "You'll be there first."

Emeric did not ask him how he knew.

"You came down that tower stair with a face on you," said Saban. "I've sold horses to men with that face. They've already bought the horse and they're looking for somebody to tell them why." He turned the hat. "So. Why?"

Emeric was quiet for a while.

"A crown raid's a fleet," he said. "A fleet's seen from the tower a day out. A day's long enough to fill a pit. The ship isn't seen. She's never been seen." He looked at his hands. "That's the reason. It's true."

"And the other one?"

"There's a woman at a counter by the north well." He had not said it to anybody, and he found he did not like the sound of it, and said the rest anyway. "I took one of her sons up the mill road. I want to be the one who brings her the other. I want it to be me." He looked at the cattle going away behind. "That's the reason too. It's not as good."

Saban did not say anything for a long time.

"It's a better reason than most I've heard for most things," he said at last. "It isn't a reason you'd put on a page." He put his hat on, finally, and kept it there. "So don't. I'll keep it. It's no heavier than the rest."



They came over the last ridge at ten past four on the Sunday, low, with the whole of the north road out to watch Old Edrin die, and he did it beautifully, all the way down onto the field below the north gate.

He got up out of the seat by himself, afterward, when Ward's ring of men came with the chair. He waved the chair away and climbed out onto the grass on his own long legs, and stood, and touched the clasp on his wrist; and looked at Emeric, and took his hand away from it, deliberately, as a man takes his hand off a door he has decided not to open.

"I'll want to dig a few more holes," he said. "Before you need them." He did not say for what. "I'll not have a duke lean on my ship with his shoulder again."

Ferra was on the grass with her book. She wrote the time before he had reached her, and drew a line under BACK SUN 5, AFTERNOON., and looked up.

"Inside your time," she said. "That's good." She looked at his face. "You've a headache."

"A small one."

"Imani will want it," said Ferra, and shut the book. "She's had a pot on since two, and sent a boy up the hill twice to ask if you were in sight." She put her hand flat on his chest, briefly, over the pocket where there was no letter now. "Go across the lane. Tell me tomorrow, at seven. I'll ask for three plates."



The door was on the latch. The front room smelled of onions and thyme and something brown that had been in a pot since two, and the fire was lit, which it never was in September, and Imani was at the hearth with a cloth round the pot handle and her sleeves still rolled.

She did not turn round.

"Boots," she said.

He took his boots off. There were two chairs by the fire, as there always were, and the cushion of the second one had a dent in it now, a shallow one, that had not been there in the spring. He looked at it.

"Sit," said Imani, "and eat. Then talk. Not the other way round."

He sat in her father's chair. She put a bowl in his hands, and a spoon, and a heel of bread across the bowl's rim, and stood over him with her arms folded until he had eaten half of it; and then took his wrist, and counted, and looked into his eyes one at a time with her thumb under each, close, the way she looked at a patient who had come in from a fall.

"Seventy," she said. "The whites are bloodshot. The left more than the right. You've had it since Friday." She let go. "Three layers."

"Three."

"Not four."

"Not four."

"Eat the rest," said Imani, and sat down in her own chair, and ate her own, and watched him do it.

He told her afterward, with the bowl empty on the hearth and the fire going down. The hole, and the old man laughing. Varek on the gate. The deserter's hands. The room, and the door under the lamp, and the reef. He told her about the quay with the blue lintel, and turning his back on it, and she did not say anything, but her face changed a little.

Then he told her about the faces.

She listened to that without moving. When he had finished she was quiet for a long time, with her hands on her knees.

"Did you stop?" she said.

"Yes."

"By yourself?"

"By myself."

"Hm," said Imani. She got up and took his bowl and her own and put them in the basin, and stood at the basin with her back to him.

"In August," she said, to the basin, "I told you you were wrong. Before Ferra did. At my bench. You'll remember."

"I remember."

"I'd tell you again, first, if you were." She turned round, and leaned on the basin's edge, and folded her arms. "You asked him. In words he could say no to. You told him how to put you out. You let his house go by." She looked at him across the room. "You asked first. I've nothing to tell you, Emeric. I've been sitting here since two with a speech ready in case, and I've nothing to tell you."

"Was it a good speech?"

"It was very good," said Imani. "I'll keep it."



She took him by the wrist to the back room, two fingers on the inside of it as if she were walking a patient down a ward; and at the bed's edge she stopped and did not let go, and he felt her count.

"Seventy-four," she said.

"You're counting."

"I'm always counting." She looked up at him in the half dark from the door. "Say it. Not because I've walked you here."

"I want to." He turned his hand under her fingers and took hold of hers. "You?"

"I've had a pot on since two," said Imani. "What did you think it was for?" But she said yes as well, plainly, with her face turned up to his, because he waited for it. Then she put her hands flat on his chest and pushed, not hard, and he sat down on the edge of the narrow bed, and she stood between his knees.

"Lie back," she said. "I'm examining you."

He lay back. She undid his shirt the way she did a dressing she had done a thousand times, every button, quickly; and then, when it was open, very slowly, with the flat of both hands on him from the collarbones down, as if something might have moved since Thursday and she meant to find where it had gone. Her hands were warm from the pot. The old burn on the back of the left one showed paler than the rest of her in the light from the door.

"Ribs," she said, with her palms on them. "Sound." Her thumbs went along the bone under his chest. "You've not eaten properly since Thursday. I can feel it." Her hands went lower, and stopped at his belt, and stayed there. "The stew was a diagnosis."

"What else did you find?"

"A great deal," said Imani, "and none of it your business," and leaned down and put her ear flat on his chest over the heart, and listened, and did not count aloud. He put his hand up to the cloth that tied her hair and pulled it free, and the curls came down all at once over his chest and his throat; and she lifted her head and looked at him through them without any vanity at all.

"That's interfering with a physician," she said.

"Report me."

"I will. To Ferra. In the morning." She took two of his locs in her fingers at the back, where they reached nearly to the middle of his back now, and wound them round her hand and held on, and kissed him; and it was a long while before the examination went any further.

The bed was the width of a ledger, and they laughed at it before they were properly in it. When it came to it he moved her, because there was no other way to manage it in that bed: his hands under her and her whole weight in his arms, turning them both, laying her against the wall in the middle of the mattress. His hair came down round her face on both sides. She put her hand up into it and held it back off her face with her wrist, so that she could see him, and kept it there.

He took his time. Nothing was waiting for either of them outside the shutters; he had been three days in the air and an hour in another man's head, and she had been at her basin and her pot, and neither of them was in any hurry to be anywhere else. The rest of their clothes went on the floor, because there was nowhere else for them to go. She was impatient with his and he was slow with hers, on purpose, until she told him so in the cross voice and he went slower still, and she laughed at him with her head back against the pillow, and then stopped laughing. She found his pulse with her mouth, under his jaw, where her fingers usually went, and he felt her lips move on it, counting, and lose it, and start again.

When he went down the bed she let his hair fall and put her fingers in it from above instead, and held on, and did not let go for a long time; until she said his name, low, once, and her heels came down hard into the mattress and her whole body went taut under his hands, and then gave, all the way, and she lay with her forearm over her eyes and her breath coming in pieces.

"Come here," she said. "I've not finished the examination."

He came back up the bed and she pulled him the rest of the way, by the shoulder and the hair, not gently; and they were still for a long breath, joined, her eyes open on his. Then she found his wrist on the pillow beside her head without looking, and put her two fingers on the inside of it, and he felt her start to count.

She got to eleven and lost it. She started again and got to six. The third time she did not get to anything at all, and laughed against his jaw, breathless, and kept her fingers there anyway, pressed hard on the place where she had always looked for him; and when she went over again she took his whole wrist in her hand and held it as if it were the only thing in the room still keeping its count, and he went with her.

Afterward she lay half on him, because there was nowhere else for half of her to go, with her ear on his chest.

"Ninety-four," she said after a while. "Coming down." She did not lift her head. "I counted that one properly. All sixty. I wanted to know I could."

"Could you?"

"Just." Out in the lane a late cart went by, and somebody's door shut. "You're going yourself," she said. "To the Isle. When it's time."

He had said that to Saban and nobody else, and she had it anyway, off his chest, in the dark, the way she had everything.

"Yes."

"I know." Her hand moved on his chest, flat. "I'll not tell you you're wrong. You're not." She was quiet. "You'll want medics. We'll talk about which, and who, and on what. Not tonight."

"In the morning?"

"In the morning I'm telling Ferra you interfered with a physician," said Imani, and put her head back down. "Both of us. Before the table." Her fingers found his wrist again, lightly, not counting. "Go to sleep. You've a headache."

He went to sleep. He could not remember afterward when he had last gone first.



Ferra had three plates out at seven, and the eggs under a cloth, and the book open at a clean page.

She heard them both out, standing: the read and the map and the cross at the field's edge, Varek's pocket, Saban's hat; and then the rest, from Imani, plainly, in her report voice, the pot and the chair and the bed, and that she had examined him and found him sound and been interfered with. Ferra listened without moving, and then looked at Emeric.

"And you."

He told her. It took him longer than it had taken Imani and said less.

Ferra wrote. He read it upside down. THE CAMP. KESRATH'S END. 4 PLATFORMS. And under it, after a while, a column with nothing in it yet, headed in her square capitals: KIT.

"The new moon's at the end of October," said Ferra. "That's the dark you'll want." She did not look up. "Sit down, both of you. And eat, Imani, because I know you didn't. He did. I can see it on him."
