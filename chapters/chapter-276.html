Chapter 276 - Valves

The bulb was the size of a hen's egg and it did nothing at all.

Tess sat in her chair at the long bench in the bulb works with her chin on her fist and looked at it. It stood in its wooden clamp in the middle of the bench under the window, with its two wires coming out of the bottom of it through Ivo's glass pinch, and inside it, in the vacuum, a filament like a bulb's filament, a little coil of tungsten, and round the filament, not touching it, a small cylinder of nickel sheet, open at both ends, like a tiny chimney with the filament standing up inside it.

The filament was lit. It glowed a dull orange on four cells, not white, because she didn't want light out of it; she wanted it hot. The cylinder sat round it in the dark of the bulb, catching the orange on its inside, and did nothing.

"It's doing something," said Emeric, behind her.

"It's doing nothing."

"Put the cylinder on the plus side of a battery. A big one. And the filament on the minus. And a meter between."

She did. She did it because he was standing there with his hands in his pockets and that face on, the face Ivo called the face, which meant he knew and wasn't going to say; and the needle on the meter lifted off its pin and stood there, a little way up the scale, steady.

Tess looked at it.

"There's a current," she said. "Across nothing. There's no wire. There's nothing in there. It's a vacuum, Ivo's best, he took three days on it." She leaned closer. "It's going across the vacuum. From the hot thing to the cold thing."

"Now turn the battery round."

She turned it round. Plus on the filament, minus on the cylinder.

The needle went back to its pin and stayed there.

She sat back in her chair.

"One way," she said. "It only goes one way. From the hot one." She thought about it for perhaps four heartbeats, and he watched her think, and she knew he was watching and did not care. "It's coming off the filament. Something's boiling off the filament, because it's hot, and going across to the cylinder, but only if the cylinder wants it. If the cylinder's plus. Like..." She stopped. "Like steam off a kettle that only goes to a cold window."

"That'll do."

"It's a valve," said Tess. "It's a non-return valve. For current." She looked up at him. "You could turn alternating current into direct with this. One way only. It'd let half of it through and stop the other half."

"Yes."

"That's useful," said Tess. "That's very useful." She looked at the bulb in its clamp a while longer. "It's not what you want it for, though."

He didn't say anything. He looked at the bulb.

"What do you want it for?"

"A third wire," he said; and went out of the bulb works into the September noon, and left her there.



It took her eight days.

She knew he had done it on purpose. He did it more and more now, since she had become the Motion Shop and not only Bram's girl in bay three: gave her the door and not the room, and went away. She had been furious about it the first year. She was furious about it now, a little, for the first two days; and then she forgot to be, because the problem was better than being angry.

A third wire. Where? Not outside the bulb; outside was nothing. Inside. Between the hot thing and the cold thing, then, because that was the only place inside there was. A third thing in the path of whatever was boiling off the filament on its way across to the cylinder. And if you put something in somebody's path...

She drew it on the fourth day, on the back of a delivery chit from the coke ovens. A coil of fine wire wound round the filament in a loose spiral, between the filament and the cylinder, like a little cage, with a third lead coming out through the pinch. Ivo looked at the drawing and said, "I can seal three. I can't promise you it'll hold a vacuum," and blew four, and two of them held.

And the third wire did nothing either, the first afternoon. She put it on a cell, plus, and the needle went up a little; on minus, and the needle went down. Which was interesting and no use, because you could do that with a resistor.

Ambrose heard it first.

Ambrose Lark came into the bulb works on the sixth afternoon because he came into everywhere, sooner or later, with his strap over his shoulder and his hair standing up, and stood behind her with his hands behind his back and his head on one side; and Tess, who was trying a cell of a different size on the third wire for the twentieth time, said without turning round, "Don't touch anything."

"I wasn't going to." He was quiet a moment. Then: "What's that doing?"

"Nothing. It goes up when I put the grid on plus and down when I put it on minus. That's all it does."

"No," said Ambrose. "It's doing more than that."

She turned her chair round.

He was standing very still with his eyes half shut, the way he stood under a thunderhead with his strap off, feeling for where it would come down. He had the lightning in him. He had always had it; it was why he went up the rods, why he had flown kites into storms as a boy and been struck four times and laughed every time. He could feel current the way other people felt a draught.

"Put it on the little cell again," he said. "The small one. Minus. Then take it off. Then put it on." He kept his eyes half shut. "Slowly."

She did it. On. Off. On.

"There." Ambrose opened his eyes. "When you touch the little wire, the big current moves. A lot. More than you put in." He looked at her. "You're putting in a whisper on the little wire, and the big wire's shouting it back."

Tess sat there in her chair.

Then she took the big battery off the cylinder, and put on a bigger one, and put a telephone receiver in the cylinder's circuit, the earpiece off the spare set in the store; and took a microphone, a carbon button, the cheapest thing in the works, and wired it to the third wire through one cell, so that when anybody spoke into the button the current through the third wire would shake a little with their voice.

"Say something," she said to Ambrose. "Into that. Quietly."

Ambrose bent to the button and said, in a whisper you could barely hear across the bench, "Tess Kerrow is the cleverest girl in the four baronies."

And in the earpiece on the bench, loud, louder than he had said it, tinny, buzzing, unmistakable, his voice said cleverest girl in the four baronies back to her; and she put her hand over her mouth.

"Again," she said, through her hand.

"Tess Kerrow..."

"Not that. Something else."

He said the weather, and it came out of the earpiece louder than he said it. He said the first verse of a song the barge crews sang, and Tess laughed so hard she had to hold on to the bench, and the earpiece sang it louder, and Ivo came out of the glass house to see what was the matter.

"It makes things bigger," said Tess to Ivo, wiping her eyes. "It takes a little thing on the third wire and makes it big on the plate. The same shape. Just bigger." She looked at the bulb in its clamp. "It's an amplifier. It's a whole amplifier in a bulb. With no moving parts. Nothing to wear." She stopped. "And you can put one after another. The second one makes the first one's big thing bigger. And the third..."

She stopped again. She had remembered something.

Ivo was looking at her.

"Nothing," said Tess. "I just thought of something. Go back to your glass." She took her book out of the bag on the side of her chair, the black one, the one she had kept since she was fifteen, and opened it at the back, where she wrote the things that weren't for anybody yet, and wrote a line.

It said: A valve could hear a muscle twitch.

And under it, smaller: Abel.

She shut the book and put it back in the bag, and did not tell Emeric. Not that day. Not the next. She had a reason. She did not know yet if she was right, and she had been wrong in front of him before, and she was not going to be wrong in front of him about Abel Dunning's hand.

Wren saw her write it. Wren was at the end of the bench with the soldering iron, where she had been every afternoon after school since the summer had ended and she should have stopped coming and hadn't; and she saw Tess write in the back of the black book, and saw her face while she did it, and looked away again at once, at her soldering, and did not ask.

Tess noticed that too.



The grids were the trouble.

The third wire, the little cage round the filament: Tess wound it by hand, at first, on a knitting needle, with fine wire off a spool, and then slid it off the needle and on round the filament, and Ivo sealed it in. And no two of them were the same. The turns were a hair closer in one and a hair wider in another, and the cage sat a hair nearer the filament in this bulb than in that, and so every valve amplified a little differently; one shouted, one muttered, one sang flat. You could build a set with three valves in it and spend a day matching them. You could not build fifty.

"I can't wind them the same," she said to Emeric, on the Tuesday of the second week, cross, at the forge-room door. "My hands aren't the same twice. Nobody's are. And it has to be the same, every one, or you can't swap a dead one for a new one without taking the whole set apart."

So the forge made her a mandrel. Not a valve; a tool for making valves. A steel rod the thickness of a knitting needle with a fine spiral groove cut along it, the turns exactly spaced, every one the same as every other to a hair; and a jig to hold it, with a crank, and a guide that fed the wire into the groove as the rod turned. You wound the wire into the groove with the crank and slid the cage off, and it was exactly the same as the last one and the next, as like as two coins from one die.

She wound forty on the first afternoon. Wren wound twelve, and only spoiled one.

And the night after the mandrel, while she was winding, Tess remembered the jar.

The jar on the forge-room shelf with the grey bar in it that had cost a night's stones, the silicon, the purest thing in Kaldo, that Ferra had written Nothing yet against. Tess had been in the forge room when they cut the end off it. She had watched the bead of molten silicon go along the bar, ten times, and thought it was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen a machine do, and had not said so, because Bram had been there.

She went up to the castle the next morning and asked for a sliver of it.

"What for?" said Emeric.

"I don't know," said Tess, which was true. "I want to see what it does with a current."

He looked at her for rather a long time, and then went and got the jar, and took the bar out with tongs, and broke a sliver off the end of it no bigger than a grain of rice, and gave it to her in a twist of paper.

She had it on her bench that afternoon. She did with it what she did with anything new: put it in a circuit and put a meter across it and put the current through it one way and then the other. It was strange stuff. It let current through a little, not much, and differently one way from the other, more or less, depending on where she touched it with the probe; and when she touched it with a fine wire, a very fine one, a single strand off the spool, very lightly, at one particular spot, it let current through one way and almost none the other.

One way only. Like the bulb. Like the first bulb, the one with two wires.

But with no filament, and no vacuum, and no battery to heat anything.

She sat looking at it. A grain of grey stuff and a hair of wire, doing what Ivo's bulb did, cold.

Then she did the thing she had not told herself she was going to do. She took the long wire off the works' roof, the one that went up the lightning rod to the top of the old powerhouse chimney, that the wireless used; and put it through the grain and the hair of wire, and through a telephone earpiece, and to the earth; and put the earpiece to her ear.

It was faint. It was very faint. It was so faint she thought for a moment it was her own blood in her ear. And then she knew what it was: the K Discovery, out over the marsh on her evening run, a dozen miles off, sending her position to the line room on the wireless the way she did every evening, in the long and short buzz of the code; and Tess Kerrow was hearing it, in the bulb works, through a grain of grey stuff the size of a grain of rice, and a hair of wire, and nothing else. No valve. No battery. Nothing.

She took the earpiece off her ear and looked at it.

Then she put it back on and listened to the K Discovery until the ship had finished and gone quiet; and then she went up to the castle in her chair in the dusk, very fast, and put the twist of paper with the grain in it on the table in front of Emeric at supper, and the hair of wire, and the earpiece, and said, "Listen."

He listened. He listened for a long time. Then he took the earpiece off and put it down, and looked at her, and his face did something she had not seen it do before; not the face. Another one.

"Where's the battery?" said Bram, across the table.

"There isn't one," said Tess.

Bram looked at the grain in its paper. He looked at it for some while. Then he looked at Emeric.

"Did you know it did that?"

"Yes," said Emeric.

"Did you tell her?"

"No."

Bram grunted, and went back to his supper, and after a moment, without looking up, reached over and put his hand on top of Tess's head and left it there for the count of three.

Emeric went up to the forge room after supper and wrote one line in the forge book, under the zone-refining run, in the column for what a thing had turned into. Ferra read it in the morning. It said: Crystal detector. Tess. He did not explain it, and when Ferra asked him what a crystal detector was he said, "That," and pointed at the grain in its paper, which the line room had already taken and put in a little brass box of Ottoline's with a screw to set the wire, as a spare receiver, in case.



Osric came in on the Thursday to hold the lamp under the bench arm, and Wren had it.

He stood in the Motion Shop's door with his hands at his sides and looked at the arm, and at the girl lying on her back on the shop floor under its great iron shoulder with the lamp held up, tracing a cable for Dov with the lamp's light, quite happily, the way she had all summer; and Tess, at her bench, watched him look and kept her face very still.

Osric had come in to hold that lamp, on and off, for a year. Since the spring, more on than off. There had always been a lamp that needed holding under something, or a crate that needed lifting, or a long piece of iron that needed somebody at the other end of it, and Osric had always happened to be passing, in his garrison coat, on his way from somewhere to somewhere else that turned out to go past the Motion Shop's door. Nobody had ever remarked on it. Everybody had noticed it. Bram had noticed it so loudly, without saying anything at all, that Tess had once thrown a spanner at him.

And now Wren had the lamp. Wren was fifteen and fearless and lay under the arm as though it were a hayrick, and was better at holding a lamp than Osric was, because she was smaller and could get her arm in further.

Osric stood in the door a moment longer.

"Afternoon," he said.

"Afternoon," said Tess.

He came in and stood by her bench, and looked at the arm, and at Wren under it, and at nothing; and did not say anything, and did not go. He was very good at not saying anything. He was very bad, Tess thought, at not going.

She let him stand there for long enough to be sure he wasn't going to say anything. Then she reached over to the end of the bench, where there was a tray of fifty of Ivo's new valve sockets, Bakelite, brown and shiny, each with its four little brass tags sticking out of the bottom that wanted a wire soldered to each; and pushed the tray across to him, and the second iron, and the solder.

"Those," she said. "Four wires each. Red to the long tag. I'll show you once."

Osric looked at the tray.

"I'm a soldier," he said.

"You're a soldier with steady hands who's standing in my shop doing nothing." She took a socket out of the tray and soldered the four wires on it in about a minute, while he watched, and put it down in front of him. "Like that. Fifty. You're useful now." She turned back to her own bench. "Don't let it go to your head."

He sat down on Dov's stool, which was too low for him, with his knees up, and picked up the iron.

He soldered fifty sockets that afternoon, slowly, very neatly, one after another, with his tongue in the corner of his mouth; and Tess did not look at him once, all afternoon, except about every minute and a half.

From under the arm, at about four o'clock, Wren said to the arm's shoulder, quite clearly, "She's going red."

"Hold the lamp," said Tess.

"I am holding the lamp."

"Then hold it quieter."



Tess went to see Imani on the Friday, because she needed to know about forearms.

She went without telling anybody what for. She had the black book in the bag on her chair, and a valve set in a box on her knees, two valves and a battery and a meter and a pair of brass discs on wires, and she went up the hospital's ramp at the end of the day when the wards were quiet and found Imani in her surgery writing up the day's cases, and shut the door, and said, "I need you to keep something."

Imani put her pen down.

Tess told her. Not all of it; enough. A man in the town, who had lost a hand. The muscles in what was left of his forearm still moved when he tried to move the hand he hadn't got; he'd said so, and she'd seen it, a little flicker under the skin when he forgot and reached for a cup with the wrong arm. And the flicker was electrical. Everything a muscle did was electrical; Emeric had told her that years ago, in the electricity book, in a sentence she had read a hundred times. Too small to feel, too small for any meter in Kaldo. But the valves made small things big.

"So I want to hear it," said Tess. "The flicker. I want to put two discs on a forearm, on the skin, and put what they pick up through two valves, one after the other, and see if the needle moves when the muscle moves." She looked at her box. "But I don't know where to put the discs. I don't know which muscle. I don't know anything about the inside of an arm except that it's got bones in it." She looked up. "And I don't want anybody to know yet. Not Bram. Not him. If it doesn't work I don't want..." She stopped.

"You don't want him to know you tried," said Imani.

"I don't want him to have known it wouldn't work and not said."

Imani looked at her a long moment, and then got up, and took the box off Tess's knees, and put it on the surgery table, and opened it.

"I'll keep it," she said. "Who's the man?"

"I'm not saying."

"Good." Imani picked up the brass discs and looked at them. "The muscles that close the hand are on the inside of the forearm, here, about a third of the way from the elbow. The ones that open it are on the back." She touched her own arm, and then held it out. "Try mine."

They tried hers. Tess put the two discs on the inside of Imani's forearm with a smear of the ward's salt paste under each, as Imani told her, and turned on the valves, and they warmed, orange in the dim surgery; and the needle sat on its pin.

"Make a fist," said Tess.

Imani made a fist.

The needle twitched.

It was a small twitch. It went up a little way off its pin and came down again as her fist closed, and lay there; and Tess and Imani both looked at it, and then at each other.

"Again," said Imani.

Imani made a fist, and the needle twitched. And again. And when she made it hard, it went higher; and when she only thought about making it and didn't, it twitched, a very little, like a dog dreaming.

"It can hear you think about it," said Tess, very quietly.

"It can hear the muscle get ready." Imani was watching the needle the way she watched a pulse. "Before it moves. It can hear the nerve tell it." She sat back. "Tess. This is..."

And the door opened, because it was the end of the day, and Emeric came in to fetch Imani for supper as he did most evenings now, and stopped.

He looked at the two of them, and the box on the table with the valves glowing in it, and the brass discs on Imani's arm, and the meter; and Tess felt her whole face go hot, and knew that this was it, he'd seen, it was over.

"Nerve study," said Imani.

She said it without turning round, perfectly calmly, in her ward voice, the one she used to tell a man he would need a stitch. "Tess built me a valve that can hear a muscle. I'm testing it. Sit down." She pulled the discs off her own arm. "Roll up your sleeve. I need a second arm."

"Imani..."

"Sleeve," said Imani.

He sat down on the stool and rolled up his sleeve, and gave her his arm, because nobody in Kaldo, not the duke, not anybody, argued with Imani in her surgery; and she put the two discs on the inside of his forearm with the salt paste, and pressed them down with her thumbs, and said, "Make a fist."

He made a fist.

The needle went up off its pin like a hare out of a hedge, and hit the top of the scale with a little click, and stayed there.

Everybody looked at it.

"Ah," said Tess.

"Gently," said Imani. "Make it gently."

He made it gently. The needle hit the top of the scale.

"You're very strong," said Imani, "since April." She was looking at the needle with great interest. "Your muscles are all..." She thought. "Loud. Relax your hand."

He relaxed it. The needle came down, slowly, to about halfway, and stayed there, trembling.

"All of it," said Imani. "You're not relaxed. You're never relaxed." She put her hand flat on his forearm, over the discs. "Let go."

"I am letting go."

The needle trembled at halfway.

"Again," said Imani.

He let go. The needle did not move.

"Again."

And the trouble was, Tess saw, that every time Imani said again, and put her hand flat on his arm, and leaned in to look at the needle, with her face close to his shoulder and her sleeves rolled up, the muscle in his forearm did something; and the needle jumped, every time, a sharp little twitch upward, which had nothing whatever to do with his fist, and which he could not stop.

Imani noticed about the fourth time.

She noticed in a way Tess had never seen her notice anything, which was by going completely still, with her hand on his arm and her eyes on the needle; and then saying, very quietly, without looking up, "Again"; and leaning in another inch.

The needle jumped.

"Interesting," said Imani.

"Imani," said Emeric, in a strangled voice.

"Hold still. This is science." She leaned in another inch, until her mouth was beside his ear, and said into it, very low, "Again."

The needle hit the top of the scale and stayed there.

Tess said, to nobody, "I'll come back," and turned her chair round, and opened the door, and went out of the surgery, and shut the door behind her, and went down the ramp so fast that the night porter at the bottom flattened himself against the wall; and did not hear, behind her, through the surgery door, Imani laugh, which she very seldom did out loud, or the meter's needle go click against the top of its scale one last time and stay there, for quite a long while, because by then nobody in the surgery was looking at it.

She went back for her box in the morning.

It was on the surgery table, closed, with a note on it in Imani's hand. The note said: Inside of the forearm, a third from the elbow, for closing. Back of the forearm for opening. Salt paste. Two valves are enough. Your secret's kept. Thank you for the rest. I.

Tess read it twice, and went pink, and put it in the back of the black book under Abel, and took the box home.



The first voice came down out of the Band on the last Saturday of September, at half past nine in the evening, to the line room at the top of the keep.

They had built the set in three weeks. Two sets: one for the line room and one for the K Discovery, each with its valves in a row along a varnished board like teeth, and Bakelite knobs Ivo had pressed in a mould, brown and shiny, with a white line on each to show where it was set; one valve to make the carrier, the wave the wireless had always made, steady now instead of the old spark's crackle; one to put a voice on it, so that the wave went up and down with the voice like a rope shaken at one end; and in the receiver, three valves in a row, each making the last one's whisper louder, and a loudspeaker on the end of it: a paper cone off a telephone earpiece, but big, the size of a dinner plate, in a wooden box.

The K Discovery had gone up at dusk with the ship's set aboard and Linus Vance at it, because Linus had kept the ship's weather and the ship's log since the first voyage and talked to anybody about anything; up past the cloud, past the cold, into the Band, four miles over the barony, where the air was thick with the other thing and the stones refilled themselves as you watched. And in the line room at the top of the keep, with the shutters open on the night, the six were there, and Tess, and Ambrose, and Bram, and the line room's two clerks, and Wren on the stair outside because there was no room for her inside and she had refused to go home; and the loudspeaker in its box on the table hissed.

It hissed for a long time. It had been hissing since nine. It was the sound of the whole sky, Ambrose said, every storm in the world at once, very far off, and he could have listened to it all night.

At half past nine the hiss changed.

It went thick, and wavered, and something came up through it, the way a face comes up through water; a voice, very small and far and full of crackle, a man's, flattened by the paper cone into something like a voice in a tin.

Kaldo, said the voice. Kaldo, Kaldo. This is the Discovery. This is Linus. Can you hear me? We're in the Band. Can anybody hear me?

Nobody in the line room moved.

Ferra had her pen in her hand and did not write anything. Bram had his mouth open. Armand, in the chair by the window, had shut his eyes.

And Tess, at the set, with her hand on the transmitter's key, which she had built, on the varnished board she had built, under the valves she had wound the grids of with a crank on a mandrel, leaned to the microphone, which was a carbon button in a brass cup, and said into it the first word anybody had ever said from the ground to a ship in the sky.

"Yes," said Tess.

The loudspeaker hissed.

Then, from four miles up, small and tinny and absolutely unmistakable, Linus Vance's voice said, Is that Tess? Tess, I can hear you. I can hear you as if you were in the cabin. Gods above, girl. And then, away from his own microphone, to somebody else in the ship, very faintly: It's Tess. It works. Tell Edrin.

Wren, on the stair, said, "Oh," very loudly, and then put both hands over her mouth.

And then everybody was talking. Bram had his hand on the back of Tess's chair and was saying something nobody could hear, over and over, and Ambrose was laughing, and Saban had got hold of the line room's senior clerk by both shoulders for no reason; and Tess sat at the set with her hand on the key and her face wet, and said into the microphone, quite steadily, because somebody had to, "Discovery, this is Kaldo. What's your weather?"

And Linus told her.



Ferra wrote a cipher that night.

She did it at the line room's table after the others had gone down, with the loudspeaker still hissing beside her and Linus reading the ship's position every quarter of an hour, by Tess's order, until the ship came down at midnight. Emeric sat with her. Tess had gone home with Bram, and Wren with Rylan, who had come to the keep's foot at eleven to collect her with the face of a man who has been looking for his daughter for an hour.

"Anybody could hear it," said Ferra.

"Nobody's got a receiver."

"Nobody's got one now." She was writing in a fresh book, a small one, ruling columns. "Tess made one out of a grain of grey stuff and a hair of wire in an afternoon. With nothing. No battery." She ruled another column. "One day somebody else will. Somebody in Grayrock with a wire on a chimney. Somebody in Duvrenne. And everything we've ever said, anybody will have heard." She looked up. "So from tonight we don't say anything that matters in words they'd understand."

"It's slower."

"Everything I do is slower. That's why it's right." She went back to the columns. "It'll be letters for letters, at first. Then a book of words; one word for a ship, a number for a place. Then a key that changes every day. I'll have the first one by Monday." She did not look up. "And I want it on every ship we ever build. Before we need it."

He watched her rule.

"You've thought about this," he said.

"Since the wireless." She turned a page. "Since the first day it buzzed. I've been waiting for somebody to make it so you could hear a voice, so I'd have to." She looked at him then, briefly, over the book. "You said once that the most dangerous thing in the world is a secret everybody can hear. You'll have forgotten. I didn't."

He had forgotten. He did not say so. He sat with her in the line room until the ship came down, with the hiss of every storm in the world very far off on the table beside them, and watched her build the first page.



He went down to the gallery under the hill on the Sunday night, and the golem's body was his bilingual.

He had known it would be, if anything was. That was the whole of the book he had read at nineteen: you looked for a thing written twice, once in what you could read and once in what you couldn't. And what he could read was the body. He had the eleven pieces on their trestles, every joint of them, the pins and the rails and the shaped stones: the rods at the elbows, the plate stacks at the shoulders, the coils at the hips. He could touch every one of them and feel how it worked. He knew what a knee did.

So he went into the stone with his hand on the chest, and went to the rails where they came into the seat, and followed them backward into the code: the rail from the left knee, into the stone, to where it ended in the writing. And then the rail from the right knee, and to where it ended. And they ended in two places that looked the same.

Not quite the same. Nearly. A block of marks, a long dense knot of them the size of a thumbnail if a thing inside a stone could be said to have a size; and the left knee's block and the right knee's block were the same block, stroke for stroke, except for a few marks near the start of each, which must be the part that said left and right.

He followed the elbows. The same, the two of them, and different from the knees, but built the same way. The shoulders. The hips. The ankles, the wrists, the neck. One block for every joint; and every block the same shape, with its own few marks at the start for which joint it was, and then the long knot that was the joint's own business.

A joint is a joint. It bends, and straightens, and holds. So somewhere in each knot were bend and straighten and hold, written the same way in every joint; and he went looking for the marks that were the same in all eleven knots and in the same places, and found them, and sat back on the trestle in the dark with his hand on the shell and his heart going.

And above the knots, further in, a different kind of writing, that touched all of them at once: that sent the same marks down to the knees and the hips and the ankles together, in an order. Stand. That would be stand: every joint in the legs told to hold, at once. And another, that sent to the left hip and then the right knee and then the left ankle, one after another, round and back: a loop. Step. And another that touched only the hips and the waist: turn. And another that set the shoulders and the elbows hard together and the knees soft: brace. And one that sent to the right shoulder and the right elbow, very fast, one and then the other, and nothing else.

He knew what that one was. He had been on the wrong end of it, in Ward's yard, for eleven years.

He took his hand off the stone and sat there with it in his lap.

Stand. Step. Turn. Brace. Strike. He had five words of it. In a library the size of an egg, he had five words, and the shape of the grammar they sat in.

And at the top of all of it, above the stand and the step and the strike, where the rails from the empty head seat came in, there was a door. He could see it: a place where the writing stopped being about the body and started being about something that came from outside it, from the seat in the head; a place built to be sat in. A rider's door. Whoever sat in the head seat, or whatever, didn't think about knees. They thought go there, and the door turned it into stand and step and turn, and the body went.

He sat on the trestle in the gallery under the hill, alone, at one in the morning, with the canvas off and the chest open and his headache coming on like weather, and looked at the shape of the thing in his sense: a body with its own small minds in every joint, and a big mind in the chest that told the joints what to do together, and an empty chair at the top for somebody to ride it.

He had built a great many things in eleven years. He had never built anything as well as this was built.

He put the canvas back, and locked the bay, and went up the gallery toward the lamps; and halfway up, alone in the dark, he found that he was laughing, very quietly, the way Tess had laughed at Ambrose's song in the earpiece, out of nothing but the plain joy of a thing that worked.
