Chapter 310 - The Plan

Bram had built the second press in June, in the week after he came home on the island boat and found his daughter sitting up in Imani's surgery with his log in her lap; and it had been a cider press, in an orchard shed outside Milford, until Paxton's brother heard that the duke's smith was buying presses and remembered that he had another one.

It stood under the Dust House's one window beside the first, a head shorter, with a thinner screw and a wheel one man could turn if he put his back into it, and the same steel block between its platens, because Bram had cast the second block off the first one's pattern and would not hear of anything else. Both screws had been down since the sixteenth of July. Both needles had stood on their pins since the end of that week. Nobody had opened either, because the orders were in Bram's coat on two sheets torn from the back of Tess's bench book, and the orders said not to.

Three, said the first sheet. Twice what the second run had. Feed to the sheet, nightly, a week. Under it ran a column of figures, a night to a line.

Four, said the second. Five times. Feed to the sheet. Then, after a gap, as if she had thought of it later, lying on her back in the hospital with the pen held up over her face: If it rings, write the hour. And at the bottom, underlined twice: Open neither till I'm there.

It had rung. It was in the log in Bram's square black capitals, on the line for the twenty-second of July: 2:10 in the morning. Small press. RANG. Needle back two marks. Fed to the sheet. He had been asleep on his crate between the two presses, as he slept every feeding night, and he said it had woken him the way a bell wakes you when somebody puts a hand on it before it has finished: once, not loud, a note that stopped short. He had looked at the needle and written it down and gone on feeding, because the sheet said to.



They came out on the K Endeavor's night run, five of them and the chair, and put down on the yard's hard before it was light. Osric pushed the chair along the works shore in the grey, with the bench book on Tess's knees and the sea going flat and silver on their left, and down the long slant of crushed shell where the track came off the bluff behind the slips to the Dust House door.

It was her own chair again. Merrin Sallis and Dov Kettle had given it back to her on the twenty-fifth of July, rebuilt round its stones in Bram's light metal, so light she could lift it off the ground by one arm, which she had done in the Motion Shop to see their faces. The brake was new; Dov had redrawn it four times because she had sent it back four times. It had a motor, and it would lighten on its stones until a child could push it, and Osric pushed it anyway. He had pushed her up the hill from the powerhouse every evening of the winter before the crate, and up and down most of the hills in Kaldo since whenever she let him, and the motor had never once been the point.

Imani had said no to the Dust House in June, and no in July, and no again on the island beach on the eighth of August, where Tess had been allowed to sit under the palms and do nothing whatever; and then yes, on three conditions, which she had made Tess say back to her on the Endeavor's bench at four in the morning. She would sit when she was told. She would eat at noon. She would not stand.

"I can't stand," Tess had said.

"Say it anyway."

"I won't stand."

Imani had looked at her feet in their boots on the chair's rest. The left had moved its big toe on the sixth of July, when asked, and most of the rest by the end of the month; and for three weeks now she had hung between the parallel bars Ivo had put up along the ward wall, two brass rails at hip height, with her feet set flat on the boards and her arms doing all of the work there was, and Tess swearing about it.

"Good," Imani had said, and had not sounded as though she believed a word.



Ferra had the cartridges out on the bench before anybody had their coat off: six fire cartridges filled at the plant, each with its brass gauge on the end cap, and an empty seventh, and her book open beside them at a clean page with the date at the top. The nineteenth of August. She had written it on the Endeavor.

"Three," said Tess.

Bram and Osric took the big wheel between them. It went back stiffly for a quarter turn and then easier, and the screw came up out of the block's throat with a sound like a cart's brake let off, and Bram swung the top platen aside on its chain.

The host came out first: a cake of grey quartzite the size of a man's fist, pressed so hard that it rang when he set it on the bench. He split it with a cold chisel along a line he had scratched on it in July, and it came apart in two halves like a loaf; and in the middle, in a small round room of its own making, sat the stone.

It was darker than the second run's. The same red-brown, the same size, the top joint of a thumb, the same skinless smoothness; but where the second stone had been wine held up to a lamp, this one was the wine near the bottom of the glass. Bram lifted it out with a pair of light tongs, because they came out warm, and laid it on Maren's cloth, and Tess held out her hand.

Bram looked at Imani.

"It's a stone," said Imani. "She's allowed a stone."

He tipped it into Tess's palm, and Emeric let his sense down beside her hand and into it.

It was full. That was all, and it was a great deal. He had held the best stones out of the Isle's fields, the ones Saban kept back for the ships' hearts and would not price aloud, and this was fuller than any of them, size for size, the way a cask is fuller than a bucket. There was nothing else in it. No seam, no cloud. It sat in her palm humming one low even note with nothing anywhere for the sense to catch on.

"Fill it," said Tess, and gave it to Ferra.

Ferra drained it into the empty cartridge and filled it off two of the full ones, three times, writing each time; then she put her pencil down beside three figures that were the same.

"A stone and a half," she said. "A hair under. Against a mined one its weight." She turned back a page to the week's feed. "And it took twice the second run. Six mined stones' worth of mana to make one that holds a stone and a half."

"So it's dearer," said Osric.

"It's dearer at every step." Tess was ruling two lines across a clean page in her bench book, one along the bottom and one up the side. "The first step past a wild stone costs more than the stone. The next costs more than the first." She put a dot low down, and another higher and further along, and a third. "One. Two. Three." She looked at the small press. "Four."



Osric turned the small press's wheel by himself, with his face going red and the cords standing out in his neck, and would not let Bram take a hand. Bram stood by with his arms folded and let him. The screw came up; the host came out and split on its line.

The stone in its room looked the same. A shade darker again, the bottom of the glass gone nearly black, but the same size and the same clean skin. Bram set it on the cloth beside the other, and Emeric put his hand out over it and let his sense down.

It was fuller than three. He had expected that. He had not expected the lines.

They ran all through it. He felt them before he understood them: thin places in the fullness, very fine, going out from the heart of the stone where Bram's pinch of fire dust had been, in rings first and then in branches off the rings, the way frost goes out across a pane from the first speck it catches on. The stone was whole; it would have taken a hammer. But along every line the fullness thinned, as paper thins along a fold, and in three places the lines came up to the skin; and there it was going out. Slowly. A breath of warm air off a closed door.

"It's cracked," he said.

"It rang," said Bram.

"Inside. All through it, from the middle." Emeric took his hand back. "And it's leaking."

Tess did not ask how he knew. She said, "Fill it," in exactly the voice she had used for three.

The needle on the first full cartridge went down, and the second, and Ferra took up a third without being told, and that one went down too.

"Two and a fifth," said Ferra.

Nobody spoke. Two and a fifth was more than twice the best stone in Saban's cellar, and it sat on Maren's cloth no bigger than the other, looking like a stone.

Emeric touched it then, because there was one more thing to know. He gave every new stone the same small test, a hair-fine line of instruction cut from one side to the other, the least a stone could carry; and he cut it now across the middle and felt the line go in clean for a third of the way and come to the first crack and stop. It did not break. It stopped, the way ink stops at a fold, and on the far side of the fold there was nothing to write on. He tried a hair to the left and it stopped at the same place.

"It won't take fine work across them," he said. "A line breaks at every one."

"Drain it," said Tess. "In a quarter hour."

They waited the quarter hour. Bram oiled the small press's screw. Imani stood at Tess's shoulder with her hand on the chair's back, not on Tess, and looked at the sea in the door. Ferra sat with the empty cartridge in her lap and her watch open on the bench, and at the quarter hour she set the stone in the cartridge's cradle on her side of the small press, with the block between her and the chair, and drained it, and watched the needle climb.

"What's it say?"

"Wait," said Ferra.

Tess could not see it. The block was in the way, a bread oven of steel at the height of her face, and she leaned in the chair to see round it, and could not, and leaned the other way.

Then she put her hand on the press's near column.

He saw her do it and thought nothing of it; she had put her hand on things to pull her chair in close all her working life. Then she put the other hand on the bench's edge, and leaned, and her boots came back under her on the boards, both of them, flat; and she stood up.

She did it the way she had done it at fifteen at the forge, to see over a crucible: without deciding to, all at once, because the thing she wanted to see was on the other side of something. She was up before anybody in the shed had drawn breath, leaning across the block with her hand on the column, looking down at the needle in Ferra's lap.

"Two," she said. "That's a fifth gone. In a quarter hour."

Nobody answered her.

Ferra was not looking at the needle. Bram had stopped with the oil can over the screw and a drop hanging off its spout. Osric had come round the end of the bench without anybody seeing him move and was standing at her elbow with both hands a little out from his sides, not touching her, the way a man stands by a wall he thinks might go.

"Tess," said Imani.

"I know." Tess did not look round. "I know. I'm up." Then she was looking down, at the boards, a long way down, at her own two boots side by side on the Dust House floor. "The left knee's going. The right's all right." It was the voice she gave a coil in. "It's further down than I drew it."

He did not understand that, and then he did. She had drawn the Dust House in the winter, the earth bank and the plate door and every bench in it; and she had drawn the benches to her own height standing, which she had taken in December lying flat on her drafting board with Osric holding the tape at her heels, because, she had said, she was going to stand at them one day and she was not having them all rebuilt when she did. Bram had built them to the inch and said nothing about it to anybody. The bench under her hand came to the top of her thigh.

"It's an inch low," said Tess. "I'm an inch out." She sounded more offended by that than by anything that had happened to her in thirteen years.

"You've grown," said Imani. She had not moved either. "The shed sets you to what you'd have been. You were fifteen when you went in that chair. You hadn't finished."

Tess thought about it. Then she turned her head and found Osric beside her, and looked up at him.

She had to look up a long way, and Emeric watched her find that out. She had measured Osric for his suit in the year of the hall team, and every spring since because he kept asking her to check it; she had him in inches on the back pages of three bench books. And she had never once in her life stood next to him.

"You're taller than I measured," she said.

"I'm seventy-five inches." Osric's voice had gone somewhere and come back without all of itself. "You wrote it on my sleeve. In chalk."

"You're taller than that."

"I'm not, though."

"You are from here," said Tess.

Bram set the oil can down on the crosshead very carefully, as if it were full of something that would spill.

"Sit down before you fall down," he said.

"No."

"Then don't fall down." He picked the can up again and bent to the screw. Emeric felt his note from across the shed, banked and low as it always was, and under it something going very hard and very fast that Bram was not going to let the rest of him do.

"Thirty breaths," said Imani. "Then you sit, and I look at your knees, and you don't argue."

Tess stood her thirty breaths with one hand on the column and the other on the inch-low bench, and Imani counted them aloud, and at twenty the left knee began to shake in earnest and Tess said a word Bram had not taught her. At thirty she let herself down into the chair by the column, slowly, the whole way, and sat, and put both hands flat on her thighs and looked at them.

Ferra wrote something in her book and did not cross it out. Emeric saw it on the Endeavor that night, when she turned the book to the lamp and let him: 19 Aug. Run four. Two and a fifth, leaking; two at a quarter hour. And under it, no larger: T. stood.



At noon Tess ate, because it was a condition. Osric ate standing in the door and told the potter's nephew, who had come from the kiln to ask what the shouting had been, what had happened; and by the third telling she had said I know as if she had been standing all her life.

"I didn't say it like that," said Tess, without looking up.

"You said it like that from where I was."

In the afternoon she had four drained every hour, and it went down every hour a little less than the hour before, the way a cracked jug goes down. By four it held a stone and three quarters. Tess put the dots on her lines, one, two, three, rising, closer together as they went up, and four above them with a short hooked tail falling off it to the right.

"There," she said. "That's where the rock won't take any more. A bit past twice." She put the pencil's end on the hook. "Under it you get a clean stone. Over it you get one that holds more while you watch and leaks the rest away, and you can't write on it. You'd never put it in a ship."

"It cost fifteen," said Ferra. "Fifteen mined stones' worth, for one that holds less every hour."

"It cost fifteen to find out. Nobody has to spend it again."

Ferra wrote one more line under her figures and turned the book so Tess could read it: Clean below twice. Cracked above. Don't.

Tess nodded. Then she said she wanted the cracks on paper.

"He can feel them. I can't draw what he feels." She looked at the small stone on its cloth. "I want to see where they go. Whether they go out from the seed like he says, or from the rock's grain, or both." She turned to Imani. "Does your tank see inside a stone?"

"It sees inside Ward."

"A stone's harder than Ward."

"A stone doesn't fidget," said Imani. Bram put the cracked stone into a padded tin out of the Dust House cupboard and shut the lid, and Imani pointed at Emeric. "You're carrying it. I'm not carrying a thing that leaks. I'll run it tonight, when we're home."



At five Imani said Tess could go up the track to the yard on her feet, as far as the top, with Osric on one side of her and the chair on the other; and that if she sat down halfway nobody was to say a word.

Tess did not sit down halfway.

She came out of the Dust House door on Osric's arm into the low gold light off the bay and looked up the track. It was not much of a hill: a long slant up the face of the bluff, sixty yards or a little more, crushed shell rolled flat for the barrows, and at the top the yard road and the long hut's chimney going. She had been pushed up worse, every evening of one winter, years ago.

Osric took the chair's handles. He did it without looking, as he had done it a thousand times, as if his hands had been waiting all day for something to hold; and he pushed the empty chair up the shell beside her, slowly, its new wheels crunching, while she walked with one hand on his sleeve and the other out a little from her side for the air. Emeric saw her look at the empty chair going up the hill without her in it. He saw her open her mouth to say something about it, and shut it again, and put her hand back on Osric's arm, and let him.

Bram had not come out of the Dust House. He was at the small press with his back to the door.

"Tell me when she's at the top," he said.

It took her twenty minutes. She stopped twice, and both times Osric stopped with the chair and looked at the sea and said nothing at all, which for Osric was a feat of strength Emeric had never seen him attempt. Both times she went on. In the door Imani had her arms folded and was counting something under her breath that was not breaths. Ferra stood beside her with her book shut on her finger.

At the top, on the yard road, Tess let go of his arm and turned round, which on those feet took some doing, and looked back down all sixty yards of it to the Dust House roof and the three of them in its door. Then she looked up at Osric, the long way up, and said something they could not hear. Osric let go of the chair.

It rolled a foot on the slope, and its new brake caught, and it stopped.

She put her hand flat on the front of his coat, the way she put it on a machine to feel whether it was running, and he bent his head to her, a long way; and she came up onto her toes to meet him, which she had not been able to do since she was fifteen, and kissed him.

Emeric looked at the sea.

"She's at the top," he said.

"Did she fall?"

"No."

"Hm," said Bram.

"He's kissing her," said Imani, with deep satisfaction, not looking at the sea at all.

"I know," said Bram. He had not turned round once. He put both his big black-nailed hands flat on the press's crosshead and stood with his head down, the way a man stands at an anvil between heats.

When Emeric looked up the hill again Osric had the chair's handles and Tess had her hand on his arm, and he was pushing the empty chair along the yard road toward the long hut with her walking beside it, very slowly, both of them looking straight ahead as if nothing whatever had happened and they were only two people going up to their supper.

"He's pushing it," said Ferra.

"She's letting him," said Imani.



Bram stayed on the island with the presses. The rest of them went home after dark on the Endeavor, on her stones, and settled on the Mere a little before midnight, with Tess asleep against Osric's shoulder on the cabin bench and the bench book sliding off her lap, and Osric sitting bolt upright so as not to wake her, for two hours, until his neck would not turn. Emeric had the tin on his knees the whole way. He could feel the stone through the padding, the thin warm breath of it going out along its cracks, a little less every hour.



Imani ran it at half past twelve, in the tank, with the ward asleep down the passage and Bettany at the frame.

The tank was built for people, and the stone looked foolish in it, a thumb's joint of red-brown in a saucer on the bath's floor where a man's chest would be. Bettany looked at it, and at the duke on the stool by the door with an empty tin on his knee, and asked no questions, which was why she had the nights.

The frame crawled. A stone was denser than any man, and the picture came up off the printer a line at a time. Imani had three views run, from above, from the side and from the end, a quarter of an hour each; and at a quarter past one she took the sheets off the printer and held them to the lamp.

The stone lay on each sheet nearly black, a small dark egg the size of the end of his thumb; it bent the frame's field harder than bone. And through the black on all three, going out from a point near the middle, there were lines: pale, finer than a pencil's, in rings round the middle and then in branches off the rings, out toward the edge, and on the top view, in three places, reaching it.

She looked at them for a long time.

Then she went to the cabinet where she kept the years' sheets and went through the second drawer with her finger, the way she went through a ward list, and took out one sheet. Emeric knew it. It had been pinned on the ward wall in year eleven, with the garrison's, after they had all lain in the tank for a hot meal: one view only, because a man like that got one part a sitting. Under it in the hospital's hand: Ward. Left forearm.

She laid it on the bath's edge beside the stone's.

The forearm was grey on the paper round its two long bones. And over the grey, from the wrist upward, ran the tracery, pale, paler than the muscle: rings round the wrist, close, and from the rings the lines going up under the skin in branches and out of the top of the sheet toward the elbow.

Imani looked from one sheet to the other, and back. Then she took her own book out of the cabinet, the ward's black one that she wrote the dead in, and opened it near the back at a page Emeric had seen once before, in the square on the day Rylan took his shirt off for the class. One in ten. Rylan's figure; Armand's sheets agree. Thirty-six, for these four. And under it, in pencil, from Ferra: Proving, yr 12. 61 to the floor. 22 stood.

She put the book down beside the two sheets and stood with her hands flat on either side of them.

"Imani."

"Where's Ward?"

He let his sense go out over the civic ring and the water and up the hill. "The wine store. On his own."

She gathered the sheets and the forearm and the book and the saucer with the stone in it against her chest, all at once, and went to the door.

"Bettany. Drain the bath."

"Yes, miss."

She looked back at Emeric from the door with everything held against her, and her face was the one she had worn in Hester's cellar over twenty dead hoppers in twenty jars: sure, and not pleased about it.

"Come if you like," she said. "Don't talk."



Ward was at the long table in the wine store with one lamp and the Water book open in front of him, not reading it. The king had gone up some time before; Emeric had felt his father's note on the keep stair while the frame was still crawling, careful on the turns, as it went up every night. Ward had stayed most nights since the overlay, until two or after, and nobody had said anything to him about it.

He looked at what Imani was carrying, and at Emeric stopping in the door, and shut the book.

Imani put it all down in front of him. The saucer. The stone's three sheets side by side. His own forearm beside them. The black book open at its column.

"Tess made a stone today," she said. "Rock and dust and mana, in Bram's press. She fed it more than it could take while it set. Five times what a good one takes. On purpose, to see where it breaks." Her finger on the stone's top view. "That's where. It held more than any stone you've ever seen, and it cracked all through, from the middle out, and it leaks through the cracks. It's leaking now." Her finger moved to the forearm. "That's yours."

Ward looked at the two sheets the way he looked at a road from a hill before he took men down it. The rings round the stone's heart. The rings round his wrist. The pale branches going out.

"It's not the same," he said.

"No. It's a stone and you're a man. It's the same kind." Imani sat down across from him, which she did not often do. "A body's a host, like the rock. You had your own bit of mana from birth, everybody does, and that's the seed. Then they put you on a floor in a stone room with a drain in it and put more into you in a night than you'd take in a lifetime, in waves, as fast as it would go. The press does it to rock over a week and the rock cracks past twice. They did it to you in nine hours." She touched the book. "Armand called it the rate. Nine in ten of you did what a stone does when you feed it past anything it can stand. You burst inside. And one in ten held." She looked at his wrist on the table, the rings pale under the brown, unlit. "Cracked. That's what this is. That's where you cracked and held."

Ward said nothing.

"It's why you're strong while you draw and an hour after you're a man your age," said Imani, more quietly. "You leak, like this does. It goes out through the cracks between times and you have to fill again. Bren doesn't. I looked into him in the stubble with the locusts down his collar and his bones were full like a stone's full, and I'd never seen it. He was never cracked. He was grown slow, at the dose, for years, from a boy. Clean, like her third." She touched the stone's sheet, the forearm, the column, one after another, like a ward round. "And the Proving goes to the floor with half of it built already, so the jump's smaller, and fewer burst. Twenty-two out of sixty-one, where six would have stood." She sat back. "It's the press. It's all the press. The tower's been running one on boys for longer than anybody's written anything down, and calling it a choosing, and never once counted, and never once asked why it went one in ten."

Ward turned his left hand over on the table and pushed his sleeve up to the elbow, slowly.

The silver lay under the skin as it had lain since the floor, rings at the wrist and the lines going up from them, quiet, the colour of an old spoon. He had looked at it every day of his life. He looked at it now as if it were a sheet off the printer that somebody had handed him with another man's name on it.

"They told us it chose," he said.

"Nobody chose. It's a dose."

"I told boys that. Before they went up. Every one that asked me." He did not take his eyes off his arm. "I told them the floor chooses, and if it chooses you there's nothing to be ashamed of either way."

Imani did not answer that. She let it stand on the table between them with the stone, which was the kindest thing Emeric had ever seen her do for anybody.

After a while Ward pulled the sleeve back down and buttoned it at the wrist.

"You'll write it," he said.

"Controls and all."

"Put my name on it. The arm." He looked up at her. "They'll want a knight's, and one that came off their own floor. That's mine." He looked at the saucer. "And the stone."

"And the stone," said Imani.

Ward looked past her then, at Emeric in the door, who had not said a word.

"You knew," he said.

"I felt it. In your note, for thirteen years. In Rylan's and Declan's and Osric's." Emeric came in a step. "I called it the rite's mark. I never asked what a mark was."

"No," said Ward. He looked across the table at Imani, at her hands flat on either side of the sheets. "She did."



Ferra was in the map room at the Works Office at a quarter to two, as she had said she would be, with the lamp low and the shutters closed and her book on the table's brass rim; and she had already slid Kaldo's relief out of the bottom tray and slid the capital's in.

Linus's office had cast it in July, in plaster, from the year's flights and the old survey sheets, and painted it in the plain colours of the Works' drawings: the river grey-blue, the streets buff, the walls dull red, the roofs white, the Church's ground a pale yellow because the painters had to use something. Under the twenty-four clear plates it lay like a city seen through still water. The palace on its rise. The river round three sides. At the top edge the north road ran off the plaster toward Brask, and the painters had ended it with a small neat arrow and the name, as if the road owed them the rest. The close in its yellow in the middle of the old town, and on it the cathedral, a long white bar with a stub at the west end for the porch and its two towers.

He sat down in the head chair and lifted the small glass lid on its arm and put two fingers on the stone in its cradle, and his band woke against it, and he went in.

It was not hard. The capital was in him already, in the shapes he had walked it in as a boy; and over those the year's flights at height, two looks at every street from two places so that the ground stood up between them; and over those, Saban's walkers, who had given him their streets in the wine store one at a time all spring, talking them through door by door over Saban's sketches while he listened with his eyes shut and built them in his head. He laid all of it into the stone the way he would have laid a drawing on a board, and asked the table for it from the bottom up.

The river lit first in the lowest plate, a dark band, because the table drew deep water dark. The ground came up round it in the next three, the low places and the rises and the long slope to the palace, until the plaster under the plates was gone behind a soft glowing floor. Then the walls, faint gold. Then the streets, all at once, like a net thrown over the ground and settling. Then the houses, layer on layer, each a block of pale light with its roof drawn on top, the long arcade down the east side of the cloth market drawn solid from the spring flights as one roof. The palace came up through ten layers and stopped.

And in the middle, the cathedral.

It came up slowly, because there was more of it. The nave, long and high, a bar of light through layer after layer; the transepts; the rounded apse. Then the porch at the west end, and either side of it the two towers going on up past the nave's roof to the twenty-second layer, two thin columns standing up out of the city. At the top of each he set two short upright strokes, very bright, because Saban had stood in the wine store in June and said bombards, two to a tower, set up on end. Off the nave's south side, on its own, as old as the nave and no part of it, the bell tower came up square through eighteen layers and stopped.

Round the close, and inside parts of it, the table drew fog: a soft grey haze where nobody of Kaldo's had ever been. The inside of the cardinal's house. The canons' range. The crypt. The table did not pretend.

The cloister garden it drew, because a walker had stood in it. Three windows over the garden on the north side, drawn plainly, each with its square of glass, where his father had lived from April to July and eaten what he was given and watched it.

Ferra had not said anything. She stood at the table's long side with her hands on the brass rim and watched the city come up inside the glass, and when it stopped she looked at all of it, as she would have looked at a sheet of figures somebody had handed her to check.

"Tess will say you should have waited for her," she said.

"Tess can stand at it tomorrow."

She heard that and put it somewhere. "Show me, then."



He did not explain it. He had thought on the Endeavor that he would, and in the chair with the city in the glass he found that he did not need to; she could see the table as well as he could. He drew a ring on the ground layer instead, a faint blue line along the streets four hundred yards out from the nave on every side, and let her look at what was inside it.

A good deal was. The whole close, the cardinal's house and the cloister and the three windows. The bishop's school on the close's south side, where the choir boys boarded. Two parish churches and their yards. The cloth market and its arcade, and the old town round it at its thickest, two and three storeys, packed.

"How many?" said Ferra.

"Saban's had the parish rolls since June. He said it was the kind of thing he got. Inside the line, on an ordinary day, about nine thousand. At the noon office in the cathedral itself, four or five hundred. The close sleeps two hundred and some. The school sleeps sixty." He looked at the small pale block on the yellow. "Boys."

"They go out."

"They all go out. First, before anything. The boys and the canons and the women who sell bread on the steps and whoever's asleep in the side chapels. Nothing happens to that building with anybody in it."

"Then they have to be told."

"Told, and believe it." He moved his fingers on the stone, and the table drew a scatter of white flecks drifting down over the old town and the squares and the quays, like snow, that came to rest on the roofs and went out. "The day before. Sheets, from a ship, on every street. What it is, who it's against, when, how far to go. Plain words. A map for the ones who don't read. The hour."

"Who it's against."

"Not them."

She looked at the white flecks going out on the roofs. "They won't believe a duke about a church," she said. "Not with every pulpit in the city telling them on the Sunday what the duke is." She was quiet. "They'd believe a priest."

"I'm not asking one."

"No," said Ferra. "You won't." She said it in the voice that put a thing in a column for later, and he did not ask what she meant.

"The morning of, they hear something they can't argue with." On the bell tower a small hard point of light lit at the top and went out. "That. It's empty and it's tall, and every street in the city sees it come down, and it's far enough off the nave that it falls on nobody. After that nobody thinks the sheet was a bluff. Then the ship comes over the square, low, and says the hour out loud. Then the cathedral's own bells, rung by our people, at the last."

"And then you look."

"From above. Me and whoever's aboard who can. Every living thing has its note. A man asleep in a side chapel. An old canon who won't go. A boy in the bell loft because he's eleven and wants to see." He did not look at the school. "And Rylan's people walk it from the door to the apse with lamps, for anybody who can't walk out. If anybody's in it, nothing falls until they're not."

"And if they won't come out."

"Then we wait."

"With a ship hanging over the capital in daylight." She looked at the bright strokes on the porch towers. "Under those."

"Yes."

"For how long?"

"Until they come out."

She looked at him across the table with the capital between them. Then she opened her book on the rim to a clean page at the back and wrote a heading he could read upside down, Waiting, and under it ruled a line, and wrote nothing on it.

"I can't price that," she said. "I'll price it after."

"I know."

"You'll put it to the table."

"When the proclamation comes. Not before."

"It's coming." She was not asking. Saban's slips on the thirteenth had set down what Corbin's council had called the night at the ferry, and what the chancery's clerks were drawing up in a fair hand because of it, and everybody at the long table had read it twice and nobody had said the word aloud since. "Ward will say defend. A wall and bastions and ships, and never once taken. He'll say sit behind it and let them come."

"Ward will say defend."

"And you'll say no." She looked at the long white bar with its two towers and the fog round its crypt. "You've not said why that one. Not the palace. Not the chancery where they'll draw the paper."

"I'll say why at the table."

"You'll say it to the table. I know why." She did not look away from the glass. "It's where they wrote him guilty, and it's where he sits. Everybody in that city understands a church coming down; nobody will have to have it explained. And nobody, after, will be able to say you killed the people in it, because there weren't any."

He sat in the high chair with his fingers on the stone.

"Is this you being angry?" said Ferra.

She asked it plainly, the way she asked whether a figure was gross or net. She had been at her own table when his finger went through it.

He thought about it honestly, because it was Ferra, and she would know if he did not.

"Some," he said.

"Then I'll ask you again in a week." She wrote it under the empty line, very small: Ask again. A week. "And again in a fortnight. If it's the same plan in a fortnight, it's a plan."

The table had begun to cloud.

He saw it before she did. The busiest layers first, as it always went: the ones the cathedral stood up through, where the most cells had been lit the longest. The porch towers had gone soft at their edges, and the nave was greying from the middle out like breath on a window, the tiles spending their charge and clouding as they spent. Down at the bottom, in the dark band of the river, the old town was still clear.

He took his fingers off the stone and closed the lid, and the city went out of the glass layer by layer from the top, until there was nothing in the case but twenty-four clear plates with faint hairline seams across them like brickwork, and under them in painted plaster the capital: buff streets, white roofs, a yellow close with a white bar on it, and Linus's pins in the gates.

"Those trays want charging," said Ferra.

"I'll take them up in the morning."

She stood with her hand on the rim looking at the plaster. "Forty thousand," she said after a while. "If it's one for every door inside the line and every square and every quay. On quarter-sheets, Hobb's big press can do that in a night if he doesn't sleep." She took a slip from the back of her book and wrote, and he read it upside down. One sheet, quarter-folio. Forty thousand. Border and rule. Map block to follow. Words to follow. F.

"There aren't any words," he said.

"There will be." She folded the slip. "They can set what they can without them."



The printing works ran its night shift on the civic ring's west side, and its windows were the only ones lit along that side of the water at a quarter past three; and Ferra went in at the press-room door as she went in every morning at six, as if it were six, and gave the night foreman the slip.

He was a narrow grey man in an apron black to the waist who had set every Almanac Kaldo ever printed, and he did not ask the Chancellor anything. He went to the stone, the long iron table where the type was locked up, and laid an iron chase on it the size of the sheet, and set a plain double rule round its edge out of the case by the window; and below the border's top, where a heading would go, a single thin rule across, for the hour, with a space above it.

Then he filled the rest of the chase with furniture, the printer's word for plain blocks of wood, every length, laid in close where the words and the map would go so that nothing could shift. He drove the quoins home at the edges with his mallet, three taps each, and the forme went tight; and he lifted one corner a finger's breadth off the stone to see that nothing fell out, and set it down.

It lay under the lamp: an iron frame, a double rule round its edge, one thin rule across its top, and inside it nothing but wood.

"That's set," said the foreman. "Give me the words and it's on the press inside the hour."

"You'll have them," said Ferra.



They walked round the civic ring under the planes with the tram rails silent in their conduit, and crossed the water by the west bridge, and went up through the gate passage into the inner ward; and in the keep Ferra did not go up to bed. She went along the passage to the long room and opened its door and stood aside for him.

There was a sheet on the wall.

It had been there since the Monday, on the cork beside the door, and he had walked past it five times without reading it, because there was always a sheet on that wall. It was headed Kaldo, as it stands. August, yr 13. Under the heading there was no preamble, as there never was; only short lines with nothing in them that was not a figure or a name.

Power. The falls dam, generating since March; the turbine station. Lines to every house inside the walls, the Port Town, the garden suburb, the lock villages and the farms on the flats.

Water. Every house on the main. Sewers under every street inside the walls, to the treatment beds. The water towers full.

Heat. Gas in every kitchen inside the walls, for the stove and the copper.

Roads. The ring road. The four avenues. The paved road along the Grayrock cutting.

Carriage. Trams on the avenues. The ring line, every twenty minutes. The Mere line on the arches. The Wayford, Grayrock and Milford lines. The vehicle works' lorries on the roads.

The refinery, on the Isle's oil.

The hospital, rebuilt. A clinic and a fire station in every ward.

The line room. The telephone to the four baronies. Radio to every ship.

The campus. The towers: eleven standing, the twelfth topping out. The census at midsummer, by Ottoline's cards: a hundred and one thousand and some, in the four baronies.

And at the bottom, under a ruled line, smaller, the one entry on the sheet that was not a figure: Seen by F., 15 Aug.

He read it twice in the lamp from the passage.

He knew what it was. A man from his grandfather's time on Earth, set down at the South Gate in a good coat with a suitcase, would have known every line of it: the trams and the wireless and the gas in the kitchen and the lorries, the hospital and the sewers and the electric light in the farmhouse at the lane's end, the oil coming in by sea to a refinery down the river. He would have looked at the glass towers and thought them very modern, and asked where he could buy a newspaper, and been sold one.

Thirteen years ago it had been three thousand people, a broken aqueduct and the plague in the lower ward.

"I wanted it written once," said Ferra beside him. "As it stands. Before."

He did not ask her before what.

"Come to bed," she said.

"Soon."

She knew what soon meant; she had kept his hours in a column for thirteen years. She put her hand on his arm, briefly, and went along the passage and up the stair, and he felt her note go up it, steady, to the turn and on and into the warm room at the top, where Imani was asleep already with one arm flung out across the empty side.



The north tower's stair came out on the leads through a hatch that stuck, and he put his shoulder to it and came up into the night.

The keep's two old towers were not the highest things in Kaldo any more. But they stood on the raised ward at the top of the hill, in the middle of everything, and from the north tower's parapet at half past three on an August morning, with no wind and no cloud and the stars down to the sea's edge, he could see all of it at once. He had never looked at it all at once. He had always been in the middle of some part of it.

The water ring first, right under him, black, with the lamps on its paths going round like a necklace. Then the civic ring: the hospital's long H with its night windows lit; Lantern Square with its fountain going under the planes and nobody in it. The towers stood up out of the ring and along the avenues, glass on steel, lit on their stair shafts and dark on their floors, and the twelfth on the Gate Avenue, the tall one, thirty storeys, with its crane still on its crown and a red lamp hung from the crane's arm. The six round towers under their blue cones, full of water. The four avenues going off straight to the gates, lamp after lamp under the trees, and between them the wards in their courtyard blocks, roofs and gardens, dark, with a window lit here and there where somebody was up with a child or a cough or a book.

Then the wall, whole, with its bastions and its lamps along the walk; the ring road outside it, black and empty, and the ring line's viaduct beside it; the garden suburb in its young trees. The Port Town along the river road, a long bright crescent; the port, its blue cranes over the quays under arc lamps and a coaster at the deep berth with her deck lights on; the heavy works beyond, the furnace's glow on the underside of its own smoke. Two miles down past the bend, small and steady at the top of its column, the refinery's flare. North-west, past the aqueduct's arches, the falls dam grey under the stars with the turbine hall lit at its foot; and up the river in the gorge, the work lights of the river dam that would not be done for two years yet, a row of yellow points at the bottom of the dark where the cofferdams were, and the night gang's lamps moving among them.

He stood with his hands on the cold stone and looked at it. He did not try to count anything. He looked from one edge to the other and back, the way you look at a face you are going to have to remember; and he thought about a city of the same size in a glass case in a dark room across the town, going grey from the middle out, with fog round its crypt, and an iron frame on an iron table with nothing in it but a border and a line for the hour and wood.

Below him, across the inner ward, the dome stood up into the night higher than the tower he stood on.

It was lit from inside. The tree's light came up through the glass blue and violet, and the iron ribs went up dark against it to the ring at the crown, and the tall arched windows round its stone base glowed in a ring, each a long lit door into the dense air. Through the nearest he could see the path, and the tree's foot, and the first great spread of its roots across the flags; and beside the trunk a tall figure in a robe a little short at the ankle, standing quite still with one hand flat on the bark, the way a man stands with his hand on the neck of a horse he has known a long time. Emeric let his sense go out across the ward and found Armand's note, young now and pleased with itself even at this hour, and very quiet.

Outside the pass-box door, on the colonnade bench, a big man was asleep sitting up with his boots off and his head back against a pillar; and beside him, empty, with its new brake on, stood a chair.

At the next window along, inside the glass, Tess was standing.

She was on her own feet, with nobody's arm. She had one hand up on the window's iron frame, flat, at the height of her shoulder, the way she put a hand on a machine to feel whether it was running; and she was not looking at the tree, or at Armand, or at the bench outside where Osric slept. She was looking out through the glass, past the ward and the water and the towers, at the city.
