Chapter 232 - Night Charge

They took her up on the fourteenth of August, at half past ten at night, from the reeds.

Five of them, in the house, with the door dogged shut behind them and every light out but the bulb shaded over the dials: Ned at the helm post under the cupola with his hand on the lever and the steady humming in its box at his feet; Tobe and Cade on the bench along the forward wall in their harnesses, with their furs across their knees for the hold; Ward at the forward panes, because Ward would not hear of anything else; and Emeric on the floor by the trap, with his back against the wall. Under the trap, in the hold at the helm post's foot, the four flasks stood in their rack, and beside them a hand pump on a frame that Bram had made from a fire engine's, and a coil of glass tube in a felt sleeve. Forward of the house, under the shut hatch, the racks went down into her in four tiers either side, oak, and in the racks were two hundred and twelve of the works' spent stones, grey and flat and dull, packed in straw like eggs.

The masks hung on their hooks, one at every seat, with the breath candles in their cups. Imani had fitted every one on the reed bank and made every one of them breathe through it for a count of a hundred, and had told them that if any one of them went down into that hold above two miles without his mask on to prove something she would hear about it and he would never go up again, and had said it looking at Cade. The chutes were on, all five; that was the third line in the book, after Ward's and the one about the mile; Ned had worn his round the reed bank for an hour the night before to get used to it, and said it sat on him like a sack of meal on a short man.

Imani was not aboard. It was the first run, and Emeric had said not on the first run, to Armand, at the table, and could not then take anybody else; and Imani had agreed, and stood on the reed bank with her bag and Ferra and Armand and the crew book, three dark shapes against the reeds, and watched the Hod go up.

Ned put the lever over.



She went up, and stayed where she was put.

That was Ned's doing. She had flown every night that week, low over the lake with her lamps dark, while he learned her: the lift on the one lever, the air on the second, which pushed her whichever way he pointed the helm, and the steady, which held her level and on her heading and which Ned had switched off on the second night to prove he could fly her without it, and on again on the third without a word. Now he put her nose into the west and gave the air a notch, and she climbed on her six lift stones over the lake and stayed over it, drinking from the rail, and the rail from the bank in her racks. It was not much of a bank. Two hundred works stones that had been flat a week ago, given only enough by Orrin and the split day to wake; enough, Emeric had worked it on paper four times and Ferra twice, to take her to four miles and hold her there an hour against the wind and bring her down, with the reserve untouched, if nothing went wrong. If something went wrong there was the reserve, and the rail, and their hands. The flasks were empty.

At a thousand feet the lake was a dull sheet below with three lamps on its bank and the town a smear of lamps beyond it, and Ned said, "Hm," and nothing else.

At a mile the cloud came. It was thin that night and they went up through it in a breath, the small panes running wet, and out above it into the stars. "Seal," said Ward, and Ned shut the vents, and Tobe went round the house and tried every dog on the door with his hand, and under the floor the pump began, a small steady thump, and the needle on the gauge by the dials lifted off its pin. The cold came at once. It came on the far side of the glass: frost feathered into the corners of the panes and the bronze grids went white; and on this side the warmth came up through the boards from Bram's tank, which Emeric had woken on the reed bank, so that the floor was warm under their boots as a kitchen's flags. Cade took his gloves off and put them in his fur. The cloud lay below them silver in the starlight, flat, to the edge of the world.

At two miles Ward read the gauge aloud, as the crew book said somebody must. The air in the house was thin and pumped and tasted of oak and pitch and warm copper, and it was enough. Nobody reached for a mask.

At three miles the cold was a wall on the other side of the glass. The panes rimed white at their edges, and through their middles the stars were very hard and very many and did not twinkle. Inside, Tobe had his collar open. Nobody said much. Emeric had his sense out through the whole of her, every stone in her racks and every lift stone under the deck and the air stones in her channels pushing her nose into the wind, and they were drinking more than they had at the bottom, because the climb cost and the wind cost, and the bank was giving it, slowly, and there was enough.

At four the Band came.

He felt it come down on them and into him: the whole sky at once, thick, warm to the sense as a bath is warm, pressing in through the oak of the walls and the seams of the glass and his own skin; everywhere but the hold, where the racks lay asleep under the iron deck and the shut hatch, as Orrin's stone had gone dark in Fletcher's iron shelves. He heard Tobe make a sound. He saw Cade put a hand flat on the bench as if the floor had moved, which it had not. And at the helm Ned said "Hm" again, sharply, because the air stones had felt it before he had and pushed harder all at once, as the crew book said they would, the ship's lesson; and his hand was already easing the second lever back a notch before the house had finished leaning.

"Hatch," said Emeric.

Cade put his shoulder to the winch on the forward wall. Out beyond the panes, in the cold, the hatch cover ran back on its rollers off the hold, slowly, rumbling, and the Band came down into the racks.

And the racks woke.

All of them. Two hundred and twelve spent stones in their straw in the oak, in four tiers down either side of the hold, and every one of them, at once, opened its throat and drank. He felt it as a man feels a field of grass bend in one gust. They went from dull to faintly warm to bright, all down the racks, in a long wave from the top tier to the bottom, as if somebody had walked along the hold with a taper; and the bank that fed the rail went from a trickle to a river; and the six lift stones under the deck, which had been drinking hard on the climb, stopped drinking hard and simply held, fed, full, with more coming than they could use.

Ned felt it. Ned had no faculty at all; but he had flown hull two two hundred times and knew how a boat felt under the lever when she was working and when she was not, and the Hod stopped working. She still wanted a touch on the air now and then, a notch against the wind and back. Her lift wanted nothing. She sat in the dark four miles up as if she had been set down on a table.

He took his hand off the lift lever. He looked at it.

Then he took his cap off, which he did not do for bishops, and said to the glass in front of him, to the black and the cold and the stars, "She's resting," in a voice of the purest astonishment; and put it back on.



The racks filled in an hour.

Not all of them evenly; the top tier filled first, being nearest the open hatch and the sky, and the bottom last. At the half hour Cade and Tobe got into their furs and took their masks down, and Ward said "Masks," which was the fourth line in Ferra's book and which he would have said to the king; and everybody in the house put his own to his face before Tobe knocked the dogs off the door. The cold came in like a slap and the pump under the floor began to thump fast. Tobe and Cade clipped their harnesses to the line along the rail and went out into the dark, and the door shut behind them, and the needle crept back. After that it was the same at every opening of the door or the trap, the word and the masks and the pump hurrying after, and nobody had to be told twice. They went down into the hold with a shuttered lamp and moved the bottom tier's stones up and the top tier's down, in their gloves, by feel, which Emeric had not told them to do and had been about to; and by the end of the hour every stone in her racks was full, as full as a stone off Orrin's bench after a morning's work, two hundred and twelve of them.

Emeric filled too.

He had gone down through the trap at the half hour, in a fur and his mask, to the flasks; and Ward had come down the ladder after him and pulled the trap shut over both their heads, as Ward came after him everywhere. It was black down there and bitterly cold. The iron round him was rimed. Forward, past the helm post's foot and the rail going away under the deck, the racks blazed to his sense in their tiers under the open hatch like a street of lit windows, and above them the sky was a square of stars.

He let it come. The Band came into him as it came into everything, and he stood in the dark by the flasks with one hand on the rail's sleeve and let it, and felt himself go full, and fuller, past where he would have stopped on the ground; and he did the only thing the split day had taught him to do with that, which was to spend it. Not outward. Not where anybody aboard could see a glyph in the dark. Inward, into the cold, into his own hands and face and feet, as a knight warms himself on a winter road; and he was warm, suddenly, all through, inside the fur, and stood there in the rimed iron dark at four miles with his body running hot as a stove and the Band pouring into him from outside as fast as he could burn it, and was not full, and was not empty, and was, for the first time in his life, simply level with the sky.

Ward, at the foot of the ladder, turned his head and looked at him. Ward could not see his face, through the mask, in the dark. Ward looked anyway.

Then the flasks.

He had two of them on the frame by the pump, coupled, the other two left in their rack, empty, because he did not know what would happen and did not want to lose four. Ivo's blue-white lining in each, three coats, smooth as a shell. He opened the first flask's valve. He ran the glass tube forward along the hold in its felt sleeve and up through the open hatch into the dark, and lashed it to the hatch coaming, and came back, and put his hands on the pump's handle, and pumped.

It was air. Only air, the thin black air of four miles, drawn in at the tube's mouth and forced down it into the flask, stroke after stroke, harder and harder, as a smith's boy works a bellows. But it was the Band's air; and the Band was in it, as it was in everything; and what went down the tube into the flask was the air and the Band both, together, squeezed, more and more of it into the same small space. He felt it go in. He felt the flask, under his hand on its neck, go from a dull iron nothing to something that was not nothing: a small hard brightness inside the iron, behind the glass, getting brighter with every stroke, as a stone gets brighter filling.

Tobe came aft along the hold from the racks and pumped when his arms gave out. Then Cade. Then Ward, who had not been asked, and took the handle without a word and pumped a hundred strokes exactly, at an even pace, as if it were a drill in the yard. Ned could not come; she wanted a hand on her air against the wind, resting or not. The gauge on the flask's neck went round, slowly, to the mark Bram had painted on it in red, which was half of what Bram had tested it to; and Emeric shut the valve.

The flask sat on the frame in the dark, iron, rimed white on the outside like everything else down there. To his sense it was a lamp.

They filled the second. Then Ward said, through his mask, flat, the one word Ferra had written into the crew book for exactly this, which was "Time," and Tobe and Cade went up the ladder, and the hatch rumbled shut over the racks on Cade's winch, and Ned put the lift lever over, and the Hod went down.



He spent the way down in the hold, on the valves.

Not the full ones. Those were shut and were to stay shut, and he had his gloved hand on the first full flask's neck the whole of the descent to feel whether it held; that was why he had stayed down there in the cold when the others went up into the warm, and why Ward had stayed with him, at the ladder's foot, saying nothing. But the two empty flasks stood in their rack within reach of his other hand, and in each one's neck, under its cap, was the valve's stem: a small brass plunger that lifted a hair to open and dropped to close. He could move a big thing without his hand, slowly; he had brought a sword down the length of a hall once, level, with nobody touching it. What he could not do was small. A thing the width of a hair, exactly, and then stop. He had been trying for a year, alone, at night, and had nothing to show for it but a great many cups knocked over; and here was a brass stem a hand's breadth from his palm, in the dark, with nobody able to see what he did, and three miles to go down and nothing else to do with his head, which had started, as it always did on the way down from the Band, to ache.

So he lifted it. Not with his hand. With the thing behind his hand, the gravity that came up in him on its own with Ferra and that he had been calling up on purpose, large and slow, for six years; very fine, very small, the width of a hair, under the stem, away from the earth, and let it down. He felt it move. A hair, and down. He did it again. It was like trying to thread a needle with a hammer; every time he tried to make it small it came out too big, and the stem jumped in its seat and clicked, and he had to let it fall, and the headache went up a notch. He did it again. And again. By two miles he could lift it a hair and hold it there, and set it down without a click, and his head was a bell.

Ward, at the ladder's foot, had turned his head again. He was not looking at Emeric. He was looking at the empty flask by Emeric's knee, where the valve's cap had stirred, very slightly, on its stem, in the dark, with nobody touching it.

Ward did not say anything. He looked back up the ladder.

At a mile the first flask cracked.

He heard it. Nobody else did; it was inside the iron, under his hand, a sound so small it was less a sound than a feeling in his palm, a tiny dry tick, like ice on a pond in the night. Then another. Then a great many, all at once, a fine crackle going round the inside of the flask under his glove like frost spreading on a pane; and the small hard brightness inside it, which had sat there steady all the way down from four miles, began to go.

It did not go all at once. It went as water goes from a cracked pot: through every crack at once, into the iron, slowly, and through the iron, more slowly, and out; he felt it come out through the iron under his hand like warmth through a wall, and go into the cold air of the hold round the flask, and be nothing. The brightness dimmed. And dimmed.

By the time she touched the water the first flask was dark. The second was dark too; it had gone at half a mile, the same way, while he had his hand on the first.

She came down on the lake. A quarter of a mile from where she had gone up, by the reeds, at a quarter to one, because Ned had held her nose into the west on her air the whole night with the wind four miles up trying to carry her to the sea, and had brought her down through it in a long slow turn over the water, which nobody had told him to do, and which he said afterward was only what a man did with a loaded boat in a river current if he had any sense. She settled among the reeds with hardly a ripple. Ferra's lamp came down the bank.



Ivo had the pieces on his bench on the Saturday morning.

He had cut both flasks open himself, with Bram's hacksaw, along their length, and laid the halves on his bench in a row with the inside up, and stood over them in his leather apron with a lens in his eye, and he did not look up when Emeric came in.

The lining was crazed all over. Every inch of it. A net of fine white lines through the blue-white glass, so fine they could only be seen with the light coming across them low, like the crazing on an old plate; and here and there along the neck a flake had come away altogether, and the iron showed through, dark.

"Cold," said Ivo.

"Cold."

"I said." Ivo did not say it unkindly. He touched the crazing with the tip of a needle. "Iron shrinks in the cold more than the glass does. It pulled away from it and the glass couldn't come with it." He took the lens out of his eye. "How cold?"

"Colder than the inside of an ice house. A good deal colder."

Ivo nodded slowly. He looked at the halves for a long while.

"Thicker," he said at last. "Five coats, not three. More flux in the first, so it bites the iron. A slower cooling in the kiln, a day, not a night, so it goes on without strain in it." He looked at Emeric. "And keep it warm. I can't make glass that doesn't care about cold. Nobody can. But I can make glass that's never let get cold."

"How?"

"Ask the sailmaker," said Ivo, and put his lens back in his eye.

Abloh made the jackets in two days, from the felt for the flying suits' linings: a coat for each flask, thick, stitched in quilted rows like a winter waistcoat, with a pocket at the bottom where a fire stone the size of a walnut could sit, set low, warm and no warmer; and a flap at the top for the valve, and a hole for the gauge. He brought them up to Ivo's in a sack, and stood in Ivo's glass house among the cones with the sweat coming out on him and watched Ivo take the first relined flask out of the slow kiln, and held out the first jacket.

"That what you wanted?" he said.

"That's what I wanted."

Abloh put the jacket on the flask himself, as a man puts a coat on a child, and did up the buttons, and stood back and looked at it, an iron bottle in a quilted felt waistcoat standing on a glassmaker's bench.

"Never made a coat for a bottle," he said. "Never made one for a prince either, till May." He picked up his empty sack. "It's been a strange summer."



The second run was the twenty-first, a week to the night. Four flasks. Five coats of Ivo's blue-white glass inside each, cooled a day, in Abloh's felt, with a walnut of fire stone warm in each pocket; and the racks full again, two hundred and twelve stones, because the first night's were in the works' hearths already and the works had sent up another barrow of spent ones.

He filled all four at four miles, down in the hold, with Tobe and Cade and Ward at the pump by turns; and on the way down he stayed below with them, Ward at the ladder's foot again, and kept his hand on each of them in turn, through the felt, and felt each one steady, small and hard and bright inside its iron and its glass and its quilted coat, at three miles, at two, at one, and on the water by the reeds.

No crackle. Not a tick.

They held.

He stood on the reed bank at one in the morning with the four flasks in a row at his feet in their felt waistcoats, the lake black and the town asleep, and Ferra beside him with the crew book and a lamp, and Imani with her fingers on Ned's wrist, and Armand, who had come down to the reeds in Wexler's coat at midnight and would not go home; and he knelt down by the first flask and put both hands on it, flat, and shut his eyes, and felt the thing inside it.

It was the Band. A flask of it. Four miles of sky squeezed into an iron bottle the length of his forearm, steady, held, on the ground, by a lake, at one in the morning, a hand's breadth from his palms; and he knelt there in the reeds with his hands on it and felt, under the headache, under the cold, something he had not felt since the first time a lamp had come on in Kaldo with the whole town holding its breath round it.

"Well?" said Ferra.

"It held."

"I can see it held. Ned's said so four times." Ferra did not look at Ned. "How much?"

He opened his eyes and looked up at her.

"A tier of the racks," he said. "One flask. What a whole tier of the racks holds, in a bottle a man can carry."

Ferra's pencil stopped.

Behind them, in the reeds, Cade made a sound, and they all turned. He was standing at the Hod's rail with something in his hands: the long pole with the net on the end of it that he had taken out on deck at the top both nights, on his line, without telling anybody why, because Emeric had told him in the shed that there might be something to catch and had not said what. The net was not empty.

"They were floating," said Cade. He sounded like a boy. "At the top. Just under where she stopped. Little ones, drifting. Like seeds on a pond. You can't see them, you've no light up there, but every so often the starlight comes off one." He held the net out over the rail. "I got three."

They were stones. Three of them, in the bottom of the net, the size of a thumb's end, grey and rough, nothing to look at at all; and to Emeric's sense, as Cade held them out, they were bright, and they were something he knew. He had felt a hundred of them. He had dug them out of the walls of Grayrock's shafts and bought them in dealers' boxes and seated every one of them in the lattice under the ship's deck.

Gravity stones. Wild ones. Floating, four miles up, at the bottom of the Band, where the mana was thick enough to bear them, as a seed is borne on water.

"Nobody dug those," said Ned, looking into the net.

"No," said Emeric.

The first gravity stones Kaldo had ever had that had not come up a shaft on a man's back. He took them out of the net one at a time and held them, and they were warm, and they were full, and they were his, and nobody had dug them, and nobody had died in a shaft for them, and nobody in the realm knew there were any up there to be had.

"How many were there?" he said to Cade.

"I don't know," said Cade. "Dozens. More. I could only reach three." He looked up, at the sky over the lake, at nothing, where four miles up in the dark they were still drifting. "I'll want a longer pole."



Armand had not said anything all this while. He had stood at the edge of the reeds in Wexler's coat with his stick, a little apart, and watched the flasks come up out of the hold in their felt, and the net come over the rail, and the three stones come out of it; and when the others had gone up the bank with the lamps toward the stones and Cade's pole, he came down to the four flasks, slowly, and stood over them.

"May I?" he said.

It was the first time Emeric had heard him ask leave to touch a thing Emeric had made. He nodded.

Armand did not kneel. He could not have got up again. He bent, with one hand on his stick and the other held out flat over the nearest flask, a hand's breadth above its felt coat, not touching it; and his face, in the starlight off the lake, did what it had done in the ship's saloon at the Band's foot the first time, with his hands on the iris rail. The face of a man listening to music in another room.

"You've bottled it," he said.

"Four bottles."

"My master came down from the last climb with his nose bleeding and his eyes like lamps," said Armand, "and told us it was up there, and that it could never be brought down, any more than a man could bring down a cloud in a sack. He said it at the high table in front of the whole tower. And the Church shut the climbs the next spring." He straightened, slowly, with his hand on the stick. "I should have liked him to see a bottle of it standing in the reeds."

He looked at the flask a while longer.

"They're proved," he said. It was not a question.

"They're proved."

"Saturday, then," said Armand, and turned, and went up the bank after the lamps, quite steadily, on his stick; and Emeric, kneeling by the flasks, watched him go, and saw that his free hand, swinging at his side, was still.

Ward came down last. He stood on the bank above the flasks with his arms folded and looked down at Emeric.

"The cap moved," said Ward. "On the empty one. On the way down."

"Yes."

Ward held his eyes a breath longer.

"Nobody saw," he said, and went up the bank.



The east half of the town got its first light that week.

Not all of it. Three lanes: the ones nearest the engine house, behind the works' east wall, where the trench gangs had been since June laying Tess's cable in its duct under the new paving behind the lane gangs, and the lamp posts had stood dark along it for a fortnight, iron, painted green, with glass globes on them like inverted bowls; and on the Tuesday evening at dusk Tess stood at the switchboard in the engine house with her hand on a brass lever and the big dynamo turning on the end of the third engine's shaft, and the three lanes outside in the dusk, every family on them, in their doors, waiting, because Tess had told them; and she put the lever over.

Emeric was not there. He heard it from the keep: a sound across the river, low, from three lanes at once, that was not a cheer; the sound a crowd makes when it has been waiting for a thing and the thing happens and turns out to be more than it had pictured. He went to the window. Across the river, through the dusk, behind the works' wall, there were three lines of lights, white, steady, every one the same, where there had been nothing but the dark and a few windows.

The rest of the east half would have them by the autumn, lane by lane, behind the gangs. Tess stood at the switchboard with her hand on the lever until midnight, watching the needle, and would not let anybody else take the board, and Bram came at ten with her supper in a cloth and stood in the engine house door and did not say anything about steam.



He took a flask down to the cellar the next night.

Not the Hod's crew; Bram and Ferra, after the lamps, by the meeting room and the passage, down to the back wall and the slab in its rebate, which he lifted with the stones' own word, and through into the warm dark of the forge room, which smelled, as it always smelled, of hot iron and oil and the faint sweet dust of spent stones.

The forge stood in the middle of it as it had stood for four years: the chamber the size of a wine barrel in its frame, the rails, the lattice of gravity stones all round it in their anchors, and against the far wall the bank. The bank was the thing Ferra hated. It was a rack of charged stones from floor to ceiling, two hundred of them, coupled to the lattice by a sleeved rail, every one of them fed into the forge a run at a time and drained by it to grey dust, which went out to the shed in a bucket; and every one of them had been bought, or dug, or filled by Orrin and Vesk on a working day that could have been spent on something else, and every one of them was a line in Ferra's book, and the line had been ugly for four years and she had said so every quarter.

Bram had made a coupling. A brass piece, short, with a seat for the flask's valve at one end and a seat for a glass tube at the other, and the glass tube Ivo's, thick, to run from the flask to the bank's rail where it went into the lattice, so that the lattice could drink from the flask instead of from the bank, through glass, all the way, with nothing between the Band and the forge but a valve.

"Run?" said Bram.

"The valve seats," said Emeric. "For the flasks. A dozen. And the tubes for their gauges, drawn to the master."

It was a run he had been putting off for a fortnight. A night's run, on the bank: the seats were small and hard and had to be true to a hair, and the gauge tubes had to be drawn thin and curved and sealed without a flaw, and the lattice had to hold them in the air in the chamber from every side at once while the fire stones did their work; and on the bank, with the lattice drawing what the bank could give and no more, that was a night's run, start to finish, and thirty stones of dust in the bucket in the morning. He had worked it on paper and Ferra had priced it and he had put it off.

He put the feedstock in. He declared it, and declared the work, and the tolerances, in the chamber's own way, through the control stone, as he did every run. Then he opened the flask's valve, with his hand.

The forge took it.

He felt the lattice wake, all of it, at once. On the bank it woke a few stones at a time, as many as the bank's draw could feed, and worked round the chamber in turns, a few stones squeezing while the others rested, round and round, slow, like a team of horses taking turns at a heavy load; that was how he had built it, because that was all the bank could give at one breath, and there had been no point building a lattice that wanted more than its feed could hold. Now every stone in the lattice woke together. Every one. And every one drew as hard as it wanted, all at once, and the flask gave it, through the glass, without any sense that it was being asked for much; and the workpiece in the chamber, which he could feel in the lattice's grip, came up into the centre of the chamber and was held there from every side at once, perfectly, with nothing resting, and the fire stones came up white.

The run took an hour and a quarter.

Bram stood by the chamber's window with his spectacles on and watched the gauge tubes draw themselves thin in the air, curve, seal, without a flaw, in the white heat, in a quarter of the time it took on the bank; and did not say anything at all, which for Bram was a great deal.

Ferra stood by the bank with her book and her pencil and watched the flask's gauge go back from the red mark, slowly, not very far.

When it was done, and the seats and the tubes lay on the catch tray cooling, two dozen, true to a hair, she went and looked at the flask's gauge, close, with her lamp; and then at the bank on the wall, every stone of it still full, not one touched; and then at the bucket beside the bank, which was empty, which had never once been empty at the end of a run in four years.

"How much of it did that use?" she said.

"A sixth. Less."

"Of one flask."

"Of one flask."

"And on the bank?"

"All night. Thirty stones."

Ferra stood looking at the empty bucket for a long breath. Then she opened her book at the page she had carried for four years, the bank's page, the row with the long ugly column under it in her small square hand, quarter after quarter; and held her pencil over it; and did not strike it.

"Not yet," she said. "That's not the feed. That's a bottle. It came down from four miles in a coat in an iron hold, and you've four of them." She closed the book on her pencil. "When it comes down every week, in a tank, to a pipe in that wall, I'll strike it. Not before." She looked at him. "But I'll want a new column. For this. Starting tonight."



He did not go up to bed. He sat on the cellar step with his back to the cold stone and the forge ticking as it cooled and Bram gone up to the house and Ferra on the step beside him, and looked at the chamber in its frame, the size of a wine barrel; and saw, quite clearly, as he had not let himself see it in four years, what it ought to have been.

"It's too small," he said.

Ferra did not say anything.

"I built it for the bank. For what the bank could give at one breath." He looked at the lattice round the chamber, the stones in their anchors, every one of them now drained back to rest. "A bigger chamber wants a bigger lattice. More stones, all round it, holding a bigger thing from every side at once. And every one of them wants feeding at the same time, all of them, or it can't hold. The bank couldn't do that. If I'd built it bigger four years ago, I'd have had to run it with half its lattice switched off, a few stones at a time, round and round, and a lattice with half its stones switched off can't centre anything. It can't do the one thing it's for." He shook his head. "I'd have built a machine that couldn't run. To have a bigger machine. On paper."

"So you built a small one that could."

"So I built one the size the feed would hold." He looked at the flask in its felt coat on the floor by the coupling, a sixth of it gone, five-sixths left, warm and bright behind its iron. "The feed's different now."

"How much bigger?"

He thought about it. He had thought about it for an hour, watching the tubes draw themselves in the white air.

"Long," he said. "Not wide. Fourteen feet, perhaps. Seven across. Beside this one, toward the old canal shaft, cut into the rock, on the same frame, the same lattice, the same index." He was quiet. "Crank shafts. Wheels. A turbine's blades, all of them in one run, not one at a time. The masters for things we can't make yet because nobody can hold a thing that big true while it's worked."

"A second forge," said Ferra.

He turned his head and looked at her. She was looking at the chamber, not at him, with her book closed on her knees and her pencil in it.

"One forge," she said. "That was the rule. Yours. Not mine. You wrote it the week it first ran, at the top of the forge book, and I priced it, and you signed under it. One forge, because one forge is one secret in one cellar and a man can carry one key." She was quiet. "I'll not have a second."

"Not a second. The same one. The same frame, the same index, the same door." He looked at the chamber. "A bigger room."

Ferra thought about that for a long while, with the forge ticking.

"A bigger room," she said at last. "On the same key."

"On the same key."

"Then it's a room." She opened her book, and turned to a clean page, and wrote at the top of it in her small square capitals, THE LONG BED, and under it a date, and under that nothing. "I'll price a room. When there's a feed that comes down every week in a tank to a pipe in that wall, and not before, you can cut it." She shut the book. "That's not a veto. I'm still saving that."

"I know."

"Good," said Ferra, and stayed where she was on the cellar step beside him with her book on her knees, not touching, looking at the small chamber in its frame in the dark and the bottle of sky on the floor beside it, until the second bell went, somewhere far above them in the keep.
