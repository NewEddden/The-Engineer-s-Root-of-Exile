Chapter 232 - Lake Kaldo by Night

The first thing the water did was get into his head.

Not the water. The sense. He had let it out on the jetty in the fitting shop's first week, in the lake up to his chest, to feel whether the medium went through water the way it went through air, and it had; and he had thought no more about it, because he had been thinking about lead and plates and a boy's scorched glove. He let it out again on the first real night, at the bottom of the lake off the jetty's end, twenty feet down in the black with his lamp off and his hand on the line, and it went out from him the way it always did, from the whole of him at once, and kept going.

In air it went out like warmth off a stone and thinned, and a few hundred paces off it was a whisper and then nothing. In the water it did not thin. It went out and out, sharper the further it went, the way a knock on a ship's hull carries under the water to a man with his ear on the planks a mile off, when he could not have heard it across a room in air. It went out across the bottom of the lake in every direction at once, and came back to him full.

Full of everything. That was the trouble. Every fish in the lake, which he had never once thought about, was a small bright knot in the medium, moving. There were a great many fish in the lake. There were eels in the mud under him, dozens, lying in their holes, each one a thin warm thread. There was a pike, a big one, hanging in the weed thirty yards off, very still, a long cold shape with a knot of attention in its head that he could feel turning toward his lamp. There were the currents, the slow cold water coming down off the hills into the lake at its head and sliding along the bottom under the warm, and he could feel the edge between them like the edge of a blade. There were Ferra and Zale on the line behind him, two warm bright shapes, one of them breathing much faster than the other.

It was like walking into a hall full of people all talking at once, and understanding every word.

He pulled it in, fast, as he had pulled it in once on the deck of a ship over the sea to Emberfell when it had given him a woman's heartbeat he had not asked for. He hung in the black water with his hand on the line and his own breathing loud in the helmet, and waited for the lake to go quiet in his head. It took a while.

Then he let it out again, a little. Only a little. Like opening a door a crack in a loud house.

Farther, he thought. And sharper. And louder. He would have to learn to listen to one voice in the hall. He had never needed to before. The air had always done the thinning for him.

They went into the lake every night for three weeks, and most of it was dull, and it was meant to be.

The lake at Kaldo had a shelf. Off the works jetty the bottom went down gently for a hundred yards, mud and weed and the old stones of a wall that had stood there before the lake was a lake, and then it stopped going down gently and dropped, all at once, into the deep water at the head of the lake, where nobody had ever found the bottom with a line and nobody had tried very hard. Emeric found it in the first week. It was a little under a hundred feet. He had Wes lay a guideline along the top of the shelf and down its face, knotted at every six feet and tied off at every turn to stakes Bram drove into the mud, the way the line would go into the cave, and that was the lake's cave, and they lived on it.

Wes had asked for the line. He had come to Emeric after the first night, cap in his hands, and said that he had poured charge at the lock at the Drop House for two years and taught four men to do it, and never once hurried, and he would like to be the one who never hurried on the rope. Emeric had given it to him. He sat on the jetty's end every night in an oilskin with the line's end over his knee and a lantern shut down to a slit beside him, and his hand on the rope, feeling it, and he did not hurry.

They learned the four pulls. One: all well. Two: give me slack. Three: take in. Four, and again, and again: come out now. Wes pulled them from the jetty at odd times, in the middle of whatever they were doing on the bottom, and whatever they were doing, they stopped and answered, and if the pull was four they came out, with nobody asking why. Twice it was four for no reason at all. The second time, Zale came up the jetty steps and took his helmet off and asked Wes what the matter was, and Wes said nothing was the matter, the prince had told him to pull four twice in the three weeks for no reason, so that nobody ever stopped to wonder whether a four had a reason. Zale looked at his brother. His brother was taking his own helmet off and did not look back.

They learned the lost line. A diver was sent along the shelf's line with his glass blacked over with a cloth tied round the helmet, so that he saw nothing at all, and somewhere along the line his partner took his hand off it, gently, and moved him a yard away, and let him go. He had to stop. Not swim; stop. Tie the end of his spare reel to the nearest stake, and hold it, and sweep out from the stake on the reel's line in an arc, feeling, until his hand or his reel's line crossed the guideline again. Then back to it, and a hand on it, and never let go of it again. The first time Zale did it he swam off into the dark to find it, the way anybody would, and came up at the far end of the shelf, in the weed, a hundred yards from the line, on a pull of four, and sat on the jetty's steps with his head in his hands and said that if that had been a cave he would be dead. Emeric agreed. The second time he did it right. The fifth time he did it right with his eyes shut and whistling, which could be heard through the water, faintly, and was forbidden after.

They learned the silt-out, which was the same drill with the cloth on and a partner's hand on your shoulder, and your own hand on the line, and nothing else, because in brown water a lamp is a brown glow a hand from your face and the line is the only thing in the world.

They learned the helmet flood. Each of them, in turn, on the bottom at the shelf's top, twenty feet down, with a partner holding him, opened the little cock Bram had set low in the glass's collar, and let the lake into the helmet, and felt it come up cold round the chin and the mouth and the nose and the eyes, and put his head down and found the mouthpiece below his chin, and bit, and breathed. The first breath was the worst. The mouth did not believe it. Ferra did it with her eyes open, and came up with them red and said the lake tasted of eels. Zale did it three times before he could do it without his free hand going to the line so hard that Emeric could feel it through the rope ten yards off. The two knights who would hold the dry city did it in their Skins with the loops clipped on over the plateless rubber, and one of them, the big one, Sir Aled Cray, came up and said he had been under water in plate in the river at the coalition war with a man holding his head down, and this was worse, and he did not know why, since it was quieter.

"Because you did it on purpose," said Sir Gavin Tull, who was younger and thinner and did not talk much.

They learned the bailout. Bram had put a second cock on the mouthpiece's hose, a black horn barrel with a lever, and when it was thrown the mouthpiece stopped drawing from the loop and drew straight from the bottle, and the breath went out past the lips into the water in a stream of silver bubbles. It spent a bottle in a quarter of the time. It was for when the loop was spoiled, when water got into the potash and the potash turned to lye and the lye came up the hose, and a man could not breathe what was in his own bag. They did it on the bottom, every one of them, a dozen times, on a word from their partner, until the hand went to the lever before the mind had heard the word. "It's for going home," Emeric said, every time. "Not for staying. You throw it and you turn round."

And they learned thirds. Every diver, before every dive, read his gauge at the jetty and said the number aloud, and Wes wrote it on his slate. A third of it was for going in. A third was for coming out. A third was for whatever went wrong. When any diver's gauge touched the first third, everybody turned. When the sand-glass on the belt ran out, everybody turned. When anybody made the sign, a flat hand drawn across the throat, everybody turned, and nobody asked why until they were on the jetty, and then only if the diver who had made it wanted to say.

"And if he doesn't want to say?" said Zale.

"Then he doesn't. We turned. That's all that happened."

It was the rule of thirds that brought them home on the ninth night, and it was Ferra.

They were at the bottom of the shelf, the three of them, deeper than they had been, sixty feet down its face on the half set, the mixed breath, with the knots counting under their hands. It was the first time on the half set. Emeric had made it that afternoon in the gas shed and checked it three times against a candle in a jar, the way he had worked out, and it was in all three bottles, and it was right. They had gone down the face of the shelf slowly, on the line, with Emeric first and Ferra second and Zale behind, and it was very dark and very cold, colder than the top by a long way, and quiet, and the sense was full of the deep water's slow weight and nothing else, for there was nothing living down there worth the name.

Ferra touched his shoulder.

He turned. In the green glow of his lamp her face in her glass was close, and he could see water in the bottom of her helmet. Not much. A finger's depth, lying along the collar, round her chin. As he looked at it it moved, and he saw a thin line of bright bubbles come up from the collar's edge at the back of her neck and go up past her glass, slowly, one after another, like a string of beads.

She drew her flat hand across her throat.

He was already turning. He pulled three on the line, and felt Zale pull three behind him, and heard, a long way up, faint, Wes take in, and the line came taut in his hand. He took Ferra's wrist and put her hand on the line in front of his own, so that she was between him and the top, and started up the face of the shelf, hand over hand, not fast, as he had told them, never faster than the smallest bubble, with the water in her helmet rising a little every time she moved her head.

He looked at her gauge as they went. A little more than a third gone. Two thirds in the bottle. Enough to go up twice.

She was breathing hard. He could feel it in the sense, the fast warm stutter of it close in front of him, and he could hear, or thought he could hear, through the water and the two glasses, the sound of it in her helmet. The water had reached her lower lip. She had her head up, tilted back, keeping her mouth over it, and she was looking at him through the glass, not at the water, and her face was quite calm, and her breathing was not.

At forty feet it was at her lip and she put her head down and took the mouthpiece, and he saw her bite. He saw her shoulders go still. Then she lifted her head again with the mouthpiece in her teeth and the water up to her nose and looked at him, and nodded, once.

He wanted to go faster. He wanted it more than he had wanted anything in a long time. He had her wrist in one hand and the line in the other and forty feet of black water between them and the jetty, and every part of him wanted to put his thumb on the lever at his wrist and let the stones at his hips take them both up that line as fast as Ferra had gone out across the lake on the fourth night, and have her on the planks, and her helmet off, and the lake out of it.

He did not. He went up the line hand over hand, never faster than the smallest bubble, and at ten feet by the knots he stopped, and held her there against the line with the water over her nose and the mouthpiece in her teeth, and turned the sand-glass on his belt, and waited for it, all of it, while she breathed on the mouthpiece and looked at him and did not look away. Zale hung on the line below them. The glass ran. It took about as long as the whole of the rest of his life so far.

Then they went up the last ten feet and came out at the jetty steps, and Bram and Wes had her by the arms and up and on the planks, and Bram had the collar's bolts off with his fingers before anybody could hand him a spanner, and the helmet came off and a gout of lake water came out of it over her shoulders and down her front, and she spat the mouthpiece out and knelt on the planks and coughed, and breathed, and put her hand flat on the wet wood and stayed there.

Emeric knelt in front of her. He did not touch her. He did not trust his hands.

"The collar," she said, when she could. She put her hand to the back of her neck. "The gasket. At the back. I felt it go cold."

Bram had the helmet upside down on his knee with the lamp on it. He turned the collar ring to the light and put his thumb on the rubber at the back, and it moved under his thumb where it should not have moved. He did not say anything. He did not need to. He looked up at the prince, and then at Ferra, and his face went a colour Emeric had not seen on it since the boy's glove.

"Mine," Bram said. "I seated it. I'll seat every one again. Tonight."

"It wasn't the depth," Ferra said. "At the top of the shelf it was dry. It went on the face. Where it's cold." She was shaking, now, all over, in the wet suit, on her knees on the planks, and her voice was not shaking at all. "The cold shrank it. Or shrank the ring. One or the other."

"Both," said Bram. "Rubber goes hard in the cold. Steel goes small." He put the helmet down very gently on the planks, the way he had put down the pane with the hammer's pieces under it. "I'll have it seated on a warm ring and tested in the cold. Ice in a tub. Every helmet."

Emeric was looking at Ferra's gauge on the frame at her hip. He looked at it for a long time.

"Two thirds," he said. "You had two thirds when we turned."

"I had two thirds," she said. "I had a helmet full of lake. I turned." She looked up at him, and there was water running out of her hair down her face, and she wiped it with the back of her wet glove, which did nothing. "That's what it's for. You said so. Anyone's call."

"It's what it's for." He sat back on his heels. "It worked."

"It worked," said Zale, from the steps. He had his helmet off and was sitting on the bottom step with the lake round his knees. He was grey in the lamplight. "Nobody's hurt. I want to say that out loud. Nobody's hurt." He put his forehead against his raised knee. "I'd still like to be sick, if nobody minds."

Nobody minded. He was not sick. Nobody went back in that night. They sat on the jetty, all of them, in the cold, with the lamps shut down to slits, until Ferra stopped shaking; and then Bram took all four helmets up to the fitting shop in his arms, two at a time, and did not come out of it until morning.

It was the second week that they found out how far she could hear him.

They had spoken through the crack since the first night: the hair's width he could hold open between his room and hers without either of them going in, that let a voice through and nothing else. It had always been across a table. Once, at the island, across the width of a ship's deck. He had never tried it under water, because he had never needed to, and when he tried it the first time, at the top of the shelf, with Ferra an arm's length off on the line, it was there at once, clearer than it had ever been in air, as if the water carried it the way it carried the sense.

You're very loud, she said, in his head, dry, as she said everything.

It's the water.

It's you. Go further off.

So he did, on the next night. He sent her along the line to the far end of the shelf with Wes's second line on her, to the last stake, where the shelf turned north along the lake's deep edge, a long way, two hundred yards and more; and went himself the other way, to the jetty end, with Zale on the line beside him; and hung there in the black, twenty feet down, with his hand on the line and his lamp off, and reached for her.

Always before, it had been all his. He had held the crack open himself, both ends of it, the way a man holds a door open with one hand and the next door with the other, and it had reached as far as his arms, which was a table's width. It was not all his now. He reached, and he felt her end of it already there, held, steady, the way she held a pen: her own room, her own door, and her own hand on it, from her own side. He did not have to stretch to her. He only had to meet her.

Ferra.

I'm here.

She was two hundred yards off through black water and her voice in his head was as near as if she had been at his shoulder. Nearer. It was her voice exactly: level, a little dry, careful with its words the way she was careful with figures, and under it something he had never heard in it across the table, because across the table there were always other people.

How far are you? she said.

The jetty.

That's the whole shelf. A pause. That's further than you can see.

I know.

Because I'm holding my end.

Yes.

Another pause. He could feel her in the sense too, a long way off down the shelf's edge, a small warm knot in the cold, very still.

Good, she said. I've been holding it for years. I'm glad it's finally good for something.

He laughed, in the helmet, out loud, and Zale on the line beside him jumped and looked round at him through the glass, and he could not explain, and did not try.

He trained the pulls anyway. He told her so, through the crack, the next night, when she asked him why they were still doing the four pulls on the line every night when she could hear him from one end of the lake to the other.

Because it's me, he said. It's me and you. It's two people. If I'm hurt, or I'm out, or my head's full of something and I can't hold it, it's gone. The rope's not me. The rope's there if I'm not.

And if I'm not?

Then it's there if you're not.

That's a very long way of saying the rope's the floor.

The rope's the floor.

She pulled one on the line, all well, from two hundred yards off, and he felt it come down the rope into his hand a moment later, small and clear. It was the first time in his life that anyone had said a thing to him twice at once, in two different ways, and meant the same thing by both.

Zale could not hear him. Zale held no stone, and never had, and the crack ran only between people who had a room. So on the third week Emeric did the other thing.

He had done it once before, two years ago, in the small room at Kaldo, and it had failed the first time because Zale held on. Zale always held on. It was the only thing about him that did not come easily. He had let go on the second try and come into the grey place, the first place, the plain grey light, and sat there looking round him as if he were in a church in a foreign country, and asked whether he was really sitting in a chair; and then Emeric had brought him out, and he had not been back since.

They were at the bottom of the jetty's end, twenty feet, on the line. Emeric clipped his own tether to it, and then Zale's, so that both of them were fast to the rope and could not drift off it, and Wes on the jetty had his hand on it and knew what they were doing. Then he put his gloved hand on his brother's shoulder, on the plate there, and held it, and looked at him through the two glasses, and reached.

Zale held on.

Of course he did. He was at the bottom of a lake in the dark with his breath on his back and a rope at his belt and a man's hand on his shoulder, and everything in him that had kept him alive at sea for half a life was holding onto all of it with both hands. Emeric felt him do it. He felt the door shut, the way a man feels a hand on the other side of a door lean on it. He did not push. He never pushed anybody through that door, and he was not going to start with his brother at the bottom of a lake.

He waited, with his hand on Zale's shoulder. After a while he felt his brother's breathing change in the sense, slow down, the way a man's breathing slows when he has decided something. And the door opened, a little, and a little more, the way it had in the small room the second time. It's like going over the side at night, Zale had said then. You don't decide to. You just stop holding the rail.

He stopped holding the rail.

Emeric brought him in. To the grey place again, the first place, the one with nothing in it, because the room above Kaldo still had too much in it that was not Zale's to see, and a lake had not changed that.

But it was not empty this time. He had been there first, a week ago, alone, and put a table in it, plain boards on trestles, the only thing he had ever built there, and on the table everything he knew about a hole under a ridge on an island. Zale came into the light standing, as a man comes in out of the rain, and stood where he had come in, as if there were a door behind him, though there was not. He looked at the light, and at the table, and at his brother, who was sitting at it.

"Oh," said Zale.

"Sit down."

"I'm at the bottom of a lake."

"You're at the bottom of a lake with your hand on a line and my hand on your shoulder, and Wes has the other end, and you've been there about a breath. Sit down. This takes a while in here and no time out there. That's the point of it."

Zale came across the grey floor slowly. Behind him, where he had come in, a shelf had put itself up out of nothing, the way they always did when somebody came in, with the things on it that were his; and behind Emeric was his own, where he did not turn to look at it. Zale did not look at either. He sat down across the table. On the table, laid out, was the island. Not a drawing of it. The island itself, as Emeric had seen it from a line an hour before dawn the winter before last, small, with the channel round it and the two forts at its ends, and the ridge, and the benches cut in the ridge, and the hollow under the lowest bench; and under that, cut away as if somebody had sliced the ridge with a knife and lifted off the near half, what the sense had told him on two nights last winter: the space going down and in, and the water standing in it, and the passage going on down past the water, and at the edge of what he had felt, very far down, something he had made as a pale blur, because that was what it had been.

Zale looked at it for a long time.

"That's the ridge," he said. "That's where the man with the lantern stood." He put out his finger, and stopped it an inch from the hollow. "You've been there."

"More than once. I'll tell you how. Not in here." Emeric put his own finger on the hollow. "That's the way down. Ansel Vey's way down. It's under Kesrath's lowest bench. It goes in, and then it goes down, and then there's water, a short way of it, not deep. That's the sump." He moved his finger. "Past it there's a space. Dry. I don't know how big. Big. And past that there's more water, and it goes down, a long way, and I don't know where it comes out. That's the deep water. That's what the half set's for. That's what the bends are for." He looked up. "This is what we'll be doing, in order, night by night. I'm going to tell you all of it, and you're going to ask me everything you want to ask, and then we're going to go back out and it'll be the same breath."

He told him. All of it, in order. It took a long time. Zale asked a great many questions, most of them good, and some of them the kind of question a man asks who has been in a great many places he could not see the way out of and had got out of them anyway. Where the air was. Who held the rope. What happened if the ones at the top were found. What happened if the water rose. Emeric answered everything he could and said I don't know to the rest, and Zale nodded at every I don't know as if it were the only answer he trusted.

At the end of it Zale sat back in the stool that was not a stool and looked at the island on the table.

"I'm still at the bottom of the lake," he said.

"Still."

"Take me back, then."

Emeric took him back.

They were at the bottom of the lake, on the line, in the dark, with Emeric's hand on his shoulder. Zale's breath was in the middle of going out. It finished going out. He hung there on his tether, and looked at his brother through the glass for a long moment, and then pulled one on the line, all well, and Wes pulled one back, and they went up.

On the jetty, with his helmet off and his hair plastered flat and the lake running out of the suit's cuffs, Zale sat on the bollard and looked at the water for a while without saying anything, which was unlike him.

"That," he said at last, "is the strangest thing I have ever done under water." He considered. "And I have been past the edge of the map. I have been past the edge of both maps. There's a place past the western sea where the fish walk up the beach on their fins at night and sit in the trees, and I have seen that, with these eyes, sober." He shook his head. "That was stranger."

On the last night of the third week, Ferra came up the jetty steps after Emeric, which she never did; she always came up first, because he always put her first on the line. She came up after him because he had held her there, at ten feet, on the knots, with the glass running, for longer than the glass, for no reason she could see.

He had his helmet off by the time she had hers off. She knelt on the planks in the lamplight with the lake running out of her hair and looked at him.

"The glass ran out," she said. "At ten feet. You held me past it."

"I wanted to be sure."

"Of what?"

"That the glass was long enough."

"It's always long enough. You said you'd make it too long on purpose." She took her gloves off, one and then the other, and put them in her helmet. "You've been holding me past the glass every night since the collar."

He did not answer that. Behind them on the jetty, Zale had stopped taking off his boots and was listening with an interest he was taking no trouble at all to make look idle. Osric, who had come down to watch the last night, was sitting on a coil of rope with a pipe he was not allowed to light and a face of perfect innocence. Wes was coiling the line. Bram was pretending to look at a helmet collar.

"Every night," Ferra said. "Since the collar. An extra sixty count. I counted."

"Of course you counted."

"I count everything." She looked at him steadily. "I'd like it in the book, what the extra sixty's for. If it's for the bends, I'll write bends. If it's for the collar, I'll write collar." A pause. "If it's for something else, I'll write that."

The jetty was very quiet. Even Wes had stopped coiling.

"Write collar," Emeric said.

Ferra looked at him a moment longer. Then she got up, with her helmet under her arm, and went past him up the jetty toward the shed, and as she went past she put her bare wet hand on the top of his head, briefly, the way you touch a child's head going past a chair, and was gone into the dark.

"She's going to write something else," said Zale, into the silence.

"She's going to write collar," said Osric, "and underline it." He put his unlit pipe in his mouth. "Twice."

Bram put the helmet collar down on the planks and said, to it, "Sixty," and nothing else, and picked it up again.

He had one more thought in the lake, and he shelved it.

It came to him on the last night, alone, after the others had gone up. He had taken a boat out onto the lake, a little way, over the deep water past the shelf, to be quiet, and sat in it with his hands on the oars and the sense out, a little, the way he had learned, a door open a crack. And he had found that he could feel down through the boat's planks and through the water, a long way, as clearly as he had felt it with the water all round him. The bottom of the deep water, which nobody had ever found. A sunken tree down there, lying on its side. A shape by the island's shore that was the hull of a boat that had gone down in some storm before his time, with its ribs showing. All of it, from a boat, with his hands on the oars and nothing wet but the oars.

He sat and thought about a harbour.

Any harbour. A king's harbour, full of ships, at night, and a man in a small boat out in the dark beyond the moorings with his hands on his oars, feeling down through the black water. The hulls. How deep each one sat, which was how full she was. What was lying on the bottom under the quays. What had been sunk there, and where, and why. A chain across the harbour mouth under the water, where the chart said there was none.

He sat with it a long time. Then he put it on a shelf in the back of the room, with the other things he had thought of and not done, and took up the oars, and rowed back to the jetty.

In the morning they began to take the fitting shop apart and put it in crates.
