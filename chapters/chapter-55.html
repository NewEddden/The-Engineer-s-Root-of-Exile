Chapter 55 - Stretch Nine

The runner came in from the farm watch at the eighth hour, out of breath, with ice in the wool of his coat, and said there were lights in the trees on the west bank.

Ward had him say it twice and then say it a third time with the parts in the order Ward wanted them in, which was where and how many and how long.

The treeline behind the old flank, where the ground came up and the ford path ran out of the woods. Twelve fires, or fifteen. Nobody was counting fires, they were counting men crossing in front of them, which was not the same thing and Ward said so. They had been there since dark and they had not come down.

"Sethe's," Osric said.

"Somebody's," Ward said.

It was the night of the new moon, which meant that the only light on the west bank belonged to whoever had lit it, and the frost had been in the ground for a week, which meant that anything on wheels could move anywhere it liked out there.

Nobody was going to take the island. Emeric had known that since the first week he owned it and Ward had confirmed it standing on the wall head with his arms folded: water on every side, one crossing, and a wall on rock. The island was not the argument. The argument was everything outside it, which was the Works, and the pump-house, and the seed store, and the silo bins, and four hundred yards of relaid main, and the farms, and the last time men had come down the river road in the dark they had burned a field a mile and a half long and it had cost the barony a year.

Ward took most of the watch out across the Left Bridge inside twenty minutes, with Declan and Osric and thirty of the line, and left the wall thinned and the gate shut behind him.

At the gatehouse door he stopped.

"You are in the keep tonight," he said.

"I am in the keep tonight."

"Say it like a man who means to be there in an hour."

"I will be in the keep, Ward."

Ward went out. The runners started coming back about an hour after that, and what they brought back was nothing, which Emeric liked less each time it arrived.

The men in the treeline moved. They came down the slope a little and went back up it. Two of them walked along the edge of the wood with a torch each, in full view, for no purpose anybody could name. They lit a fire, let it die, and lit another one thirty yards along. Ward's first note said holding. His second said they will not close. His third said one word, and the word was theatre, and Emeric sat with that in the keep with the shutters open and the cold coming in and tried to work out what a man buys with an evening of theatre.

He got as far as time, and was still turning it over when the knock came.

Rylan was in harness, with snow in the shoulder of it, and he was breathing like a man who had come up the hill fast.

"Nine is faulting," he said.

Emeric was on his feet before the next sentence.

"Flicker?"

"Flicker. Not dark. It went twice while I was watching it and then a third time longer, and I have read that card enough times to know what it says and what it does not say."

"Who has the chair?"

"I sent for Hollis. He will be down by now, or he will be in the next ten minutes. I know the rule, my lord, and I wrote it, and I have broken it tonight, and I will take that in the morning, because there is nobody in that gatehouse to send and the card says fetch you."

They went down the hill together. Emeric's reasoning took him about the length of the courtyard and he checked it twice on the way and it held both times.

Nine was the blind run. Nine was the one stretch of the ring the keep could not see and the only door in the wall that opened onto water, and tonight most of the watch was standing in a frozen field on the far side of the island because a dozen men had decided to be visible. If there was a night in the year on which he could not afford sixty yards of unwatched wall over deep water, this was that night. And if it was a starved stone it was twelve minutes of his time and a spare out of the box, and he would be back in the keep before Ward was over the bridge.

The gatehouse was empty when they came through it. The chair was empty and the brazier had burned down to almost nothing and there was no Hollis.

Nine was flickering.

Emeric stood in front of the board and watched it do it: a low glow that came up and fell away and came up again, unevenly, in no rhythm he could name at that distance, while the other eleven sat dark and steady the way they were meant to.

"He will be coming," Rylan said.

"Take a lantern."

They went east through the town. It was the coldest night of the winter so far and the lanes were empty and their own boots were the loudest thing in the barony. Past the market square with the payroll board frozen and unreadable, past the shuttered front of the soap shop, and along the wall of the churchyard, where the stones were white with it.

There was a light in the parish house. Doran kept his own hours and always had, and Emeric registered the light the way you register a lit window in a cold street, which is to say with something like approval, and walked on.

Behind the church the wall ran blind for sixty yards and the stair went down through the middle of it.

Rylan went first with the lantern because the steps were narrow and iced at the top. Emeric came down after him with his hand on the inside curve of the wall and the cold coming up off the water to meet them, and at the bottom the lantern showed the water gate, and the ledge beyond it, and the black moving surface of the branch four feet below that.

The bar was off its pegs.

It was standing against the wall in the corner where a man would stand it if he had taken it down and meant to put it back.

"I lifted it on my round," Rylan said. "To look at the ledge. I have not put it back yet."

Emeric said nothing to that, because the node was in the inner arch at eye height and he had already knelt to put his hand on it.

It was whole.

He knew it inside a second and a half. There was nothing wrong with the working at all. It was as he had written it fourteen months ago, holding its small steady push out across the ledge and the water under it, and every part of it was where he had put it.

What was wrong was the draw.

It was jumping. It went up and fell back and went up, and the intervals were not the intervals of a working coming apart, which is ragged and gets worse, and they were not the intervals of a stone running out, which is a long slow sag. They had a shape. He held on to it and counted, and the shape was four seconds, then five, then four, and it was not a shape a stone makes.

It was the shape water makes.

He took the lantern out of Rylan's hand without asking for it and put it to the grate of the water gate, down and to the right, and the light went out across the ledge and found the line.

It was a cord, wet, running from a ring bolt in the ledge stone straight down into the current, and six feet out and two feet under, coming in and going back out on the swing of the branch, was a charged stone the size of a fist.

It was in and out of the field. In and out, four seconds, five seconds, four.

Somebody had put it there so that the lamp at the gate would lie.

Emeric turned round to say so.

Rylan was standing two steps up with his empty hand still half out from where the lantern had been taken, and his face was not the face of a man who has just watched his lord find something.

Emeric had spent nine days listening to him and had not once, in those nine days, looked at him.

The water gate swung outward on its hinges.

Three men came up off the ledge fast and quiet with their boots wrapped, and a fourth behind them, and Emeric got his hands up and one breath into the second drill, the blade of moving air, which he had built for a line of men across open ground at forty feet and had practised ten thousand times against nothing at all; and the first man was already inside it, close as a dance partner, in a stairwell four feet wide with Rylan in the only other direction the working could go.

A hand came over his mouth. A knee took the back of his leg. The wall came up against his shoulder and somebody had his wrist and was turning it the way a man turns a wrist who has done it for a living.

Then cloth over his head, and a cord at his wrists, pulled hard enough to stop being about his wrists.

Nobody said anything for four or five seconds. He could hear water, and six men breathing, and the lantern being set down on stone.

Then a voice came up from the ledge, from outside, below.

It was a level voice. Unhurried. It put its weight on no particular word and it did not rise at the end of anything, and it had the flatness of a man reading a name off a list in a room where the list is long and nobody is in a hurry.

"Rylan," it said. "Finn is warm. He has been fed twice today and he slept the night in a bed."

The stairwell was very quiet.

"Carry your lord down to the water. Gently. Mind his head on the arch."

And Rylan did.

He came up the two steps and got an arm under Emeric's knees and one across his back, and picked him up the way a man picks up a child who has fallen asleep somewhere inconvenient and has to be got to bed without waking, and turned sideways at the arch so that Emeric's head went under it clear.

His hands were shaking. That was the only thing about it that was not steady.

The cold of the ledge came through the cloth as they went out onto it, and then the wood of a deck took the weight instead of stone, and the whole of it moved a little under them, the way a floor never does. There was pitch. New pitch, close and thick, a hull tarred within the last month.

A hatch grated back. A ladder. Hands under his arms, taking him down, and the smell changing to bilge and old rope and tar.

They set him on boards and the hatch came down.

In the dark, close by, something breathed in twice and did not breathe out cleanly, the way a child breathes who has been crying for a long time and has stopped only because he was told to.

A small voice said, "Da?"
