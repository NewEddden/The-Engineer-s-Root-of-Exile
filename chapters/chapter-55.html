Chapter 55 - First Light

The wire went up to the walls in the first days of March, on poles and insulators like the line's, from the powerhouse door across the mill yard and up the bank to the foot of the east bridge, and on up the slope to the corner of the wall above it, and from there round the whole circuit of the wall and back.

It was iron. Iron where it could be, copper where it must. The copper was in the lamps, in the coils of Tess's clutch feeds, and in the short leads from the wire to each lamp, and nowhere else. The rest was iron as thick as a reed, drawn on the big holes of Tess's draw plate on Harl's wheel, because iron carried the current grudgingly and the thicker it was the less it grudged. It cost the dynamo about a lamp's worth of its push to drive the current all the way round that much iron and back. Emeric had built that into the three sheets in December, and had not told anybody, and was glad now that he had not had to.

The gate crew put it up. They had put up the line, and they had opinions about poles, and they said so to the carpenter at every one. The eight housings went onto Ward's eight places on the third and fourth of March, and Tess went round after them with a boy carrying her tools and set every washer herself, as she had been told, and then set them all again because she had not liked the first one.

By then the whole town knew.

Nobody had told it. Nobody had needed to. Eight iron hoods had gone up on its battlements in two days, with wire running to them from the river, and it had been hearing about the prince's light for a month from anybody who had been down to the mill at night and seen the bar of white on the water. In the market, Emeric was told, there were two opinions. One was that the light would be brighter than the moon. The other was that it would set the roofs on fire. A good many people held both.

The old women at the north well had a third, which was that it would bring the rain, and they were not asked to explain it.



On the sixth of March, a little before dusk, he went up onto the wall at the corner above the east bridge with a lantern.

From there he could see across the river to the moorings and the roofs of the dock quarter, and down the near bank, below the bridge, to the mill and the low new shape of the powerhouse beside it, with its red tile roof gone dark in the evening. Tess was down there, at the lever. Harl was down there, on his stool. When Emeric swung the lantern three times from this corner, Tess would pull the lever, and that was the whole of the plan. He had wanted to be at the lever himself, and he had given it to her, and standing on the wall now he minded much less than he had expected to.

The arc lamp at this corner hung over his head in its hood, dark, with two new carbons in it. Ward's place. He could see the next one along the wall, toward the river tower, a black shape against the sky.

He heard her on the stair before he saw her.

Ferra came up the last steps of the wall stair with her book under her arm and her shawl over her head against the wind, and stopped at the top to get her breath, and looked at the lamp, and then at him.

"I've paid for eight of those," she said. "I want to see them."

It was the rule she had kept on a fallen beam in the old works, in August of the year before last, with a stub of chalk: she did not price what she had not seen. He remembered it. He did not say so.

"You'll see it from here," he said.

She came and stood at the parapet beside him, a little apart, and looked down at the roofs going blue in the dusk, and the first lamps being lit in windows, small and yellow, here and there. The wind had pulled a strand of her hair out from under the shawl and was doing what it liked with it. She did not push it back. She was watching the town.

She had never been up here. Nobody from the forge district went up on the wall; it had always been the garrison's, and before that the baron's. She looked along the walk going away round the town in the dusk, toward the river tower and beyond it, and he could see her measuring it.

The light went out of the sky slowly, the way it did in March, and then all at once.

Emeric lifted the lantern, and swung it, once, twice, three times, toward the mill.

Nothing happened. He had known that nothing would happen at once: a girl at a lever down the bank, a belt, a machine coming up to its hum. He counted. Beside him Ferra had stopped looking at the town and was looking at the dark hood above them.

There was a crack overhead, small and sharp, like a stick broken over a knee.

Then the wall went white.

The lamp above them came on all at once, and he heard the next one along the wall crack and come on a breath later, and the one beyond it, and then he could not tell them apart, because the light was everywhere. It fell out over the parapet and lay across the river in a long white blaze from bank to bank, and on the far side it found the roofs of the dock quarter and turned them from blue to bone. Every tile and every patch of thatch stood out. Every chimney threw a black shadow beside it on the roof, edged like cut paper. Below the roofs the moorings were lit, the black hulls on the mud, the bare piers, and back on the near bank, small and clear, the mill, and the powerhouse, and the door of the powerhouse standing open, and a figure in the door that might have been Tess.

He could hear the lamp hissing above his head. He could hear, very faintly, the hum of the dynamo, coming up the wire.

In the other life he had grown up under lights like these, on every street of a city that never got dark, and he could not remember once looking up at one.

And then he could hear the town.

It began with dogs. Every dog in Kaldo, it seemed, at once. Then a door banged, and another, and a woman's voice somewhere below them said something high and sharp, and a man's answered it, and then there were people in the street under the wall, coming out of their houses with their hands up against the light and their faces white in it, looking up. A child screamed, and went on screaming, and then laughed in the middle of a scream and went on laughing instead. Somewhere nearer, a woman was praying aloud, quickly, the same few words over and over, until a door slammed on her. Somebody in the lower ward began to shout a name, over and over, calling whoever it belonged to out to look. Somewhere in the forge district a bell started ringing that was not a church bell and had no business ringing, and stopped, and started again.

Ferra had not said anything. She had both hands flat on the cold stone of the parapet. Her face was turned up to the lamp.

"Not at it," he said. "It burns."

She looked down at the town instead, and the light took all the color out of her face except the freckles, which stood out across her nose and cheeks like a spatter of rust, and the strand of hair the wind had pulled loose, which that light had bleached nearly white.

"Well," she said. That was all.



He had meant to walk the wall that night anyway, the whole circuit, from lamp to lamp, to see each of Tess's feeds hold on the first night they had all burned together. He said so. Ferra looked at him as if he had said he meant to breathe, and came.

They went north along the wall walk toward the river tower, with the town below them on one side and the river on the other, and the next lamp came toward them, white and hissing in its hood. The garrison men on the wall stood aside to let them by and did not salute, because they were all looking at the town.

"One," Ferra said, under her breath, as they passed under the lamp at the corner.

He did not think she knew she had said it.

The river tower. The lamp there hung out over the water, as Ward had wanted, and below it the river was lit white for a hundred yards upstream, every ripple of it, and past the edge of the light the dark water went on up toward the bend where the Grayrock barges came round.

"Two," Ferra said.

Far up past the bend, on the far bank where nobody lived, there was a small low light, a fire. While he looked at it, it went out. He noticed that, and did not examine it, and walked on.

Along the north stretch the town came right up to the wall, and the light fell into the lanes, and the lanes were full. People stood in their doors and in the middle of the lane and on the steps of the houses, looking up. A man had brought a chair out and was sitting in it, in his nightcap, with a cup in his hand, as if he had bought a seat. Two boys were running up and down under the wall with their arms out, watching their shadows run beside them, enormous, all the way up the house fronts.

"Three."

The forge district. Emeric looked down a lane and saw Bram standing in the middle of his own yard in his leather apron, under the white light, with his arms folded, looking up at the wall. Bram did not see them. He was looking at the lamp. He stood there, and then went back into his forge without having moved anything but his head.

"Four," Ferra said.

Then the long west stretch, where the wall ran above the poorest streets in Kaldo, low and wet, lanes that had never had any light at night but what people could afford, which was none. Nobody cheered there. People stood in their doors with their children held against their legs and looked up at the wall without saying anything, and an old man in the middle of the lane took his hat off, and held it, and did not seem to know what to do with it after that.

"Five."

"Six," Ferra said, as they came down the walk to the South Gate.

Ward was there, on the wall above the gate, with his back to the lamp. He had stood the gate crew in the dark of the gate passage, under the wall, where the lamp could not reach them, and the road outside the gate was lit white for a long bowshot, every rut and stone of it, out to the first bend, and beyond the bend the dark. He was looking at the road.

"Anything on it?" Emeric said.

"Nothing." Ward did not turn. "If there were, I would see it. And it would not see me." He considered the white road. "Good."

That was all Ward said about the light.



Past the gate the wall turned east again, above the lower ward, and there she stopped counting.

Emeric did not notice at once. He noticed that she had slowed, and then that she had stopped altogether at the parapet, and was looking down. He came back to her. Below them the roofs of the lower ward climbed the slope away from the wall toward the canal ring and the keep, every one of them white on top and black at the eaves, and above them all, on the shoulder of the inner ward, the church stood out pale against the dark, with a small bareheaded figure alone on its steps that could only be Doran, looking up. Ferra was reading the roofs.

"That's the roof," she said.

He looked. He found it: one roof among fifty, sound and plain, with new timber in it that showed paler than the rest even in that light.

"Fifteen months," Ferra said. "And the landlord's still paying it off, a copper a week." She moved her hand along the parapet, pointing without lifting it. "That's the cooperage. That's the soap yard. There's the exchange. That's where the tap is, at the top of the ward, you can't see it but it's there. The granary's out on the farm, past the wall." She stopped. "I can see half my book from here."

She stood there looking at it. The lamp hissed above them. Down in the lower ward somebody had found a fiddle, and was playing it very badly, and somebody else was laughing at him.

Emeric waited for her to go on counting. She had said six. There were two more lamps between here and the corner where they had started, and she counted things; it was what she did; she had counted every stake and every sheaf and every bar of copper in Kaldo for nearly two years, and he had never once known her leave a count unfinished. He waited for seven.

She did not say seven. She went on looking at the town.

When they walked on again she walked with her eyes on the roofs, and they passed under the seventh lamp without her saying anything at all, and he saw that she had not gone back for it, and was not going to, and he did not say anything about it either.



The eighth lamp went out as they came round the last stretch toward the corner.

It went out with a click they heard from forty yards off, and a small spit of sparks under its hood, and that stretch of the wall went dark. Emeric stopped. The seven others burned on, white and steady, hissing, the town below them still lit. Down at the powerhouse the machine would hardly have changed its note.

"Emeric," Ferra said. "That one's out."

She said it as she might have said that a column was a copper short. She said it without looking at him, pointing at the dark lamp with her chin, and she did not seem to notice that she had said his name, or that she had never said it before, in nearly two years of managing, with great skill, to call him nothing at all.

He noticed.

"A carbon cracked," he said, when he could. "The rod's dropped onto the cut-out. Tess's copper. That's why the rest are still lit."

"Will it stay out?"

"Until Tess comes up in the morning with a new carbon."

Ferra considered the dark lamp, and the seven lit ones going away round the wall behind them, and the town under all of it.

"Then it's seven tonight," she said.

They came back to the corner where they had started, above the east bridge, and stood at the parapet again under the first lamp, which was still burning. She stood closer to him this time. He did not think she had meant to; there was the wind, and the parapet was narrow there. Her shoulder was against his arm. Neither of them moved it.

Below them the music in the lower ward had got worse and louder, and people had begun to dance to it in the white light, badly, in their nightclothes, in March.
