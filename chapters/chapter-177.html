Chapter 177 - Terms of Retreat

There were thirty-one of the duke's knights in the Wayford counting house, and they were ransomed on the old terms, because there were no others.

Pennock had found the terms. They were in a book in the rolls room that nobody had opened since the coalition war, and before that since the war before it: so much for a household knight, so much for a banneret, so much for a lord, by the head, paid in coin or in kind within the quarter, and the knight to go home on his word and not bear arms against his captor for a year and a day. The figures were old. They had been set when a knight's horse cost what a farm cost, and they were a great deal of money still. Ferra had read them and said nothing and entered them in a column of their own.

The duke's steward came down from the north under a white cloth in the second week of autumn with a string of pack mules and a clerk, and sat at Pennock's long table, and paid. He did not argue a single figure. He counted the coin onto the table in stacks and Pennock's clerks counted it again, and the steward watched them with his hands folded, and said nothing at all to anybody except the figures, and when it was done he stood up and bowed to Emeric correctly and went out to the yard to see to the mules.

The knights came down from the rolls room one at a time to give their word. Most of them were young. Most of them had been unhorsed in the charge and taken on the ground in the smoke, lying in their plate in the grey grass, unable to get up. They gave their word standing in front of Emeric in the counting-house hall, one after another, in the old form, and none of them looked at him, and he did not blame them.

The last of them was an old man.

Emeric had seen him in the rolls room on the first day, when the prisoners were brought in, and noticed him because he was the only one who had been sitting. A tall grey man with a lined face and a bad back, who had been knighted, Pennock's list said, forty years ago, and served the duke's household for thirty of them. Sir Anselm Cray. He had tracery to the shoulder; Emeric had seen it at his collar. He came down the stair slowly with one hand on the rail and stood in front of Emeric and gave his word in the old form in a quiet, level voice, and then did not go.

"Highness," he said. "May I ask a thing?"

"Ask."

"There was a man in your line," said Cray. "A farmhand, by the look of him. Young. Big in the shoulder, fair, with a cut on his cheek. At the west end, a little in from the knights. He was forty feet from me when I came off my horse, reloading." He paused. "I should like to see him, if I may. Before I go."

Emeric looked at him.

"To say what?" he said.

"I don't know," Cray said. "I've thought about it for a fortnight. I don't know. I think I only want to look at him."

Emeric thought about the line. Six hundred and eleven men in three ranks behind Tessaly's bank. He had not been at the west end; he had been on the grey horse at the edge of the dead ground with his sense gone out like a lamp and his hand to his head, and he had seen the line only as a line, and the smoke. He did not know how many of the six hundred were young and fair and big in the shoulder. A great many of them were. They were off the rail works and the camp and the island and the Wayford farms. They were farmhands. Every tenth one of them had a cut on his face.

"I don't know which one you mean," Emeric said.

Cray looked at him for a moment longer.

"No," he said. "No, I suppose you wouldn't." He bowed, correctly, the way the steward had, and turned and went out to the yard to the mules, walking carefully, with one hand at the small of his back.

They buried the five at Kaldo on the last day of the first month of autumn.

They had come home in the cart from the oak, covered, with the column, on the same road the column had gone out on, and been laid in the church overnight with candles and a man of the levy at each end of each bier, and in the morning the whole island came up the hill.

Doran buried them in the churchyard, not in the lakeside row. He had said so to Emeric before Emeric could ask. They had been hanged as outlaws on a duke's tree under a board that said so, and the law that hanged them was not Doran's law, and they would lie in consecrated ground in their own parish with their names on the stone, and if any bishop in the realm had anything to say about that he could come to Kaldo and say it to Doran.

He read the names at the grave in his old cracked voice, loud enough for the back of the crowd. Arn Heddle, of the rail works. Piers, son of Sela, of the island. Dickon Farr, of Milford. Wil Ashby, of the camp. John Tarn, of the lower water. He read the prayer for the dead, and not the soldiers' prayer. He did not look at Emeric while he read.

Sela stood at the head of the second grave.

She stood where Emeric could see her from where he stood, at the side, with Ward. She was in her good shawl, and the market apron was gone, and she stood very straight with her hands folded in front of her and looked down into the grave while Doran read, and while the levy's men let the coffin down on the ropes, and while Doran threw the first earth. She did not weep. Other women round her were weeping. She stood with her hands folded and looked at the coffin and did not move her eyes from it at all.

She did not look at Emeric.

He watched her not look at him. He knew she knew where he was. She had known where he was at every market cross for twelve years; she had always found him in a crowd before he found her. She stood at the head of her son's grave in the same churchyard and kept her eyes on the coffin, and it was not an accident, and it was not grief only. It was the one thing she could do, and she was doing it.

He did not go to her. He had thought, the night before, that he would. He had thought about what he would say. He stood at the side of the churchyard with Ward and let her not look at him, until Doran had finished, and the crowd began to go down the hill, and Sela's neighbours came round her and took her arms and walked her down with them, and she went without once turning her head.

"You could have gone to her," Ward said.

"No," said Emeric. "I couldn't."

Ward did not say anything else.

The duke's file came south in the third week of autumn, by the glove-maker under the chancery arches, who had bought a copy from a chancery clerk for more than Saban liked to think about.

Emeric read it at the long-room table with Ferra and Ward. Saban was in the next room on a daybed with a blanket over his knees, where Imani had put him and where he stayed for most of every day now, and he had read it first, lying on his back with the pages held up over his face in his good hand, and sent it through with a note in Wenna's writing: He has left out two things. See if you find them.

It was a good file. Emeric could see that at once, and see why the glove-maker had paid what she paid. It was drawn by a lawyer who knew what he was doing, on good paper, under the duke's seal, and it was addressed to the king in council, and it laid out, in numbered articles, what the duke of Aldane complained of against the lord of Kaldo, with every article sworn to by a named witness.

That the lord of Kaldo's men in arms had struck first at Harrow Bridge upon the servants of the house of Lisle as they lawfully kept the toll of that bridge under the Crown's charter, and slain two of them, and broken the house of Lisle's gate. Sworn by the toll-keeper and by three of Lisle's men.

That the lord of Kaldo had accepted the Crown's mediation in writing, and then, before the Crown's commissioner could come, had marched in arms upon the house of Lisle's ground.

That the lord of Kaldo's commoners, farmhands and tenants and men of no degree, had in the field north of Harrow Bridge slain knights of his grace's household and his grace's vassals, being their betters, by means of fire out of iron tubes, which was no lawful weapon of any war in the realm.

That in the night before the field, things had been seen to fly over his grace's host, high, against the stars, and cross the ford where the lord of Kaldo's host lay. Sworn by a scout of his grace's household, named, and by the captain to whom he reported it.

That the lord of Kaldo's own men, and those of his own household, had said that he was no man, and that demons came at his call. Sworn by men of the late Lord Dray's house who had heard it said, and by the late Lord Dray himself on the day of his death before the whole town of Wayford.

And that on all of this the duke of Aldane asked the king's justice.

Emeric read it twice.

"Well?" said Ferra.

"Every article's true," Emeric said. "Or true enough to swear to."

"Saban says he's left out two things."

"He has."

There was nothing in it about the dead field. Not a word. Not the three hundred and forty stones, nor the fourteen mages in grey breaking them one after another across the stream ground at dawn, nor the relay post on the ridge going dark, nor the duke's own knights stripped to men in the grass and lying there in their plate, unable to stand. The file said the duke's knights had been slain by fire from tubes. It did not say the duke had turned off everything in the field that was not fire from tubes, on purpose, to make it knights against farmers, and that it had been knights against farmers, and that the farmers had won.

And there was nothing in it about Grayrock.

Emeric looked at that one longer. It was not the kind of thing that would be in a file about a toll and a fight. There was no reason for it to be. But it was the kind of thing that a duke who had spent a winter buying three hundred and forty stones in the capital, and who knew what a stone was worth and what a barony that raised the realm's gravity stone might have that it did not sell, might have thought to put in a file to the king in council, if he had known about it.

He had not put it in. Either he did not know, or somebody had told him not to.

"The dead field's not in it," Ward said.

"No."

"Because it'd tell the king he broke three hundred stones in the field and it didn't work."

"Because it'd tell the king he broke three hundred stones in the field," Emeric said. "Full stop. Nobody does that. The high mages have told every king for three hundred years that nobody does that." He put the file down. "He'll say we did something in the field that made the stones fail. That we did something. If anybody asks. Nobody will ask."

He wrote to Keller that week.

He had written to Keller about Wayford over four nights two winters ago, and every night he had written round the same hole: had reason to believe, standing for fifteen knots and a root and two printers and a year of other men's letters read in a back room. It had been a good record. It had had a hole in it the shape of the ink, and he had known it, and so had Ferra, and so, he had always assumed, had Keller.

He wrote this one in two nights.

He wrote it all. The toll. The relay post closed and the keeper sent down to the tap-room with nothing to do. The mediation offered and accepted and never come. Dunmore and the pole, and Declan's twelve, and four stories from four people, all true, none of them settling it. The men in brown gone north at dusk the day before. The stones: the wool factor's clerk's letter in the spring, and the steward's book from the quartermaster's tent in Saban's memory, three hundred and forty of them, against the widows' estates, eleven Copper the stone. The dead field. The bank. The volleys. The count of the dead on both sides, by name where he had names. The oak. The five. Hull two over the ford at night he wrote as the reserve came up from Kaldo by night and was landed at the ford, and that was true, and it was not a hole, because Keller had not asked how Kaldo moved its carts.

At the oak he stopped for a long time, and then he wrote: The column halted at the oak for the rest of that day and the night. I was not fit to go on, and the captain stood with me. And that was true too.

He read it back when he had finished. Ferra read it after him.

"There's nothing missing," she said.

"No."

She looked at him. "You're glad."

"Yes," he said. He had not known it until she said it. He sat at the table with the record in front of him and was glad, the way a man is glad to set down a heavy thing he had got used to carrying and forgotten he had. There was nothing in it that he had had to write round. Every line of it was a line he would say in the king's council with his hand on a book.

He noticed that he was relieved, and he noticed what it meant that he was, and he did not say anything else.

The summons came at the end of the month, by the Crown's rider, in the Crown's form.

It was not from the chancery this time. It was under the king's own seal, the great one, and it was addressed to the lord of Kaldo by all his titles, and it said, in the old words, that the king in council, having before him the complaint of his grace of Aldane, and the answer of the lord of Kaldo as laid before the Crown's officer, required both parties to appear before him at the capital at the opening of the spring council, in their own persons, to answer to each other and to him. It named a day. It was signed with the king's name, Harrold, in a clerk's hand, and under it, in a different hand, very small, the initial that meant the king had seen it himself.

Emeric read it standing at the long-room table.

Years. Years since his father had said anything to him in public, in any form, by any hand. There had been the stones, by Ward's hand, and the one line after Wayford by the ordinary post. There had been the road, the private one, Ward's carrier and the cipher that Kassim read aloud; and Emeric had known about the road for two years and more and had never once asked Ward to use it, and had told himself every time he did not that he would not have used it, and did not know any more whether that was true.

And now the king had spoken, in his great seal, before the whole realm, and what he had said was: come and answer.

He put the summons down on Ferra's table.

He went down to the east tower that evening with it, because he had said he would tell Marrow when it came, and he did not quite know why he had said that.

Marrow stood by the wall under the window, as he always did, with his coat buttoned to the throat. It was dark outside the window now, and the lamp was lit, and his forgettable face in the lamplight looked older than it had in the spring. Emeric did not show him the summons. He told him what it said.

Marrow listened without moving.

When Emeric had finished he was quiet for a long time. Emeric had heard Saban describe it, the silence, and the one sentence, and had not quite believed it. He believed it now. Marrow stood by the wall and looked at nothing and Emeric could see him turning it over, the way a man turns a coin to see both faces.

"Now he goes to your father with a file," Marrow said, "and you go with a war."

He did not say anything else. After a while Emeric went back up the stair.

The summons was on Ferra's table when he came back up, where he had left it, in the lamplight, with the king's great seal on it in red wax and the small initial under the name.

He sat down across from it and looked at it.

He thought, for no reason he could name, of the chancery.

Of a morning there, years ago now, when he had gone north to the capital on the Wayford road and stood in the long room with the tall windows and the clerks at their desks and the grey light coming off the river, and a charter had been put in front of him with the seal already on it, and he had signed it standing up. Nobody in the room had said what was being done, because everybody in it knew. A thing was being given to him. Handsomely, in the king's name, before witnesses, so that it would be written that it had been given, and so that he would take it and be grateful and go away south again.

He had gone north that morning as the one being given something, and he had taken it, and made it into this.

He was going north again now. This time nobody was giving him anything. This time he was the one being called to answer for what he had made.

He sat for a long time with the summons on the table in front of him, while the lamp burned down, and did not pick it up again.
