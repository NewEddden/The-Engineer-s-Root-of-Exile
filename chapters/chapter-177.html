Chapter 177 - The Captured Stone

The pay cart went up the capital road to the railhead every second Friday, and on the twenty-second of May it was robbed at the gorse bend, three miles out, a little after ten in the morning.

Emeric heard it on the wire before he heard it from anybody. The railhead had a sounder now, on a branch off the Wayford wire, on a shelf in Fennick's powder hut, and at half past ten it began clicking in the gate tower's telegraph room with nobody's hand on the key steady enough to send it clean; and the clerk who read it ran up the keep stair with his face white to say that Declan was hurt and the pay was safe and there had been fire on the road.

"Fire," said Ward.

"Fire, sir. That's what the railhead said. A great sheet of it, across the road, and the robbers ran."

Ward looked at Emeric. Neither of them said anything. Then Ward went down the stair for the horses.



Declan was sitting on the bank at the gorse bend when they got there, with his left leg out in front of him and his breeches cut away from the thigh and a bolt's hole in it bound up with somebody's shirt, and the pay clerk from Ferra's office beside him holding the strongbox on his knees with both arms, as if somebody might still come out of the gorse for it. The cart horse was dead in the road, on its side, with two bolts in it. The cart was on its side beside the horse. Across the road from bank to bank, a stride wide, there was a long black scorch in the dust, and the gorse on the uphill side was burnt back to the roots for twenty feet, the stems still smoking.

And on the bank on the other side of Declan, by himself, with his knees drawn up and his arms round them, sat Wat.

He was one of Armand's. Not a student; Armand had fifteen students who were mages, and Wat was not one. He was a carter's son from the lower ward, seventeen, who had come to the school in its first year and turned out to have hands like Tess's and an eye for a straight line, and Armand had taken him, and five others like him, into the small room off his own, the etching room, to cut glyphs. Fire, mostly. Into stone, with a steel point and a glass, line by line, as Tess had cut her first mark into a pebble with a knife at the back of Bram's forge seven years ago. They were good at it. The stones they cut were the barony's precast stones: a glyph already written, fed by a charge already in the stone, that anybody with even the smallest trickle of his own mana could set off by pressing a thumb into the cut. Most of Kaldo's commoners had that trickle. Most commoners everywhere did. Nobody outside Kaldo had ever been shown what it was for.

The precast stones did not leave the walls. That was the rule. It had been the rule since Tess's pebble, written in Ferra's book in Emeric's own words: inside the walls only.

Wat looked up when the horses came round the bend, and saw who it was, and put his face down on his knees.



Declan told it, on the bank, with his teeth set, while Imani's girl from the ward, who had come up behind them on the second horse, took the shirt off his leg and looked at what was under it.

A tree across the road at the bend, where the gorse came down close on both sides and the cart had to slow. Five men in the gorse. Crossbows, two of them. The first bolt had been for the horse, which was sensible of them, and the second for the horse, and the third for Declan, who had been off his own horse and halfway to the gorse by then, knight-fast, and had taken it in the leg instead of the chest because he was moving. He had put two of them down in the gorse with the flat of his sword and the hilt. The two garrison men behind the cart had a third. That left two, going for the strongbox, with the clerk lying on it in the road.

"And then the boy," said Declan. "I didn't see what he did. I was on my back in the gorse with a man's knee on my arm. I heard it." He hissed as the girl touched the wound. "Like a furnace door opening. A whump. And I felt the heat go over me where I lay, and the man on my arm screamed and let go and ran, and when I got up the road was on fire from side to side and the two by the cart were running uphill with their coats alight and the boy was standing in the middle of the road with his hand out." He looked at Wat. "Like a mage."

"They all ran," said the pay clerk, from the strongbox. "All five. The two that were down got up and ran too. Into the gorse and over the ridge." He swallowed. "The boy saved the pay."

Emeric went and crouched in front of Wat on the bank.

"How many did you have?"

Wat did not lift his head. "Two."

"Why did you have them?"

A long silence. Then, into his knees: "The railhead smith. He's always short of coal for the rivet fire. Fennick's lads use it for the powder hut stove as well, and the coal cart's always late. I said I could light it for him with a stone. A fire stone. Just a little one, a practice cut. I wanted to show him." He lifted his face at last. It was wet. "I was only going to show him. I'd have brought them straight home."

"Where's the second one?"

Wat's face changed.

"I had it in my other hand," he said. "When I pressed the first. I had them both out, to show him, I was holding them when the tree came down, and I pressed the one, and the fire went, and I..." He stopped. "I dropped the other. I must have. When the heat came back at me. I put my hands up." He looked at his hands. "I don't know where it went."



They looked for it until dark.

Ward brought out forty men from the levy, and they walked the gorse in a line, shoulder to shoulder, from the burnt patch up the hillside to the crest and down the other side, poking under every bush with sticks; and Emeric walked the road and the near bank with his sense out, as wide as it would go, thin, feeling for it.

A charged stone with a glyph cut in it had a note. A small one, a fire stone's, warm and quick; he would have felt it under a foot of earth if it was there. He had felt a dropped lamp stone in the river once from the water stair. He walked the bend from end to end three times, and up the hill as far as his sense would reach from the road, and found the first stone, spent, grey and cold and cracked across its cut, lying in the dust by the cart's wheel where Wat had dropped it after it fired. He found a crossbow bolt in the gorse with its fletching burned off. He found a hat.

He did not find the second stone.

At dusk he stood at the top of the ridge with Ward, looking down the far side at the capital road going away north in the half light, and the gorse, and the sheep track the five men had run along over the brow.

"It's not here," he said.

"You're sure."

"If it were here, I'd feel it." He looked at the sheep track. "One of them picked it up. On the way past. He saw the boy throw fire out of his hand, and then he saw the boy drop something, and he was a man who picks things up."

Ward was quiet.

"Then somebody north of this ridge," he said, "has a stone that a carter's boy can throw fire out of with his thumb."

"Yes."

"And five men who saw him do it."

"Yes."

Ward did not say anything else. He did not need to. They went back down to the horses in the dark.



Every precast stone in the barony was inside the walls by noon the next day.

Ferra counted them. The etching room had cut a hundred and twelve since the first, over four years, and most of them had been fired and gone back to the stone bank as blanks; there were forty-three still charged and whole on the day of the gorse bend. She went through the book and Armand's tally and Wat's own scrap of a list, which was in his pocket and which he gave her with a hand that shook, and found every one. Forty-two, in the etching room's locked case and the ward's cupboard and the works' fire-stone rack and two in Tess's line room drawer. And one in the gorse, which was not in the gorse.

Armand had the case moved into his own room that afternoon. The etching room's door got a lock, and the lock's key went on the chain round Armand's neck beside the other one. And Armand sat in his chair by the window with his eyes shut while Wat stood in front of him, and said nothing to the boy at all, long enough that Emeric, in the doorway, began to think he would not.

"You saved the pay," said Armand at last.

Wat said nothing.

"And Declan's leg, very likely, and the clerk. Five armed men ran from a carter's son." Armand opened his eyes. "Do you understand what you've done?"

"I broke the rule," said Wat. "I took them outside."

"That's what you did wrong. I asked what you'd done." Armand looked at him. "You've shown five strangers on the capital road that a boy with no ring on his hand can stand in a road and throw fire. That's the thing the tower and the Church have spent longer than anybody can remember making sure no commoner ever finds out. Not that it's hard. That it's easy." He shut his eyes again. "And then you gave them the stone to prove it with."

Wat began to cry, standing up, without making any sound.

"Go back to the etching room," said Armand. "Tomorrow. At the usual time. You'll cut nothing but practice blanks till the autumn quarter day, and you'll not touch a charge." He paused. "You're still the best straight line I've got. Go home."

Wat went.

Armand sat in his chair. Emeric came in and sat on the stool under the window, where everybody sat to be read.

"You were kinder to him than I'd have been," said Emeric.

"I was a great deal less kind to a boy once," said Armand, "about something a great deal smaller." He did not say what. "It didn't help him either."



Saban took it personally, which surprised everybody, because Saban did not take things personally. He said so himself.

"I don't," he said, at the long table, on the twenty-fourth. "It's bad for trade. But that's my road." He put his finger on the table. "Five men don't stand up out of the gorse at the one bend on the capital road where a cart has to slow, on the one Friday in two when the pay goes up, without somebody's told them which Friday. And then they don't pick up a stone and walk off with it over a ridge and keep it in a sack. A stone like that's worth a lord's horse to the right buyer, and nothing at all to a man who doesn't know a buyer." He took his finger off the table. "Somebody on my road knows a buyer."

"Can you find it?"

"I can find who sells it," said Saban. "The stone's gone, Highness. I'd put money on that, if anybody were taking it. It went north the night of the bend with the first rider out of the first posting house." He folded his hands. "But a man who knows a buyer for a stone like that has sold to that buyer before, and will again. And I know every man on that road who could have."

It took him twenty-six days.

Emeric did not ask how, while he was doing it, because Saban plainly did not want to be asked; and when Saban came up to the keep on the evening of the nineteenth of June and sat down across the counting room desk from him with his hat on his knee, he told him anyway, without being asked, which was how Emeric knew what it had cost him.

"I've four men on the capital road between here and Wayford who hear things for me," Saban said. "The crossing inn. The ford chapel's sexton. A carrier who works the stretch above Lisle's. And a man at the Wayford posting house. I've had them some years. I pay them for what they hear and I don't ask what else they do." He turned his hat round on his knee, once. "On the twenty-sixth of May I sent each of them a piece of news to keep under their hats. A different piece each. The crossing inn, that Kaldo was moving its pay day to Thursdays. The sexton, that the prince was buying a stud of horses from the coast. The carrier, that Armand was dying. The posting house, that Ferra's office had a second strongbox, for the Wayford line's money, and it went up on the first of every month." He stopped turning the hat. "None of them true. Every one of them something a man in the north who wanted to rob us, or know us, would pay to hear."

"And one came back."

"Last week. From my man in the capital. One of the Wayford wool houses has a clerk who sells what he hears to anybody, and he'd heard from a man who'd heard from a buyer in the north that Kaldo's pay goes up on Thursdays now." Saban looked at the hat. "The crossing inn."

Emeric was quiet.

"The man who stood the Visitor his supper," he said.

"The same." Saban put the hat on the desk. "I've had him nine years. He's carried more down that road for me than any man alive. He's the reason I knew the Visitor was coming a week before he came." He looked at the hat. "And for nine years, it seems, he's been selling the north whatever I paid him to tell me, and a little extra. Not all the time. When it was worth it. A man who sells both ways doesn't do it every day, Highness. He does it when the price is right. And a stone that fires off a commoner's thumb was the best price he'd ever been offered in his life."

"Who bought it?"

"That I don't know. North. Through two other hands. Somebody with money and a reason to want to know what Kaldo's commoners can do." Saban's face did not change. "Which is a short list, and every name on it is a lord's."

"What will you do with him?"

Saban was quiet for a little.

"Nothing," he said.

Emeric looked at him.

"Ward will want to do something," said Saban. "Ward will want to go up the road with four men and ask him questions in the stable yard. And the man will tell Ward the name of the man he sold to, and that man will be a carter in Lisle's country who knows nothing, and the next man up will hear that Kaldo's asking, and the trail will go cold in a week." He picked up the hat again. "So I'll go on paying him. I'll go on sending him news to keep under his hat. Only from now on it'll be news I've chosen for the north to have." He stood. "A man who sells both ways and doesn't know he's been found is the most useful man on the road. I'd be a fool to waste him on a stable yard."

He went to the door.

"I'll miss him, though," Saban said, with his hand on the latch, not turning round. "That's what I don't care for. Nine years. He'd a way of telling a thing that made you laugh." He opened the door. "I'll not laugh at it now. That's the cost."



Ward came to the counting room after Saban had gone. He had heard. He stood by the window looking down at the yard and the gate and the road going away north beyond it in the long June dusk, and did not say anything for a while, and Emeric waited, because Ward plainly had something to say and would say it when it was ready.

"Five men ran from a boy," said Ward at last. "On an open road. In daylight."

"Yes."

"Next time it won't be five, and it won't be daylight, and it won't be the road." He turned from the window. "Somebody north has one of our stones in a box, and five men's word for what it did, and they'll be asking themselves what else is behind our wall that a carter's son can pick up. And sooner or later one of them will come and see." He looked at Emeric. "I've a garrison. I've four knights. I've a wall the old baron built for wolves. And a gate that two men with a ram could open in an afternoon, if they didn't mind the noise."

"What do you want?"

"A gate," said Ward. "That can throw a hundred men back off it with nobody on the gate but a boy at a lever. No mage. No knight. Nothing a man in the north can count." He paused. "You've a cellar. I've never asked what it can do. I'm asking now."

Emeric thought about the forge, behind its wall, and the sleeping stones in the box under its door, and the Hollow Crystals in their case, one of which had been set aside for a gate since the winter the forge was built.

"Not till the summer," he said. "Not with the Visitor's report still on its way north and every posting house on the road watching. When nobody's looking at us."

"Nobody's ever not looking at us now," said Ward. "That's my point." But he nodded, once, and went back to the wall.
