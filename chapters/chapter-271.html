Chapter 271 - Third Spin

The first blade let go at eleven minutes past two on the twenty-ninth of June, in the erecting shop of the express works, and went through the casing's inner wall and stopped in the outer one, and nobody was hurt.

Afterward, everyone who had been in the shop could tell you exactly where they had been standing. That was how you could tell how frightened they had been.

The turbine sat on its test bed at the shop's far end, on a cast-iron frame bolted down into a block of cement that went into the ground deeper than a man was tall, where the express's first boiler had stood in March for its water test. It did not look like much. That was the first thing everybody who saw it said: a long low iron casing like a fat drainpipe split along its length and bolted shut, as long as a cart, with a pipe the thickness of Emeric's waist coming into one end from the shop's test boiler and a wider one going out of the other to the condenser in the yard; and out of the far end of the casing, the shaft, bright, running through its bearings to a heavy iron coupling and a flywheel, because the alternator was not built yet and the shaft had to turn something. Inside the casing, out of sight, the rotor: the shaft with its rows of blades on it, each row on its own wheel, each wheel a little bigger than the one before. Seven rows. Four hundred and twelve blades, every one cut on Dov's copying mill off the Forge's masters, every one checked on the gauge, every one set in its groove in its wheel by hand, by Bram, and wedged, and checked again.

Bram had stood at the steam valve. That was where he had been. Emeric was at the gauge board on the wall, ten yards back, watching the needles. Ottoline was beside him with her watch and her notebook, because it was her tachometer on the shaft, a thing of brass flyweights and a dial she had built in a week out of a clock's governor, and she would not let anyone else read it. Tess was in her chair at the shop door, where Bram had put her, as far from the casing as she could be and still see. Osric was behind Tess. Dov was at the far wall with his arms folded, because he had cut every one of the four hundred and twelve and did not want to look as though he was worried about any of them.

Bram had opened the valve a quarter turn at two o'clock, and the steam had gone in, and the shaft had begun to turn.

Slowly at first. Then less slowly. There was no noise to it at the start but the hiss of the steam and a sound from inside the casing like a great many small birds flying in a closed room; and then, as Ottoline called out the turns from her dial, the sound went up, a hum, a whine, higher, and the floor began to feel it through the soles, and the flywheel at the far end went from a wheel to a grey blur to a disc of light. Ottoline called the turns. Five hundred a minute. Eight hundred. A thousand. Emeric watched the needles. Bram opened the valve another quarter turn. Two thousand. Two thousand five hundred.

At three thousand one hundred, something inside the casing went bang.

It was not a loud bang. It was sharp and small and flat, like a stone hitting a tin roof, and it came from the casing's middle; and then, all at once, the whine broke. It broke into a hammering roar, a sound like a cart full of iron going over a cliff, and the whole casing on its frame began to shake, and the shaft at the end was not a bright line any longer but a blur, jumping in its bearings; and Bram had the valve shut before Emeric had turned his head, his whole body thrown against the wheel, and the steam was cut off, and the roar went down, and down, and the shaking slowed, and the flywheel came back out of its blur into a wheel, wobbling, slower, slower, until it stopped.

The shop was so quiet that Emeric could hear the condenser in the yard dripping.

Bram let go of the valve wheel. He looked at the casing. There was a lump on its side, on the upper half, near the middle: a round raised place the size of a fist, where the outer wall had bulged outward, as if something inside had punched it from within and nearly got through.

Nobody had been standing there. That had been the rule; Emeric had made it the night before, and Bram had chalked it on the floor in a long white line on both sides of the casing, and nobody had crossed it. Nobody had needed telling twice. But everybody in the shop looked at the lump on the casing's side, and then down the line of where it pointed, past the chalk, across the floor, at the wall; and at the place on the wall at chest height where, if it had come through, it would have gone.

"Well," said Dov, from the far wall, after a while, "that's one."



They had the casing open by six. The blade was in the outer wall, as Emeric had thought: one of the fourth row's, snapped off clean at its root, and driven edge-first through the inner wall of the casing and two fingers into the outer one, where it had stuck, bent double, still hot.

Bram looked at its root for a long time under the lamp.

"It's not the blade," he said at last.

"What is it?"

"It's my wedge." Bram held up the root, where it had sat in its groove in the wheel. There was a mark on one side of it, bright, where it had worn. "The wedge was a hair slack. I set it. I checked it. It was a hair slack and I passed it." He put the root down. "It sat in its groove and it ground in its groove and at three thousand it ground through."

"It's four hundred and twelve wedges."

"It's four hundred and eleven I set right and one I didn't." Bram did not look up. "That's the one that counts."

He reset all of them that night, by himself, every wedge in all seven rows, with a lamp and a mallet and the feeler gauge, and did not let anybody help him; and in the morning there was a new blade in the fourth row, cut by Dov at five off the master, and the casing had a patch riveted over its lump that Ottoline said made it look like a man with a black eye.



The second run was on the second of July, and it got to four thousand, and then the rotor shook itself off its bearings.

It did not go bang. It was worse than a bang. It went up through three thousand smoothly and through three thousand five hundred, and everybody in the shop let out the breath they had been holding since the first; and then at three thousand eight hundred the hum got a beat in it. A slow throb, under the whine, like a man's heart heard through a wall. Emeric heard it and looked at Bram, and Bram had heard it, and had his hand on the valve wheel. Ottoline called four thousand. The throb got faster. The floor felt it. The casing on its frame began, very slightly, to walk, a hair to the left and a hair back with every throb, and the bolts holding the frame down into the cement began to sing; and Bram shut the valve, all the way, at once, and the shaft ran on, slowing, throbbing, and at three thousand on the way down, with a crack like a dropped plate, the far bearing's cap split in two and the shaft jumped half an inch in its seat and ran on, grinding, down, and stopped.

Nothing broke. Nothing came through anything. But the far bearing was in two pieces, and the shaft was scored where it had sat in it, and the coupling's bolts had sheared, three of the six; and when Bram put his hand flat on the casing afterward, it was hot.

"It's out of true," said Bram. "The rotor. It's heavy on one side."

"Every wheel's been balanced," said Dov. "On the stand. Every one. I did them."

"Every wheel's balanced on its own." Bram did not look at him. "Seven of them on a shaft isn't a wheel. It's a rotor. Each one's a hair heavy somewhere, and the hairs add up, and at four thousand a hair weighs what a horse does." He took his hand off the casing. "It's heavy. Somewhere. And I don't know where."

Emeric knew how to find out.

He had known since he drew the turbine. He knew the machine they used for it on the other side of everything, a balancing machine, with the rotor spun up slowly on soft bearings and the shake read off them with gauges, so that you could see at what angle round the shaft the heavy side lay, and how heavy, and take a little metal off it, or add a little, until it ran true. He could see the drawings of it. He could have drawn one for Bram that afternoon. It would have taken the shop a month to build, and another month to learn to use.

He kept his face still.

Ottoline was looking at the rotor.

She had come up to the test bed with the others, after, and stood at the casing's open end with her notebook shut, looking at the shaft where it went into the casing; and she had a look on her face Emeric had seen before. He had seen it on her the day he first showed her the tabulator's drawings. It was the look of a woman watching men make a hard job of something she had done at her kitchen table every week of her life.

"It's a balance wheel," she said.

Bram turned round.

"In a watch," said Ottoline. "The balance wheel. It's a little wheel with a hairspring, and it goes back and forth, and if it's heavier on one side than the other, the watch runs fast lying on its face and slow standing up, and you can't regulate it." She was still looking at the shaft. "You poise it. Every watchmaker poises a balance. You take it out of the watch and you lay its pivots on two knife edges. Two straight edges, hard, polished, dead level. And you let it go. And it rolls, very slowly, on the edges, until the heavy side's at the bottom. And it stops there. And you mark the bottom." She looked at Bram. "And you take a little off the bottom, with a file. And you let it roll again. And when it'll stay still wherever you put it, any way round, it's poised."

There was a silence.

"It's a rotor," said Bram, "as long as a cart."

"It's a wheel," said Ottoline. "A long one."

Bram looked at the rotor in its casing for a long time. Then he looked at Emeric, and Emeric did not say anything, because he had nothing to say that was better.

"Knife edges," said Bram. "As long as the bed. Hard. Dead level." He was already thinking. "Ground. On the long planer. Off the express's frames' rails." He looked at Ottoline. "You'll show me."

"I'll show you," said Ottoline, "if you'll stop calling it a rotor while I do it. It puts me off."

They built the knife edges in four days: two bars of hardened steel as long as the rotor, ground on the long planer and lapped by hand on a plate until Ottoline could lay a sheet of tissue across them and see light under it nowhere; set on two cement blocks on the shop floor, levelled with a water level and then with one of Ottoline's own bubble levels from her clock bench, which she brought down from her room in her apron pocket and would not let anybody else touch. They lifted the rotor out of its casing on the gantry, all seven wheels and four hundred and twelve blades of it, and laid its shaft on the edges with a care Emeric had only seen men use with newborns.

Then Ottoline let it go.

It rolled. Very slowly. A quarter turn, so slowly you could not see it move unless you looked at a blade's tip against the wall, and then it stopped, and rocked a little, and was still; and a blade in the third row was at the bottom.

Ottoline chalked it.

They did it for two days. She would chalk the bottom; Bram would take a whisper of metal off the third wheel's rim at that place with a file, under her eye, a few strokes; they would turn the rotor a quarter round and let it go, and it would roll again, less, and stop with a different blade at the bottom, and she would chalk that. On the second afternoon it rolled a hair and stopped and did not choose. Ottoline turned it a quarter round, and let go, and it stayed. She turned it a half, and let go, and it stayed. She turned it three quarters, and let go, and stood back with her hands in her apron pockets; and the long rotor lay on its knife edges with its blades all round it in their rows, in whatever way round she had left it, perfectly still, as if it had been carved there.

"Poised," said Ottoline.

Bram took his hand off his mouth, where it had been for some time.



The third run was on the eleventh of July, the same day as the Proving class came to the base, and it held.

Emeric was not at the base, because of it. He was at the gauge board in the erecting shop at nine in the morning, with Ottoline beside him calling the turns off her dial, and Bram at the valve, and the patched casing on its bed with its new far bearing and its new coupling bolts; and the steam went in, and the shaft turned, and the birds in the closed room became a hum, and the hum a whine, and the whine went up, and up, past three thousand one hundred, where the blade had gone, past three thousand eight hundred, where the throb had come, past four thousand, where the bearing had split; and there was no throb. There was nothing in it at all but the one long clean high note, going up, and the floor humming with it under their feet like the deck of a ship. Ottoline called five thousand. Bram opened the valve to the last notch. Ottoline called five thousand nine hundred, and her voice did something on the nine, and she called it again.

It ran an hour, at full. Nobody spoke for the whole of it. At the end of the hour Bram shut the valve slowly, a quarter turn at a time, and the note came down, and down, and the shaft slowed and slowed and came to a stop with a long sigh of steam from the casing's bleed, the way a horse sighs at the end of a long day; and Bram took his hand off the wheel, and turned round, and the shop cheered.

Emeric did not cheer. He went down to the casing, past the chalk line, and put his hand flat on it; and it was hot, very hot, and still, and true.

And then he thought of the next thing.

It was a thing he had read once on the other side of everything, in a book about ships, and not thought of since. A great rotor, very hot, left to stand still as it cooled, sagged. Only a hair. Its own weight bent it, between its bearings, the way a hot iron bar laid across two bricks sags in the middle as it cools; and if it set like that, it set bent, and the next time you spun it up, it was a hair out of true again, and all Ottoline's poising had been for nothing.

"It wants turning," he said.

Bram looked at him.

"While it cools. Slowly. A turn every few minutes. All night, till it's cold." He took his hand off the casing. "Or it'll set with a bow in it. Its own weight. We've got to keep it rolling so it sags all ways at once and none."

Bram looked at the rotor, and then at Ottoline, and then at the turbine shop's roof, where it was past ten in the morning and the summer sun was coming in through the skylights.

"All night," he said. "By hand. With a bar."

"Tonight, by hand. With a bar." Emeric looked down the shop to the door, where the Motion Shop had its bench along the wall under the windows, and Tess's chair stood empty by it because Tess had gone back up to the works at nine to see to the meter coils. "After tonight, with a motor. A small one. Geared down. Very far down. A turn every few minutes."

"How far down?"

Emeric told him.

Bram looked at the Motion Shop's bench. Then he raised his voice.

"Dov."



Dov cut the gear in nine days, and grumbled about it for every one of them.

He had come to Kaldo at the end of August the year before, from a gear-cutter's shop up the river with a box of files and an opinion of every gear he had ever seen, most of it low; and Tess had taken him into the Motion Shop because he could cut a gear tooth by eye that Emeric could not have cut with a gauge, and because he had stood in front of her chair on his first morning and told her the shop's drive gears were a disgrace, and she had agreed with him. He had cut every gear the Motion Shop had used since. He liked spur gears. He liked a worm and wheel. He liked a gear that did what a gear was meant to do, which was turn round and stay where it was put.

"It wobbles," he said, on the first day, with Emeric's drawing in his hands. "The middle one. The disc. It wobbles."

"It's meant to."

"A gear doesn't wobble." Dov put the drawing down on the bench. "A gear that wobbles is a gear that's wrong."

"It rolls round inside the ring," said Emeric. "Look. Here. The ring's got more teeth than the disc has lobes, one more. So every time the input goes round once, the disc rolls round the inside of the ring, and comes back one lobe short of where it started. So it's turned backward by one lobe. One lobe for every turn of the motor." He drew it again. "Make the disc with forty lobes and the motor turns forty times for the disc to turn once. Make it two discs. Make it two stages."

Dov looked at the drawing.

"And these," he said. He put his finger on the pins in the ring, and the holes in the disc, and the round lobes of the disc's edge, drawn very carefully by Emeric the night before as curves that rolled along each other without ever sliding. "These aren't teeth."

"They're lobes. Rolling pins. Nothing slides. It's all rolling. It wears hardly at all."

"It's a gear," said Dov, "that wobbles on purpose."

"Yes."

Dov said nothing for a while. He picked up the drawing again, and held it to the window, and looked at the lobes' curve against the light for a long time, the way he looked at a tooth he had cut.

"Who drew this curve?" he said.

"I did."

"It's not a circle."

"No."

"What is it?"

"It's the curve you get," said Emeric, "if you roll a small circle round a big one and watch a point on its rim."

Dov looked at him with his mouth a little open. Then he looked back at the drawing, and something went across his face that Emeric did not know how to read; and he folded the drawing in four and put it in his apron, and said he would want a week, and walked off to his own bench with his back very straight.

He wanted nine days. He grumbled for all of them: to Tess, to Osric, to the shop's sweeper, to his own files, out loud and at length, in the voice of a man who had been asked to do something stupid and was going to do it properly anyway to show how stupid it was. A gear that wobbles. A disc rolling round in a ring like a coin in a bowl. Nothing but curves. Who had ever heard of such a thing. On the eighth day he went quiet. On the ninth he brought it across the shop in both hands, wrapped in a cloth, and set it on the bench in front of Emeric and unwrapped it, and stood back.

It was small. A drum of iron the size of a loaf, with a shaft in one end and a shaft out of the other. Through a window cut in its side, the disc, bright, with its forty round lobes, and the ring of pins round it.

"Put the motor on it," said Dov.

They put the motor on it: one of Tess's small ones, off the bench, on the lamp line. Osric threw the switch. The motor sang. The input shaft spun. And through the window in the drum the bright disc went round in the ring, wobbling, round and round, in its odd smooth rolling dance, like a coin settling in a bowl that never settled; and the output shaft, at the drum's other end, with a chalk mark on it, turned so slowly that they all stood and watched it for a full minute before anybody could swear it had moved.

It had moved. It went on moving. In twenty minutes the chalk mark had gone a quarter round.

"Sixteen hundred to one," said Dov. "Two stages. As drawn." He folded the cloth. "It'll want oiling."

"Does it work?" said Tess.

Dov looked at the drum. He looked at it for some time, with the motor singing and the disc rolling through its window and the chalk mark on the slow shaft creeping round.

"It'll want oiling," he said again, and went back to his bench.

They put it on the turbine that night, on the end of the shaft by the far bearing, on a bracket, with a dog clutch so it could be thrown out when the steam went in. When the steam was shut off and the rotor slowed, Bram threw the clutch in, and the little motor took the long hot rotor and rolled it, very slowly, a turn every few minutes, all night, while it cooled; and nobody had to sit up with a bar.

Turning gear, Emeric wrote on the drawing, in the morning. And under it, smaller, the drive's name, that he had thought of in the night and decided Dov should have: cycloidal.

Dov saw the word that afternoon, on the drawing, pinned over his bench. He did not say anything. But he wrote it in his own notebook that night, in his own hand, very carefully, every letter; and the next week, when a man from the port's crane yard came to the Motion Shop's bench and asked whether anybody in it could give him a drive for a slewing crane that would hold a load still with the power off and not wear out in a year, Dov said, before Tess could open her mouth, that he could, and that it would be a cycloidal, and that he would want a fortnight.



Wren came to the Motion Shop in the third week of July, for the summer, because she asked.

She did not ask Tess. She asked Emeric, on the castle steps, on a Sunday, as he was coming down them; she was waiting at the bottom, in a school frock she had outgrown at the wrists with her hair pulled back so hard it made her eyes look surprised, and she had clearly been waiting there some time, and had clearly been practising what she was going to say. She was fifteen. She was Rylan's daughter, and Finn's little sister; and she had been, years ago, a small girl in a doorway in the lower town who had looked up at a strange young man in a good coat, the day he came to their house, and asked whether he was a real prince, and where his crown was.

He had told her he had left it in the carriage.

"School's out till September," she said. "Finn's at the yard all summer. Mother says I'm to help in the house. I don't want to help in the house." She took a breath. "I want to work at the shop. Tess's. The Motion Shop."

"Why?"

"Because of the arm." She said it at once; she had been waiting to be asked. "The one on the bench. That picks things up. I saw it in May, when Finn took me in to see him. It picked up a cup and put it down again and it didn't spill." She was looking at him very straight. "I want to know how."

"Have you asked Tess?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because she'll say ask you," said Wren, "because you're the duke. So I'm asking you." She thought about it. "Your Grace."

He looked at her for a long moment, on the castle steps in the Sunday sun.

"Ask Tess," he said.

Wren's face fell.

"And tell her I said you should," said Emeric. "And tell her I said you're not to be put to sweeping. And tell her I said you're the only person in Kaldo who's ever asked me where my crown was, and I've still not found it, and if she wants a summer hand who isn't afraid of anything, I know one."

Wren looked at him. Then she went pink, from her collar to her hair, all at once, the way her mother did; and turned, and went down the lane toward the works at very nearly a run.

Tess took her on the Monday.

She did not put her to sweeping. She put her at the end of the bench beside Dov, with a file and a block of brass, and told her to make it square; and Wren made it square, by the Friday, very nearly, and Dov looked at it and said nothing, which from Dov was a speech. In the second week she was holding the lamp for Tess under the bench arm while Tess traced a relay fault, lying on her back on the shop floor under the arm's shoulder with the lamp held up and the great iron forearm hanging over her face, a hand's breadth off it, not moving; and Osric, coming in, stopped in the door and said, "Wren, come out from under that," in a voice Emeric had never heard him use; and Wren said, from under it, "Why? It's off," and went on holding the lamp.

It was off. Tess had pulled its stones and thrown its breaker, both, before she let Wren under it; Tess pulled the stones and threw the breaker before anybody went under anything. But Wren had not asked. She had not looked to see. She had simply gone under it, because Tess had said hold the lamp here, and that was where the lamp went.

"She's not afraid of it," said Tess to Emeric, that evening, at the shed door, watching Wren and Dov argue at the bench over the squareness of something. "Of any of it. The arm. The turbine. The motors. I put her at the test bed's chalk line on Thursday to read Ottoline's dial for her, and when the turbine went up to speed she leaned over the line to hear it better. Osric had to pull her back by the collar."

"Is that good?"

Tess thought about it.

"It's good in a shop," she said, "if somebody's watching." She looked at the girl at the bench. "I was like that. Before."

He did not say anything. She did not need him to.

"I'll watch her," said Tess.



The meter standards went into Ferra's book on the eighteenth.

He had not told her they were coming. He had told her, in the spring, that there would be meters: that when the station's wire went into a house, as the old powerhouse's wire had gone into Bram's kitchen and a good many kitchens since, there would be a little glass-fronted box on the wall by the door with a dial in it that counted what the house had used, and that Ottoline would come round and read the dials every month and her tabulator would send the bills. And Ferra had said, at once, without looking up from her book, that a meter that read high was a tax nobody had voted for.

"Then they'll not read high," he had said.

"How will you know?"

He had not had an answer that would satisfy her. So he had gone away and made one.

It was in a box on her desk when she came into her room on the morning of the eighteenth: a long box of Kaldo oak with brass corners, about the size of the Almanac, and a brass plate on the lid that said STANDARDS in Tess's engraved capitals. Ferra looked at it for a while before she opened it.

Inside, in fitted hollows in green baize, were four things.

Four coils of wire, wound on bobbins of white porcelain, each sealed in a little brass can with two terminals on its top and a number stamped on its side. They were the colour of old copper and not quite copper; a mix of copper and nickel and the manganese out of the Grayrock ore, that the Forge had found for him in the spring after two hundred tries, that barely changed its resistance at all however hot or cold it got. Standard resistances. One ohm. Ten. A hundred. A thousand. Then a flat board of polished wood with four brass terminals and a dial and a fine needle in a glass case at its middle and a sliding contact on a long straight wire stretched along its edge: a Wheatstone bridge. Then a glass cell, small, sealed, sitting in a little cradle of cork, with mercury in the bottom of it and two wires out of the top, that Tess had made and remade twenty times over the winter until three of them in a row gave the same pull to the thousandth part: her standard cell, the one thing in Kaldo that said what a volt was. And under them, in the box's lid, folded, a sheet of paper in Tess's hand, that said how to use them.

And a card, under that, in his.

Ferra read the sheet first. She read all of it, slowly, standing at her desk. Then she read the card.

It said: Every meter that goes on a wall in Kaldo is checked against these first. On this bridge. With this cell. By Ottoline or Tess, in the works, before it leaves. Any that read more than one part in a hundred high or low are sent back. Every year, the meters on the walls are taken down by turns and checked again. These standards are kept in your room, under your key. Nobody checks a meter without your box. The bill should be as true as your books.

Ferra put the card down.

She did not go and find him. He had half thought she would; he had stayed in the hall till eleven on the chance. She did not. She sat down at her desk, with the box open in front of her and the coils in their baize, and opened her book; not the works book, nor the barony's, but the other one, the small one with the soft cover that she kept in her pocket and never left anywhere, the one with the column on the back pages that she had kept for ten years and never shown anyone, of the things he did instead of saying them.

She wrote one line in it.

He did not know what it said. He never asked. He found out, years after, by accident, when the book was open on her pillow one morning and he was looking for his shirt; and it said, on that page, in her square hand, under a date: The meters. He made them as true as my books. To my own tolerance. Before I asked. One part in a hundred.

And under that, smaller, as if she had come back and added it later: That's my tolerance. I never told him.



Tess became a delver on the evening of the twentieth, in her own room over the works, with the window open and the sound of the river coming in.

Emeric brought the stone. Ferra brought it with him, in its felt box, with her key; she had turned the key in the lock of her desk and taken out the box and opened it and taken out one of the five long grey stones, and closed the box on four, and written in her book, Inventory, sundry, and a line under it, before she let him take it out of the room. She came with him because it was hers to see go, she said. Imani came because Imani came to everything that happened to Tess and had done since the night of the dynamo house.

Tess was in her chair by the window. She had her hands in her lap. She had been told, at the table on the Sunday, in front of the six, what it was and what it was for and what it was not: that it was not for eating, not this year, not until Imani had her season of records and Armand his proof; that it was to hold; that it would go in a band at her wrist, under the leather, as his did, and she would wear it every day and every night, and take it with her into the slow place when she went there, until the slow place knew her and it was hers. That she would be the first new delver in Kaldo since the six. That nobody outside the room would know.

She had asked one question. She had asked why her.

"Because you're the one whose body most needs what a stone can do," Imani had said, before anybody else could, "when it's time. And you'll want to know it first. Not meet it on the night."

"And because the shop needs a delver," Emeric had said. "To write the arm's stones. Only a delver can. I can't do it all."

Tess had looked from one of them to the other.

"Both, then," she had said.

Now she held out her hand, palm up, in the evening light, and he put the stone in it.

She closed her hand on it. She held it a long time. It was grey and cool and dull and smooth, a long small egg, like any river stone; and she looked down at her closed hand, and he saw her feel it: the small dense silence in her palm, the closed door.

"It's quiet," she said.

"Yes."

"It's like the bottom of a well." She looked up at him. "How do I go in?"

"You don't, the first time. I take you."

He knelt beside her chair and put his own hand over her closed one, so that the stone was between her palm and his; and he went.

He went the way he always went, down, through the door in himself into the slow place, and the long room with the table opened round him; and he held her hand on the way down, in the stone's silence, the way you hold a child's hand on a dark stair. It was not hard. It had been hard once, for him, alone, with his mother's stone, in the thin air of the first winter: months of it, night after night, beating on a closed door. Bringing someone else in by contact was a different thing. He only had to go through, and keep hold.

She came with him.

He felt her come. He felt her note go thin and long and strange, and then catch on something, and steady; and he let go of her hand, there, on the far side, and stood back, and looked.

Her room built itself round her.

He had seen each of the six's rooms build, the first time; it never happened the same way twice. Bram's had come up out of the floor like a forge being lit, all at once. Ferra's had come in from the walls, a page at a time. Tess's came the way she did everything: from the middle outward, in parts, each part fitting to the last. A bench, first, long, under her hands; and on it, as he watched, her relays, rows of them, the brass and the coils, the ones she had built in the winter and the ones she had not built yet, that she had only drawn. A wall of drawers. A window, with a river going by outside it in no light at all. A lamp. A stool by the bench, though she was not sitting on it; she was sitting in her chair, her own, the one he had built her, in the middle of the floor, with her hands in her lap.

And along the far wall, rising, a rail.

It was an iron stair rail, old and black and worn bright along its top by hands, coming up out of nothing at one end of the room and going up the wall and out of sight at the other. He knew it. It was the rail on the old stair track up the hill above the town, the one her chair had been pushed up years ago, a step at a time, with him beside her the whole way and his hand on that rail.

Tess looked at it from her chair.

She did not say anything for a long time.

"I didn't put that there," she said.

"No," said Emeric. "You did."

She sat in the middle of her room and looked at it. Then she looked at her bench, with the relays on it; and at the window; and down at her own hands in her lap, where the stone was not, because in here it was everywhere.

"It's a workshop," she said.

"It's yours. It'll be what you need it to be. It'll change. Mine's changed." He sat down on the stool by her bench, because it was there. "You'll come here at night. With the stone. On your own, after the first few times. It'll be hard to get in, at first, and then less. Then one night it'll be easy, and that's when it's yours." He looked at the relays. "And you can work in here. Slowly. Thirteen hours to the hour outside. You can lay out a whole arm's logic on that bench in an evening and it'll still be there in the morning, and you'll still remember it."

Tess turned her head and looked at him from her chair, in her room, with the rail on the wall behind her.

"Thirteen hours," she said.

"About."

"To the hour."

"Yes."

She looked at her bench for a long time.

"Go away now," said Tess.

He went.

He came up in her room over the works, kneeling by her chair, with his hand over her closed hand and the river coming in at the window; and Ferra was standing by the door with her book, and Imani by the bed with her watch, and outside the open door, in the corridor, Osric, who had not been told and had come anyway, with his back against the wall and his arms folded, not looking in; and past him, on the top step of the stair, Bram. Bram had been told. He had said at the table on the Sunday that a first time wanted fewer people in the room and not more, and he had come up the stair anyway and sat down on the top step of it, with his hands hanging between his knees.

Tess's eyes were still shut. Her face was very still.

Imani looked at her watch.

Tess opened her eyes at a quarter to nine. She looked at the room, and at Emeric kneeling by her, and at her own closed hand under his; and then at the door, and past it, at Osric in the corridor, and at Bram on the step.

"I'm going back in," she said. "In a minute. Somebody stay."

"I'll stay," said Osric, from the corridor, before anybody else could.

On the top step Bram cleared his throat, and did not get up.



The Assembly sat for the first time on the twenty-seventh of July, in the records hall, and the first thing it did was fail.

It failed the budget. Emeric had known it would; everyone at the table had known it would, because Ferra had told them in April exactly which line the ward councils' delegates would not swallow, and why, and that they were right. The Standing Order put the duchy's budget to the Assembly's vote, line by line, as the barony's rules now said it must; and the forty-two delegates from the ward councils and the hamlets and the three other baronies' towns sat on the benches in the records hall in their best coats and listened to Ferra read it all, every line, for two hours, in her square flat voice; and at the end they voted down the line for the new wall round ward 4, thirty-one to eleven, because ward 4 was empty ground and ward 2 had no drains, and a man from ward 2 stood up and said so, and was cheered.

The budget went back to the table. That was the rule. Ferra wrote it in her book, the vote and the date, and closed the book, and Emeric, at the back by the door where he stood again because standing was what he did in this hall now, felt something in his chest go loose that had been tight since the winter.

It had failed. It had failed because forty-two people from the streets had looked at the duke's money and said no. He had wanted it to fail more than he had wanted almost anything that year.

But that was not what the town talked about afterward. The town talked about Hob's girl.

There had been a roll at the door. There had to be. Every man and woman over eighteen on Kaldo's register had a vote for their ward's delegate, and the ward councils had held those votes in May, and the delegates had been chosen; but the Assembly was open to anybody on the register who wanted to come and listen, and on its first day the Assembly would also vote, by the whole room, on whatever the room chose to bring before it, if anybody did. So there was a roll at the door, and a clerk at a table with the register, and every person who came in had to show who they were.

They showed their cards.

The cards had come out of the works in the fortnight before, in their thousands, in Ottoline's trays: a slip of stiff Kaldo paper in a sleeve of horn, the size of a playing card, with the holder's name and ward and line on the register printed on its face, and the Kaldo tree, and a number. And in the horn at its corner, sealed in, very small, a chip of stone. A sense stone, of the kind the wall sensor used, cut from the inside by Emeric's hand in the delve over four long nights, one after another after another, each one set to know one person's note and no other. You held the card in your hand at the clerk's table and the chip warmed, very faintly, a little colour in the horn; and if anybody else held it, it stayed grey. Nobody but the six knew how. To the town, it was a card that knew you, and that was enough.

Hob came to the door at nine with his card in his hand and his daughter beside him.

The clerk took the card. It warmed in Hob's hand, a little amber in the horn. The clerk found his line in the register. Then, as the rule said, he turned the register round on the table, so that the man in front of him could read his own line and say it was true.

Hob looked at the register for a long time.

He could not read. Everybody at the door knew that he could not read; half the men in the queue behind him could not read either, and the rule had been written by people who could, and nobody had thought. Hob stood at the clerk's table with his card warm in his hand and his own name on a page in front of him that he could not see, and did not say anything, and his face went a dark red from the neck up.

His daughter put her finger on the line.

She was eleven, Nell, and she had been at the school since the spring, since the week they came down the line; and she read it aloud, in a high clear voice that carried to the back of the queue and into the hall. "Hob Tanner. Ward 3, first terrace, the end house. Printer's crew, the winch. Married, Bet. Three children." She looked up at her father. "That's us."

"Is it true?" said the clerk, to Hob.

Hob looked down at his daughter's finger on the page.

"It's true," he said.

The clerk wrote him in.

An old fisherman from the port, two behind in the queue, who had stood at that same table at the ward council's vote in May and been sent away because he could not read his line and had nobody with him who could, said into the quiet, quite loudly, that if a man's daughter could read it to him then he did not see why a man's neighbour couldn't, and that he had a neighbour, and the neighbour was standing behind him.

The neighbour was. The clerk looked at the register. He looked at the old fisherman, and at the neighbour, and at the rule pinned on the table in front of him, which said nothing about it either way; and then, because he did not know what else to do, he looked down the hall at Ferra on her bench, and Ferra looked back at him and nodded once.

The clerk turned the register round.

By the end of the day the rule had been changed. The Assembly did it; it was the first thing the room voted on, after the budget, a delegate from the port bringing it, the old fisherman's neighbour as it turned out, who had stood for the port's ward in May. A man on the register who could not read his line could have it read to him, by anybody he chose, and vouched for. It passed without a voice against it. Ferra wrote it in her book.

Then she stood up.

She did not bring it herself. She had said she would not, on the wall, in the dark. She had given it in June to a delegate from ward 3: the cooper, who had sat on Hob's court and drawn the lot and spent the twenty minutes in the back room, and who had said in the back room, Ferra told Emeric afterward, that he had not known until that day that a court could be a thing you were proud to have sat on. The cooper stood up on the benches with a sheet of paper in his hand in Ferra's square writing and read it to the room, in a voice that shook on the first line and did not on the second.

It was two lines.

Nobody whose name is on the register of Kaldo shall be taken out of Kaldo, or held, or punished, without a hearing in an open court. Any person may read the record of it.

The room was very quiet.

Emeric stood at the back by the door, and did not move. He knew the words for it. He had known them since before he was born, in a manner of speaking, from a world where men had died for them; he could have said them aloud in that hall in another language, and nobody but the two women at the front would have understood a word, and he did not say them.

The Assembly voted on it by show of hands. Forty-two delegates and the whole room behind them. Emeric watched the hands go up, all down the benches; and in the second row the widow from the bakery by the north well, who had sat on Hob's court too, put hers up with her chin out; and at the end of the third row, by the aisle, Gale the mason put up his, slowly, broad and grey, a hand that had laid the north well's apron twenty years ago, and held it there.

Ward voted last.

He was not a delegate. None of the six were. But the barony's rules said the Standing Order must assent to a law the Assembly passed before it was law, each of them, in the open, by name; and Ferra read the names at the end, one by one, from her bench, and each of them said yes. Bram, from the side wall, gruff. Saban, from somewhere nobody had noticed him. Armand, from his chair under the window, in his lecture voice. Imani, from the front bench, before her name was finished. Ferra herself, writing it.

Then Ward. He was standing by the door, a yard from Emeric, with his arms folded, where he had stood all day.

"Ward," said Ferra.

Ward looked down the hall at her. He looked at the room, and the benches, and Hob in the fourth row with his girl beside him and his card in his hand.

"Yes," said Ward.

Then, after a moment, as Ferra was already writing it, he said, "And I'll see it kept," which was not on any form and which Ferra wrote down anyway.



There was one more vote that day, and it did not pass, because it was not meant to.

At the very end, with the lamps lit and half the room gone home, a delegate from the printer's crews, a young man with a voice like a crane chain who had been made the crews' man in May because he was the only one of them who would stand up and talk to Bram, stood on the benches and said that he would like it written down that the Works Rule's forty-eight hours was a good rule and a great deal better than anywhere else in the kingdom, and that the crews were grateful for it, and that he would like the Assembly to think about forty.

The room laughed. Not unkindly. Some of it laughed because it thought he was joking, and some because it knew he was not.

"Forty," said the young man, when the laugh had gone down, "and two days of rest, not one. And the same for the overtime." He looked round the hall. "Not today. I know not today. I'm asking us to think about it. By next year."

Nobody voted on it. Ferra wrote it in her book, as proposed, with his name and the date; and under it, in her own column on the back pages where nobody looked, a year.



Rylan closed his first ring on the twenty-ninth of July, a week after he had told Ward he would, and did not tell anybody.

Declan told them, at supper, on the thirtieth.

He told them the way Declan told anything, between the meat and the bread, as if it had only just occurred to him. He had gone out to the base's yard at five in the morning on the twenty-ninth to see to a horse with a stone in its hoof, and found Rylan alone in the middle of the yard in the grey light with his sleeves down and his arms loose at his sides, as Ward had stood in March; and he had watched from the stable door, because he had not wanted to interrupt. He had watched Rylan let his arms go, as Emeric had taught them, slowly, the knight's hold opening on the mana in them a little at a time; and the ring come up before Rylan's right palm, amber, and shake, and shake, and draw itself again a hair to the side, and back; and close.

"Earth," said Declan. "Same as Ward's. He lifted the mounting block a hand off the ground and put it down again. Then he looked round the yard to see if anybody'd seen, and I'd gone back in the stable." He took more bread. "Then he went in to breakfast and said nothing to anybody. And he's been saying nothing all day."

Everybody at the table looked down it at Rylan.

Rylan was looking at his plate.

"A week late," said Ward, after a while.

"A week late," said Rylan.

"Why didn't you say?"

Rylan looked up from his plate at last, and down the table at Ward, and at Emeric; and then at his son Finn, at the far end, with his mouth open.

"Because I'd told you a week," he said, "and it wasn't a week, and I didn't want to say it till I'd done it twice." He looked back at his plate. "I've done it twice now."

Ward looked at him for a long moment.

Then he picked up his cup and held it out down the table, across the dishes and the bread, toward Rylan; and after a moment Rylan picked up his own and held it out, and they did not touch them together, because Ward did not do that and never had. They only held them there, across the table, for the length of a breath, the two of them, with everyone watching. Then they drank.

Finn, at the end of the table, had shut his mouth. He was looking at his father as if he had never seen him before.

"Earth," said Wren, beside him, very loudly, into the quiet. "I knew it'd be earth. I said so to Tess on Tuesday. Didn't I say, Finn?"

"You never," said Finn.

"I did. I said he's got earth hands. You can tell." She looked down the table at her father. "Can't you?"

Rylan looked at his daughter, and at his own hands, broad and brown on either side of his plate; and for the first time that day, he laughed.
