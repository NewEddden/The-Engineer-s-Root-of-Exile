Chapter 225 - Our Old Master

"No," said Imani.

She said it before Armand had finished asking. She had seen it coming from the far end of the long room, the way she saw a fever coming in a child's face an hour before the child knew it was ill: the old man sitting forward in his chair with his readings in front of him, and the prince standing at the table's head saying one number, all night, and Armand saying yes, I rather thought you would, and then taking off his spectacles and folding them, which he did when he had decided something and was about to be reasonable about it.

"I haven't asked yet," said Armand.

"You were going to ask to go up. All night. In the Hod, at three miles, in the cold, at your age, to sit at a rail and read stones every hour until dawn." She did not raise her voice. "No."

Armand looked at her with great courtesy.

"Somebody has to read them," he said. "The prince wants one number. The band's density at night, held, so that the dome can be set to it and kept at it. I have three years of readings of the band and not one of them is that number. They were taken on the way up and the way down, in a hurry, with my hands shaking, at whatever hour the racks were full, for the racks' sake. They're a range. You can't set a dome to a range." He unfolded the spectacles again. "The number has to be read at set hours, all night, from dusk to dawn, at the same rail, by the same instruments, by the same hand. And the hand has to be a hand I trust, or I won't believe the number, and if I don't believe it, I won't let him set the dome by it." He put the spectacles on. "I trust mine."

"Then teach somebody else to read them."

"I have. Paskin reads them very well. I wouldn't set a dome to Paskin's number either." He said it without any unkindness at all. "It's the same reason I asked for a control on the seed stones. A number somebody else read is a number I have to take on trust, and I have never once in fifty years set anything on trust that I could read myself."

Imani looked at him.

She had had two fingers on his wrist on the bank by the lake, twice, in the years when the prince was learning to fly things. She had felt his heart go under them like a bird in a hand. The second time she had not been able to get him off the settle by the ward's fire until dark. She had written the order that morning, in her own hand, and he had signed it: the Secretary of the Arcane catches nothing. No load, no stone, no man, nothing that is falling. He had never once broken it. She had watched him, on the flat, with a whole wall's worth of mages lifting earth in pairs all round him, keep his hands in his sleeves.

"You've been up," she said. "In the Hod. I've entered every one."

"Four times. When my hands were bad."

"Up and down. A few hours. The rule for the Hod is up, fill, and down, the same night. You were never up past the middle of a night. You were never up at dusk, or at dawn, and you were never up for twelve hours in the cold at three miles with nothing to do but sit." She put her hand flat on the table. "It isn't a night in the band I'm frightened of. It's the cold and the thin air and the hours. And you're past eighty."

"Well past," said Armand, mildly.

The table was quiet. The prince did not say anything. Neither did Ferra, who was writing, or Ward, who was at the door. It was hers. They all knew it was hers. It was the one thing at that table that was only ever hers, and they let her have it.

She looked at the old man for a long time. He looked back at her over his spectacles, patiently, and she could see that he would wait. He would not argue with her. He would not go over her head. He would sit there, with his readings and his range that was not a number, and wait for her to come to it, because he knew she would, because she was a physician and he was right.

"Conditions," she said.

She wrote them herself, on a single sheet, that evening, and he signed them the next morning without reading them twice, which she noticed.

One. He goes up once, for one night, from dusk to dawn, and no longer, and comes down at dawn whatever the readings say.

Two. He does not stand at the rail. He sits, in the deckhouse, in the warm, and the instruments are brought to him, and taken back to the rail by somebody else, at the hours he sets.

Three. He is the only one aboard with a bed, and he lies down in it between the hours, whether he sleeps or not.

Four. The Secretary of Health goes up with him, and her word aboard is final, and if she says down, the ship goes down.

Five. He eats.

Ferra entered it. The prince read it over Ferra's shoulder and said nothing, and then said, "The Hod," and Imani said, "The Hod," and neither of them said why, because they both knew. The Hod had a corner now.

They went up at dusk from the far end of the lake, in the first week of the summer, on a clear still evening with the water like a sheet of pewter.

That was new too. The Hod went up after the last bell, always, in the full dark, when nobody on the flat was awake to look up, and came down before the first. Tonight they went while there was still light in the west, because the number had to be read from dusk, and Armand would not begin it late. Ned Tallis brought her off the water at the lake's far end, under the reed beds where there was nobody for a mile, and took her straight up, hard, faster than Imani had ever felt her go, so that the lake went away under them like a stone dropped down a well.

Armand sat in the deckhouse, in the corner, in his chair.

It was a good corner. Imani had seen it in the dock, before the Hod came out, when Fletcher was fitting it, and had thought nothing of it but that somebody was going to be comfortable. Now she sat on the bench across the deckhouse from it with her bag at her feet and watched the old man in it. The padded bunk along the wall, with its lip. The little stove in its iron box with the fire crystal in it, already warm, with a kettle on it. The floor under the corner warm to the soles of her boots, and the rest of the deckhouse floor not. The curtain on its rail. And the shelf.

Armand had brought books. Six of them, in a strap. He had not asked where to put them. He had sat down on the bunk to take his boots off, carefully, the way he did everything now, with one hand on the edge of it, and leaned back against the wall, and put his hand up without looking for the shelf to put the books on, and found it exactly where his hand went.

He stopped with his hand on it.

Imani saw him stop. She saw him look at the shelf, and then at his own hand on it, and then at the height of it, and then at the bunk he was sitting on, and she saw him work it out the way he worked out a reading that had come in where he had not expected it. A shelf at the height of a man's eye standing would have been a reach. This one was not. It was where a man's hand went who was sitting on that bunk, leaning back, tired, not wanting to get up.

He did not say anything. He put the books on it, one by one, spines out, and took his hand away, and sat for a moment looking at them. Then he looked out of the deckhouse glass.

The world was going away underneath them.

Imani had been up before. On the K Discovery, to Emberfell and back, and she knew what it looked like from above. But she had never seen it at dusk. She had never seen the light go off it.

They were over the lake and climbing, and the lake was below them, the whole of it, long and grey and still, going away to the north between its hills, with the island in the middle of it where it split round. She could see the island's wall, a thin dark ring. She could see the keep on the hill inside it, square, with its tower at the corner, and a light in the long room's window, which was Armand's hall now, where one of his students was working late. The market street was a line of tiny gold points in two rows, the gas lamps, burning for nobody. The bridge. The works on the far bank, with the foundry's glow and the stacks and the long roof of the paper mill. The rail going away west along the lake shore toward Milford in a straight bright line where the last of the sun caught it.

And beyond it all, on the flat, the new city.

From here it was a circle. She had known it was a circle; she had seen the drawing a hundred times. She had never seen it be one. It lay on the flat below them in the dusk, a ring of grey wall with its towers standing out of it like the pins in a surveyor's chain, and inside the ring the rings of water, two of them, catching the last of the western light and shining like two wedding bands laid one inside the other on a grey cloth. The first quarter lit, and the second, and the third, window after window, the lights coming on as she watched, the whole north-east of the circle pricked out in gold. The fourth quarter still dark, still stakes. The towers in their ring round the middle. And in the middle, on the rise, the palace, and on the palace, the dome.

It caught the light. It was the last thing on the ground that did. The sun was down behind the western hills and the whole of the flat had gone to shadow, and the dome still stood up into the last of the light on the top of the drum like a drop of water on a stone, and held it, and shone. Then the light went off it too.

Armand was standing at the deckhouse glass. She had not seen him get up.

He stood with one hand on the frame and looked down at it, all of it, the lake and the island and the works and the city, for a long time, without saying anything. His face was very still. She had expected him to say something. He always said something; he was the man at the table who could not look at a thing without explaining it, and correcting the explanation, and adding a qualification. He did not say anything at all.

At last, as the dome went dark, he said, "I've never seen it in the light."

"No."

"I've been up four times. In the dark. I've read the band four times and looked at the stones and never once looked down." He did not take his hand off the frame. "Fifty years in a tower. I have measured every student who ever came through its door. I have measured the wall of this city and the water in its towers and the stones in its hull. I don't think I have ever once looked at a thing I was part of from far enough away to see the shape of it."

Imani did not say anything. Neither did Ned, at the helm. The Hod went on up into the dark, and the city went small and gold under them, and then the cold came, and then the band.

Armand went back to his corner and sat down.

"Readings," he said. "The first at the top of the hour. And then every hour on the hour until dawn. At the starboard rail, amidships, where the stand is fixed. The same stand, the same stones, every time." He looked at Imani. "Brought to me. As per the conditions."

It was a long night. She had known it would be.

Ned held the Hod in the band far out over the sea, south, where nothing ever went, and she hung there in the dark with her racks humming and her engine banked and her new deckhouse warm, and every hour Garrick went out to the starboard rail in his oilskin and took the instruments off their stand, the brass cups on their rods with the reading stones in them, and brought them in to the corner with the cold coming off them in a cloud, and Armand read them.

He did it the same way every time. He put the kettle aside. He took the first rod in his left hand and looked at the stone in its cup, and put his right hand over it, not touching, and closed his eyes, and was still for the space of a long breath, and opened them, and wrote a figure in his book. Then the second rod. Then the third. Then he gave them back to Garrick and Garrick took them out to the rail again and put them back on the stand, and Armand lay down on the bunk with his hands folded on his chest and his eyes open, as per the conditions, and looked at the ceiling of the deckhouse until the next hour.

Imani took his pulse at the second hour and the fifth and the eighth, with two fingers on his wrist. It was steady. It was steadier than it was on the ground. She had known it would be, and it still unsettled her. His hands, when he held the rods, did not shake at all. At the fourth hour she saw him take a book off the shelf and open it and read two pages without his spectacles, and then look at his hands holding the book, and put the book back.

At the sixth hour, which was the middle of the night, he said, "It isn't moving."

"The number?"

"The number." He had his book open on his knee. He turned it to her. The figures went down the page in a column, one for every hour, three to an hour for the three stones. She could not read them; they were in the tower's notation, and she had never learned it. But she could see that they were the same. The same shapes, hour after hour, down the page, with the little variations a hand makes and nothing else.

"On the way up and the way down, it moves," Armand said. "Every night. It moves at dusk, and at dawn, and when the air is changing, and when we're climbing through it, and I've always read it on the move, and so I've always had a range." He tapped the column. "It doesn't move once it's night. It sits. It sits like water in a basin, all night, the same." He looked at the column as if it had done something clever. "It isn't a range at all. It was only ever a range because I never sat still long enough to see it wasn't."

"One number," said Imani.

"One number." He closed the book. "I'll want the rest of the night to be sure. But that's it. I'd bet the tower on it, and I don't bet."

He lay down again on the bunk with the book on his chest, and folded his hands on it, and after a while Imani saw that his eyes had closed. She did not wake him for the seventh hour. She went out to the rail herself with Garrick, in the cold, and brought the rods in, and held them in front of him until he woke, which he did at once, the way old men and soldiers wake, and read them, and wrote, and it was the same.

She made the tea at the ninth hour, on the little stove, because Armand had asked for it and the conditions said he was to eat and nobody had written down that he was to drink, and she thought that was an oversight.

He held the cup in both hands and looked at the shelf.

"Who told Fletcher?" he said.

"Nobody." She sat down across from him. "He was told to put up a shelf for books in the corner. He asked who it was for and nobody would tell him. So he sat on the bunk and put his hand up and put it where his hand went."

Armand looked at the shelf for a while longer.

"I've lived in a great many rooms," he said. "Tower rooms. Lodgings. That damp study at the keep. The prince's old rooms, now, which are too big and have a draught. I've had a great many shelves. I have never once had one that anybody put up for me." He drank his tea. "I've always had to stand up to get a book."

Imani did not answer that. She did not think it wanted an answer.

They came down at dawn.

Ned brought her down out of the band into the grey, fast, the way he took her up, and the sea came up under them, and then the coast, and then the lake with the mist lying on it in the first light, and the island in the mist with the keep standing up out of it. Armand did not go to the glass this time. He sat in his corner with his book shut on his knee and his hands on it and his eyes closed, and when they were down on the water at the lake's far end and Ned had the screw in and the engine turning, he opened them.

"Well?" said Imani.

"Twelve hours," said Armand. "Twelve readings. Thirty-six stones read. One number." He opened the book and looked at it, and closed it again. "And I feel," he said, with great precision, "about fifty."

She took his pulse. He let her. It was a young man's pulse, strong and slow. She wrote it down in her register, the hour and the figure and after a night in the band, seated, warm, fed, and looked at it, and did not write anything under it, though there was a great deal she could have written.

"It'll go," she said. "In two days. It always does."

"I know."

"And you'll want to go back up."

"I shan't ask," said Armand. "It's in your conditions. Once." He stood up, carefully, with a hand on the edge of the bunk, and took his six books off the shelf one by one and put them back in their strap. At the last one he stopped, and put it back on the shelf, and left it there. "For next time," he said, "if there is one, which is your affair," and went out on deck.

Ferra painted the line that afternoon.

The gauge was in the drum's wall by the pass-box: a reading stone behind a small window of glass set through the stone into the dome, and beside the window a strip of brass as long as a man's forearm, marked off in the tower's notation, that Bram had fixed there and Armand had marked. When the stone behind the window read, you could see where it stood against the brass. It had stood at two thirds of the band since the second cartridge.

Armand read her the number. She did not ask him to say it twice. She opened a small pot of red lead, and took a brush, and drew a line across the brass at the number, straight and thin and exact, as she ruled a line in a book, and stood back from it.

"There," she said.

The prince stood at the gauge and looked at the red line for a long time. Then he looked through the pass-box's two glass doors into the round room, at Imani's beds, and the willow slips, and the round bed in the middle with nothing in it but a mark where a hand had pressed it.

"That's the band," he said.

"That's the band," said Armand. "At night. Held. One number." He looked at the red line, and then at the prince, over his spectacles. "Not a grain over it. Not until you've read something that says otherwise. Not because you think more might be better." He did not say it unkindly. "You'll want to. I've watched you want to since the first fill. Don't."

The prince did not answer.

Imani, who was at the pass-box with her hands in the sleeves, watering the feverfew, looked up at the two of them, and then back at her beds, and said nothing either. She had seen the prince's face. She knew what it looked like when he had heard a thing and was going to do the other thing anyway, and it looked like that.
