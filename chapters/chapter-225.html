Chapter 225 - Duke

The gallery above the kitchens had not changed.

He had walked it last at twenty-two, with a stone in his pocket he could not explain and the court's laughter still in his ears, and he walked it now behind a page in the king's livery at half past one in the morning, in a waistcoat with a slit in it and a steel plate showing through the slit; and the gallery was dark, and the kitchens below it were dark, and the same lamp hung at the same turn of the stair, and at the end of the corridor beyond it a man stood where a man stands to announce footsteps, not to stop them.

Two men on the outer door. The page knocked, and opened it, and stood aside.

The office was not large. It had a desk and two chairs and a window with a shutter, and a fire that had been let go down to coals, and the king standing in front of the fire with his back to it, in his shirt, with the crown's red coat thrown over the back of a chair and the circlet on the desk beside a cup.

The page went out. The door shut.

Harrold looked at him for a while. Then he went past him to the door, and opened it, and said into the corridor, not loudly, "Go down."

Emeric heard the man at the end of the corridor go. He heard him all the way down the stair, his boots on the stone, round the turn by the lamp and on down into the dark, until there was nothing to hear at all. His father stood at the open door until there was nothing, and then shut it, and came back to the fire.

There was nobody listening. Emeric felt for it, out of nine years' habit, with his sense along the corridor and down the stair; there was nobody. The two men on the outer door, and beyond them the whole dark gallery, empty to the kitchens.

It was the first time in his life they had been in a room together with nobody at the door.

"The man is in the crown's cells," said Harrold. He did not sit. He stood in front of the fire with his hands behind his back, as he had stood nine years ago a foot from his son, and his voice was the voice from then, low, dying at arm's length. "Keller will see him at seven. You and your knight may be there if you choose. Nobody else will be, by my order. The Lieutenant has asked to be, and been told the crown's magistrate needs no help." He was quiet. "I told you not to stay for the cups."

"Yes."

"You stayed."

"Yes."

Harrold looked at the slit in the waistcoat, and at the grey edge of the steel in it with the bright scratch across. He looked at it for a long breath without any expression at all. Then he looked at the fire.

Emeric waited for the next instruction. He had come up the gallery knowing there would be one: go home, go home tomorrow, go home by such a road, say nothing of tonight, trust nobody the crown's guard did not send. He stood a pace inside the door and waited for it, as he had waited nine years ago for a correction that did not come.

"Your mother wore gold roses," said his father.

The fire settled. Down in the dark, somewhere, a door went.

"At her wedding." Harrold did not turn round. He was looking at the coals. "Down the skirt, and at the sleeves. Gold, worked close, on black. The court thought black was a strange thing for a bride, and said so for a year." His voice did not change at all. "She chose them herself. The embroiderers told her roses took the gold better than anything else and read from the back of a hall, and she said then she'd have roses." He was quiet. "I hadn't seen them since."

Emeric did not say anything. He could not have said anything. He stood a pace inside the door of the small room with his hands at his sides and the fire in front of him and his father's back, and the floor under his boots seemed a very long way down.

"Ferra chose them," he said, at last. "For the embroidery."

"Yes," said Harrold. "So did she."

They stood there. The coals ticked as they cooled.

He had a question. He had not known he had it until it was in his mouth; and it was not about the man in the cells, or the cups, or who had sent the knife, or what he was for, or how long. It was a small one. It had been in him since a dining hall in Emberfell, more than a year ago, when a queen with a sword at her hip had told him he hummed.

"Did she hum?" he said.

His father turned round.

He looked at his son across the small room. His face was an old man's face in the low light, white at the temples, lined from the nose to the mouth, and it did nothing at all for a long breath; and then, very slightly, at one corner of the mouth, it did something.

"All the time," said Harrold. "When she worked. Badly." He was quiet. "She couldn't hold a tune to save her life. You did it at your slate, at five. The same tune. I used to stand outside the door."

Then the face closed again, as a shutter closes, and he went back to the desk and picked up the cup and looked into it and put it down.

"That's all," said the king.

Emeric stood a breath longer. Then he went to the door and opened it and went out, and did not bow, because there was nobody at the end of the corridor to see it; and went down the long gallery above the dark kitchens and down the stair past the lamp, and out into the yard at the bottom, where Ward was waiting with Saban's carriage.

Ward was not alone. A man stood at the near horse's head with its off forefoot up between his knees, bent over the hoof in the light of the carriage lamp and working at it with something small; and the coachman stood a little way off with his hat in his hands and the look of a man who has been told to stand there by somebody he cannot argue with.

Ward had not sent him off. That was the first thing Emeric noticed. The second was the eyes, when the man looked up into the lamplight.

"There was a stone in it," said Waylon. He let the hoof down and held out his palm with a sharp grey chip of gravel on it, and dropped it. "Your man's been driving on it since the gate. He'd have had her lame by the tier."

"She's not mine. She's hired."

"She's a horse." Waylon wiped his hands down the front of his coat, which had straw on it, and on the sleeve, and in the short beard; he had come, evidently, from the mews, and not by the hall, though Emeric had heard him laugh in it an hour ago. He had been a thin boy of eighteen the last time Emeric had seen him, across a courtyard, turning away. He was square now, and brown from riding, and he did not turn away. He stood by the horse's shoulder with his hand on her neck and looked at his brother in the gold and black, and the slit, and the steel in it.

"I asked at the judiciary," he said. "The first year. Which day."

"I know."

"You know." Waylon looked at Ward. "Him?"

"A groom. In an inn yard."

Something happened at the corner of Waylon's mouth. "Grooms." He looked at the horse. "I got as far as the steps. Twice. I didn't know what I'd say. I'd been angry since I was four and I didn't know how to stop in front of people." He was quiet. "I'm not good in front of people. You'll have noticed. I laughed."

"I noticed."

"He'll not forget it."

"He never forgot anything. Now he's got a reason." Emeric looked at him across the lamp. He had not known until that moment that he was going to say it, and then he found that he had been carrying it for twenty-three years and that it was not heavy at all, only old. "You were four when I shut the door."

"I remember."

"I didn't shut it on you."

Waylon's hand stopped on the horse's neck.

"It had to be everybody," said Emeric. "Or it wouldn't have worked."

"What wouldn't?"

"Keeping me alive."

The yard was very quiet.

Waylon did not ask who. Emeric watched him not ask. He watched his brother's eyes go up, past him, to the dark front of the palace and the long gallery above the kitchens, where one lamp still burned at the turn of the stair; and stay there for a breath; and come down again, slowly, as a man's eyes come down from a sum he has finished in his head.

"You could have told me," said Waylon. "After."

"You were a child. Then you were fourteen and hated me, which was safer for you than anything I could have told you."

"And now?"

"Now I heard you laugh."

Waylon looked at him for a long breath. Then he laughed again, quietly, not because anything was funny, and put his forehead down against the horse's neck, and took it away.

"You used to build things on the floor," he said. "In the long room. Out of anything. You let me hold the pieces."

"You held them very badly."

"I was three."

"You were very bad at it."

"I hear you build bigger things now."

"Somewhat."

"I'd like to see." Waylon looked at the palace again. "Not yet. Somebody ought to be here who isn't him." He was quiet. "I keep horses in the mews. Nobody looks twice at a man who keeps horses. He doesn't." He gave the mare's neck a slap and stepped back from her. "The grey's in foal. If it's a filly I'll send word."

He did not offer his hand, and Emeric did not offer his. He went toward the arch at the yard's end, where the dark of the mews began; and at the arch he stopped, and turned round, and came back, three steps, quickly, as a man comes back who has decided a thing before he can think better of it, and put his arms round his brother, hard and short and awkward, with the straw on his coat against the gold, and let go.

Then he went, and the dark under the arch had him.

Emeric got into the carriage, and sat in it, and did not say anything all the way to the tier.

Ward did not ask him anything either. Halfway there, in the dark of the carriage, Ward reached across without a word and put a hand on the back of his neck, once, hard, where the lace had rubbed it raw, the way he had done when Emeric was seven and could not stop shaking; and took it away again, and looked out of the window.



The man would not say who had sent him.

Keller saw him at seven in a cell under the palace's west range, a stone room with a bench and a grating, by the light of a lantern on a hook, with the captain of the king's guard at the door and Ward and Emeric at the back against the wall, and Keller sitting on a stool in front of the bench with a board on his knee and a pen and the least that could be said to the purpose.

The man sat on the bench with his left arm bound across his chest where Ward had broken it and a dark bruise down one side of his face from the table, and looked at Keller with polite interest, and answered nothing. Not his name. Not where he was from. Not who had put him into the palace's brown, or when, or how, or for what fee. He was courteous about it. When Keller asked him a question he waited until Keller had finished asking it and then said nothing, pleasantly, and waited for the next.

After a quarter of an hour Keller put his pen down.

"You understand," he said, "that a man who tries to kill a duke of the realm at the king's own table is hanged."

The man looked at him. Then, for the first time, he smiled, the mouth that had been laughing on a road in the north by a burning coach lamp; and said, in an ordinary northern voice, "I understand that a man who talks is hanged sooner," and went back to saying nothing.

Emeric could have had it out of him.

He knew it standing against the wall. He could feel the man's note across the cell from where he stood: close, hard, tired, a little afraid under the courtesy, and very sure of itself, and under that the shape of a great deal the man knew and was keeping. A breath. One forced breath into the room behind that note, the door kicked in and the man's whole winter laid out on the floor of it, every face that had paid him and every room he had been paid in. He had done it once, to a clerk in a strongroom at Wayford, and come out of it with a kitchen he had never stood in and a woman's voice he would never place, and Armand had told him in his own room that it was a bill and not weather.

He stood against the wall and did nothing.

Ward, beside him, did not look at him and did not ask. He had seen Emeric look at the man and stay where he was, and that was the whole of what Ward said about it, then or afterward.

"He will be held at the crown's pleasure," said Keller, and stood up, and put his board under his arm. "Under the crown's lock." He looked at the man on the bench. "Somebody will want him to talk, one day, more than he wants not to. It is very seldom the crown." He looked at Emeric. "Good morning, Your Grace."



They went up off the lowest tier at noon.

There were several hundred people on the terraces and the steps and in the street below, because word had gone round the city overnight, as it would; and they watched the new duke of Kaldo come up the steps in a plain dark coat with his knight beside him and his Chancellor on his other side in grey, and the physician of Kaldo a step behind with her bag, because she had refused to walk in front of a crowd twice in one week. They did not cheer. They watched him. They watched to see whether he walked as a man walks who was stabbed at his own girding, and he walked as a man walks who has slept three hours, which was true.

Vantic was not aboard. She had sent a note to the tier at nine, in her own hand, on the House's paper, four lines: that the House had matters in the city that would keep her a fortnight; that she would come south by the coast road for the quarter day, and wished then to speak with the Duke about where the House should keep its seat; that the Chancellor's left sleeve had been admired by three duchesses and a cardinal's niece and she wished it to be known. Ferra read it twice and put it in her book, under the date, without a word, and her mouth was not quite straight.

Saban's man at the tier, the tier master's clerk with the ink on his fingers, came down the steps at the last moment with a paper still damp from the press and gave it to Saban without a word, and went back up.

Old Edrin sat in the casting seat and put his hands on the brass plate and his fingers on the levers under its lip. Along the terrace wall the casters who had come back from their morning runs sat in a row in the sun, grey, done, and watched him do it with an expression Emeric could name now, because he had had three days to learn it. They watched the old man's shoulders go down and his breath come hard and the colour go out of his face; and the young one who had asked him on Monday where he had come from got up off the wall and came to the cradle's edge and stood there with his cap in his hands.

"South again, grandfather?"

"South," said Old Edrin.

"Three days. On your own." The young caster shook his head. "God keep you."

"He'd better," said Old Edrin, and lifted her out of the cradle.



Saban read the damp paper in the bows when they were over the first ridge and the capital was a smudge of smoke behind them. Then he gave it to Emeric without saying anything, which was itself a thing Saban said.

It was in Aldane's fine new fount. It said that on the evening of the investiture, after the girding, His Grace of Aldane had dined at the hall of the scribes' guild in the old town with the guild's masters and men; that His Grace had given the guild a hundred Gold for the relief of those of its members put out of their living by the presses of the south; and that His Grace had promised the guild his patronage and his voice in the council, so long as he had either, against the ruin of an honest craft older than any house in the realm by machines that knew no God and no master. The scribes, the sheet said, had called His Grace their friend. It called them, as the town had begun to call them, the Inkhands.

"There was a crowd in the street when he came out," said Saban. "My man saw it. Four or five hundred. Scribes and their wives and the men who sell them ink and the boys who used to carry their copies. They cheered him." He looked out at the country going by. "Nobody in that street ever cheered a duke in their lives before last night. They cheered that one." He turned his hat round once in his hands. "And the Lieutenant was at the cathedral's chapter house at eight this morning. My man saw his coach. An hour with the dean, who keeps the chapter while the cardinal's at Ostrel." He was quiet. "He's never set foot in the chapter house in his life. His uncle's come out of the year with nothing, and His Highness has gone to church."

Emeric read it again.

It was not a bad case. That was the first thing he made himself see, and he made himself see it fairly, as Ferra would have. There had been copyists in the capital for six hundred years, men who had spent their lives learning a hand that could copy a deed or a gospel without a blot, and kept their families on it, and taught it to their sons. And the open press in Kaldo, and Ambry's press in Wayford in Wayford's own type, and the paper that came out of the mill by the ten thousand sheets, had made all of that, in four years, a thing nobody needed to pay for. He had done that. He had done it on purpose, because a town where every child could have a book was a town that could read the man on the cart's broadsheet; and the men in that street had paid for it, and nobody had offered them anything, until a duke came and gave them a hundred Gold and his voice. Their sympathy was real. His was too, very likely; Aldane's northern towns held the old scriptoria, and their scribes were his tenants and his tenants' sons.

All of that was true, and it was a good case, and Emeric granted it.

And then he went down what it did not explain, one line at a time, as he went down a column.

It did not explain the box of carved soldiers at the banquet nine years ago, handed to a grown man with the court watching. It did not explain a man standing in the chancery's window on the day Grayrock was struck, longer than a man stands by accident. It did not explain a commission on chancery paper in a dead baron's box, or six articles filed at court with the best of them left out because the best of them would have hanged the man who brought them. It did not explain a page in an under-quartermaster's book. And it did not explain a hundred and eighty-eight knights of the north, and a boy of nineteen in a white hart on green who had not been supposed to be in the first line, spent in a morning against farmers on a quarrel about a toll on a bridge.

A hundred Gold to the scribes. A hundred and eighty-eight knights.

He folded the paper and gave it back to Saban.

"He'll do well with it," said Saban.

"Yes."

"Do we answer it?"

"Not with a broadsheet." Emeric looked south, ahead, where the country went on flat and green to a horizon he could not see past yet. "There'll be scribes in Kaldo who went to the press when the press came. Find them." He thought about it. "Ask them what the others would want. Not a hundred Gold. What they'd want."

Saban looked at him for a breath with his hat in his hands. Then he took out his pencil and wrote something on his cuff.



Ward would not land that first night.

He said so at four in the afternoon, at the hour when a capital hull with one old caster aboard would be looking for a field, standing over Old Edrin at the casting seat with the map in his hands and the country going by underneath, flat and open and full of farms.

"Not within a day of the city," he said. "Not after last night. A man who is paid to put a knife into a duke at the king's table has friends who are paid to stand in a field at dusk with crossbows when a slow boat comes down into it." He folded the map. "We hold her up. Dark. Lamps out. Over the water meadows, where there's nobody for miles. Nobody can see a hull at night with her lamps out. Nobody looks up."

"A caster can't pour all night," said Old Edrin, from the seat, in his own voice. "Not one. Anybody who knows hulls knows that."

"Then nobody who knows hulls will see us do it," said Ward.

So when the sun went down they did not come down with it. They held, a thousand feet up, over a long empty country of water meadows where the river spread into a dozen channels and nobody had built a house in living memory, with the lamps out and the stones under the iron holding her height as they had been told to. Old Edrin stopped dying as soon as it was too dark to see him from the ground, ate a very large supper, and went to sleep in the seat with a blanket over his knees and his fingers off the levers. The hull did not care. She held.

Maren went to sleep on the bench in the bows. Saban went to sleep sitting against the rail with his hat over his face. Ward did not go to sleep. He sat on the cabin roof with his back to the mast, looking out at the dark country all round, and his note in Emeric's sense was a wall facing every way at once.

The cabin was the women's.



They called him in at ten.

He had been at the rail with his arms on it, looking down at the water meadows in the starlight, the channels shining and the willows black, and not thinking, or not about anything he could have named. The cabin door opened behind him a hand's width and Ferra said his name, once, quietly, and shut it again.

He went in.

There was one lamp, turned low, hung from the beam, and the shutter over the skylight, and the two narrow bunks, one above the other, and Abloh's long box open on the floor between them. Imani was sitting on the upper bunk with her legs hanging over its edge and her back against the boards, still in her black, with the one gold rose over her heart and her sleeves pushed up past the elbow, as she had said in April they would be; and she had Maren's jar in her lap. And Ferra was standing between the bunks, in the black.

The whole of it. The high neck, and the gold piping down the front, and the lattice of gold diamonds over the bodice and the gold straps crossing it, pinned where they crossed with two small gold roses on pins; and the bare shoulders, and the banded sleeves, and the roses scattered down the skirt to the floor of the cabin, which was too small for it, so that the hem lay over the toes of his boots when he shut the door.

"You never looked at it," she said.

"I looked at it all morning."

"You looked at it for a breath on the hull at ten and then you were walking up a hall in front of two hundred people and then you were being stabbed." She did not move. "Vantic and I spent two hours on the sleeves. Nobody looked at them properly but three duchesses and a cardinal's niece." She lifted her chin. "I want you to look at it. Here. Without them."

"I've been told to stay," said Imani, from above. "In case you need a physician."

"Term two," said Ferra, not looking up at her. "Nobody has a thing the other hasn't."

"I was there when you wrote it." Imani took the cork out of the jar. "Look at her dress. I'll do your neck."

So he looked at it, with Imani's cold fingers going in under his collar from above and behind, at the back of his neck, where the lace had been all day.

He took his time. Ferra let him. She stood in the lamplight between the bunks with her hands at her sides and her chin up and the colour coming slowly up from the high gold collar into her face, freckle by freckle, and did not look away from him once; and he looked at the sleeves, both bands sitting exactly level, and at the lattice over the bodice, and at the place at her collarbone where the black stopped and the pale skin began, and at the roses, every one, all the way to the hem lying across his boots.

"They're good embroidery," he said.

"They're very good embroidery." Her voice was not quite even. "I chose them out of fourteen."

"My father told me something about them."

"I know. We saw your face in the carriage." She was quiet. "You don't have to tell us tonight."

Imani's fingers had stopped on his neck.

"His mother," said Emeric, and stopped, and started again. "My mother. Wore them. At her wedding. On black. She chose them because the embroiderers said they took the gold well and read from the back of a hall."

Ferra stood very still. Above him he heard Imani put the cork back in the jar, slowly, as if the sound might break something.

Then Ferra put her hand up to the place where the gold straps crossed on her breast, and took hold of the first of the small rose pins, and drew it out, and held it in her palm and looked at it.

"Then they're yours," she said. "The duchy's. Not mine. The rose. On the seal, and the gate, and the flag on the hull." She closed her hand on it. "I'll draw it for the seal cutter at home. From this." She looked up at him. "I'll not have them think I chose your mother's flower to be clever. I chose it because it was good embroidery." Her mouth moved. "Which is a much better reason."

He crossed the cabin, which was one step, over the hem of her skirt, and kissed her.

She kissed him back at once, with the pin still closed in her fist against his chest, and then not only at once; she put her other hand up into his hair, hard, and pulled his head down, and kissed him as she had kissed him in Sethe's chair in August and on the slip and in the orchard, all of it at once, as if she had been saving it in a column since the morning and was entering the total. He felt the dress under his hands at her waist, the stiff silk and the lattice, and her warm through it; and he lifted her, without meaning to, a hand's breadth off the cabin floor, the gravity coming up in him on its own, as it always did with her, and the glyph on the back of his hand lit the cabin pale for a breath through her hair.

"Not on the ceiling," said Ferra against his mouth. "There's a beam."

"There's a physician," said Imani.

He put Ferra down. She laughed, the laugh from the exchange with the ink, and pulled him in again by the front of his shirt; and Imani, above them, leaned down off the edge of the bunk and took his face in her two hands from behind and turned it up and kissed him upside down, briefly, on the mouth, with her hair coming down round both their faces, and let go, and sat back.

"That's mine," she said. "For the hall. Go on."

Ferra had the second pin out somehow, he did not see how; it went somewhere into the bedding of the lower bunk, and the gold strap it had held came loose at her breast and slid off her shoulder, and she did not put it back. She went for the third and could not find it. She had to look for it, frowning, with her chin tucked, her fingers going over the lattice of the bodice, while he kissed the bare shoulder the strap had come off and the place at the side of her neck below her ear where the hair was pinned up and bare; and she said, "Where is it, there were three, I put in three," in a voice that was not her rent-roll voice at all, and he said, "Two," and she said, "Three," and found it, at the back, where she could not reach it, and turned round in his arms and said, "Get it out."

He tried. His hands were not as steady as they had been on the knife at the feast, and the pin was in under the strap at an angle Abloh must have chosen out of spite.

Imani leaned down off the bunk again and got it out with two fingers in one movement, and put it in his palm.

"Three," she said.

Ferra turned round again and went for his collar, and found the lace there, still stiff, and had to fight it, and lost; it stood up between them under his jaw like a hedge, starched, scratching, and she pushed at it with both hands and it sprang back, and she said something to it that she would not have written down, and he laughed into her hair, and she said, "Don't laugh, it's your collar," and got the first button of the shirt under it undone, and the second, and put her mouth to his throat under the lace, where it was raw, and felt him flinch.

She stopped. She leaned back and looked. In the lamplight the skin of his neck, all round, under the collar, was red and broken in a line where the lace and the fur had sat on it all day.

"I put it on at ten," said Imani. "Nothing lasts under a fur collar for fourteen hours. I've just put it on again." She slid down off the bunk, which in that cabin meant that she was suddenly standing against both of them, the three of them in a space the size of a cupboard, with the lamp swinging very slightly on its hook over their heads. She put her hand flat on Emeric's back, between the shoulders, through the shirt. "He's warm," she said to Ferra. "He's always warm. It's the hybrid. It's indecent."

Ferra laughed again, shakily, with her forehead against his chest; and he had one arm round her and the other came round Imani of its own accord, and pulled her in, and she came; and for a while there was nothing in the cabin but the three of them, and the lamp, and hands, and breath, and the stiff silk, and Imani's mouth at the side of his neck on the place that was not raw, and Ferra's fingers on the third button of his shirt.

And stopped there.

He felt it. He felt Ferra decide, the way a correction goes into her book, not quickly, all the way down; and he felt Imani feel it too, through him, because Imani's hand on his back went still at the same moment, and stayed where it was.

"Not here," said Ferra.

She lifted her head and looked at him. Her face was flushed to the hair and her mouth was red and she was perfectly serious.

"No," said Imani. "Not here." She was looking at Ferra, not at him. "Not for hers."

"Not in a cupboard on Saban's hull," said Ferra, "at a thousand feet, with Ward on the roof and an old man ten feet aft pretending to be asleep." She took her hands off his shirt and put them flat on his chest instead, over the place where the steel had been sewn in, under her palms. "I said mine was the bigger occasion. I meant it. This is a very good dress and a very bad bunk." Her fingers pressed. "Even if you did look at it very well."

"Hers was under glass with the stars going round," said Imani. "Yours isn't going to be in a bunk you can't turn over in." She took her hand off his back. "Nobody's first. Nobody gets the cupboard either."

"Yes," said Emeric.

He meant it. It came out of him at once, before either of them had finished, and he found he did not have to look for it anywhere; it was simply there, and true, and had been the whole time under everything else.

Ferra looked at him a breath longer, to see that he meant it, and saw that he did. Then she let out the rest of her breath, and put her forehead down against his chest, between her two hands, and stayed there.

"Good," she said into his shirt. "I'd have hated to argue. I'd have lost."

"You'd have lost to me," said Imani. "Not him."



Ferra slept in the dress.

She would not take it off, and could not, she said, without Vantic, who had pinned her into it at ten that morning in a way that needed two people and a pair of scissors to undo, and the scissors were Imani's and Imani would not lend them for this; so she lay down on the lower bunk in it, roses and all, with the loose strap tucked in, and pulled him down beside her by the wrist, and the bunk was not made for two people and certainly not for two people and a skirt, and they lay there wound together in black silk and gold thread and his shirt with three buttons undone, his back against the cabin's boards and her head on his shoulder and one of her knees over his, and she was asleep inside a quarter of an hour.

Imani went up into the bunk above, in her black, on her front, and put one arm down over its edge in the dark to find his pulse at the side of his neck, she said; and found it; and counted, under her breath, and lost count somewhere past forty, and did not take her hand back. It lay there on his shoulder, open, warm, and after a while it was heavy, and she was asleep too.

He did not sleep.

He lay in the dark, with the lamp out and the shutter closed and the hull holding still under them at a thousand feet over the water meadows, and their weight on him, all of it: Ferra all down the length of his side through the silk, her breath against his throat, slow and even, on the raw place, which he did not mind; her hand open on his chest; her hair, which had come down at last, every pin of it, across his mouth, so that he could have moved it and did not. Imani's hand on his shoulder from above, gone slack, two fingers still at the side of his neck where she had been counting. He could feel every rose on the skirt against his leg through the cloth, small and hard and raised, a hundred of them.

He could have woken them. He knew it, lying there, quite precisely, as he knew where every stone in the hull was. He did not. He lay there and wanted to with the whole of himself and did not, and the only thing in the cabin stopping him was himself, and he found, lying there, that he was quite glad of it, and that being glad of it did not help at all.

And his mind would not stop. It went round and round and would not lie down. A small room with nobody at the door. A cell, and a man smiling. A hall full of notes leaning one way. A grey chip of gravel on his brother's palm. A young caster with his cap in his hands. A hundred Gold, and a hundred and eighty-eight. The roses on a guard under his father's thumb.

And then, somewhere after two, with Ferra breathing against his throat and Imani's fingers gone still on his neck, it was none of those. It was Bram.

Bram's face, in the forge, over his spectacles, filing a petal on the sword's guard. You'll not take it out because it's hot. Bram, who had stood up at the long table four years ago when his daughter said yes to him in front of everyone, and said "Your mother," and sat down. Bram, who was going to be standing on the field when the hull came down at home, in his leather apron, with his arms folded, looking at his daughter coming off it in grey with her book under her arm, and at Emeric beside her.

He found that he was thinking about Bram's face, and not his father's, and that he could not for the life of him have said why.



In the morning Old Edrin sat in the casting seat with his blanket round his shoulders and his hands on the brass plate and looked straight ahead over the bows at the country, very carefully, and did not once look aft.

He did not look aft when the cabin door opened. He did not look aft when the Chancellor came out of it in grey, with her book under one arm and every pin back in her hair and her face rested and composed and pink; nor when the physician came out behind her with her hair in a ward knot and looked at the morning as if it had disappointed her personally. He did not look aft when the Duke of Kaldo came out behind her in yesterday's shirt with the collar open and his neck raw and his face the colour of a man who has not slept at all, which was true, and sat down on the cabin step and put his head in his hands. He kept his eyes on the horizon with an expression of enormous and settled delicacy, the face of an old man who has seen a great deal in seventy-one years and intends to see a great deal less of this; and Emeric, looking at him, understood exactly what Old Edrin thought he was not looking at, and that it was not what had happened, and that there was nothing in the world he could say about it.

Maren came up from the bows with a cup of tea, and looked at Ferra, and looked at Emeric, and put the cup down beside him on the step very loudly.

"Well," she said, "two of you slept."

Ferra opened her book.

Ward was on the cabin roof where he had been all night, with his back to the mast. He had not moved, so far as Emeric could tell, since ten. He looked out at the morning over the water meadows, the mist lying white in the channels and the willows standing up out of it, and the sun coming up red behind them; and then he said, to nobody in particular, in his flattest voice, to the country:

"Is she trimmed right? She feels heavy aft."

Nobody answered him. Saban, against the rail with his hat over his face, made a sound under the hat that might have been a man waking.

Old Edrin, at the casting seat, did not look aft.



Four days home. One night aloft and three landings, once they were a day out from the city and Ward allowed it: a posting house's field, a manor's park where the steward came out with a basket, and the last, on the evening of the first of June, a farm on the edge of Kaldo's own barony, where the farmer's wife knew who they were and had the whole hamlet out along the hedge.

Ferra drew the rose on the second day. She sat on the cabin roof with her board on her knees and the small gold pin from the dress on the board in front of her, and drew it four times in pencil, and once in ink, and then once more in ink inside a circle, for a seal, the size of a coin. She did not show it to him until she was done. Then she held it out.

It was a single rose, open, with two leaves, plain, in a ring. Nothing else on it at all. No crown, no letters, no lion.

"It reads from the back of a hall," said Ferra.

He looked at it a long while.

"Yes," he said.



They came over the last ridge at noon on the second of June, low, with Old Edrin dying beautifully in the casting seat for the benefit of the whole of Kaldo's north road, and the town came up out of the haze ahead of them, the wall and the keep and the river, and the works on the far bank with its chimneys.

Emeric saw the sign from a mile out, because he was looking for nothing in particular and the sun was on it.

It was on the old wool store, below the keep on the river side, the long low stone building where the families who came for the works' wage had slept four to a room in lofts and sheds before the terraces took them, and which had stood empty since the last of them moved out. It was not empty now. There were carts at its doors, and men going in and out with rolls of paper and long poles, and a new board along the front of it, above the doors, white, with black letters on it a yard high that he could read from the air.

The Works Office.

He had not set it up. He had not asked for it. Bram's department had done it while he was in the capital, because the pipes and the plates and the bins and the kiln and the steam and the survey all wanted somewhere to keep their drawings and their men and their stores and their cards, and there was a long empty building below the keep with nobody in it; and somebody had painted a board.

"Bram," said Ferra, beside him at the rail, reading it. "That's Bram's lettering." She looked at it a while longer. "He's done the W wrong. He always does the W wrong."



Bram was on the field below the north gate when they came down, in his leather apron, with his arms folded, exactly as Emeric had seen him at two in the morning over the water meadows.

He did not come to the gangway. He waited at the edge of the ring of Ward's men with the ropes until Old Edrin had been lifted out of the casting seat, for form's sake, and walked toward the gate between two of the garrison with his feet doing the minimum and his eyes, Emeric saw, quite bright; and until his daughter had come down the gangway in grey with her book under her arm and stopped in front of him and said, "Father," and he had said, "Ferra," and looked at her a breath longer than he looked at anything that was not iron; and until Imani had come down behind her with her bag, and nodded to him, and gone straight past toward the gate and the hill, because there was a hospital at the top of it and she had been away from it for ten days; and until the Duke of Kaldo had come down behind them, in a plain coat, with his collar turned up.

Then Bram crossed the grass to him and held out his hand, palm up.

Emeric understood after a breath what he wanted. He unbuttoned the plain coat and took out of its inside pocket the thing he had carried there since Imani had unpicked it from the waistcoat on the first night out with her scissors: a curved grey scale of steel the size of a palm, with a bright scratch across it from one side to the other.

Bram took it and turned it to the light and looked at the scratch with the same deep personal offence he had given the score in his anvil.

"Did it hold?" he said.

"It held."

Bram nodded once and put the scale in the pocket of his apron. "Then I'll not make another," he said. He looked at Emeric over his spectacles. "I'll make three. One for every coat you've got." And he turned and went back up the field after his daughter, with his hands behind his back, and did not look round.



The letter was on his desk in the study, on top of the post, where Ferra's clerk had put it because it was the only one marked urgent.

It was from Bryda, at Grayrock, in her big square hand that went straight across the page without a margin, and it was dated the twenty-sixth of May, the Tuesday, while he had been sitting in the bows of a boat at the end of the lowest tier watching a black carriage come back up a hill.

Fennick had gone down the old copper workings on the first of May with his licensed powder in its book and Bryda's two best crews, to open the deep level below the last gallery, which the old baron's ore book showed as worked out forty years ago and flooded since. He had pumped it dry with the hill engine in a fortnight. He had put in his first shots on the eighteenth, small, Bryda said, the size of a man's fist, entered in the book by the ounce, and laughed before every one, which had frightened her crews more than the powder; and on the twenty-first the third shot had opened a face that was not in the old baron's ore book or any book.

Copper. A lode of it. Green and black in the face, a man's height and more, running down into the dark below the old workings as far as the lamps would show, and the samples on the assay table at the pit office richer than anything Grayrock had sent down the river in Bryda's life or her husband's. She had had them assayed twice. Then a third time, because she did not believe the second.

She had put a number at the bottom, underlined, as she put figures she did not trust: if the water could be kept out of it and the crews could be got, and the hill engine could be given a second to help it, it would run to a thousand tons a year inside two years. She did not know. She was guessing. She had never guessed at a number that size in her life.

And under that, in a hand that had pressed harder, so that the pen had gone through the paper at the end:

The crown will want this hill back when it hears. Tell Ferra before you tell anybody else.

Emeric sat in his study chair with the letter in his hand, and the window open on the town, and the sound of the works coming across the river, and the white board on the old wool store below the keep, and Ferra's clerk somewhere on the stair, and a raw neck, and very little sleep since Thursday.

Then he got up, and went to find Ferra.
