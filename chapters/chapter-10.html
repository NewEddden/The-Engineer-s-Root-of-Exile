Chapter 10 - The Shop, the Cousin and the Ground

The shop was two rooms off the lower market, with a board over the door that said SOAP and nothing else.

There was no name on it, and no money in it that could be traced to the prince. That was deliberate. A soap shop belonging to the crown's disgraced son would be a curiosity at best and a target at worst. A soap shop belonging to nobody was a soap shop.

Abloh made the soap in the yard. The women from the north well wrapped it, in squares of cheap cloth tied with string, and were paid every evening in coppers counted out into their hands. Somebody had to stand at the counter and take the money.

Imani gave the counter to Sela. It took her about four seconds. She walked in, looked at the room, looked at Sela, who'd come in with a basket of wrapped bars and was setting them out in rows straighter than anyone had asked her to, and said, "You. The counter." Sela put the basket down and went behind it, and that was that.

Emeric came in the first afternoon to see how it was going.

Sela looked at him for the first time since the day she'd left her buckets in the lane. She looked at his eyes, and then at the rows of soap, and then back at him.

"Two coppers," she said, at the volume of a market. There were four other people in the shop. "Same as everybody."

He paid it. He paid it every time after that, at the counter, in front of whoever was there, and never once mentioned to anyone whose shop it was.



He hadn't been to see Petram.

It had been ten weeks. He'd asked after him every few days, through Declan, who looked in on the door most evenings on his way back from the well sites and brought back reports: Petram was eating, Petram was sleeping, Petram was saying nothing. Emeric had found a reason every day not to go himself, and every reason had been a good one.

"That's not a plan," Ward said, one evening, when Emeric had given him the latest. Ward's arm had stopped being a subject weeks ago; the stitches were long out and the scar was clean. He flexed the hand while he said it. "That's waiting."

So he thought about what Petram had given him and what he hadn't. Petram had given them the money, the road and Garrin, and then stopped. And the one thing Petram had said that wasn't about the plot was about his cousin. Corliss. In Kaldo. In the same quarter as Rylan's family.

He took Rylan and went to find him.

Corliss was in the garrison: a knight, though a poor barony's knight, in a coat that had been mended at both elbows. He was perhaps thirty, broad and quiet, with a soldier's short hair. When Emeric told him who was being held in the keep and asked if he'd be willing to see him, he said yes at once.

He said it too fast, and there was something in his face that Emeric read without effort. Relief. The relief of a man who has been waiting for a knock at the door for a long time, and is glad, in a way, that it has finally come.

Guilt, Emeric thought. He filed it.

There was one other thing, on the way out, that he couldn't place. Corliss's hands were blistered across the palms, the old, hard, healed-over blisters of a man who used a spade. A garrison knight had no reason to dig anything. And Corliss, as they left, looked at Emeric once more, like a man expecting to be asked a question.

Emeric didn't ask anything. Corliss didn't offer anything. They walked back up the hill.



He had them meet with the door open.

He'd thought about that. An open door is a record. Anyone could walk past and hear anything, and so nothing said in that room could later be claimed to have been something else. Declan stood in the corridor. Ward stood across from him. Emeric sat on a stool just inside the door, and Rylan leaned against the wall beside it.

Petram looked up when his cousin came in, and something happened to his face that Emeric hadn't seen in ten weeks.

He told Corliss everything. Plainly, without being asked twice, in the same flat voice he'd used on the road: the money, the stable yard, the road, Garrin, the tea. He didn't spare himself any of it.

Corliss listened to the end. Then he said quietly, "Did you give them my name? At the fire."

"Yes."

"Why."

Petram didn't answer.

There was no paymaster to give. He'd never met one, he said, and Emeric believed him. But when he'd finished, Petram asked a question of his own, and he asked it of Corliss, not of anyone else in the room.

"Has anybody come to your door? Since spring. Asking after me."

Corliss looked at him. "No," he said. "Nobody's come."

Petram sat back against the wall and let his hands fall open in his lap.

And Emeric understood, finally, what ten weeks of silence had been. Petram had given a real cousin's name at that fire because he'd expected nobody to live to repeat it, and then everybody had lived, and he'd sat in a locked room for ten weeks waiting to hear that someone had gone to Corliss's door.

"It worked," Ward said afterward, in the corridor, low. "Just not for what you built it for."

That night Emeric decided he'd start going to see Petram himself, with no questions, just to see him, like a man. Turning it over, he couldn't tell whether it was a decent thing to do or a useful one. It came to him, unpleasantly, that this was probably what it had been like to be his father, for fifteen years.



"There's a problem with all this," Rylan said, a few days later. "If it comes to a trial."

There was no magistrate in Kaldo. There hadn't been one for eleven years. The nearest sat at Wayford, three days up the road, and answered to the crown, and the crown had not answered a letter from Kaldo since before the plague.

Emeric added it to the list.

Then Doran came up to the keep.

He came himself, on foot, in his good cassock, and asked at the gate for the prince. Hale brought him up. On the stairs Hale mentioned, in a low voice, that he'd been in this house twenty years and had never once seen the priest set foot in it.

Doran didn't sit down. He stood in the middle of Emeric's room with his hands folded and spoke like a man who has rehearsed on the walk up and means to get through it before he loses his nerve.

The rows had to move. He'd known it since the night of the map. But they couldn't just be dug up, and they couldn't be moved by an order from the keep, because that ground was consecrated. It belonged to the church, not to the baron and not to the crown. It had to be done his way.

One family at a time. Not from the pulpit, never from the pulpit, because people would hear the graves are being turned out and there'd be blood on the street. He'd go to every house with someone in the rows and sit in their kitchen and ask them. It would take most of a season. And there was a place to move them to: a hollow outside the south-east wall, below every well in Kaldo and downstream of everything. He'd walked it himself.

It would cost lime, and cloth, and paid diggers, and a new consecration. And fees. The parish charged to bury and to pray, and always had.

Then he stopped. Emeric waited.

"There's something else," Doran said. "Since the map." He looked at the floor. "There are people in this town who die and can't pay. A dozen, some years. More, in bad ones. The parish doesn't bury them. There's no entry in the book, because nobody pays for one. And there's no rite, because the rite has a fee too." His hands tightened on each other. "Somebody's been burying them. For years. Somebody buys a box, and somebody digs, at night, and in the morning there's fresh earth and no name. I've never asked who." He made himself look up. "I've never asked."

Emeric wrote it down. He had the notebook open on the table and he wrote it sideways in the margin, beside the costs, the way you'd note something you meant to come back to.

He did not look at that page again for a year.

"Would they have died anyway?" Doran said. "The ones in the rows. If I'd put them somewhere else. Would they have died anyway?"

It was a real question, and Emeric knew the real answer, which was no. Most of them wouldn't have.

He looked at the priest standing in his room in his good cassock.

"You buried four hundred people in eighteen months," he said. "On your own. Nobody alive could have known. Nobody did know, anywhere, not in the capital, not in the Circle." He paused. "You did what there was to do."

Doran was silent. Then he nodded once, and went out, and Emeric heard him on the stairs, going down slowly.



That night he sat at the desk with the stone in his hand.

He'd stopped noticing he held it. It lived in his pocket in the day and on the desk at night, and his fingers went to it the way other men's went to a beard or a ring. He'd carried it every day as a small boy; he knew that as he knew other things from before the wall. Then fifteen years on a shelf. Then three months, every day, without thinking.

There was a dream that had started coming back, these last weeks. His mother's hands over his, small. A slate. And under it, lately, something else, something with walls, that felt less and less like a dream and more like a place on the far side of the wall that he'd been to once and forgotten.

The slate was on the desk beside the stone. Three months of dry stylus lines on it, rubbed out and redrawn, rubbed out and redrawn. The newest ones were finally straight.

That morning, at the dawn drill, something odd had happened. Ward had come at him from the left, the same feint he'd used fifty times, and Emeric's guard had come up to meet it before he'd decided to raise it. Faster than three months of losing should have allowed. Ward had stepped back and looked at him.

Neither of them had said a word. Ward had come at him again, and he'd lost the way he always lost, and they'd gone in to breakfast.

He turned the stone over in his fingers, and put it down, and went to bed.
