Chapter 10 - The Crown's Judgment

The judiciary chamber wasn't large. Emeric had expected spectacle, the sort of room a kingdom builds to remind people what power looks like from underneath. What he got was close and plain. A long table, hard chairs, windows set too high to see out of. Built for work.

That told him something about Keller before the man said a word. Older than he'd expected, grey at the temples, papers already squared in front of him like he'd read every page twice before anybody arrived. He didn't stand when they came in. He didn't need to.

"Prince Emeric." A nod, nothing else. "Baron Wexler."

Wexler sat across the table, composed in a way that was visibly costing him. Cold and measured, the same as he'd been in Kaldo, except the wariness had a new edge on it, a man running his own odds and liking the answer less each time he ran them.

Petram was three streets away in the judiciary's own holding, where Emeric had put him the hour they arrived. Keller never called for him and Emeric never offered. A confessed conspirator's word against a baron is worth nothing at all, and worse than nothing, because it hands the other side a coerced witness to talk about instead of a ledger.

"I petitioned this hearing," Wexler said, "because rumor becomes record if it's left unanswered. I'd rather answer it directly."

"Then answer it directly," Keller said. "What rumor?"

"That I've misused the barony's debt records for personal gain. I haven't. I'd like that stated plainly, for the record, before anything else gets attached to my name that doesn't belong there."

Keller didn't respond to that at all. He turned to Emeric instead. "You have evidence or you don't. I'm not interested in which of you says it louder."

Ferra stepped forward before Emeric could answer for her, which he preferred. She had ridden in the night before, ahead of nothing but her own courier, and slept about as much as the horse had. This was hers to carry.

"Three ledger pages out of the barony's own accounts. Scraped and rewritten." She laid them flat and turned them so Keller didn't have to. "The original entries recorded a debt discharge routed through a waypoint marker, not a merchant house. That marker belongs to a dispatch route, and the route belongs to a man called Cobb."

She set a second paper beside the first, one that hadn't existed two days earlier, still creased from hard travel.

"This is the rest. Cobb's payment records, cross-referenced against the coin marks in the barony's own remittance log. The coin that discharged those debts came out of Baron Wexler's treasury under his own seal. Not laundered through a house. Direct."

The room went quiet in the specific way rooms go quiet when something has just become harder to argue with.

"Timed," Ferra added, "to within a week of the ambush on the road into Kaldo."

Wexler's composure didn't break. It stopped moving, which was worse.

Keller studied the pages longer than felt necessary, reading them twice the way a man reads something not because he doubts it but because he wants to be certain how bad it is.

"Baron. Explain the seal."

"I don't control every payment that leaves my treasury down to the coin."

"You control the seal." Keller didn't raise his voice. "That is what a seal is for. Try again."

"Then the pages are forged. His Highness has had my ledgers in his possession for weeks with no oversight of any kind, and he arrived in my barony carrying a grievance."

"The pages are yours," Ferra said. "Both hands are yours. The one underneath and the one that came back over it."

"And the grievance." Keller turned. "Your Highness."

"I have one." Emeric didn't dress it up. "I'd have brought this whether or not I did. You don't need my grievance and you don't need my word. You have the barony's own accounting disagreeing with itself in the baron's own hand, and a coin trail ending in his treasury. Rule on that. Not on me."

Keller looked at him a moment longer than the answer required, then went back to the pages.

Wexler tried the forgery line twice more from different angles. It didn't hold any better the second time or the third, and Emeric watched something happen that he hadn't expected to enjoy and briefly did anyway: a man's story failing to survive contact with a process that didn't care whose title was in the room. Wexler had run to the crown believing procedure would shelter him. Procedure was the thing taking him apart.

He noticed himself enjoying it and put it down, because he had a fair idea what the enjoyment was made of and it wasn't justice.

Keller set the pages down.

"I have what I need." He looked at Wexler without anything that could be called sympathy or its opposite. "The judiciary finds sufficient evidence of treasonous conspiracy, specifically the financing of an attempt on the life of a prince of the blood. Sentence is death, to be carried out by crown order. Not baronial discretion. Not personal grievance."

A glance at Emeric, brief, close to a warning.

"This is the crown's judgment. Not yours. Remember the difference."

"I will," Emeric said, and meant it more than Keller probably expected.

He had wanted to do it himself. He had carried that want for three months without examining it, and standing in a small room watching a man's face stop moving, he examined it, and did not care for what it was made of. It wasn't for Tam. Tam wasn't in the ruling anywhere. It was for a night at a campfire when a knife had gone toward a friend's back and he had seen the whole thing and sat perfectly still, and no amount of anybody else's blood was ever going to be a payment against that.

The choice had been taken off him. He found, somewhat to his own disgust, that he was relieved.

Men came for the baron, the sort who did this kind of work without needing to feel anything about it, and the chamber began emptying the way rooms empty once the actual work is finished.

At the door Wexler stopped, and turned, and spoke only to Emeric.

"You'll want to know why."

Emeric said nothing, which was the same as yes.

"Nine years ago I rode eleven days to this city because my town was dying and I had exhausted every other thing a man in my position can do. I stood in a corridor in this building for two days. On the third a clerk came out and told me the matter had been noted."

His voice didn't rise. It hadn't risen once all morning.

"I came home and I stopped writing letters, because a man who keeps asking is a man who has agreed to keep being told no. I buried a third of them and I kept the rest breathing on what was left, and no one from this city ever came, and no one from this city ever asked."

"And then a rider arrived, and told me the crown was giving four generations of my family's work to its embarrassment, to get him out of the capital." A pause, exact. "So I paid for it. I want that plain. I'm not asking you to think better of me."

"The debt cleared the same week as the road," Emeric said. It came out level. "Before I ever reached Kaldo."

"Yes."

"So you never saw me. You paid for what you were told was coming."

"I paid for a drunk." Wexler looked at him a moment. "And I have spent four months watching what actually got off that carriage. You have done more for that town since the harvest than I managed in twenty years, and I have had to sit at my own table and know both of those things at once."

He straightened his coat, which did not need straightening.

"That is not a defense. Keller's ruling is correct and I'd have made the same one. It's only the thing I'd rather you carried than the version you'd have built for yourself."

Then he went out with the men who had come for him, and Emeric stood in a small plain room built for work and did not move for some time.

He kept thinking about what Wexler had said.

Emeric had seen men like him back on Earth. People who had spent years working for a position, only to watch someone get it because of their family or who they knew. It had never been fair, but that did not make murder the answer.

Wexler had been wrong. Emeric knew that.

Still, he couldn't shake the feeling that if things had been different, if he had been the one watching his life's work get handed to someone else, he might have understood the anger.

Understanding it was not the same as forgiving him.

Ward found him before he left the capital. He didn't ask how it felt.

"Cobb's still out there."

"He is." Emeric filed the hearing next to the other things he'd stopped expecting to resolve cleanly. "That's a thread for later. Not this one."

Ward didn't press, and didn't leave either, and after a moment said the part that had actually brought him.

"Wexler kept a garrison. Whatever else he was, he paid armed men to answer to him, and now there's no him for them to answer to."

Emeric had known that in the abstract, the way he'd known the fields were his now. Hearing it out loud gave it a weight the abstract hadn't. A body of trained men, loyalty unknown, some of them almost certainly bought, all of them suddenly looking up the chain and finding him at the top of it.

"How many?"

"Enough to matter. Not enough to feel safe about." Ward's jaw did the thing it did when he was choosing not to say more than he could back. "I'd want to know which of them were soldiers and which were Wexler's friends before I put a blade in any of their hands and told them to stand between us and anything."

"After the farm," Emeric said, and heard how easily it came out, the way he set down every problem that couldn't feed the town this season. "The farm eats first. The garrison keeps."

Ward accepted that the way he accepted most of Emeric's ordering of the world, which was to say he filed his disagreement somewhere Emeric would find it later if he turned out to be wrong.

The mage tower hadn't changed. Emeric stood at the base of it longer than he meant to before he made himself go up.

Armand looked older than the memory, though the memory was fifteen years out of date and that was hardly fair to either of them. He came down when word reached him that a prince was asking at the gate, and whatever he had braced for, it took him a moment to square it with the man standing in the courtyard.

"Your Highness." Then, quieter, dropping the formality like it had never quite fit him. "Emeric."

"I read your notebook."

Armand's face did something complicated. "All of it?"

"Including the page at the back. Eleven tests. Nine years."

"Then you know I found nothing."

"I know you were looking correctly and stopped too soon, and I'm not going to explain that standing in a courtyard." Emeric heard himself do it, no greeting, no easing in, and didn't correct course. "I need you to see it. Not hear about it. Put your hands on it."

Armand's eyebrows went up a fraction, the specific look of a man who had spent decades being difficult to surprise.

"That isn't how you used to talk to me."

"No. It isn't."

Something moved behind the old man's expression. Old instinct recognizing itself. A teacher hearing a student describe something worth walking toward rather than something he'd been assigned.

"I have obligations here," Armand said. "The tower doesn't run itself, and I've spent longer building what runs it than I've got left to spend rebuilding it if I walk away carelessly."

"That's not a no."

"It isn't." His mouth moved, not quite a smile. Closer to a man recalculating a decision he had already half made. "Settle what needs settling there first. Then, best believe I'll come."

Emeric nodded once and didn't push. He had learned that much, at least. Some things go worse the harder you press them.

He looked up one more time before he left. The floating gallery, the same one he'd watched them pass hand to hand as a boy, ten men holding it aloft on nothing but discipline and cost, and ten more behind them waiting to step into the gap.

Three nights ago, in a cold room, he had found a thing that held its shape with nobody watching it.

He stood in the courtyard doing arithmetic he had no business being able to do and arrived, without wanting to, at the fact that every one of those men was standing there because a whole order of mages had never once considered that a stone might be able to remember something on its own.

He didn't say so. Some things you let a man arrive at himself.

They rode back to Kaldo without much said. When they reached it, it was a barony without a baron for the first time in longer than anyone there could remember. The fields. The unassigned labor. The administrative knots that had existed for no reason except that Wexler had wanted them to, and which Emeric now understood had been a man building handholds into a wall he expected to have to climb. The garrison in the lower ward, drilling out of habit because nobody had told them to stop and nobody had told them why they should keep going.

All of it sitting open, waiting on somebody to decide what came next.

Word came three days later, secondhand, the way most executions reach most people who aren't standing close enough to make it personal. A line in a crown dispatch. Sentence carried out. Nothing more.

He read it twice anyway. Habit now.

It should have felt like something closing. It didn't.

The coin trail and the route and the timing had all held up exactly the way Ferra built them to hold up. Clean, provable, undeniable even to a man as procedurally cold as Keller. Emeric had done everything right and the system had worked precisely as designed.

Tam's name was still sitting where he'd left it, and no ruling anywhere had touched it. Nobody had asked about the guard on the duty roster. Nobody could prove Wexler had ordered that specifically, only that he had paid for the attempt in general, and general was enough for a treason charge and nowhere near enough for the thing Emeric had actually wanted answered.

Which left the possibility he had been walking around with for three days and not putting into words, which was that the hand that reached Tam might not have been Wexler's at all, and might still be in his castle, and might have watched him ride out and ride back.

And underneath that, refusing to settle, a man who had ridden eleven days for a dying town and been told the matter was noted. Who had come home and buried a third of them. Who had paid to have a stranger killed on a road and had been entirely correct about the stranger and entirely wrong about the man, and who had said so, at a door, on his way to be executed for it.

Both of those were true. Emeric could not get them to sit in the same column and could not make either one go away, and he suspected he was going to be carrying them side by side for a long time.

He had gotten a win. He turned the word over the way he turned the stone over, without fully trusting either of them anymore.

Somewhere on the north end of town was a woman with two sons in the ground and a purse she hadn't asked for, and he was going to have to live in the same barony as her for however long this took.

He already knew what came next. He just hadn't let himself think about the farm until now, because thinking about the farm meant admitting the other thing was as finished as it was ever going to get.

And under the farm, quieter, the thing he'd promised Ward he'd get to. A garrison of men he did not yet know how to trust, sitting in his lower ward with nothing to do but wait and find out what kind of lord had inherited them.
