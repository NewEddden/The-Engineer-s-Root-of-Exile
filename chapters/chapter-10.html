Chapter 10 - The Crown's Judgment

The judiciary chamber wasn't large. Emeric had expected spectacle, the kind of room a kingdom builds to remind people what power looks like from below. What he got was close and plain. A long table, hard chairs, windows set too high to see out of. Built for work.

That told him something about Keller before the man said a word. Keller was older than he'd expected, grey at the temples, a stack of papers already squared in front of him like he'd read every page twice before anyone arrived. He didn't stand when they came in. He didn't need to.

"Prince Emeric." A nod, nothing else. "Baron Wexler."

Wexler sat across the table, composed in a way that was visibly costing him. Cold and careful, same as he had been in Kaldo, but with a new edge on it.

"I petitioned this hearing," Wexler said, "because rumor has a way of becoming record if it's left unanswered. I'd rather answer it directly."

"Then answer it directly," Keller said. "What rumor?"

"That I've misused the barony's debt records for personal gain." His voice stayed even. "I haven't. I'd like that stated plainly, for the record, before anything else gets attached to my name that doesn't belong there."

Keller didn't respond to that at all. He turned to Emeric instead. "You have evidence, or you don't. I'm not interested in which of you says it louder."

Ferra stepped forward before Emeric could answer for her, which he preferred. She'd ridden in the night before, ahead of nothing but her own courier, and slept about as much as the horse had. This was hers to carry.

"Three ledger pages out of the barony's own accounts. Scraped and rewritten." She laid them flat and turned them so Keller didn't have to. "The original entries recorded a debt discharge routed through a waypoint marker, not a merchant house. The marker belongs to a dispatch route. A courier named Cobb runs it."

Keller looked up. "And?"

"And that's the end of what the paper says." She didn't soften it. "I can prove the pages were altered. I can show you what was underneath. I can't tell you whose hand did the scraping and I can't put a coin from that treasury into Cobb's hand. If you want me to say more than that, I'll be making it up."

Something in Keller's expression moved for the first time. Not warmth. The brief attention of a man who has spent thirty years listening to people overstate their evidence and has just heard somebody decline to.

"Timing?" he said.

"The discharge is dated to within a week of the ambush on the road into Kaldo." Ferra set the page down. "That's a coincidence. I'd want more than a coincidence and I don't have it."

"Then we agree on something," Wexler said, and Emeric felt the room tilt half an inch.

"Those pages have been in His Highness's possession for over a month, with no oversight of any kind, in a castle where his own men control the muniment room. He arrived in my barony carrying a grievance he has never once troubled to hide. I'm told there are two hands on the page. I'd ask how anyone in this chamber proposes to establish which of them arrived first."

"The ink," Ferra said.

"The ink tells you one entry is older than another. It doesn't tell you it's older than my ledger leaving my hands."

And there it was. Emeric heard it land and knew immediately that it worked, because it did work, because Wexler had found the one seam in the whole thing and put his thumb on it in front of the only man whose opinion mattered.

He had spent a month making the ledgers say something. It had not once occurred to him that a man could simply decline to accept the ledgers as evidence and stand there being reasonable about it.

Keller read the pages again, unhurried, and Emeric sat with his hands flat on his knees and made himself not speak, because everything he had was a version of I am telling you the truth and he already knew exactly how much that was worth in this room.

Keller set the pages down.

"Baron. A procedural question, and then I'll be finished with both of you."

"Of course."

"Your petition." Keller drew a single sheet out of his stack without looking for it. "You filed it on the fourth. In it you state that you wish to answer a rumor concerning the misuse of the barony's discharged debt records."

"I did."

"His Highness's inspection order is in front of me as well. It requests revenue and expenditure for two years. It names no category. It names no pile."

The room did not go quiet. It had already been quiet. It got worse.

"So," Keller said, in exactly the tone he had used for everything else, "when you petitioned this office, no one had accused you of anything. No one had told you which of your accounts had been looked at, because no one outside a castle you no longer had the run of knew. Explain to me how you arrived at discharged debts."

Wexler's composure didn't break. It stopped moving, which was worse.

"It's the obvious place a man would look."

"It is the fourth place a man would look, and I have watched enough of them look." Keller didn't raise his voice at any point. "Try again."

Wexler tried twice more, from different angles.

Emeric watched it happen and found he did not enjoy it as much as he had expected to. A man's story failing to survive contact with a process that didn't care whose title was in the room. Wexler had run to the crown believing that defining the ground first would let him choose the fight. The document he had written to protect himself was the only piece of paper in the chamber that nobody could claim had been tampered with, and it had his name at the bottom of it.

Keller squared his stack.

"I have what I need." He looked at Wexler without anything that could be called sympathy or its opposite. "The judiciary finds sufficient evidence of treasonous conspiracy, specifically the financing of an attempt on the life of a prince of the blood. Sentence is death, by crown order. Not baronial discretion. Not personal grievance."

A glance at Emeric, brief, close to a warning.

"This is the crown's judgment. Not yours. Remember the difference."

"I will," Emeric said, and meant it more than Keller probably expected.

He had wanted to be the one. Somewhere under the month of ledgers and the careful letters and the flat procedural voice he had practiced using on this man, he had wanted to stand in a room with Wexler and settle it himself, with his own hands and on his own terms. That had never been available and he had known it wasn't, and he was surprised at how much it cost to hear somebody else say so out loud.

He caught up with him in the corridor while the escort was still forming up, and he had one question and no time to be clever about asking it.

"The accounts. You told me I'd find them thin, and you told me to understand why before I decided what it meant. I never got the why."

Wexler looked at him for a long moment. There was nothing left for the man to protect. He had been sentenced eleven minutes ago, in front of witnesses, on paper, and every ordinary reason a man has for keeping his mouth shut had stopped applying to him.

He kept it shut anyway.

"You'll find it," he said at last. "You have the disease for it. You'll go on pulling until you get to the end of the thread, because you can't do otherwise, and it won't occur to you until much too late to ask what's on the other end holding it."

"Then tell me and save us both the walk."

"No." Something in his face that Emeric could not read at all, and would spend a considerable number of nights trying to. "I've a sister in Wayford, Your Highness. She's done nothing. I'd like her to go on doing nothing."

Then the escort had him, and that was the whole of it. Men who did this sort of work without needing to feel anything about it, and a chamber emptying the way rooms empty once the actual business is finished, everyone already thinking about the next thing.

Ward found him before he left the capital. He didn't ask how it felt.

"Cobb's still out there."

"He is." Emeric put the hearing away, filed next to the other things he'd stopped expecting to resolve cleanly. "That's a thread for later."

Ward didn't press. He didn't leave either, and after a moment he said the part that had actually brought him.

"Wexler kept knights. A garrison. Whatever else he was, he paid armed men to answer to him, and now there's no him for them to answer to."

Emeric had known that in the abstract, the way he'd known the fields were his problem now. Hearing it out loud gave it a weight the abstract hadn't. A body of trained men, loyalty unknown, some of them almost certainly bought, all of them suddenly looking up the chain and finding him at the top of it.

"How many?"

"Enough to matter. Not enough to feel safe about." Ward's jaw did the thing it did when he was choosing not to say more than he could back up. "I'd want to know which of them were soldiers and which of them were Wexler's friends before I put a blade in any of their hands and told them to stand between us and anything."

"After the farm." Emeric heard how easily he'd said it, the way he set down every problem that couldn't feed the town this season. "The farm eats first. The garrison keeps."

Ward accepted that the way he accepted most of Emeric's ordering of the world, which was to say he filed his disagreement somewhere Emeric would find it later if he turned out to be wrong.

The mage tower hadn't changed. Emeric stood at the base of it longer than he meant to, looking up at the same climb he half remembered, before he made himself go up.

Armand looked older than the memory. The memory was fifteen years out of date, so that was hardly fair to either of them. He came down when word reached him that a prince was asking at the gate, and whatever he'd braced for, it took him a moment to square it with the man standing in the courtyard.

"Your Highness." Then, quieter, dropping the formality like it had never quite fit him. "Emeric."

"I need you to see something."

No greeting, no easing into it. Emeric heard himself do it and didn't correct course.

"Not hear about it. See it. Better, put your hands on it."

Armand's eyebrows went up a fraction, the specific look of a man who'd spent decades being difficult to surprise. "That's not how you used to talk to me."

"I've made a discovery." He left it there on purpose. "Crystal work. The kind that changes what you think a crystal is, not just what you think it can do. I'm not going to explain it standing in a courtyard. You'd have to be there for it to mean anything."

Something moved behind Armand's expression. Old instinct recognizing itself. A teacher hearing a student describe something worth walking toward instead of something he'd been assigned.

"I have obligations here," Armand said. "The tower doesn't run itself, and I've spent longer building what runs it than I've got left to spend rebuilding it if I walk away carelessly."

"That's not a no."

"It isn't." His mouth moved, not quite a smile. Closer to a man recalculating a decision he had already half made. "Settle what needs settling there first. Then you'll have me."

Emeric nodded once and didn't push. He'd learned that much, at least. Some things go worse the harder you press them.

He looked up one more time before he left. The same floating gallery he'd watched them pass hand to hand as a boy, initiates still holding it aloft on nothing but discipline and cost. He knew now exactly what that discipline was worth and exactly how little of it the job actually required.

He didn't say so. Some things you let a man arrive at on his own.

They rode back to Kaldo without much said. When they reached it, it was a barony without a baron for the first time in longer than anyone there could remember. The fields. The unassigned labor. The administrative knots that had existed for no reason except that Wexler had wanted them to. The garrison, quartered in the lower ward, drilling out of habit because nobody had told them to stop and nobody had told them why they should keep going.

All of it open now, waiting on someone to decide what came next.

Word arrived three days later, secondhand, the way most executions probably reach most people who aren't standing close enough to make it personal. A line in a crown dispatch. Sentence carried out. Nothing more. He read it twice anyway. Habit now.

It should have felt like something closing. It didn't.

Everything had held. The pages, the route, the petition date. Clean, provable, undeniable even to a man as procedurally cold as Keller. Emeric had done it correctly and the system had worked exactly as designed, and he sat with the dispatch in front of him and counted what the correct outcome had actually bought.

Nobody had asked about the guard on the duty roster. Nobody could prove Wexler had ordered that particular thing, only that he had paid for an attempt in general, and general was enough for a treason charge and nowhere near enough for the question Emeric had actually wanted answered. Tam's name was still sitting exactly where he'd left it, and no ruling anywhere had come within a mile of it.

And the only man in the kingdom who could have explained why a barony this size ran that thin was three days dead, by a sentence Emeric had spent a month building the case for.

He got the ledgers back out that night and read the discharged debt pile again from the top, and it said the same thing it had said before, which was that somebody had been careful. He had killed the only person who knew who, and he had been thorough about it, and he had been right the whole way through.

He turned the word win over the way he turned the crystal over, without fully trusting either of them anymore.

Then he put the ledgers away, because the fields wouldn't wait on his mood, and under the fields, quieter, a garrison of men he did not yet know how to trust sat in his lower ward with nothing to do but wait and find out what kind of lord had inherited them.
