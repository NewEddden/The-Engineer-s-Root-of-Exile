Chapter 10 - Six Hours at the Forge

Bram saw him coming and did not stop working.

That was the opening position and Emeric respected it immediately. The man had a bracket in the fire and a prince in his doorway and had made a ranking on the spot.

Emeric came in, moved out of the light, and said nothing.

He did not ask for a chair. He did not send Rylan forward to speak for him. He did not, at any point in the next six hours, fill a single silence.

The first hour was easy, because the work was real.

The second hour was less easy, because it became apparent that the work had stopped being real somewhere in the middle of it. Bram finished the bracket. Then he looked at it, and put it back in the fire, and unmade it, and started it again.

By the third hour he had done it twice.

Somewhere in the fourth hour Emeric began, privately, to keep score.

It was not a fair contest. Bram had a hammer, a fire, and a legitimate reason to be standing where he was standing. Emeric had a wall to lean on, nothing whatsoever to do with his face, and the specific misery of a man who has committed to a position early and is now finding out what the position costs.

He passed the time taking the forge apart in his head. The bellows arrangement was sound. The tuyere sat too high. The water trough had been in the same spot long enough to wear a dish into the floor, and the floor sloped the wrong way, which meant that every time Bram quenched something a quantity of it went under his own bench and had been going under his own bench for years.

That one nearly finished him. He had to physically stop himself from saying it, twice, and the second time was harder than the first, and he learned something in that moment about his own character that he did not particularly enjoy.

An apprentice came in around the fifth hour, took one look at the pair of them, and left.

Emeric stood at his back with his hands behind him and understood that he was watching a man who would rather reforge the same piece of iron four times than have a conversation with a noble, and he respected that so enormously that he did not permit one muscle in his face to move.

Bram knew he had noticed. Emeric knew that Bram knew.

Neither of them acknowledged any part of it, and the fifth hour passed in a silence with a great deal going on inside it.

Bram cracked first, because nobody makes it six hours.

"Most of your sort would have sent a boy." He did not look up. "Or threatened me. Or got bored in a quarter of an hour and gone off to find someone who'd bow properly."

"I considered the boy."

"Mm."

His guard came down a finger's width. It was not an invitation. It was a door being unlocked and left shut.

Emeric was straight with him, because being straight was the entire tactic and he had been building to it for six hours.

He wanted the death records. The priest had them. He did not believe the priest would hand them over to him, and if he forced it, this town would never listen to another word he said as long as he lived in it.

So he needed Bram to walk him in and say that he was worth hearing.

He did not offer money. He did not mention his rank once in the whole of it.

Bram set the hammer down.

"I'm not vouching for you. Not yet." He wiped his hands on his apron without hurrying. "I'll walk you to the priest myself and tell him you're worth hearing out. What he does after that is his business. What this town makes of you after that is yours to earn, not mine to hand over."

"That's more than I came in with."

"It isn't the speech." Bram looked at him properly for the first time. "The speech was fine. Speeches are cheap and I've heard better from worse men. You stood at my back six hours and didn't open your mouth once while I had work in my hands. Nobles don't do that." He picked the hammer back up. "Don't make me regret the walk."

Emeric put his hand out and took a soot-black one without hesitating, and Bram marked that too.

Behind them, Rylan gave the smallest nod available to a man in uniform.

Coming out of the forge district they passed a woman carrying water.

She brightened at Rylan, honestly, the way you brighten at somebody's boy come home. Then she registered the noble beside him and her face closed like a hand.

She averted her eyes and turned and went, and dropped the bucket doing it, and did not stop to pick it up.

"Was that me?"

"Not you specifically." Rylan watched her go. "Sela. The crown's war took one of her sons and left her the other, and nobody ever told her what happened to the first one. Not where, not how, not whether there was a body." He shrugged, and there was no comfort anywhere in it. "You're the crown today. Whatever else you are on other days."

"That's not something I fix by being right about the water."

"No. That one you don't fix." Rylan picked the bucket up and set it upright against the wall for her. "You just don't make it worse."

Imani's door was the last one before the light went.

Low, and old, and there was a smell coming out of it that was not the town's smell. Vinegar, something bitter underneath, herbs hung to dry in a room that had no spare room to hang anything in.

She was twenty-five and holding a strip of boiled linen, and she looked at him the way the town had been looking at him for three days.

"Whatever it is, I'm out of it."

"I'm not sick."

"Then you're worse. Sick I can do something about."

He said he wanted to know what had killed the ones who died.

She said the priest kept a book.

He said the book only had the ones somebody had paid to bury.

He heard it about half a second after saying it, and what he heard was that it had also been a hole in his own knowledge, and that he had walked straight into it in front of a witness.

He had no idea what happened to a person in this kingdom whose family could not pay. None. Twenty-two years of memory and the drawer was empty, because nobody had ever thought it worth telling him.

What he could produce instantly, whole and unasked for, was the other end of it.

A noble of sufficient standing is encased in mana stone and kept, body and face intact, indefinitely. He knew the sizes. He knew what the good ones cost and what the difference bought. He could have named four houses that commissioned one in his lifetime and told you what the crystal had been cut from and which manasmith had done the setting.

The rich are kept forever in stone. The poor go into the ground uphill of the drinking water and are not written down at all.

That was the shape of what he had been raised to know, and it was the first time in his life he had looked straight at it.

She stood back from the door.

Her front room was the only orderly space he had seen in Kaldo, and the order had a tell in it. Everything hung where it could be reached one-handed without turning around. The room had been arranged by somebody who had once needed to work faster than she could think.

There was a second chair by the fire with no dent in the cushion, and a shelf above it holding six or seven objects that were plainly not medical and were just as plainly not hers.

He did not ask about the chair and he did not ask about the shelf.

"Past nine hundred," she said. "I stopped counting the winter it got past what I could carry."

The number in the priest's book was wrong. Wrong low.

She had held the post since she was nineteen. It had been Mother Sabel's before it was hers, and Sabel had been her mother, and that was the whole of what she said about it, delivered the way people in this town say everything about that year, which is quickly and once.

He did not ask a second question.

Her theory came out flat, and tested, and five years in the making.

"The ground. It comes up off the ground where the water sits. You'll see it in which houses go first, if you stand here long enough. The low ones. The wet ones. The ones out by the burial rows."

She was right about every house and wrong about every reason.

Something in his chest came loose that he would have called relief if he had been honest enough to name it. She had the map. She had had the map for five years. She had a bad name for the thing drawn on it and nobody in the world to correct it with.

He did not correct her.

He had been in this town three days and had not earned that conversation.

He asked, instead, whether anyone had ever come from the capital.

Her hands stopped for the first time since he had walked in.

Three letters. The first year. Sent through the castle, because that is the road it goes down and there is no other.

She did not know whether anybody had read them. She knew that nobody came.

The Circle keeps healers. She had never met one. She had met the idea of one.

Through the castle, he thought, and put it somewhere he could find it again, and let the subject go.

"Your big one," she said at the door. "Won't sit down. He walked past my door twice today and didn't knock."

"I'll send him again."

"Send him if it makes you feel better. He'll come when it goes red and not an hour before." She was already turning back to the linen. "Same for every one of you, in my experience."

Late, in a cold room in the keep, he opened Armand's notebook and started writing down everything wrong with Kaldo.

He started with water because water was the thing that would kill people this month.

By the time the candle was low he was on a second page under a heading he had written as a joke and then found he could not laugh at.

Latrines. Wells. The burial rows. The fuel it takes to boil anything at all in a town with no wood. The port. Fletcher and Abloh, somewhere downriver. Fifty-eight men in a garrison that answered to somebody else. A prisoner two doors down who had stopped talking. A baron he could not remove without burning his own house. A healer with nine hundred dead and no help coming. A sill dropping free power past a dead town every second of every day.

And underneath the whole of it, sitting there and refusing to be edited out, the thing that kept the list from being despair.

He was enjoying himself.

He put the pen down and sat with that for a while, because it deserved sitting with.

Nine hundred people were dead. A woman three streets away had carried the counting of them until the counting got past what she could carry. And he had spent the day walking through the consequences of all of it with a rising, unmistakable, entirely inappropriate interest, the way a man walks through a building with a fault in it and finds himself wanting the drawings.

He noticed that, and was disturbed by it, and wrote the next item down anyway.

He put the candle out and slept properly for the first time since the fire.

Two floors below him, a man who had run this barony for thirty years sat with a lamp still lit, doing arithmetic of his own.
