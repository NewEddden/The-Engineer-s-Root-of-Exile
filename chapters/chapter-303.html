Chapter 303 - Closed Circuit

The mill race below the works ran fastest at two in the morning, when the night shift at the furnace took the most power and the wheel took the most water, and that was when Declan and Rylan went into it.

They went in off the wheel house's step, where Bram had lowered the cement blocks nine years before to see which one the river would eat, and they went in without a sound, which was the point. No lamp on the bank. No hose going up to anybody. No bubbles. Emeric sat on the step with his boots in the water and his sense in the race, and felt the two notes go down into the black, slow and close, and settle against the race wall at the bottom of the pool, eleven feet down, where the water came off the wheel and hit the stone and turned back on itself; and stay there.

At twenty-six minutes Rylan's note came loose from the wall and went down the pool toward the sill, and Declan's caught it, and the two of them came up the wall hand over hand, and Rylan's head broke the water by the step with the mouthpiece in his teeth and the round bag on his chest gone flat against his plate.

"Lost my grip," he said. "The wall's got moss on it like a dog's back."

"You held twenty-six minutes," said Ward.

"I'd have held thirty." Rylan spat river. "I'll hold forty if he gives me something to hold on to."



It was called a rebreather. Emeric had said it in the drawing office at the end of May with the sheets on the board, and Ottoline had written it on the first sheet in her small upright engraver's hand, because she wrote the name on everything she made, and it had been the rebreather in the works ever since.

It did not look like much. That was part of what he liked about it. A bag of Isle rubber, round, the size of a cushion, worn on the chest under a canvas cover, with two short hoses coming up from it over the shoulders to a mouthpiece. A steel bottle the length of a forearm along the left ribs, where the open flasks had ridden at the Isle, but slimmer; and on the right side a brass can the size of a quart pot, with a screw lid and a wire basket inside it. That was all. A man breathed in from the bag and out into the bag, and his breath went round and round through the can and came back to him, and none of it ever left. Nothing went up to the surface to tell anybody he was there.

The bottle was the plant's oxygen. Tobe had been making it at the secret dock since the year of the gas torch, out of the same cold and pressure that turned the Band's mana into a blue pool in a flask: air squeezed and chilled until it ran, and the part of it a man breathed boiled off and caught. The welders had used it for three years. Imani's ward had used it since the spring before last, in a mask, for chests that would not fill. It was only a matter of how little a man needed, and Emeric had stood at Ottoline's bench with a watch and a pencil and Declan sitting on a stool with a clip on his nose, breathing into a bag, until he knew.

The can was the other half. A man did not drown in his own breath for want of air; he drowned in what he had already breathed out. So the breath went through a basket of white grains the size of lentils, and the grains drank the bad part of it and gave the rest back. Soda lime, he had called it: the kiln's lime, and salt-works lye, and two weeks of Merrin Sallis in the Motion Shop's back room in Imani's rubber gloves, swearing at the grains when they caked; and in the end it was Merrin, not Emeric, who found that a damp grain drank more than a dry one, and how damp, and wrote it on the tin.



On the second night in the race Emeric went down with them.

He had no rebreather. There were three sets, and two were for Declan and Rylan, and the third was in a crate in the drawing office with its can unfilled, and was not for the river. He went down on his breath in his shirt and drawers, as he had gone down in the Mere the night after his week, with his sense out ahead of him in the black because eyes were no use at the bottom of a race at two in the morning; and found them where they had been the night before, at the foot of the wall below the wheel's tail, eleven feet down, clinging to moss.

Emeric put his palm flat on the wall beside Rylan's hand.

He pulled. It was the thing he had learned at Maren's barrow and on the Mere's shore, and burned his palm learning, and after five years it came the way a breath comes. The heat came up out of the stone and the thin skin of water on it and into his hand; and then, because he was under eleven feet of moving river with more cold water round him than any man could ever warm, he did not have to look for anywhere to send it. Armand's rule, the old one: the heat must have somewhere to go that isn't you. Here it went everywhere. It went out of his other hand and off his skin and away down the race toward the sea before he had finished letting go of it, and he did not feel it go through his chest at all. Nothing ached. It was the first time in his life it had cost him nothing.

Under his palm the water on the stone went thick, and white, and hard.

He drew his hand back. On the race wall, where it had been, stood a knob of ice the size of a fist, frozen into the moss and the mortar's crack and the stone's own face, with its back to the current like a boulder in a stream. The water went round it. It did not move.

He felt Rylan's hand find it in the dark, and close.

He did eleven more, along the foot of the wall a yard apart at a crouching man's hands, and six on the sill where Rylan had come off. When he came up the last time his hands were not even pink.

Ward was on the step with his watch.

"Well?"

"They'll hold an hour," said Emeric, and heaved himself out and sat streaming on the step. "The ice'll go before they do. Running water eats it from the back." He looked at his palms in the furnace glow. "It's easy down there. It's the easiest thing I've ever done."

They held fifty-one minutes. At fifty-one the first knob went, and Declan's hand came off it and onto the next one before his body had moved an inch; and at fifty-four Rylan came up the wall with his bag flat and the mouthpiece out and said, round the river in his mouth, "Cold."

"The air?"

"The can. It's gone cold." He held it up out of the water by its strap, dripping. "Like Declan said."

Ward wrote fifty-four in his book, and under it COLD MEANS UP, which had been Declan's rule on the first night at Ottoline's bench, when he said the lime ran warm in the mouth while it worked. Under that, in Imani's hand from the drill tank, was the other rule: fifteen feet and no deeper, because breathed pure under enough water the bottle's gas stopped being medicine and fitted a man without warning, and Emeric had told her he did not know the depth exactly, and when Imani did not know a dose exactly she took a third off.



The third set was Emeric's idea, and nobody but Ferra asked him about it.

Ottoline had made it in the same week, on the same pattern, but bigger: a can a hand longer with a second basket behind the first, a bottle the size of the Isle flasks, and a little screw valve on the bag that the river sets did not have, so a diver going a long way down could feed it by hand as the water squeezed it. Emeric had written on the sheet: for cold salt water, two hours, depth as Imani rules. Ottoline sealed the can in a tin with wax round the lid, crated the set in straw, stencilled the lid, and brought him the bill.

Ferra read the bill in the counting room with her pen in her teeth.

"Three sets," she said, round it. "Two go up the river. What's the third for?"

"Later."

She took the pen out of her mouth. "That's not a column."

"It's not for the river. It's bigger. It's for colder water than the river and longer than the river and deeper, if Imani lets it be deeper." He looked at the bill rather than at her. "I don't need it this summer. I'll need it before I have time to build it."

"Where?"

He thought of a square passage going down under a dune in black salt water, and a stair at the far end, and a dark beyond the stair that his lamp had never reached the end of; and of a city cut out of rock that four men had walked one street of in a night and come out of with eleven pieces of one guard, and of every street they had not walked.

"Somewhere I've been once," he said. "And didn't finish."

Ferra looked at him for a while. Then she wrote a line on a clean page at the back of the works book, where she kept the things that were bought and not yet used, and turned the book round so he could read it. Rebreather, third set, long can. In store. Wanted: later. E. would not say where. F.

"That's a column," she said.



Tess woke on the ninth of June, a little after four in the morning, on Imani's hour.

Armand had written August on her card. It was eight weeks less two nights.

Imani said afterward that she knew before she was through the inner door. Tess had been lying on her back since April with her cuffed hands on the blanket, as straight as the old man had lain; and when Imani came down the path with the lamp at four, Tess was on her side with her knees drawn up and one arm under her head, the way a person sleeps who is only asleep. Imani knelt and put two fingers on her throat, and Tess opened her eyes and looked at her.

Imani asked her name, as she asked every patient who woke, and Tess said, "Don't be stupid," and Imani laughed out loud in the dome at four in the morning until Bettany came in to see what was wrong.

Emeric was there by twenty past. Ferra by half past, with her hair down and her coat over her nightdress, which nobody in Kaldo had ever seen and nobody mentioned. Bram was on the island and would not be home until the boat on the eleventh; Ferra went straight up to the line room in her coat and sent it to the Dust House herself, in clear, because there was no reason on earth to put it in cipher. TESS AWAKE. WELL. COME HOME. F.



Imani's surgery was full of morning when they had her in it, and Emeric stood by the window and looked at her, which was what Imani had told him to do, because Imani wanted to know what he saw before she told him what she had seen.

She looked like Tess: the sharp chin, the heavy brows, the eyes nearly amber in the lamp, her hair grown past her shoulders under the tree with the old singed ends hanging at the bottom of it like the rings in a tree. But something was gone out of her face that he had looked at for thirteen years and never once seen until it was not there: a tightness round the eyes and mouth, the look of a body holding itself up against something since it was fifteen. It had been the chair.

"Hands," said Imani.

Tess held them out. They were not the same. The skin on them was new, smooth and faintly shining in the window's light, the way the old man's had been on the last night of January, and the calluses that the chair's rims had laid across both palms for thirteen years were gone as though they had never been there; and she turned the left one over, and looked at the thumb.

It was a thumb. There was nothing on it.

She looked at it a long time, the way she had looked at it on the boards in April, all of it, once. Then she put it down in her lap.

"I knew that was going," she said. "I looked at it so I'd know where it was."

"Arms," said Imani.

The arms were the chair's, thirteen years of rims, and now something more. Imani put her wrist in Tess's hand and said squeeze, and then said let go three times, and looked at the white marks of four fingers going pink, and said not anybody's hand for a week.

"Feet," said Tess.

Imani looked at her.

"You were going to do the feet next. Do the feet."

So Imani did the feet. She sat on the edge of the surgery bed, as she had sat on the edge of a bed in the ward on the second day after the dynamo house, thirteen years before, and took Tess's right foot in her hand. The skin was new on it too, and the foot itself was not a foot that had stood on anything since Tess was fifteen; it was long and narrow and soft as a child's, and there was muscle in the calf above it that had not been there in April, a little, laid down by the shed as if from a drawing of what a calf ought to be. Imani pressed the sole of it with her thumb, hard. Then she took the needle out of her sleeve and pricked it, and watched Tess's face.

Tess watched the wall.

"Anything."

"No." She said it as she had said it at fifteen, as she would have reported that a coil had come out a turn short. "Nothing in the feet. There's something in the shin. Not feeling. Like a room next door with somebody in it." She looked at her own leg. "I can't move them."

"You've not had them for thirteen years. They've not had you." Imani laid the foot down on the blanket, gently, like a thing that was not hers. "The roads are mended. I've been in you every night since April and I've watched it lay every one. The roads aren't the trouble now. The trouble is nobody's walked on them." She looked at Emeric. "Is that right?"

He had his sense in the girl's spine already. He had been in it since the dome. Where the blade had stopped thirteen years ago, in the knuckle of bone he had once rebuilt in a delve and watched Imani suture behind, there was nothing to find. No scar. The cord ran through it whole, like a wire through a clean joint, and on down into the legs, and every thread of it was there, and quiet.

"It's all there," he said. "It doesn't know it's there yet."

"Then it'll learn." Imani took the needle and put it back in her sleeve. "The old man's knees learned. His were new and they'd been old for sixty years. Yours have been asleep for thirteen. It'll be months. You'll hate it."

And then, with Imani still sitting on the bed and Ferra in the door and Emeric at the window, she said the first thing she had asked for since she opened her eyes in the dome; and it was exactly what Emeric had known it would be, and he found when she said it that he had to look out of the window for a little while at the morning on the hospital's roofs.

"Can somebody get my bench book."

Ferra had it under her arm. She had brought it down from Tess's room over the works at five, before she came to the dome, without anybody telling her to; and she crossed the surgery and put it on the blanket by Tess's hand, with a pen, and a pot of ink, and stood back.

Tess opened it to the first clean page and wrote the date at the top, in her careful hand. Under it, after looking at the page, she wrote: Feet. Not yet.

"And Osric," she said. "When he's up. Not before he's had his breakfast. He'll come without it and fall over."

"He's been on the bench outside since half past four," said Imani.

"Then send him in," said Tess, "and somebody get him some bread."

They did not hear what she said to him, because they went out, all three of them, and Imani shut the door. They heard Osric say, quite loudly, "I wasn't," and after a moment Tess laugh, and then nothing for a long while; and Maren's girl came up the hospital stair with a loaf and a pot of butter on a tray and stood outside the surgery door with it, looking at the three of them standing in the passage with their backs to the wall, and Imani took the tray from her and said, "Later."



Bram came home on the eleventh, on the island boat, and went to the hospital from the quay with the salt still on him and his bag on his shoulder and did not stop at the forge.

Emeric was not there for the first of it. He came up the stair an hour after, because Imani sent for him, and found Bram in the chair by the surgery bed with his hands hanging between his knees and his face the colour of a man who has been up since the boat left at three, and Tess sitting up against the pillows with Bram's bag open on the blanket and a fat book in oilcloth in her lap. Bram's book. The Dust House log, the one she had given him on the night of the hearing, with the red slip still standing out of it near the back and a good deal more after the red slip now than there had been in April, in a hand that was not hers.

"You ran it," she said, without looking up.

"You said run it."

"I said if it matters." She turned a page. "You ran it twice."

"The first one was wrong," said Bram. "So I ran it again."

Tess was not looking at the stones. She was looking at the log.

She read Bram's pages through twice, slowly, the second time with her finger on the lines; the press, the host rock, the dust he had weighed onto the seed bed, the flask of liquid mana he had let in under the plate, the hours, the gauge. The first run. The second, with three times the mana fed in. She did not say anything for a long time, and Bram did not say anything either, which for the two of them in one room was a sort of conversation.

"It's not wrong," she said at last.

"It held a third."

"It held what you gave it." She turned back to the first run and put her finger on the line where Bram had written the flask's measure. "There. That's all you gave it. And it took all of it." She turned forward. "And the second time you gave it three times as much, and it took all of that too, and held a whole stone." She looked up at her father. "It's not the press. The press is right. It takes what it's given while it sets, and that's all it'll ever take. Whatever you put in it while it's forming, that's its size. Inside. Not the rock. The room in it."

Bram looked at the book a long time. "So if I'd given it more," he said slowly, "it'd hold more."

"Nobody knows," said Tess. "There'll be a place where the rock won't take any more. There always is. That's what I want to find, when I can sit at it." She put her hand flat on the log, the new skin and no callus anywhere. "I've got arms like yours now. Imani says I'm not to shake anybody's hand for a week."

Bram put his big black-nailed hand out on the blanket, palm up, and said go on, and Imani said from the door that she was in the door; and Tess squeezed, and Bram went slowly red from the beard upward. "Hm," he said at last, rather high, and put the hand in his pocket, and did not get up out of the chair until Imani made him go and eat at two.



On the twentieth they drew it on the long room's old table, with Linus's survey sheets pinned at the corners by the inkwell, Ward's knife, a rebreather can and the old man's cup.

The river ran across all four: not Kaldo's, but the big slow brown one north of the capital, with reeds a man's height along both banks, Linus had written in the margin. The road north and east came down to it on the twelfth sheet and crossed at a ferry, because the old bridge had gone in a flood in Emeric's grandfather's time. And on the fourteenth sheet, a day's ride on in the hills, a long walled building with a church at its end, and beside it in Linus's hand: the Church's own house. They do not open the gate to anybody.

"That's where he's going," said Saban. "Waylon's hour stands. The escort's remounts are ordered for the night of the first. Out by the north gate on the second, early. At the ferry by evening, ten or a little after; the ferry takes them over a cart at a time, and the king's carriage on its own. He'll be on the far bank by midnight, and in that house by the next night, and once he's in it nobody in this room will see him again." He took his hat off his knee and put it on the table. "That's not my guess. That's what the man in Corbin's household said, word for word, to a woman who sells him gloves."

"Then it's the ferry," said Ward.

Ward had the three books at his elbow in a row: Close Quarters, with Rylan's name under the title; Moving Unseen; and the Water book, swollen from the boat shed's damp. He did not open them. He had read them every winter for nine years.

"The escort," said Ward. "Saban."

"Forty," said Saban. "Household cavalry. Four Church men riding with them, two of them temple guard. The fourth, my woman with the gloves couldn't name. Only that the others give him the road." Ward looked at Emeric once, and neither of them said anything. Then Ward put his finger on the river: a ferry, at night, cut forty men in two with a thing they could not cross, and left the king's carriage alone on it in the middle.

So they drew it.

The K Discovery would put them down. Once: a field two miles upriver of the crossing on the near bank, behind a wood, at nine, in the last of the light, with her stones only and no burn and nothing on her but the dark. Then she would go away north and sit in the cloud over the hills where nobody would look for her until the radio called her, and come back for them and the king to the same field.

"Once," said Ferra.

She had not spoken all afternoon. She was at the table's end with her book, writing what they said in her own short hand; and she put the pen down now and looked at Ward, and then at Emeric.

"She flies once, to put you down, and once to take you up. Nobody flies in between. Not you." She looked at Emeric. "Not with a suit, not without one. Not for a look. Not because a thing goes wrong and you think you can mend it from the air. If a thing goes wrong on that river you mend it on the river, on your feet, like men, or you come back to the field and get in the ship." She picked the pen up again. "The night Grier was read the capital saw a red star go the wrong way, and the sky-ship men are still saying it was a ship. Once more and somebody believes them. That's my rule. It's not a veto. I'm asking for it."

"Once," he said.

She wrote it. Then she wrote something under it that he could not read from where he sat, and turned the page.

From the field they would go on foot along the reeds to the crossing in the order Ward gave them. Arthur and Mira and Bren first, in the black suits with the hoods up, because the suits did not sound and the mesh hid them from anybody who could feel mana, and the escort's Church men could; and the three of them would take the near bank's two sentries when the escort's van came down, from the reeds, with the batons, as they had taken Grier's watchmen in May. Nobody was to be killed who did not make them.

Declan and Rylan would be in the river. They would go in from the reeds a quarter mile up and come down with the current along the bottom in the dark on the rebreathers, without a bubble, without a light, and wait under the ferry's landing stage on the near bank, where the river was seven feet deep and the piles were thick with weed; and when the ferry was out in the middle with the carriage on it, they would come up under it on either side, quietly, and take the four men on the deck. Off the deck. Into the water, if it came to that, because a man in a cavalryman's coat and boots in a river at night is a man who wants to be pulled out, and will give you anything for it.

Ward would have the near bank. He had the two stones out of his kit on the table in front of him, where everybody could see them: the spare gravity stone and the spare flash, from the Staple raid, two of Ambrose Lark's and two of Grayrock's own seam, and one of each spent in the Staple's gallery and on its stair, so that these two had sat in Ward's iron chest in the armoury under his own seal ever since, waiting for a night when nothing else would do. They were very small on the table. The flash stone was a lightning stone in a fist-sized case with a brass pin through its eye. The gravity stone was six ounces of grey in a cup of iron, with its own pin.

"When the ferry's taken, the near bank will know it," said Ward. "The van turns back to the water and bunches at the landing, and the flash goes into it. The Church men's wagon will try to go, and the gravity stone goes under it, and nothing within ten yards lifts a sword for a count of sixty." He looked at the small grey stone. "Sixty is a long time on a road at night."

"And then the ferry drifts downriver with the king on it," said Rylan, "and I've the pole. I've always wanted to be a ferryman."

The rifles would be six of the old three hundred, not the smokeless ones under the crown's wax: breech-loaders with the air crystal under the breech and the lever set to air. No powder, no flash, no smoke; a sound at the muzzle like a book shut hard on a table. "All six on air," said Ferra, writing. "Under the licence. Then nobody can say otherwise."

And the radio. Ivo had built it in a week from Tess's drawings and the valves Merrin had wound for a second capital set that never went north: a box the size of a bread crock on a frame for a man's back, a battery in its bottom, a wire up over his head on a cane like a fishing rod, and a handset on a coiled cord. A field radio, Emeric called it, and Ivo scratched FIELD on the lid with a nail. It would reach the K Discovery in her cloud, ten miles off, and she would reach Kaldo. Saban would carry it, because it was heavy and wanted a man who would not have to fight.



They practised the ferry every night in the race, with four barrels and a door on a rope for the ferry and the garrison in their shirts on both banks with lanterns for the escort, and the garrison enjoyed it. Tess watched from a chair at the top of the bank on the sixth night, because she had asked Imani and Imani had said yes on three conditions and Osric had carried her down. Not her own chair. Her own was in pieces on the Motion Shop's bench, where she had put it on the second day awake with Merrin and Dov Kettle and a list, because, she said, a chair that was going to have to be a chair for months more might as well be a better one. This was a plain kitchen chair with a rug on it. She sat in it with her bench book on her knee and a lantern shuttered at her feet and watched Declan and Rylan come down the race in the dark on the rebreathers, and counted them, and did not see them, and wrote that down.

"Twenty-two minutes," she said, when Rylan's head came up under the raft. "I never saw a thing. But when he comes up under a man with a lantern, the man's going to see a bag. Black the cover, lamp black and tallow, like the hoods. And put the hoses down the back so his arms are clear. I'd have said that in May if I'd been awake."

"Did you freeze him things to hold on to? Declan said."

"On the wall. I can do it on a pile. But I won't be in the river. Ferra's rule."

"Then you'll have to do them before," said Tess. "When you get there. Before the ferry." She looked at him over the lantern. "How long does your ice last in running water?"

"An hour."

"Then do them an hour before. Not more." She wrote that down too. "And tell them where. Exactly where. In the dark they'll be feeling for them."



On the night of the first of July he came up from the race at eleven at night soaked to the waist, and went up the keep stair, and found both of them in his room.

That was not usual; they slept in Ferra's room, which was warm. But Imani was sitting on the end of his bed in her scrubs with her feet tucked under her, and Ferra was at his table in her grey with every pin in and a pen in her hand and nothing on the page.

He shut the door behind him. He stood with his back against it, and his wet trousers dripping a little on the boards, and looked at them; and he knew, as he had known on the night before the Isle, and the night before the war, that nobody was going to say why they were here, and that nobody needed to.

Imani told him to get out of the wet things before he gave himself a chill in July. He took his boots off, and his trousers, and hung them on the back of the chair by the cold hearth, and stood in his shirt. Ferra had not turned round from the table. He could see her back, very straight, and the strand of red that had come down out of its pin at the nape, the way it always came down by evening; and the pen in her hand, not moving.

She put the pen down. Then she stood up, and came across the room to him, and he waited, as he had waited across a trestle in a long room full of grain thirteen years ago with a dog barking somewhere in the dock quarter where there should not have been dogs.

She reached up and turned his collar down.

It had not been turned under. It was a shirt, open at the throat, with nothing wrong with its collar at all; and she turned it down anyway, on the left side, with two fingers, and smoothed it flat, slowly, the way she had smoothed it in the exchange the night before the war and on the dock in December with the frost on it. And her fingers found the cord of the key under the cloth at his collarbone, where they had found it twice before, and touched it, and stayed.

This time she did not take them away.

She held it. She hooked one finger under the cord, against his skin, and closed her hand on it, so that the cord drew taut round the back of his neck and her knuckles lay against the hollow of his throat; and she stood there in front of him with her hand round the thing she had never once asked about in thirteen years and looked up at him, and did not let go.

"It's the drawer," he said. His voice had gone somewhere. "In my room. The first one. Paxton's letter. The page about the school and the wage. It's still in there."

"I know what's in the drawer."

"You've never asked."

"I've never had to." Her hand did not move. "I know what's in every drawer in this castle. I know where the key is. I've always known where the key is." She looked at her own hand at his throat. "I'm not letting go of it, Emeric. I'm telling you so you don't wait for it."

He put his hand over hers, over the cord, and held it there.

Behind her, on the bed, Imani had got up onto her knees. She came off the end of it barefoot onto the boards and across to them, three steps, and did not say anything either; she put her hand on his chest, flat, over his heart, through the shirt, the way she put it on a patient she was not yet ready to listen to. Then she moved it up, to his throat, beside Ferra's knuckles, and laid two fingers under his jaw, and shut her eyes, and counted.

He felt her count. He always could.

"Eighty-eight," said Imani.

"I came up the stair."

"You come up that stair every night of your life. You're eighty-eight because there's two of us in your room and you don't know what we want." She opened her eyes. "Ninety."

"I know what you want."

"Then it should be going down." She did not take her fingers away. "Ninety-four."

Ferra made a sound that was nearly a laugh, against his shoulder, and was not quite one; and he bent his head and kissed her, with her hand still closed round the cord at his throat and Imani's fingers on his pulse beside it, slowly, the way you kiss someone you have kissed a thousand times and are about to leave for a night that might go wrong; and she kissed him back the same way, as if she were reading something she had read before and meant to get every word of. When he lifted his head she did not open her eyes for a moment. Then she did, grey-green and steady, and looked at Imani.

"Well?"

"A hundred and four," said Imani, with great satisfaction, and took her fingers away from his pulse and put her hand in his hair instead, at the back of his head, among the locs, and pulled his mouth down to hers.

She kissed him as she did everything she had decided on, which was completely. Her other hand was still flat on his chest and he felt it close in his shirt, and Ferra's hand at his throat did not let go of the cord, and between the two of them he could not have moved if the keep had been on fire, and did not want to. When Imani let him go she was breathing hard, and so was he, and Ferra had put her forehead against his collarbone, beside her own fist.

"You're not going to say anything," said Imani. "Either of you. I can see it."

"There isn't anything to say."

"There's a great deal to say." She pushed the shirt off his shoulder with the hand that had been on his chest, and it went, and she put her mouth to the bare skin there and spoke into it. "You're not going to say it tonight. Good. I don't want it tonight. I want it when it's not because of tomorrow." She lifted her head. "Ferra."

"I'm not letting go," said Ferra into his collarbone.

"You'll have to let go of the cord to get his shirt off."

Ferra lifted the cord over his head with both hands, carefully, as if it were a thing in a collection, and while Imani took the shirt the rest of the way off him she put the cord round her own neck and the key down inside the front of her grey dress, under the top button, against her skin.

"There," she said. "Now I'm not letting go of it."

He took her face in his hands and kissed her again, much less carefully; and Imani was behind him now with her arms round his waist and her mouth at the back of his neck, and he could feel her heart going against his spine, faster than his.

"Bed," said Imani against his neck. "Not the floor. Your floor's cold."

They undid Ferra between them, because it took two: the pins first, which Imani took out one by one and put in Emeric's hand, and he put them on the table among the drawings without looking where they went, and the copper came down over Ferra's shoulders all at once, a great deal of it, bright as the coals at her father's back even in that light; and then the buttons down the front of the grey, which were small and many, and which Emeric did, slowly, because his hands had stopped being entirely steady and because Ferra was watching his hands, with her chin up and her colour coming up from her throat to her face the way it always came, as if she had never done this with the two of them in her life. At the top button his fingers found the key on its cord against her skin, warm now from her. He left it where it was.

He drew his sense in. He did it without thinking. Off the race, where the night's ice was going knob by knob into the dark; off the ward and the dome with the tree humming in it and the empty boards beside the place where two people had slept that year; off the hospital, where Tess was asleep with her bench book under her hand and Osric asleep in the chair beside her with his boots off; off the garrison and the walls and the town. In, until there was nothing in it but two notes very close and very warm, and his own between them, going faster than either.

"There," said Imani, against his mouth. "You've stopped listening."

"Yes."

"Good. Listen to this."

It was slow. He remembered that afterward, more than anything: that none of them hurried, that they had the whole of a short summer night and used every hour of it as if it were a long one. Ferra over him in the lamplight with her hair round both their faces like a curtain and the key on its cord swinging against his chest between them each time she moved, a small cold weight going warm, and her eyes open on his the whole while, never looking away, the way she never looked away from a thing she had decided to do. Imani beside them with her mouth on his throat and then on Ferra's shoulder and then on his again, counting under her breath against his skin and losing the count and starting over and losing it, until she said a word into his neck that was not a number at all. Later, the other way round, with Imani's hands in his hair and her heels at the backs of his knees and Ferra's hand flat on Imani's back between her shoulder blades, the way she put it on a page to keep it from turning, and Ferra's mouth at his ear saying his name, only that, low, over and over, as if it were the one entry in the book. He was careful of them, and they of him; and then not careful, and then careful again, because the night was short and they were going to use all of it, and it did not have to be one thing.

At some point the lamp went out on its own and nobody got up to see to it.

At some point after that, in the dark, with Imani's head on his chest and Ferra's knee across his thighs and all three of them breathing as if they had come up a long stair, Imani found his throat again with two fingers, by feel, and held them there a long while.

"Come back under a hundred," she said.

He did not understand her at first. He was at a hundred and something; he could feel it himself in his ears.

"Not now. Now you can be at what you like." Her fingers stayed. "Tomorrow night. On that river. Whatever happens on it. When you get back to that field and the ship comes down, and you walk up her ramp, I want you under a hundred." She pressed, very slightly. "That's all I want. I'm not asking you to be careful. You'll say you will and you won't. Ward will be careful for you. I'm asking for under a hundred, on the ramp, so that when you come in through the door of the line room I'll know by looking at your throat that it was nothing. That you walked up it."

Ferra had not said anything. She lay with her face in his shoulder and her hand round the key at her own breast, under the blanket that somebody had found after all, and he thought she was asleep; and then she said, into his skin, very quietly, as if it were going into a column:

"Once."

"Once."

"I'll know if you don't. I read the ship's log." Her hand tightened on the key. "Come back and give me this. I'll give it back to you when you're on the ramp."

"Under a hundred," said Imani.

"Under a hundred, on the ramp," said Ferra. "And I give him the key."

He lay there between them in the dark, with the key on its cord between Ferra's breast and his arm and Imani's palm flat over his heart, and wanted very badly to say the thing he had not said, to either of them, in thirteen years, and had never once needed to; and did not, because Imani was right, and it should not be said because of tomorrow. He said it instead in the dark with his hands, to both of them, one and then the other, slowly, the whole length of their backs, and felt them both hear it. Ferra's breath went out against his shoulder all the way down. Imani turned her face into his chest.

"You're doing it again," she said, muffled.

"Doing what?"

"Not saying it." She did not move her face. "Loudly."

They were asleep by three, both of them, knotted round him, Ferra's arm across his ribs and the key's small hard shape pressed between them, and Imani's knee in the small of his back where it always ended up, breathing slow and deep and even; and the heat of the three of them under one blanket in a narrow bed in July was like the heat off a banked forge, and he was going to have to get up at six and look like a man who had slept.

He did not sleep.

He lay awake with his sense drawn in to the edges of the bed and thought about a slow brown river, and a ferry in the middle of it, and a fourth man on a horse to whom the others gave the road. He thought about the knobs of ice he would freeze on a landing stage's piles an hour before, not two, by feel, with Tess's line under the hour in her book. And he thought about the third set in its crate in straw, its long can sealed in wax, waiting for a passage under a dune and a dark he had not reached the end of; and was glad, in a small foolish way, that one thing in Kaldo tonight was waiting for later.

Imani said something in her sleep that might have been a number. Ferra's hand on his ribs closed, on nothing, and opened, and lay still.

Ferra woke at twenty past five, as she always did, and lay still for a moment with her eyes open on his shoulder, deciding something. Then she did not get up. She did not go to the gauge. For the first time in all the years he had known her, she lay where she was past half past five, with her hand closed round the key at her breast, and her eyes on his face in the grey light, and did not say a word about it; and at a quarter to six Imani woke all at once the way a healer wakes, and sat up with her hair everywhere, and looked down at the two of them.

"You haven't slept," she said to him.

"No."

"You'll sleep on the ship." She looked at Ferra. "You haven't gone to the gauge."

"No," said Ferra.

Imani looked at her a moment longer. Then she lay back down on her side of him, with her hand on his chest, and shut her eyes again.

"Then nor am I going to the ward," she said. "Bettany can have it till seven. It's the second of July. Nobody in this castle's going anywhere till seven."
