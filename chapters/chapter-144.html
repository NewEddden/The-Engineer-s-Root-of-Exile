Chapter 144 - What Was Under the Floor

He could not write the letter.

He had the paper. He had Pennock's second-best pen, sent over from the counting house by a knight, and a lamp, and the long table in the warden's room with the ash tracked across it from the strongroom door, and he had sat at it since a little after midnight with the sheet in front of him and the first line written, which was the Crown's style and the date, and nothing under it.

It was not that he did not know what to say. He knew exactly what to say. He had been composing it since the spring. It was that every time he put the pen to the sheet he could hear, through the broken door at the end of the room, five people breathing in the dark on a strongroom floor, and through the other door, the one to the little side room where they had put Marrow, one more, and Rylan, who was not asleep and was not moving, and the sound of all of it lay on the page and he could not write through it.

At the second hour after midnight he put the pen down and did what he did when he could not sleep in a new house, which was to walk it.

He did not get up. He let the sense out.

He had been using it all night in pieces, a room here, a yard there, the way a man uses his eyes, and he had never once let it go wide in a building full of people who were not his own, because it was not polite and because he did not like what it told him about them. He let it go wide now. It went out of him slowly, like breath let out in cold air, through the warden's room and the strongroom and the little side room, and down the stair, and out along the gallery and down into the hall, where the knights slept in their cloaks along the wall under the weights and the house men who had taken the terms slept along the other wall, and the torches had been let go down to two, and the beam stood in the middle of the floor on its iron frame with nothing on its pans. Out into the yard. Into the stable range and the bale sheds and the back house, where there were men he had not counted, three of them, asleep, and he noted them and went on. Down.

It was through the whole building now. He knew that the way he knew where his own hands were. Every room of it lay in him, stone and timber and the still grey layer of the air in each, and the mana in all of it, thin and ordinary, the ground's mana that is in everything like damp in a wall, lay in the sense the way water lies in a cupped hand. It was not his. It was only held. But it was held by him, all of it, from the ram on the roof to the flags of the weighing floor, and he noticed that, and did not think anything of it, and went on down under the floor.

There were cellars under the weighing floor. He had known there would be. Saban had said wool cellars, old, not used since the wool went to the north road, and the sense found them: long low vaults, brick, running the length of the hall under it, with a trap in the floor at the beam end and a stair, and nothing in them but old bale-hooks on the walls and dust and a smell of sheep that had outlived the sheep by fifty years.

At the far end of the last vault there was an arch that had been bricked up.

He found it because the sense went into it and did not stop. A bricked arch should have been a wall, and a wall stopped the sense the way the keep's rubble stopped a knot, or near enough. This did not stop it. It went through the brick as though the brick were a curtain, and when he looked more closely he saw why. The arch was bricked from the floor to a little above a man's height and then not, and the gap at the top had a sack hung over it, and the bricks at the bottom on one side had been taken out and put back so many times that the mortar between them was gone, and they sat in the hole loose, like a door.

Beyond the arch the vaults went on. They went on under the yard and under the back house, and they were not empty.

He sat very still at the table in the warden's room for some time with his eyes open and the lamp in front of him.

Then he got up and took the lamp and went down.

Ward was on the stair.

He did not know how Ward knew. Ward had been asleep on the landing below the warden's room in his cape with his back against the wall, and when Emeric came past him with the lamp he was on his feet and behind him without a word, and they went down through the hall between the sleeping men and across the weighing floor to the trap at the beam end, and down.

The wool cellars were as the sense had said. Brick, and dust, and hooks. Their boots were loud. At the far end the arch stood in the lamplight bricked to a little above head height with a sack over the gap, and Emeric set the lamp down and knelt and took out the loose bricks on the left side one at a time and set them on the floor, and the hole they left was the size of a small door, and there was a lamp burning on the other side of it.

He went through. Ward came through behind him.

The first thing was the people.

The vault beyond the arch was long and low and whitewashed, and cleaner than the wool cellars, much cleaner, and it was lit by a lamp on a hook, turned down low, the way a lamp is turned down low in a sickroom at night. Along both walls were benches, and on the benches were people.

They were alive. The sense had told him that. It had told him eleven, and there were eleven, and he counted them anyway, twice, the way he counted anything. They were sitting up, most of them, on the benches, with their backs to the whitewash, and some of them were lying down, and one was lying on the floor between the benches on a pallet. They were dressed in grey smocks all alike. None of them moved when the lamp came through the hole in the arch. Two of them turned their heads, slowly, and looked at it.

They were old.

That was what he saw first, and his eyes saw it before the rest of him would let it be true. They were old. They were older than anybody he had seen in Wayford all summer, older than anybody in Kaldo, older than Armand. The skin on their hands and their faces lay on them loose and thin and marked, the way skin lies on the very old, and their hair was thin, and white or nearly, and their eyes were sunk in their heads, and the arms that came out of the grey sleeves were sticks. He had seen people like this in the old world. He had seen them in beds at the ends of long corridors, in their nineties, at the end of everything, and he had not seen one since he woke in this body, not once, not in any village or any city, because in this world a man of sixty looked forty and a man of ninety looked like a man of sixty, and nobody in Randillia had ever looked like this. His Earth eye knew exactly what it was seeing. It had a word for it. It had a word for it before he did, and the word was not old.

The word was spent.

The nearest of them was sitting on the end of the bench with his hands on his knees. He looked up at Emeric. His face under the loose skin was not an old man's face. The bones of it were not. The teeth were not. It was a young man's face, with a young man's jaw, that had had forty years laid over it like a sheet.

"Is it the night?" he said. His voice was a young man's voice, cracked. "Is it the hour?"

Emeric did not know what it meant. He said, "No."

The man nodded, as though that were a relief, and looked down at his knees again.

The second thing was the stones.

At the far end of the vault the benches stopped and the room widened, and there were chairs. There were eight of them, heavy, of oak, with arms, set in a row along the end wall, and on each chair's right arm there was a cradle of iron bolted to the wood, and in each cradle a stone. Grey stone. The ordinary cut, the plain charge-and-hold stone, a little bigger than a lamp stone. Some of the cradles were empty. Some of the stones in them were dark, and some were bright to the sense, full, humming with it. On the left arm of every chair there was a strap of leather with a buckle, and the leather was dark where a wrist had lain in it, and the wood of each arm under the strap was worn pale and smooth in the shape of a forearm.

On the floor in front of every chair there were marks. They were the marks a man's heels make when he sits in one place for a long time, day after day, and pushes against the floor, and does not know he is doing it.

There was a rack on the wall beside the chairs with more stones in it, full, a great many, forty or fifty, and a crate by the rack half packed with straw and more stones in the straw, ready to go.

The third thing was the ring.

It was on the floor in the middle of the widened end of the vault, painted, a circle of white a little wider than a man lying down is long, with eight marks on it at equal distances. At each mark, on the floor, a stone. Big stones. Bigger than the ones in the chairs, the size of a man's two fists, the kind of stone that sat in a knight-rite's rack in the capital under a caster's hands, eight of them, in the eight places where eight casters would stand. In the middle of the ring there was a frame of wood, a kind of low bed, with straps at the head and the foot and the wrists.

He knew what it was. He had never seen the rite. He had heard it described once, by Ward, in the gatehouse at the third bell, and once by Rylan, in pieces, and he had read Armand's notes on it, and he knew the ring and the eight and the frame, and he knew what happened on the frame, and how often it killed.

The fourth thing was the floor.

Beyond the ring, against the end wall, the floor was not floor. It was boards. It was a square of heavy boards laid on joists over a pit, the width of a room, and there was a smell coming up between the boards that was lime and was not only lime, and the sense went down between the boards before he could stop it and came back.

He did not count them. He did not let it. He took the sense back out of the pit the way a man takes his hand off a stove, and stood there, and did not look at the boards.

The fifth thing was on a desk against the side wall by the rack, with a lamp on it that was not lit, and a pen, and an inkstand. It was a book. A ledger, bound in brown calf, a good one, and it was open at a page two-thirds of the way through, and the page was ruled in columns, and the hand in the columns was small and upright and very neat.

He knew the hand. He had had it off the printer in the Ambry back room in the spring, a fortnight apart, twice. Intake. Held against rent. Yield. Kept. Losses. It was the other half of that. It was the book those tallies had been summed from.

He picked it up and read it.

He read it the way he read anything, which was as a thing that worked.

He could not help that. It was how his mind was made. He stood at the desk in the whitewashed vault with the lamp beside the ledger and the eleven on their benches behind him and Ward somewhere behind them, and he read Dame Oriel Strade's books from the front, where they began six years ago in the spring, and he understood them one column at a time.

Intake was people.

They came in by name, one to a line, with the date and the holding and the name of the house that held the tenancy and the sum. The sum was the debt. The debt was the reason. A man owed his lord rent, or owed for tools charged against his wages, or owed a bad year's shortfall rolled forward into the next and the next, and the tenancy law said that a man who owed his lord could be held till he paid. Held against rent. The houses had held a great many. The book had them in a separate column, a running figure, the men and women on the five houses' holdings who owed more than they could pay and could not leave, and it went up every quarter, and every quarter a few of them were taken from it, and entered as intake, and came here. The families were told the same thing. It was in the margin against the first line, in the same small hand, as a note to be followed: the family to be told he has gone to work for the Staple.

Hours were chairs.

A man sat in the chair with his wrist in the strap and his other hand on the stone in the cradle, and was made to push. Anybody could push. It was the first thing Emeric had ever taught a farmhand in Kaldo, that a man with no gift at all can put raw mana into a stone if he is shown how and leans on it, the way a man with no gift for carpentry can still drive a nail. So they pushed, and the stone took it, and the man's work was in the stone. And the man was not let take it back. That was the whole of it. That was the first rule the tower taught and the only one it never relaxed, that a man who leaves his work in a stone and walks away is tied to it, and when the stone runs dry, it runs dry through him. They had not let them walk away. They had kept them in the chair. And when the stone was full, somebody took it out of the cradle and drew it down, and as it went down it pulled, up the thread, out of the man in the chair, to fill itself again. The man in the chair did not have a gift. He did not have a store. He had only what he had, what every living body has, whatever it is that mana carries. So that was what the stone took.

A stone in a cradle on a chair with a man tied to it was a stone that filled itself. It was a well.

Yield was the stones that went out. They went in crates, in straw, by the stable gate, and were sold. The book did not say to whom. It said how many, and at what price, and the price was good, because a stone that has been filled by a man is a full stone like any other and nobody who buys one can tell.

Kept was the five.

It was a column by itself on the right-hand side of each page, under five sets of initials, and the entries in it were hours. The five came down here. On the usual night, when Marrow brought the book up to the quarter, and on other nights too, they came down and sat, each of them, for an hour or two, with a full stone in their hands, and drew. Slowly. Not the way the rite drew, all at once, hard, till the body broke. Slowly, at the rate a body could take, an hour at a time, for years. And what they drew was what the man in the chair had put in. And mana carries life. Everybody in Randillia knew that the way everybody knew that water is wet; nobody had ever thought what it meant. The five had thought what it meant. They had thought it through to the end and done it, and it had worked, and they were upstairs now on the strongroom floor, and they looked young for their years, all of them. He had noticed it at their tables. He had noticed Placit's hands on the knife at dinner, and Strade's clear eye across her own table, and Hollin's full dark hair, and thought nothing of it, because in this world everybody looked young for their years.

Losses was the pit.

And there was a sixth column, which had not been on the tallies, at the back of the book, on its own pages, under a heading in the same hand: The Ring. It was short. It had dates, three years of them, and names, and the names were taken from the intake column, the youngest in it, and against each name there was a note, and the note was always the same word or one of two others, and the word was failed.

They had bought the rite. The book did not say from whom. It said the price, which was very high, and it said the eight to be stones, not men, because they had no casters and could not get any, and it said they had tried it on the youngest of the held, with a ring of the big stones filled from the chairs in place of eight men's hands, to see whether a stone could do what a caster did and make a knight out of a boy. Every one of them had failed. They had kept trying. The last entry was in the spring.

Emeric closed the book.

He stood with his hand flat on the brown calf, the way Ferra laid her hand on a page.

He had understood it. That was the thing. He had understood every part of it, the way he understood a plate or a heart or a suit, from the first column to the last, and he could have drawn it on a sheet of the works' paper for Bram in a quarter of an hour and Bram would have understood it too. It was not a mystery. It was not magic. It was a method, and it was a good method, and it worked. It was slow induction. It was the thing Armand had been measuring in Kaldo for five years with a written right to refuse, the thing Emeric had worked out in the pattern store from Tobin's clean arms and a guess about rate, the thing he had been proud of in the middle of the night. Low rate, no forcing, day after day, and a body that takes it slowly takes it in and keeps it. It was right. He had been right.

And the only other people in the world who had ever worked it out had built it out of human beings.

He did not know when it started.

He knew it had started when the lamp on the hook went down. It did not go out. The flame shrank, and leaned, and went flat along the wick, blue at the root, as though a door had opened somewhere onto a wind, and there was no door and no wind. The lamp on the desk beside him did the same. On the benches one of the eleven made a sound.

Then the dust.

The floor of the vault was clean, but a floor is never clean, and there was dust in the corners and under the benches and along the foot of the whitewash, a grey film of it, and it came up. It came up off the floor all at once, the whole film of it, a hand's height, and hung there. It did not settle. It did not drift. It hung in the air of the vault in a flat grey sheet at the height of a man's ankle, perfectly still, in the flattened light, and the grains of it caught the light and did not move.

He felt it before he understood it. He felt the sense, which had been lying through the whole of the Staple like water in a cupped hand, stop lying still. It had been telling him things. It had been telling him where the walls were and where the people were and what was in the stones, the way it always did, quietly, the way a man's skin tells him where the air is. It stopped telling him. It began to push.

It pushed outward from him in every direction at once, through the vault and up, and the mana that lay in every stone and brick and grain of dust in the building, the thin ordinary ground's mana that is in everything like damp in a wall and that he had been holding all night without thinking anything of it, moved.

It moved the way deep water moves over something very large turning slowly on the bottom. There was no sound. There was a heave, and then a long slow swell, and it went up out of the vault through the arch and the wool cellars and the weighing floor into the hall, and he felt it go, he felt all of it go, and he could not stop it, because he did not know what it was, and he did not want to.

In the hall the two torches that were still burning leaned toward the floor, both at once, and the men sleeping along the walls woke without knowing what had woken them, and put their hands to their jaws, because their teeth hurt. In the yard the knight on watch at the stable gate felt it in the roots of his teeth and in the rivets of his plate and turned round and looked at the Staple and saw nothing. Up the stair, in the warden's room, the lamp in the window dipped, and the man who had sat beside it for six years put his hands over his face.

And on the strongroom floor, in the ash, five people who had been lying awake in the dark sat up.

He knew that. The sense was still telling him that much. He felt them sit up. He felt them feel it, coming up through the floor under them, through the stone and the boards and the ash, something moving in the building that was not a sound and not a shaking and was worse than both, and they did not know what it was, and he felt them know that it was him.

He wanted them to know.

That was the worst of it, afterward, when he went back over it: not that it had happened, but that for as long as it lasted he had wanted it to go on. He had wanted it to go up through the floor into them. He had wanted them to sit in that strongroom in the ash in the dark and feel something enormous and slow turning over underneath them in their own building, and not know what it was, and know whose it was, and not be able to do anything at all about it, the way the people on these benches had sat in those chairs.

The swell went round again, slower, heavier.

"Emeric."

It was Ward. He did not know where Ward was. He could not find him with the sense; the sense was not finding anything, it was only pushing, and the vault in front of his eyes was a flat grey room full of hanging dust and leaning light.

"Emeric."

Ward was walking toward him. Emeric saw him now with his eyes, coming down the vault between the benches, slowly, the way a man walks into a strong current, leaning, one step and then the next, with his face set and his jaw clenched hard against something. His hood was down. His hand was out. He did not understand it. Emeric could see that he did not understand any of it, the dust or the light or the thing in the air that was hurting his teeth, and that he was frightened of it, more frightened than Emeric had ever seen him of anything, and that he was not going to stop walking.

He reached him. He put his hand on Emeric's shoulder.

It was a heavy hand. It was in a black gauntlet with a pad in the heel, and it closed on the plate of Emeric's shoulder hard enough that he felt it through the steel.

"It's done," Ward said. His voice was not steady. He made it steady. "What they did. It's done. You can't undo it standing here." He did not let go. "We go up. We make them pay by going forward. Not down here. Up."

It was not quite right. Emeric knew that even then, even inside it. It was not quite the right thing to say and it was not quite true, and it was the most practical thing anybody had ever said to him, and it was Ward's, and Ward had walked the length of the room into this to say it.

It stopped.

It did not stop the way it had started. It went out of him the way the heave of a swell goes out of the water once the thing underneath has turned over and settled, slower and slower, lower and lower, until the water is still. The lamp on the hook stood up straight. The dust came down, all of it, all at once, a whisper on the flags, and lay where it had been. In the hall the torches straightened. In the yard the knight at the stable gate took his hand off his jaw.

Ward did not take his hand off Emeric's shoulder.

Emeric put his fingers to his upper lip, because something was running down it, and they came away dark in the lamplight.

He looked at the blood on his fingers for a while.

Then he reached for the sense, the way a man reaches for a rail on a stair in the dark, without thinking, because it had always been there. It was not there. There was nothing. The vault was a whitewashed room with a lamp in it and some people on benches, and that was all it was, all he could see of it, all he could feel of it, the way it would be to anybody. The building above him was a building. He could not find the five in the strongroom or Rylan in the side room or the knights in the hall. He could not find the stones in the rack beside him. He could not find anything.

He stood there with the blood on his fingers and understood that he was more frightened than he had been at any moment of the night: more than on the wall with the gun, more than on the gallery with the gravity stone, more than at the sight of the benches and what was on them.

Everything he had was built on that. Every plate. Every heart. Every stone on every suit on every frame in the pattern store, and the root in its socket in the Ambry back room, and the ship, and the forge, all of it, built by a man who could put himself inside a thing and hold it exactly where he wanted it and never, not once, let it go. That was the whole of it. That was all he was. And a minute ago it had got out of his hands and gone through a building by itself, because he was angry, and he had not been able to stop it, and he had not wanted to.

"Can you see?" Ward said.

"Yes." It was his eyes Ward meant. "Not the other thing. It's gone."

"For good?"

"I don't know."

Ward was quiet a moment. Then he took his hand off Emeric's shoulder, and took a cloth out of his belt, and held it out.

Emeric took it and held it to his nose.

He said it to Ward in the wool cellar, on the other side of the arch, with the loose bricks at their feet and the lamp on the floor between them. He did not say it to anybody else, then or afterward, until he said it in the square.

"I was going to send them north."

"I know."

"To the Crown. In a cart. With the book. Keller would have had all of it. Tried them. Hanged them, probably, or sent them somewhere for the rest of their lives. And it would have been the Crown's, and not mine." He took the cloth away from his nose and looked at it and put it back. "I wanted it to be the Crown's. I wanted to be able to say I'd found them and caught them and handed them over, and keep my hands clean of the rest."

Ward waited.

"They die here," Emeric said. "In Wayford. In front of the people they took them from. By my order, as the lord of this barony. Not by writ. I'm not sending them anywhere."

Ward looked at him for a long moment in the lamplight. Then he nodded, once, and that was all.

After that there was a great deal to do, and he did it, most of it, with the sense dark and a cloth held to his face, which was how he learned that he could.

He wrote two lines at the counting house by the lamp in the relay room, in his own book against the key, and the keeper put them on the line south in the dark, the lamps going up and down under the louvres at the back of the building like somebody signalling to the stars. Ferra. Imani. Osric comes for you now. Imani, bring everything for eleven, and a great deal of lime. He did not say why. There were no marks for why.

Osric walked out to the thorn with a shuttered lamp at the third bell, alone, because Emeric could not fly her and would not have trusted himself to if he could, and Ward was needed in the Staple, and Osric was the third man in Kaldo who could. Emeric watched him go out of the square by the south side and did not see him again until he came back.

They brought the eleven up one at a time.

Ward did it, and Declan, with two of the knights, and nobody else, because Emeric would not have the rest of the column down there. They carried the ones who could not walk, and there were five of those, and walked beside the ones who could, slowly, up through the hole in the arch and the length of the wool cellars and up the stair through the trap, into the weighing hall, where Tobe and one of the knights had made up pallets of straw and cloaks along the wall under the weights and put the house men who had taken terms out into the yard. The eleven came up into the hall's torchlight and stood, or were set down, and looked round them at the arches and the beam and the knights, the way people look round a place they last saw a long time ago.

One of them, a young man with an old man's face, stopped at the top of the stair and would not go on. He stood there on the top step with his hand on the edge of the trap and looked at the hall. Declan stood beside him and did not touch him and did not hurry him. After a long time he went on by himself.

Another asked Declan, as he was carried past, what day it was, and Declan told him, and he asked what year, and Declan told him that too, and he did not say anything else.

The one Emeric had spoken to first, who had asked if it was the hour, told Ward when he was set down that his name was Perrin, and that he was from Tollet, and that he had come at midsummer in a cart. They had taken him from the Tollet holding on a Thursday after dark, he said, round the back of the town in a cart with a cover on it, down the south road a way and back round by the lanes and in at the stable gate, because they didn't want to come across the square with him. He knew that because the man driving had said so to the other man. He had been in a chair since. He was nineteen.

Ward wrote it down, in his own slow hand, in the back of the roll book he carried for the column. Emeric saw him do it.

Emeric did not go back down. He stood at the foot of the stair up to the warden's room with the cloth at his face and watched them come up, and counted them as they came, and did not need the sense to count eleven.

The three men in the back house were found a little after the third bell by a knight sent to see who was in it. They had slept through the gate and the smoke and the gun, or said they had. When the knight put his head in at the door they went out of the back window in their shirts and over the yard wall into the back lane, and the pickets on the back lane, who had been standing there all night waiting for exactly that and had not got it with Dray, got it now, and brought them back across the yard with their arms behind them, barefoot, swearing.

One of them was a thick heavy man with a burn scar on his neck, who when Declan asked his name said it was none of Declan's business. The other two said it for him. It was Crisp.

The other two were Garth and Madoc, and they were not brothers but looked it, and they had both started talking before they were across the yard.

The sense did not come back.

He was sitting on the cellar stair, on the third step down from the trap, with the cloth in his hand and the blood dried on his lip, because it was the one place in the Staple where nobody would come and find him and ask him anything. The hall was above him and behind him, full of lamplight and low voices and the eleven on their pallets along the wall, and Ward at the door, and the knights. The wool cellars were below him and in front of him, dark, with the hole in the arch at the far end of them and the lamp still burning on the other side of it, because nobody had gone back down to put it out.

It had been dark for the best part of an hour. He had counted the eleven up the stair without it, and read the relay key without it, and watched the three men brought in across the yard without it, and none of that had needed it, and all of it had felt like walking with one leg.

He sat with his back against the wall of the stair and waited for it the way a man waits for feeling to come back into a hand he has slept on. Now and then he reached, carefully, the way a man tries the hand, and there was nothing there. The stair under him was a stair. The wall at his back was a wall. Below him the cellar was only dark.

He thought about what it would be like when it came back. He thought about holding it, exactly, the way he held a bar in the forge, the way he had always held it, and about whether he still could, and about what it had done the last time it was in his hands and he had not wanted to hold it at all.

He sat on the cellar stair with blood on his lip and the sense still dark, and waited for it to come back, and was not sure he wanted it to.
