Chapter 144 - The Crate

The crate was in the middle of the works floor on the morning of the fifth, where the day shift found it when they came in, and walked round it, and stood and looked at it with their hands in their pockets and their breakfasts in their cheeks.

It was big enough to hold a cart. Fletcher had planked it, over two nights, from the yard's best oak offcuts, with the planks tongued and nailed and the nail heads punched below the surface, because Fletcher did not know how to build a box badly and had not tried to learn. It had a lid like a door, held down by a bar across it in two iron hasps. On the side facing the door somebody had written, in white chalk, in large plain letters, slanting a little uphill:

TESS.

It was Bram's hand. Everybody in the works knew Bram's hand. Nobody asked him about it, because Bram was at his bench with his back to the crate, scraping, and had been since before light, and the back of his neck said that he was not available for questions.

The ones who were in on it were there by seven. Ward, by the door, with his arms folded. Vesk, by the wall, with his hands in his sleeves. Imani, who had come up from the hospital ground early and was standing by the crate pretending to read a list. Maren, who had walked down from the castle kitchen in her apron with a covered basket on her arm and a face that dared anybody to ask what was in it. Fletcher, who had come to see his box opened. Osric, who had been sent to fetch Tess from the ward, as he did every morning, and had been told to bring her straight to the works, and not to say why, and had been visibly suffering from it since the night before.

Emeric stood at the back, against the bench where the drawings were pinned, and tried to look like a man who was not waiting for anything.

Osric brought her in at a quarter past seven. He pushed her through the big doors and across the flags and stopped, as he had been told, ten paces from the crate, and stepped back. Tess sat in the old chair, the ward's chair, the one Emeric had once pushed up the hill with his shirt stuck to his back, with her hands on the wheels and her tool roll in her lap and her hair tied back in a rag. She looked at the crate. She looked at the name on it. She looked round the works, at the people standing round it pretending not to look at her, and her eyes went from one to the next and added them up.

"Oh," she said. "So that's where my copper went."



She would not have help with the bar.

Fletcher had made the bar to lift out of its hasps, and it was heavy, and the hasps were at a height a standing man could reach easily and a sitting one could not. Osric stepped forward. She said, "No," without looking at him, and Osric stepped back. She wheeled herself up to the crate and looked at the hasps, and at the bar, and then she unrolled her tool roll on her lap and took out a short pry bar and a mallet and, reaching up at full stretch with the pry bar under the end of the bar, knocked it up and out of the first hasp in four blows. Then she wheeled herself to the other end and did the second. The bar fell to the floor with a clang that made Maren jump.

The lid was too heavy for anybody to lift alone, sitting or standing, and Fletcher had known it. He had made it in planks, with every plank its own piece, nailed only to the battens behind it. She took the nails out one plank at a time.

It took a while. Nobody helped. Nobody said anything. The day shift had stopped work entirely and was standing round the walls watching; Bram had stopped scraping and turned round on his stool. She worked the claw of her pry bar under each nail head and levered it out, and laid each nail on her knee in a row, and lifted each plank off when its nails were out and handed it to Osric, without looking at him, and Osric took it and stood holding it. After the fourth plank he had to put them down against the wall. After the eighth there was a gap in the lid big enough to see in through, and she stopped, with her hands on the next plank, and looked through it.

She did not say anything. She went on to the next nail.

When the last plank came off, the chair was in the crate on its own wheels, packed round with sawdust and straw, and it was the most beautiful thing the works had ever made.

It was black. The frame was blackened steel with the same soft satin finish as the knights' harness, slim and curved, the curve of the back running down into the seat and the seat into the frame without a join anywhere that showed. The seat was deep green leather, quilted, buttoned. There was dark bronze at the edges and on every handle and lever, polished to a soft shine, and on the arms, and round the rims of the big rear wheels, where her hands would go. The spokes were steel, very fine. Here and there, set flush into the frame so that you could hardly see where the steel ended and the glass began, there were panels of glass, dark, smoked, the deep violet of Kaldo's windows, and behind them, if you looked hard, nothing at all.

No stone showed. Anywhere. It was a chair. A very fine, very expensive wheelchair, the kind a rich widow in the capital might be pushed about in by two footmen. That was all a stranger would ever see.

Tess looked at it from where she sat.

Then she said, to nobody in particular, "Not the carriage ones?"

"The carriage has to get you to court," said Emeric, from the back.

She did not turn round. "I'm not going to court."

"Not yet."

She made a noise in her nose that was not quite a laugh, and reached into the crate, past the chair, into the straw at its feet, and came out with a book.



It was thick. That was the first thing about it, and it was a thing on its own. It was bound in plain board, and it was as thick as a family Bible, and on the cover in Emeric's hand it said: Chair. Operator's manual. And under that, smaller: Read all of it.

She opened it. The hand inside was the same hand as the electricity book he had written for her, the one she had learned the dynamo from and still kept on the shelf over her bed. The same neat upright letters. The same drawings, in the margins and across whole pages, every part, every system, cut away to show what was inside. Every page had its faults on it at the bottom: what would go wrong, how she would know, what to do.

She turned pages. She did not read; she turned them, one after another, quickly, and then more slowly, and the works watched her face go from one thing to another and stop on none of them.

Under the manual in the straw was a second book, thinner, bound in the same board, and blank. On its cover, in the same hand: Data. Tess. Nothing else. She opened it to a blank first page and looked at it, and closed it, and put it on her lap under the manual, and kept her hand on it.

Under that, in a small wooden box, was her band.

Then they got the chair out of the crate. Fletcher and Osric lifted it down, very carefully, and set it on the flags in front of her, and stood back. And Imani, who had been standing by the crate the whole time with her list, came round beside Tess and stood where she could see the chair from the same side, and began, without being asked, to count.

She did it the way she went down a ward. Plainly. Pointing.

"Two hearts," said Imani. "One under the seat. One behind your back. Either one runs it." She pointed. "The lever under your left hand. Glide, that's nothing, it's a chair. Lighten. Lift. Heavy, graded, five notches, read the page on floors. And under that guard, Rise, which I'll tell you about separately because it's mine." She pointed. "The stick, on the right. Push where you want to go. Twist to turn on the spot. It runs through a strip of the barony's ink, so it's as much or as little as you push. Four corner ducts, and the ring under the base." She pointed. "Heating. That's mine too. Warm air only, through the cushion. Nothing hot touches you below the waist, because you wouldn't feel it if it did. Cooling, three ways, mine. Air through the cushion all day for the sores." She pointed at the back. "Bladders in the back, for comfort. Sleeves on the calves, for your blood, which is not comfort, which is medicine, and you'll wear them." She pointed at the glass. "Every stone behind smoked glass or under iron. Watchdog. Armed and safe, that's the toggle, that's yours. Trail rope under the seat. Parachute behind the back, which he says you'll never need and Ward says you will, and Ward won." She pointed lower. "Cork in the side panels in case you go in the canal. The motor, in the right hub." She looked at Tess. "That's your copper."

Tess did not say anything.

"Smoke," said Imani. "Two cartridges, under the seat, sealed. Flash, behind the front glass, a light stone that dumps all at once. The push handles sting, when they're armed, which you arm. The left arm folds out into a crossbow with three bolts, which Ward will teach you." She pointed. "Grabber. Drafting board. Slate. Tool roll, with a slot cut for your draw plate. Lamp. Bell. Mirrors. Rain hood. Brakes, both wheels." She pointed. "A cup holder. Warmed."

"A warm cup," said Tess. "On a chair that flies."

"Yes," said Imani.

She had gone past twenty. Emeric had lost the count somewhere after the cork. Imani had not. She opened her mouth for the next one and looked at the chair and shut it again, and did not go on, and did not say why.

The works was very quiet.



Tess looked at the chair.

She looked at it the way she looked at a fault on the line: all of it at once, and then each part. Then she looked past it, at Bram, who had made the frame and every joint in it and wound her motor from her own wire, and at Vesk by the wall, who had charged test stones every morning since November, and Ward, and Maren with her basket, and Imani. And then she turned her chair round, slowly, on its rims, and looked at Emeric, at the back of the works, against the bench.

She knew. He could see her knowing it. She had seen the store ledger, and she had seen the copper go, and the scorch marks on the powerhouse boards that she had built a theory round. She had seen his hands at dawn all winter, burned and cut and stained with Kaldo ink to the wrist. She could add. She knew what was in the crate and roughly what it had cost in money, because she had seen what the chair was made of; and what it had cost him, in nights, in his head, in his body, she could see from where she sat.

She looked at him long enough that he had to look away. He looked at the drawings pinned to the bench. He could feel her still looking.

When she spoke it was in her ordinary voice, half joking.

"What didn't you add?"

The works laughed, a little, the day shift round the walls, because it seemed to be a joke. Emeric did not laugh. Tess did not either. She had the manual open again on her knee, and she was turning the pages back to the beginning, to the stick, and the lever, and the drawing of the controls; and she stopped there, and put her finger on a place on the drawing, between the stick and the lever, where there was a small round boss on the arm of the chair, polished bronze, with nothing on it.

"What was going to go here?"

He came forward then, from the bench, across the flags, and stood by the chair.

"A governor," he said. "A top speed. A cap on the lift, a hand's width. A limit on the turn. A lock on Rise. A key." He looked at the boss. "I drew it. On the first drawing. It's what I'd have made for somebody I didn't know."

"And?"

"And I took it out."

Tess looked at the empty boss on the arm. She put her thumb on it, and ran it round the polished edge.

"Thank you," she said.

For that. Not the chair. He understood it, and so did everybody in the works who had any sense, and so, he thought, did Osric, who had none and was standing behind her with an expression of great confusion. She had not thanked him for the chair yet. He did not think she would for some time. She had thanked him first for the one thing that was not in it.

Then she looked at Osric.

"You're out of a job," she said.

Osric opened his mouth.

"You'll have to go back to being a normal knight," said Tess. "Standing about. Hitting people." She looked at him. "Poor Osric."

Osric, who had pushed her back up the hill from the powerhouse every evening since the autumn, and liked it, and had told nobody he liked it, looked so plainly hurt that the day shift laughed again, properly this time, and Tess relented, and said that he could carry the drafting board. Osric took the drafting board out of its slot in the frame, very carefully, with both hands, and held it, and did not put it down for the rest of the morning.



Ward told her the rule before she got into it. He did it plainly, standing in front of her with his arms folded.

Lift, Lighten past a hand, any hovering at all: inside the works, and inside the keep, and nowhere else. In the street, in the market, on the hill, the chair rolled on its wheels, on the motor, lightened enough to be easy, which would look to anybody watching like a very good wheelchair and a strong girl. Nobody was to see it come off the ground. Nobody was to see a stone do anything with no mage standing by it. If the Church ever heard that there was a chair in Kaldo that floated, it would not stop at the chair.

"And Rise," said Ward.

"Rise is if I need it," said Tess.

"Rise is if you need it."

"Then the secret's the smaller problem." She looked up at him. "I know. If I'm under that guard, something's already gone so wrong that the Church can have the chair and welcome." She looked at the lever. "I'm not stupid, Ward."

"No," said Ward. "I've noticed."

She understood it better than Emeric did, he realised. He had made the rule as a rule. She had heard it as what it was: a fence round her, put up by men who were afraid for her, which she would keep because the fence was round all of them.

Then Fletcher and Osric lifted her across.

The first thing she did was put her hands on the rims and push herself once round the crate, on the wheels, with the stones off, the way she had pushed the old chair. She was frowning. Then she found the motor's switch, and touched the stick forward a hair, and the chair went forward a hair, quietly, with a faint hum from the right hub, and she stopped it, and went forward again, and back. Then she drew the lever to Lighten, and the chair rolled at a touch, as if it weighed nothing. And then she looked at Ward, and Ward looked at the doors, and Osric shut them.

And she drew the lever through the notch, into Lift.

The chair came up off the flags a finger's width and floated there. The sound in the works was not a cheer. It was the sound a crowd makes when a man on a rope above it lets go with one hand.

The first thing she did in Lift was turn.

She twisted the stick, a very little, and the chair began to turn on the spot, slowly, all the way round. She let it. She did not look at the stick or the lever or the ducts or the crowd. She looked at the works. At the furnace, and the lathes with their new screws, and Bram's bench, and the doors, and the high windows with the light coming through them in long bars full of dust, and the floor going round under her, the whole of the place she had worked in since she was a girl with a draw plate, from a finger's width up. All the way round. When she came back to where she had started she let the stick go, and the counter-burst fired, softly, against the turn, and she stopped exactly where she had begun, facing the crate, with Osric holding the drafting board in front of her.

Nobody said anything.

Vesk spoke.

He had been against the wall the whole morning with his hands in his sleeves. He did not talk. He had come to Kaldo in chains, a hedge mage in the old baron of Grayrock's company, and had sat on a bench in the guardroom and looked at his hands, and had been put to work by Armand and had stayed, and had charged stones and mended hearth stones and kept every secret he was trusted with for two years and more, and in all that time Emeric had heard him say perhaps a hundred words. He came off the wall now and stood by the chair, floating, a finger's width off the floor with no mage near it, and looked at it, and then he looked at Emeric.

"The baron's plate," said Vesk.

His voice was hoarse, from not being used.

"At the church. After." He looked at the chair. "Everyone said a bolt. A fire mage's bolt." He shook his head, slowly. "A bolt burns. The edges were not burned. That was punched. Square, small on the front, big on the back. Something pushed through it." He looked at Emeric. "Pushed."

The works was very quiet. Emeric looked at him.

"Yes," said Emeric.

Vesk nodded once. He had known for more than two years. He had sat on the bench in the guardroom the week after the war and known, and said nothing, to anybody, then or since, and come down every morning to charge the stones for a chair that he must have guessed, months ago, did the same thing. He did not say any of that. He nodded, and put his hands back in his sleeves, and went back to the wall.



She outdrove him by evening.

It had taken him a week of nights to learn the stick without being sick, or crashing into a wall, or setting fire to Bram's bench. It took her the afternoon. Her hands were better. She was not afraid of it. She took the chair up and down the works in Lift, round the lathes, between the furnace and the bench, into the far corner and back, faster each time, and turned it on the spot so tightly at the end of each run that it seemed to pivot on a pin. She tried Heavy, on the flags, and asked Ward to try and tip her, and he could not. She tried the bell. She tried the lamp. She folded out the drafting board, and put it back, and took it out again, and did a sum on it.

At four she went down the length of the works so fast that the shavings Bram had been sweeping up came off the floor in a cloud, though the ducts were lower now and the cloud never got above an ankle, and stopped dead in front of Emeric at the bench, and looked up at him.

"Your twist is too fast at the edge," she said. "Of the stick. It's fine in the middle. At the edge it jumps." She wiggled the stick at him. "And the ripcord's in the wrong place. You put it where my hand should be. My hand's not there. It's here." She put her hand on the arm of the chair, an inch and a half back from the ripcord's handle.

"I measured."

"You measured where it ought to be," said Tess. "I'm telling you where it is."

She went off again. He stood at the bench and watched her go up the works in Lift, a finger's width off the flags, and turn at the end, and come back.



The coin went into the east tower that afternoon.

It went up from the counting room in four iron boxes on a handcart, with two of Ward's men and Saban walking beside it and Ferra in front, at a quarter to three, through the keep yard in plain sight of anybody who cared to look, because the House's factor did not take a payment in secret and the barony did not make one. At the top of the east tower stair Vantic was waiting at her desk with her scales and her book and her grey ink. She had the boxes carried in and set against the wall, and opened, one at a time; and she began to count.

She would not write the receipt until the count was done. That was the House's rule and her own. It would take her the rest of the day and most of the next morning, and she did not intend to be hurried, and she said so, very evenly, to Ferra, who had not tried to hurry her.

Ferra stood in the door of the tower room for a while and watched the first box come out onto the scales, coin by coin. Then she went back down the stair to the counting room, and sat down at her desk, and did not do anything at all for the first time since the loan, for nearly a quarter of an hour, before she picked up her pen.



Imani walked the works yard with him at dusk.

He had not asked her. She had come out of the works behind him when the day shift went, with her basket, and fallen in beside him on the cinder path that went round the outside of the long sheds, the way she might have walked a stretch of road with anybody who was going her way. The light was going. The furnace stack was smoking against a sky the colour of a bruise, and somewhere inside the works behind them, quite faintly, a bell was ringing, over and over, which was Tess testing her thumb.

They went the length of the first shed without saying anything.

"Twenty-three," said Imani.

He looked at her.

"I stopped at twenty-three. There were more." She shifted the basket on her arm. "I didn't want to count them in front of her. It seemed rude."

"Rude to who?"

"To you." She walked a few paces. "I counted the rest at the bench while she was in the far corner. Thirty-one. Not counting the cup."

He did not say anything. They came to the end of the shed and turned the corner, and the river was below them, grey, with the evening coming down on it.

"It's the most frightened thing I've ever seen a man build," said Imani.

She said it kindly. He heard that she meant it kindly; it was in her voice, the way it was in her voice in the ward when she told somebody that what was wrong with them was not their fault. She was not looking at him. She was looking at the river.

"Thirty-one answers," she said. "To everything you could think of that could happen to her. Fire, water, a fall, a thief, a crowd, a dead zone, rain. Cold tea." She shook her head. "And not one thing in it that stops her doing anything at all. You took that out." She looked at him then. "I don't know if you can see it. I can. You took the fence off her hands and put it all round the outside of her instead. Every bit of it. Thirty-one pieces of fence." She looked back at the river. "It's very well made. It's the best thing anybody has ever made in this town. I'm not telling you to take any of it out."

"Then what are you telling me?"

"That I saw it," said Imani.

They walked on. After a while, on the slope above the river where the path went down to the mill, her hand came out from under her shawl and found his, and held it, briefly, as they went down the steepest part of the slope, and let it go again at the bottom, where the path was flat. She did not say anything about it. He did not either. It was not quite dark.



He swore Tess to the cellar that night.

He had to. The cells in her two hearts had come out of it, and she would open those hearts one day with her own tools, because she opened everything, and she would see the egg and the neck and the square face and the casing with the glass liner, and know that nothing in Kaldo she had ever seen could have made them. Better she heard it from him first, in the room it came from, than worked it out on a bench.

The others knew the chair. Vesk, Maren, Fletcher. Fletcher knew the ship in the shed, or what he had been told of her, and Maren would know her by the summer. None of them would ever know the cellar, and it was not a thing he was proud of, and it was a thing he would keep.

Ward carried her down. The narrow stair behind the panel in his office was not wide enough for any chair at all, and she did not argue, which surprised Emeric, until he saw her face at the top of the stair, looking down it, and understood that she had been waiting to be carried down a narrow stair into the dark since she was carried out of the dynamo house, and had decided that if it had to happen, it would happen in Ward's arms and nobody else's. Ward carried her like a sack of something precious that might go off, and set her on a stool by the stone door, and Emeric lifted the door into its slot, and she looked in.

She looked a long time.

At the chamber, and the lattice of stones round it like the ribs of a basket, and the bank of feed stones in their row, and the rails, and the control stone. At the vent line going up the wall. At the sand pit under the chamber, and the lever on the wall that dumped it.

"My hearts came from here," she said.

"The cells did."

"Who else knows?"

He told her. The six. Now her. Nobody else, ever.

She nodded slowly. She was looking at the lever on the wall, the plain iron one with no stone in it, that dumped the whole run into sand if everything else failed.

"That's the only thing in here I'd have built," she said. "The rest of it I couldn't." She looked at him. "That's not a complaint."

He gave her the sheet. She read it twice, with the lamp held close. Then she signed it on her drafting board, which Osric had sent down with Ward, under Ward's square capitals, in her own: TESS.

When she handed it back she said, "How many nights?"

He did not answer.

"The chair," said Tess. "How many nights did it cost you?"

"I didn't count."

"Yes you did." She folded the drafting board. "Never mind. It'll be in Ferra's book. Everything is." She looked up at Ward. "I'm ready to go up now."

Ward picked her up. At the foot of the stair, over Ward's shoulder, she looked back once at the room with the forge in it, and at Emeric standing by the stone door with the lamp, and her face did a thing that he was not sure about, in that light, and would not have been sure about in any light.

Then Ward took her up the stair, and he heard her, halfway up, telling Ward that if he dropped her she would arm the handles.
