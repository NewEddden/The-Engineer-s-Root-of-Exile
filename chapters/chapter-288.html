Chapter 288 - The Swarm

The first swarm came over Wayford on the third of July, at four in the afternoon, from the north-west, with the wind.

Emeric was not there. He was a hundred miles north of it, in the Hod, over the plains, spraying. But he heard it from Wayford's own telegraph clerk, who wired it down the line to Kaldo at a quarter past four in plain words because he could not think of any others, and the line room sent it on to the Hod's set at five; and he heard it again, a week later, from a hundred people, because by then everybody in the kingdom who had been under one could tell you exactly what it had been like, and they all said the same things.

The sky went dark first. Not as if a cloud had come over; as if the day had been turned down, the way a lamp is turned down, from the north-west edge of the sky inward, until at four in the afternoon in July the town of Wayford stood in a brown dusk. And then the sound came. Like rain, everybody said. Like a great rain coming across the fields toward you, a long way off and then not; a dry rain, a hiss and a rustle and a clatter, billions of wings, so many that the sound of them was like no sound anybody had heard. And then they were down.

On everything. On the streets and the roofs and the market square and the river and the people in the square, who ran, and the people who did not run, who stood with their arms over their faces while the things hit them and clung and crawled; on the corn in the fields round the town and the gardens behind the houses and the trees along the river and the washing on the lines. They were the length of a man's finger and brown and yellow and they did not bite people. They did not need to. They ate. Every green thing in Wayford and for two miles round it, in an hour and a half, while the town stood under them in the brown light and listened to them do it.

And then, toward evening, with the wind, they lifted, all at once, in a roar, and went on south-east; and left Wayford standing in the last light with its fields brown stubble and its gardens bare and its trees stripped to the twigs, and the streets ankle-deep in the ones that had died, and every child in the town crying.

That was the first. By the tenth of July there had been eleven.



They sprayed.

It was the only thing that could be done that mattered, and it was too late for the winged ones and they all knew it. Hester's green rot did nothing to a swarm in the air; nothing could. But a swarm came down. Every evening, wherever the wind left it, it came down on the land to eat and to rest; and at the end of the summer, on the land it came down on, it would lay. Ten thousand eggs to every patch of ground it settled on, in little pods in the earth, to lie there through the winter and hatch in the spring and walk. That was where the green went: on the swarms where they came down, at dusk and at dawn, while they sat; and on the ground where they had been, after, where the eggs would be. Every patch. As much of the kingdom as the ships could reach.

So the ships flew. All of them that could fly low and slow and hold still in a wind: the Hod, and the K Discovery, and the public lift hull taken off the Milford route with its passengers' benches stripped out and tanks put in, and hull two, the old one, from the Wayford line's mail run. Four ships. With Tess's booms hung off their sides, two to a ship, and Hester's green in their tanks, mixed in water from the vats at Milford and brought up to the railheads on the Milford line in casks, and pumped aboard at the railheads from the casks by hand, by anybody who would pump.

They flew from first light to the dark, every day. They flew low, so low over the fields that the hedges went by under their hulls at the height of a cart, at the pace of a trotting horse, with the booms spread out on either side of them like the arms of a man swimming and the green mist coming out of the booms' nozzles in a long pale sheet that hung behind them in the still evening air and settled, slowly, on everything below. On a swarm, if they were lucky and it was sitting, brown and heaving on a field like a great dirty blanket, so thick that the ship's shadow on it hardly showed. On the brown stubble the swarm had left, if they were not.

And a week later, on every patch they had sprayed, there were dead ones. Not all. Never all. But on every sprayed patch, a week later, a great many of them lay in the stubble on their backs with their legs folded and the pale green fur coming out of their joints; and on the patches the ships had not reached, they did not.

The Hod did the most. She was slow and she was steady and she could hold still in a wind on her fins and her steadies like nothing else Kaldo had, and Ned flew her as if she were a plough horse, up and down, up and down, field by field, in long straight lines that he laid out the evening before on Linus's map with a rule. Arthur and Mira and Bren worked the booms. That was the job. Each boom had a rig at its root, out on the hull's side over the drop, a narrow platform of dural slats with a rail round it, where a man stood on a harness with his hand on the pump's lever and his eye on the nozzles, and kept them clear; because Hester's green was thick, and the nozzles clogged, and a boom with a clogged nozzle left a stripe of unsprayed ground behind it like a missed row in a field. So somebody had to be out there. On the rig, on the hull's side, with the field going by under his boots at the height of a cart and the boom's arm going out beside him over the drop and the green mist blowing back into his face.

Ned did not like it. He said so every morning.

"It's a harness," said Mira, every morning, buckling hers. "It's Abloh's. It'd hold a horse."

"I've seen a horse fall out of a harness," said Ned.

"When?"

"When I was nine."

"Was it Abloh's harness?"

Ned did not answer that, and went up to his helm.



Bren fell on the seventeenth.

It was not his harness. Mira said afterward, in the ward, with her arms folded, that she had said so; it was the rig. The dural slats of the starboard boom's platform, riveted to their frame, had been flexing under a man's weight for three weeks at the end of a long arm over a moving field with the pump going under them, and one of the rivets at the frame's corner, which Cade had looked at that morning and thought was fine, was not; and at a little before seven in the evening, over a field of stripped barley twenty miles north of Wayford, with the Hod forty feet up and the sun going down red behind the swarm on the field below, the corner let go.

The platform tipped. Not all of it. The corner. And Bren, on the platform, leaning out to clear the third nozzle with a wire, went out over the rail sideways with his hand still on the pump lever; and his harness held him, as Abloh's harnesses held everything; and the harness's line, which was clipped to the platform's rail, and not to the hull, because the rail was nearer, went with the platform's corner; and the rail's end tore out of the slats.

Emeric was in the house, with his sense out through its iron as it was on every spray run, and the ache behind his eyes to show for it. He felt it before he heard it. Bren's note, at the starboard rig, out on the hull's side over the drop where it had been all evening, steady and cross and tired; and then going away from the ship, fast, down, with a piece of rail going with it.

He was at the starboard hatch in two strides. He was not in time. He was at the hatch with his hand out and the pull ready in it, the glyph already coming up round his fingers, as Bren hit the field.

Forty feet. Onto stripped barley stubble, on hard dry ground baked by a summer without rain, on his side, with the rail's end coming down a yard beyond him and the swarm going up off the field all round him in a great brown roar, like the ground itself exploding upward into the sky.

Ned put the Hod down in the field in less time than Emeric would have believed possible, in the middle of the swarm, in a storm of wings, with the booms still spraying because nobody had shut the pump. Emeric was out of the hatch before her hulls touched. He went across the stubble through the swarm with his arm over his face and the things hitting him like hail, toward the place where Bren was, with his sense on Bren's note the whole way.

And Bren was sitting up.

He was sitting on the stubble, in the swarm, with locusts all over him, in his hair and his collar and his shirt, crawling on his face; and he was brushing them off with one hand and feeling his leg with the other, slowly, from the hip down to the boot, with an expression of the most profound bewilderment Emeric had ever seen on anybody.

"I fell," he said, when Emeric got to him.

"You fell."

"Off the rig. I fell off the rig." Bren looked up at the Hod, sitting in the field forty yards off with her booms still going and Ned's face at the cupola, white; and then up at the sky, where the rig was, or had been; and then down at his leg. "I heard it go," he said. "My leg. When I hit. I heard it." He pressed on his shin, hard, with his thumb. "It doesn't hurt."

Emeric went down on his knees in the stubble beside him and put his hand on the leg.

He did not need to. He could feel from where he knelt that nothing was broken; he had felt Bren's whole body as he came across the field, every bone of it, and none of them was broken, and that was not possible. Forty feet. Onto ground like iron. He had seen a man fall half that from the yard's scaffold in the third year and break both legs and his pelvis and never walk right again. He had seen Bram go down on his shoulder on broken stone from his own height, in November, and break his collarbone clean across.

Bren was bruised. Badly. All down his right side, from the shoulder to the hip and the knee, a great dark bruise coming up already under his skin like a stain coming through cloth; and his elbow was scraped raw where he had put it out to save himself; and he had bitten his tongue. That was all.

"Can you stand," said Emeric.

Bren stood. He did it slowly, like a man who expected to be told he could not, and then stood there in the stubble in the roaring swarm on both legs, and looked down at them.

"I should have broken it," he said.

"Yes."

"Why didn't I break it?"

Emeric looked at him. He looked at him with his sense, close, the way he would have looked at a stone he did not understand. Bren's note, which he knew as well as he knew Mira's and Arthur's: a note he had known since Bren was a boy at Sela's school asking Ward if he could be a knight. Steady, and bright, and clean, like all three of them; clean all the way through, with none of the cracks in it that Ward's had, and Rylan's, and every knight's who had been through the rite. And under it, in the bone, a weight. A density, in the bones, that was not in the flesh round them. As if the bones were full of something. As if they were packed with it.

He had felt it before. In all three of them. Faintly, for a year, and he had put it down to the Band, to the months of riding at the Band's foot on the racks with their channels clean. He had not thought about what it was for.

"I don't know," he said, honestly. "Get in the ship. Imani's at Coldwell. We'll take you to her."



Imani was at Coldwell because the poultry trains were.

That had been Milford's idea, and then Hollis's, and then Imani's, who had made it work. The leaflet said to turn the poultry out on the hoppers; and Milford had more poultry than any land in the kingdom, because Milford had become in six years the four baronies' farm and every steading on it kept hens and ducks by the hundred, for eggs and for Maren's kitchens and for the trains' cold cars. So in June, Hollis had come to the table with his hat in his hands and said that if the leaflet said turn the hens out, then the hens should go where the hoppers were; and nobody had laughed at him, though Saban had looked as if he wanted to.

And in July they went. Trains of them, north on the Milford line and then the Wayford line, every day: long trains of open cattle trucks with crates in them, hens in some and ducks in others, ten thousand birds to a train; and at the railheads in the hopper country, wherever the bands were still walking, the trucks' doors were opened and the crates were opened and the birds were let out into the fields.

Emeric saw it once, from the air, the day before Bren fell. A field of stripped wheat north of Coldwell with a band walking across it, dark, toward the next field; and along its edge, a train standing on the line; and coming out of the train, in a long white and brown flood, across the field toward the band, ten thousand Milford ducks.

They went into the band like children into a sweetshop. He could see it from four hundred feet. The white flood met the dark river and the dark river stopped, and churned, and went pale, and was gone. In an hour the field had no hoppers on it and ten thousand ducks sitting down on it in the evening sun, very full and very pleased with themselves, and Milford's poultry men walking among them with long sticks, rounding them up to go back on the train.

Imani rode the trains. Not for the birds. For the people.

Because wherever the trains stopped, at every railhead in the north, there were people. The people from the parishes the swarms had eaten, and the parishes the bands were eating, and the parishes that had not had a leaflet; who had heard that Kaldo's trains were coming with food and had walked to the line to meet them. Families, with everything they owned on a barrow. Old men. Children who had not eaten for two days. They stood along the line at every railhead in the heat, in their hundreds, and watched the trains come in; and the trains had poultry in them, not bread, because bread was on the other trains, Ferra's trains, coming behind.

So Imani rode the poultry trains with four of her nurses and a medical car coupled on at the back, a cattle truck scrubbed white inside with a table in it and shelves and a boiler; and at every railhead, while the poultry men let out the birds, she got down from the medical car with her bag and her nurses and walked along the line, among the people, and looked at them. Every one. The children first.

She was at Coldwell when the Hod came down by the line at a little after nine on the seventeenth, in the last light, with Bren in her house on a stretcher he did not need.

Emeric saw her before the ship had settled, through the cupola's glass: standing at the medical car's step in her scrubs with her sleeves rolled to the shoulder and her hair tied up in a cloth, with a child on her hip, a small one, asleep, and a queue of perhaps forty people going away from her along the line in the dusk; and she looked up at the Hod coming down out of the sky beside her train, and put the child into a nurse's arms, and came.

She did not ask what had happened. She saw Bren carried down the Hod's ladder on the stretcher by Arthur and Cade, protesting, and she got to him as they set him down on the cinders by the line, and went down on her knees beside him, and put her hand flat on his chest.

"He fell," said Emeric. "Forty feet. Off the starboard rig. Onto stubble."

"I'm fine," said Bren.

"Be quiet," said Imani.

She went over him with her hands first. She always did. From his scalp to his boots, slowly, every bone she could reach, pressing, with her lips pressed together and her eyes on his face to see where it hurt; and it hurt where he was bruised and nowhere else, and she went over him again, and then a third time, and then sat back on her heels on the cinders and looked at him.

"You should have broken your leg," she said.

"That's what he said," said Bren, nodding at Emeric. "That's what I said."

"Both legs. And your hip." She looked at his right side, where the bruise was. "At least your hip. Forty feet onto that ground." She looked at him a while longer, frowning, not at him; at something behind him, as if he were a column of figures that did not add up. "Lie still."

She put her hand on his chest again, flat, over his heart, and shut her eyes.

Emeric knew what she was doing. He had taught her. The hollow view: her mana let out of her very thin, thinner than breath, and pitched to Bren's own note, and let down into him at his own rate, slowly, so that his body took it as its own and did not push back; spreading through him the way water spreads through a sponge, and coming back to her from inside, a picture of him. The heart bright, and the great vessels. The lungs dim, branching. The bones faint, because bone held little mana.

Except that Bren's did not.

He saw her face change, in the dusk. Very slightly. A small line came between her brows and stayed there.

She was a long time. When she opened her eyes she did not say anything at first. She sat back on her heels and looked at her own hand, which had been on Bren's chest, as if it had told her something she did not believe.

"His bones," she said, to Emeric, quietly. Not to Bren.

"I know."

"They're dense. They're..." She stopped. She looked for a word and did not have one. "They're full. Like a stone is full. Like the inside of a stone." She looked at Bren, who was watching her with his eyes very round. "The flesh is ordinary. The blood. The heart's a good heart. And the bones are packed with it, all through, like a charged stone's packed." She shook her head, slowly. "I've never seen it. Not in Ward. Not in anybody. I've looked into every knight in Kaldo with this and their bones are faint, they're bone, they're what bone is." She looked at Bren again. "Yours aren't."

"Is that bad?" said Bren.

"It's why you've got two legs," said Imani. She did not smile. "I don't know what it is. I don't know what to call it. I don't know why it's there." She looked at Emeric. "Do you?"

"No," he said. "I've felt it in all three of them. For a year. I thought it was the Band."

"It might be the Band." She frowned at Bren's chest. "It might be the way they came up. Slowly. Since they were children. Clean." She was quiet. "I'll have to look at Mira and Arthur. And Ward, again. And I'll have to think." She put her hand on Bren's shoulder, briefly, which from Imani was a great deal. "You'll be stiff as a board tomorrow, and blue as a plum all down that side for a fortnight, and you're not to go out on a rig again until I say. Is that clear?"

"Yes, Imani."

"Good." She got up off the cinders, and brushed her knees, and looked at Emeric; and her face, for one breath, in the dusk by the line, with the queue of forty people still standing behind her waiting and the child asleep in the nurse's arms, was not her ward face at all.

"Forty feet," she said, very low.

"I wasn't in time."

"No." She looked at him. "You'd have been in time if it was you." She did not say anything else. She put her hand flat on his chest, inside his shirt, for a moment, over his heart, and he felt her count it; and then she took her hand away and went back to her queue.



Ferra was on the grain.

That was what she called it, when she called it anything. She had been on the grain since the twentieth of May, since the leaflet; she had moved her office out of the castle to the Wayford junction, the great yard north of Kaldo where the Wayford line and the Milford line and the Grayrock line met, and set up her clerks in a row of goods sheds by the signal box with the doors open for the air, and her map on the shed's back wall, and the telegraph to every railhead in the four baronies on a bench by the door; and from there she moved the grain.

All of it. Every bushel in the four baronies' barns that was not needed where it was. Milford's, first: the great spread storage that she and Paxton and Hollis had built across Milford's steadings over six years, for this, so that no one barn's fire or flood or rats could starve anybody; eleven steadings, each with its granary and its siding, each with a year's grain for its own people and a surplus besides. She drew the surplus out of all eleven. And Grayrock's, and Kaldo's own. And then the grain that came in by sea: Saban's grain, bought in May from Duvrenne's merchants across the strait before Duvrenne's merchants had heard the word locust, at May prices, and shipped across in the K Endeavor's hold and in Fletcher's coasters and landed at the port; and Rowena's, from the Emberfell coast, sent east along the sea lane in her own ships, with a letter from Rowena in her own hand saying that she wanted it paid for at cost and not a copper more, and that if Emeric argued with her about it she would come and argue back in person.

Ferra put it on the trains.

North. To the railheads in the hopper country, to the parishes the swarms had eaten, at the price it had cost Kaldo and not a copper more: Ferra's rule, written on the shed's wall in chalk above her map in letters a foot high, because two of the lords in the north had asked in June what Kaldo's grain would cost them and Ferra had wanted every clerk in the shed to know what to say. COST. And some of it, a great deal of it, in cold cars: the long white insulated cars of the cold chain that had been carrying Milford's meat and milk and butter to the four baronies for four years, with Tess's cold stones in their roofs and ice in their walls when the stones were not to be had; carrying Milford's eggs north now, and its cheese, and its salt meat, food that would keep, to the railheads where the people were.

And the grain went where the people were, before they ran out. Not always. Not everywhere. But Ferra had the map, and the map had every band and every swarm on it that Linus and the ships had seen, and every parish they had eaten, and every railhead, and how many people were at it, by Imani's count, every evening by telegraph from the poultry trains; and Ferra stood in front of the map in the shed at the junction with her pen, every night until two in the morning, and moved the trains.

Emeric came to the junction on the twentieth, at midnight, because the Hod was laid up at Kaldo for a day with Cade re-riveting every rig on her hull, and found her there.

She was standing in front of the map with her back to the door and her pen in her hand, and her hair coming down, and a cup of cold tea on the bench beside her with a skin on it; and the shed was empty except for her and one clerk asleep with his head on his arms at the telegraph bench, and the telegraph ticking softly under his elbow.

She did not turn round.

"Coldwell," she said, to the map. "Six thousand people at the railhead tonight. Imani's count. Six thousand. There were two last week." She put her pen on Coldwell. "The crown's road block's ten miles north of it. It turns them back from the road and they come to the railhead instead because the railhead's ours." She moved the pen. "I've got two trains of Milford grain on the Wayford line tonight and one of them's for Coldwell. That's eight hundred bushels. That's enough for six thousand for a week." She moved the pen again. "And Brackenfold. And Torrow. And the parishes round Lisle's land, whose lord won't have the mould sprayed on his fields because the Church says it's a witch's brew." Her voice did not change. "So his people are eating the grain we send them, and the hoppers are eating his fields, and next spring there'll be ten thousand eggs to every yard of them and his people will be at Coldwell again."

"Ferra."

"I know." She did not turn round. "I know. I can't make him. You can't make him. Nobody can make a lord spray his own fields." She looked at the map. "I've written to him four times."

He came across the shed and stood behind her and put his hands on her shoulders.

She let him. She did not turn round, but she let her head go back, a little, until it rested against his chest; and stood there, with her pen still in her hand and her eyes still on the map, for a long time.

"You're filthy," she said, at last.

"I've been in a swarm."

"Bren's all right."

"Bren's blue down one side and very pleased with himself."

"Imani told me. At ten." She was quiet. "She said his bones are full. She said she doesn't have a word for it." Her head was still against his chest. "You'll have to find her one. She hates not having a word for a thing."

"I don't have one either."

"Then make one up," said Ferra. "You always do." She closed her eyes. "How many days."

"I don't know. Until the end of August. Until they've laid, and we've sprayed where they've laid." He looked at the map over her head, at the hundreds of black crosses, the red lines of the swarms' tracks going south-east across it. "Then the egg beds, all autumn. Then next spring, to see what comes up."

"Then I'll need more clerks," said Ferra, and opened her eyes, and moved her pen to Torrow.



Vantic's bank lent the seed.

That had been Vantic's own. She had come to the table in June, uninvited, which she had never done, and sat at its end in her grey with her gloves on and said that when the swarms had gone over a parish and eaten its harvest, the parish would have no seed to sow the next one; and that a man with no seed in the autumn was a man on the Wayford line in the spring. And that the House would lend it. Seed corn, at the autumn's price, against the next year's harvest, to any parish in the kingdom that asked, for a share of that harvest she named, one part in twenty, which was low enough that Saban had made a face. And that the House's clerks would go with the seed, and count it, and count the harvest that came of it, and be repaid out of it, and if the harvest failed, would wait.

"Why," Saban had said.

"Because the House has a bank in Kaldo," Vantic had said, "and a bank in Kaldo needs a kingdom that has harvests." She had looked at him without expression. "And because a parish that has been lent its seed by the House in a bad year remembers who lent it, for about a hundred years."

Saban had stopped making a face.

By the end of the month, the House's seed was on Ferra's trains, with the House's clerks riding beside it in their grey coats with their ledgers on their knees, going north to every parish that had asked; and three hundred had asked.



Not everyone was grateful.

Lord Lisle would not have the mould. He was not the only one. There were eleven lords in the north by the end of July who had refused it, in writing, to Ferra's office: Lisle with the most land and the most words, because the priest at his church had preached at Midsummer, on the Prince's day of prayer, that the swarm was the Light's judgment on a kingdom that had let a duke in the south make knights without the rite and teach commoners to read; and that a mould grown in a witch's cellar in a duchy that was half heresy already was not a thing to put on Light-fearing land. Two other priests had said the same in the same week, and one had said that the leaflets were a heresy in themselves, because they told men to fight a thing the Light had sent; and had burned a sack of them on his church's steps.

And the carters in the north who had been paid by the crown to haul the crown's requisitioned grain from the counted barns to the crown's depots, and paid by the load, found in July that Kaldo's trains had got to the parishes first, and that the parishes did not want the crown's grain at the crown's price when Kaldo's was at cost; and lost their loads, and their pay with them, and said so, loudly, in every inn on the Wayford road.

And at Coldwell, on the twenty-third, a man at the railhead whose children Imani had fed for a week threw a stone at the poultry train as it pulled out, because he had heard at the inn that Kaldo had made the swarm, with its ships and its stones and its heresies, to sell the kingdom its own grain back. It broke a window in the medical car. Nobody was hurt. Imani told him afterward that she had put her head out of the broken window and looked at the man for a long moment, and that he had stood by the line with his empty hand and looked back; and then she had pulled her head in and gone on with what she was doing, and the train went north.

"He'll be at the railhead tomorrow," she said, at ten that night, on the set, uncoded, a whole message of her own and not a figure or an order, which she had never sent before. "With his children. And I'll feed them."

D.I.



The air mages tried it at Milford on the twenty-eighth.

They were Armand's. Three of the air mages who had come south to Armand's letter in the second year and stayed, and Linnet Shaw at their head, who had been one of his first three; and they had asked Emeric in July if they might try, because a mage who could do nothing while the kingdom was eaten was a mage who might as well be a farmer, Linnet said, and she did not know how to farm.

So they tried. At Hollis's own steading, on the Milford bench above the river, where the best wheat in the four baronies stood a fortnight from harvest, gold and heavy and unbroken, the whole of it, a square mile; and at four in the afternoon on the twenty-eighth a swarm came over the hills from the north-west with the wind, dark, roaring, the sky going brown, straight for it.

The four of them stood at the field's north edge in a line, with Hollis behind them with his hat in his hands, and put up their hands; and turned it.

They turned it. Emeric, standing on the steading's granary roof a quarter of a mile behind them, watched them do it, and could hardly believe it. Air: four mages' air, together, from four hands, not a push but a wall, a long invisible slope of moving air laid across the swarm's front from east to west and tilted, so that the front of the swarm came down the wind toward the wheat and met the slope and went up it, and over, and away to the east, sideways, like water poured onto a tilted board. The whole front. A mile of it, roaring, brown, bending away east over the river, away from the wheat.

They held it for an hour.

For an hour, the four of them stood at the field's edge with their arms up and their faces white and the sweat running down them in the wind, and held a wall of air across a mile of sky, and the swarm went over it and away, and the wheat stood gold and untouched behind them; and Hollis stood behind them with his hat in his hands and his lips moving, and Emeric on the granary roof found that his own hands were clenched at his sides.

And then Linnet's knees went.

She did not fall. She went down on one knee in the stubble at the field's edge with her arm still up, and held it, from her knee, for another twenty breaths; and then the mage beside her went, all at once, onto his face; and the wall broke.

It did not come down. It went. All at once, like a door blowing in. The slope of air that had been tilting the swarm away east was not there any more, and the swarm's front, which had been bending away over the river for an hour, came straight on, down the wind, onto the wheat.

Emeric tried.

He could not do what four had done, and he knew he could not. He did not try. He did what one man could. He stood on the granary's roof with the swarm coming down onto the field below him in a brown roaring flood, and put up both hands, and made the hole: the air cast out of a patch over the granary, and the air from round it rushing in to fill it, and with it, the near corner of the swarm, the part that was coming down on the granary's roof and the yard round it and Hollis's house, swept up, and round, and away in a great brown spiral over the house, out over the river; a corner of it, a few acres of sky, turned off the granary roof.

For a minute.

He held it for a minute. He felt every breath of it go out of him, the way the firestorm had gone in May, all at once, a great cold emptying; and at the end of the minute his arms would not stay up, and he let it go, and the swarm came down on the granary roof all round him in a roaring brown rain, and on the yard and the house and the steading and the whole of Hollis's gold mile of wheat, and ate it.

It took them an hour and a half.

He stood on the granary roof in the middle of it the whole time, with the things in his hair and his collar and crawling on his face, and his arms at his sides, and watched them do it; and when they lifted, at dusk, in a roar, and went on south-east toward Kaldo with the wind, Hollis's square mile was brown stubble from edge to edge, and the four air mages were lying in the stubble at its north edge where they had fallen, and Hollis was going from one to the next with a water skin, kneeling by each one, very gently, with his hat still in his other hand.

Emeric came down off the roof.

Linnet was sitting up when he got to her, with Hollis's water skin, white to the lips. She looked at him.

"An hour," she said.

"An hour. A mile of it. For an hour." He sat down in the stubble beside her because his legs would not hold him either. "I couldn't have done it. Not for a minute. I did a corner of it for a minute and I'm done."

"I know," said Linnet. "I saw." She looked at the brown field. "It didn't matter."

"It mattered for an hour."

"It didn't matter," said Linnet again, quite gently, as if he were the one who needed telling. "It's a mile of sky. It's a mass of things the size of a county. You can turn it. Four of us can turn it. For an hour. And then we're done, and it isn't, and it comes down, and eats." She drank. "We can't fight that. Nobody can. It's too much of it. Magic costs by the weight of the thing, and that thing weighs more than a mountain." She looked at the field. "It's Hester's mould. It's the ducks. It's the ditches and the spades and the trains. It was always going to be the trains."

Emeric did not say anything.

"Write it down," said Linnet, and lay back in the stubble, and shut her eyes. "That we tried. That it was an hour. And that it was never going to be enough. Somebody'll think it would be, next time. They ought to know."



Kaldo lost a third.

Ferra counted it on the last day of July, when the worst of the swarms had gone over and the ships were out every day on the egg beds; at her table in the castle, not the junction, with Paxton's figures and Hollis's and the stewards' of Grayrock and Wayford, and her own. A third of the four baronies' harvest, gone. Hollis's mile and a great many miles like it. A third.

And Kaldo had grain. That was the other column, and Ferra wrote it beside the first, in the same hand, the same size: that after the third, after every train that had gone north at cost, after the House's seed and Rowena's grain and Saban's, the eleven steadings of Milford still had in their granaries a year's grain for the four baronies' people; because they had been built to, six years before, for exactly this, by people who had known it would come one day and had not known when. Nobody in the four baronies would go hungry that winter. Nobody had. Ferra wrote that too.

And the kingdom's harvest: she could not count that. Nobody could. But she had Imani's railhead counts, every night, from the poultry trains, all July, and she added them; and they came to more than sixty thousand people who had stood at the line at some railhead in the north at some time that month and been given something, by Kaldo, at cost or for nothing.

She wrote the number at the bottom of the page, and drew a line under it, very straight, and did not write anything else.



Every night at ten, the set.

All of July, wherever he was: the Hod, the K Discovery, a railhead, a field. At ten the set came alive in his ear, in Ferra's cipher, with the day's grain and the day's trains and the day's counts and the day's refusals; and at the end of the cipher, uncoded, in the operator's hand, which meant they were both leaning over his shoulder in the line room, or in the shed at the junction, or wherever Imani's train was that night with its own small set in the medical car, and Ferra on the line at the other end:

D.F.: How many days.

D.I.: How tired.

And he would send back what was true. Many. Very. Or: Fewer. Not very. Or once, on the night after Hollis's field, sitting in the stubble in the dark by the Hod with the earpiece on and his arms still shaking: Too many. Too.

And the set would be quiet a long time; and then:

D.F.: Then come home and be tired here.

D.I.: I'll count it.

And on the last night of July, from the Hod, at ten, over a field of egg beds north of Wayford with the last of Hester's green going out of the booms in the dusk and Bren back on the starboard rig on a new harness clipped to the hull, not the rail, by Ned's order and Imani's and Mira's and everybody else's, he sent back, when they asked:

Two. Not very.

And then, after it, because it was true, and because the operator in the line room had been made to hand over the pencil so many times that summer that he had stopped reading anything at all:

Wait for me on the slip.
