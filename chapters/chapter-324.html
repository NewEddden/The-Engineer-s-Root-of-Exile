Chapter 324 - Night Landing

Wend had the first watch on the Lantern's forward rail, and for the better part of an hour he had been looking at a patch of sky and trying to decide whether it was worth the captain's time.

The black ships had gone out like a pinched wick. Far below, the one the small hulls had pulled down lay dark in the river, and the city's lamps went round her the way water goes round a stone.

The valley's low cloud lay under them too, torn into rags by the night wind, and every rag of it was going east. The patch off the starboard bow was not. It sat at the foot of the high air like a lid, flat along its underside, a mile and more across, and the stars stopped at its edge as if somebody had cut them with a knife. Wend threw the lamp at it twice, long and short, as he had been taught. The first answer came back from the hull beyond it, a little late. The second went into the white and did not come back at all.

He went to the deck officer. The deck officer went to Captain Orsolo, who was eating.

"A cloud."

"It isn't moving, sir. Look at the rest of them."

"Clouds do as they like, Wend."

But Orsolo came, with bread still in his fist, and Calder was at the rail already with a slate on his forearm and the look of a man who disliked being right. Every chalk cross on it since the dark was in one corner. Every Kaldo shot had come out of that one patch of sky, and the patch had not turned with the wind.

"Brother Ildan."

The sensing mage was a thin man who hated being woken, and had been woken three times since dusk. He shut his eyes and stood with the wind in his sleeves, and the deck stones ticked under their feet as the Lantern trimmed herself.

"I get a lot, sir," said Ildan at last. "And wrong. Like one voice in a choir at full cry. There's a thing in there, or forty things. I couldn't point to one."

Calder tapped the slate. He did it quietly, as if the others might not want to hear it.

"They're in the cloud."

Orsolo's hand stopped halfway to his mouth. Forward, a lamp on the Lantern's fin-tip began to talk to the ship astern, and was answered, and the answer came a long time late.

Brother Orme had come up from the dome while they stood there: an old man in a plain grey habit with his hands in his sleeves, who had read men's bodies through his fingertips for nineteen years as a Visitor and now commanded sixteen hulls. He let Calder say it again for him. Then he took his hands out of his sleeves.

"Every hull above it," he said. "Fire down into it, on the flashes. Full shells on the great hulls; bid the bleeders sit to the stones." He looked at Wend. "Throw it, child. To all of them. Twice."

Wend threw it. The nearest hulls took it and threw it on, and beyond them it went into the white as a glow and then as nothing. Off the port quarter a small hull began to climb. Off the starboard bow the third of the great hulls, which had read the first half and not the second, began to come down.



The bay smelled of wet iron and cold powder.

The cloud came in at the K Amara's gun ports in slow grey breaths and beaded on the breeches, and the lamps were hooded down to red slots that lit a man's hands and nothing else. Sound came wrong in it. The hoist's ratchet, three yards off, clicked as if it were in Ruan's ear.

Ruan had his crews clipped to the jackline and their heels on the plate, and under them the deck stones ticked. She was creeping. He could feel it through his soles: propellers feathered, nothing turning to stir the white, the held stones easing her sideways at a walk, because a ship that fired and stayed put in a cloud had told the sky where to send its iron.

Tolliver had forgotten twice in the last hour to set his trim down, and was a hand's breadth off the plate again, hanging from his clip like a shirt on a line, with only his boots telling him where the deck was.

"Trim," said Ruan, not turning.

"Sorry."

"You'll be sorry in the river. Trim."

Tolliver reached round to the stone at his back and came down onto the plate with a grunt. He had been sick over the rail when escort two went down. Ruan had moved him to the hoist afterward, where there was more to do with his hands.

The set on the bulkhead hissed, and it was the duke, from the K Discovery, flat and unhurried, in Ferra's cipher, which the bay's clerk took down by the red lamp and read aloud a breath behind. Bearing. Range. Height. Nobody in the bay could see a Church hull. Nobody could see past his own muzzle. One of the great hulls, the duke said, was coming down toward the bank's crown alone, a mile and a quarter off.

"K Amara," the clerk read. "Forward turret and broadside, on bearing. Escort one with you. On my word."

The turret captain called the bearing down the tube. The breech crews laid. Ruan put his palm flat on the nearest breech, as if it were a horse.

The word came.

The K Amara fired, and the bank lit from inside.

None of them had heard her guns in a cloud. The noise went out and came back in the same breath, as if the cloud were a bell and they were inside it; and the flash did not go away but stayed and spread, a soft orange bloom that filled every port and showed Ruan, for one breath, every face in the bay, red and open-mouthed, Tolliver's among them with his eyes shut. The deck kicked. The pin held. The boots held, and the line, and nobody moved.

Off to port, far off in the white, a second bloom: escort one's twin thirties, a breath behind.

Ruan took a stub of chalk from his breast pocket and made marks on the bulkhead beside the set, one for each round from the forward battery and one, a little apart, for each that escort one's clerk called. The chalk ran in the wet. He pressed harder.

"Ten," he said. "Load."



Emeric felt them stop.

He was in the K Discovery's forward cabin with the glass dark and his sense laid out over the bank like a net, and he felt the ten shells cross the gap and stop, all ten. The great hull's shell took them, and the faster they came the harder it took them, and each one cost it. Her shell stones were a hard bright knot in her prow under the violet glass, and with each shell the knot flinched and dimmed by a hair, and then, slowly, came back up as somebody behind it fed it.

Somebody. A row of small grey notes against the stones in her hold, men with their hands on the knot, pouring. The Church had not solved its ships' hunger with iron. It had solved it with people.

"Held," he said. "All ten. Tell them to move."

The K Discovery hung at the bank's western edge with her nose in the white and her stern in the open night. Fletcher had her helm, his eyes on dials lit by a hooded lamp no bigger than a thumbnail. In the second seat, where Ward would have sat if Ward had not been on the K Amara's tower, sat the king, in his plain brown coat with the collar up. He had come forward when Emeric crossed from the K Amara in his suit once she was in the white, and sat down in the empty seat with his hands on his knees, and had not said anything since. He was watching Emeric.

"How many," said the king, "to break one?"

"I don't know." Emeric did not take his sense off the hull. "More than ten."

The king nodded, as if that were an answer.

The second volley came from two new places, and the knot took it, deeper, and came back slower. The third. One of the bleeders was going thin, like a lamp running down its oil, and another came in to take his place. The great hull had stopped coming down. She was turning, her bow swinging toward where the last flash had been, which was the wrong place, because the K Amara was already two hundred yards below it.

"She's looking for the flashes," he said. "They all will. K Amara, one more from where she is, then down and east."

The second set hissed: the one tuned north. A different voice, flat, blurred by four hundred miles of night.

Escort four on station. Off the west end. Swimmers going in.

Rylan, who could not tell a story without three people and a dog in it, reading his own night off a pad like a man counting sacks.

The fourth volley went in, and the knot in the great hull's prow went down to a dull glow, and did not come back.



Orsolo watched the third great hull light up for the fourth time and knew what he was watching.

Every time her shell stopped one of the things coming out of the white, it lit, a pale shiver round her like frost forming on a pane, and every time the shiver was weaker. In nineteen years in the wing he had never seen a shell lit from outside. There had never been anything in the world to light one.

"They move after every one," said Calder, chalk going. "Two at a time, never the same place twice. They're firing on something they can't see." He looked up. "They can't see her any better than we can see them, sir. And they know where she is."

Orsolo was watching the third hull's lamps. They were talking, over and over, toward anybody, a sentence he could read even through the rags of cloud. Shell failing. Shell failing.

And every time her lamps spoke, the white answered them. Not with a lamp.

"Tell her to put out her lamps," said Orsolo.

Wend put his hand on the shutter, and stopped, and looked at him. The order to put out her lamps would have to go by lamp.



The forty-first went through.

Ruan knew it was the forty-first because of the chalk. Ten and ten and ten and ten, and escort one's called into it; and then the forward battery fired from its fifth place in the white, and in the bloom's last glow, far out past the muzzles, there was another light. Small, deep, steady orange, like a forge door opened in the night. It did not go out.

"She's afire," said somebody at the next breech, not loudly.

The clerk read the duke's voice, and his own went up at the end in a way it was not meant to. "Through on the prow. Her shell's out. Her lift's on the same bank, she's going down by the head. Apron knights to the rail. Men going over."

For an hour they had seen nothing, and now the great white hull was burning and the burning lit her: half a mile off at the bank's thin crown, bow tipped down, her stained-glass dome glowing like a church window at evening and the fire coming up out of her prow where the violet glass had been. And her deck had gone. Not the planks. The hold that had pulled her people onto them had gone with her shell, and her people were coming off the planks.

Men in white surcoats rising off the deck with their arms out, slowly, as if it had turned to water under them. A man caught by a rail with his legs going up behind him. Another wrapped round a stay like a boy round a mast. A bucket, a hat, turning over. And under them all, coming up the deck from the prow, the fire.

A man let go.

He did not mean to, Ruan thought. The hull tipped and the rail went one way and he went the other, out over the side, slowly and then not slowly, a white shape against the burning hull and then against the dark; and something went past the K Amara's ports after him, close enough that its wind put the red lamp out. A knight, in black flight armour with the hearts lit faint along his arms, going down off the forward apron head first like a cormorant into a harbour.

He missed.

The knight took the falling man by the surcoat, and the surcoat tore, and the white shape went on down and the knight's fist came away with a long strip of white in it. He turned in the air, too low and too slow, his dive spent. And a second dark shape went past him from above, smaller and faster, with a plait streaming out under its helm, and took the falling man round the chest from behind with both arms; and the hearts went bright all down her back as she put every node into stopping, and the two of them slowed, and slowed, and held.

Then the dark took them, going up and away toward the K Endeavor's aprons with the white shape hanging in the black one's arms.

"That's Mira," said the man at the next breech, as if he had money on her. "That's the Hod girl."

Ruan looked away from the port.

Tolliver had not. He had watched all of it with a charge in his arms and his mouth open: the rising and the clinging and the torn surcoat. Now he put the charge down very carefully on the hoist's tray, and was sick into his own hand, quietly, and wiped it on his trousers, and kept his boots on the deck.

"Load," said Ruan.

Tolliver loaded.

Ruan drew the forty-first stroke long, through the other forty, and wrote the hour under it by the bay's clock. Four minutes to nine.



Osric had waited on escort one's forward rail for most of an hour with his hands in his armpits, because hands in flight gauntlets in a wet cloud went first, and he would want them.

The fourth of the great hulls was theirs. She had read Orme's order right and climbed to come down on the bank from above, and climbed straight across the line between the K Amara's new place and escort one's; and the two ships put five volleys into her from two sides in a quarter of an hour, moving between each, and on the fifth her shell went out.

It went out cleanly. The last shell was simply not stopped, and went through her low where there was nothing to catch, and out the other side. Her deck hold went with her shell and her people came up off the planks. But her lift held. Somebody aboard, some sharp captain or some bleeder with his wits about him, had thrown everything the stones had left into the lift; and she hung there unshelled and unlit, with her people floating over her deck like weed over a sunk boat, and did not fall.

"Hooks," said escort one's captain into the tube.

"Not yet." That was Osric, before he had thought. "She's got mages."

He was no delver, not yet; but any knight could feel a thing gathering, and her deck was full of it. Among the floating men, here and there, the hard bright prickle of men who were not only afraid, with their hands up.

The grapple came first.

A small hull, one of the eleven, had come down into the white on the flashes like a dog on a scent, so close that Osric felt her stones only as she fired; and the line came out of the fog over escort one's port quarter, silver and thin, with a hook on it as long as a man's forearm, and bit into the after rail. Everybody aboard with a sense felt it bite: a cold hand laid on the escort's shell, and the shell going out of her the way warmth goes out of a bath when the plug is pulled, and the deck hold going soft under Osric's boots and the harness's pull on his shoulders going light.

The boots held. The line held. Nobody went anywhere.

The escort's after gun captain, a short man with a broken nose, unclipped his hook-cutter from its bracket, walked six locked steps aft with his lanyard sliding behind him on the jackline, hooked the silver line where it came over the rail, and pulled once with his whole weight. The line parted on the inside of the hook with a noise like a fiddle string and went whipping back into the white, and the cold hand came off, and Osric felt her stones begin to come back.

The gun captain clipped the hook-cutter back in its bracket and wiped his face.

"That's what they're for," he said, to nobody.

The small hull did not get a second line off. Escort one's after turret was already coming round, and the duke was reading the small hull's bearing on the set before it stopped.

"Hooks," said the captain again, and this time Osric did not argue.

The boarding hooks were nothing like the grapple. Plain steel on plain steel cable, nothing in them for anybody to drain. They went out from the spring launchers on the starboard rail with a bang and a long rising whine off the drums, four of them, and three bit. Then the winches took up, with the long grinding scream of cable under load, and the ratchets Tess had made right in October held, every pawl; and out of the white, slowly, the great hull came.

She came broadside, her flank growing out of the fog like a cliff out of sea mist, gilt rails and ribbed fins and lit ports with faces in them. And over her deck and caught in her rigging, her people in white, floating and clinging, more than a hundred, looking down at the long black hull coming alongside and the men on it in black.

The twenty knights on escort one's rail looked back at them.

Osric had done this a hundred times off the island, and his hand would not open on the clip. Nobody's would. They stood in the cold with the cables groaning and looked at the white ship.

And a big Grayrock boy beside him, whose voice had not finished breaking, said it before anybody else could, loud, in plain disgust.

"She's just Saint Nobody with people on."

Somebody laughed. Then all of them did, a short ugly bark down the rail in twenty helms; and Osric's hand opened, and he went over.



The deck was oak and wet and gave under his boots like a living floor, and the first thing that happened to him on the Church's deck was that a lay brother drifted into him from behind like a log in a millpond and clung to his arm with both hands.

Osric pushed him down onto the planks by the belt and put a boot on his surcoat's hem. "Hold the rail. Stay there."

His team came over in twos, twelve of them, and went forward in two lines, pulling men down out of the air and putting their hands on the rail, the way the island had drilled them with sandbags in Church white; and the first eight yards were exactly as the island had been, and the ninth was not.

The mages were at the foot of the mast, six of them, each with one hand on its iron bands and the other lifting. As Osric saw them their glyphs came up: six rings of light a yard across, all at once, wheeling white and red and blue, lighting the whole deck; and inside the rings the patterns building, line on line, the old way.

And between the mages and Kaldo, coming aft on their own feet as if nothing were wrong with the deck: four men in white plate, gilt at the edges, with long blades. Temple guard. Their own mana held them to the planks. Osric could feel it in them, dense and old, full traced, every one of them twice his age.

The first glyph closed, and fire came off it across the deck in a sheet.

Two of his knights went into it with their blades up, and the charged edges went through the ring's lines and the ring broke like a pane, all at once, and the fire died half way and left a smell of hot iron. But the second glyph was closing, and the third, and a temple guard was on the first of his knights before the knight had his blade back from the cut; and the knight went down with his arm laid open from the shoulder and his hearts going out one by one, and Osric was on the temple guard, and the old man took Osric's blade on his own and turned it as if Osric were a boy.

He got his left hand to the radio at his collar.

"K Amara. Osric. Give me the shell."

Ward's voice, from the K Amara's tower, very calm. "On you?"

"Between us. Half way down her deck. Now."

"Everybody in the air on that side of her," said Ward, on the fleet set, in the clear, so every knight in the sky heard it in his helm, "chutes. Dead air in five."

Osric counted it himself as he took the old man's second cut and gave a yard of deck for it. On four the K Amara fired one gun, somewhere out in the white. On five the shell burst between the two hulls a little above the deck, with a flat soft crack like a door slammed in another house.

The glyphs went out.

All six, built half way, then not there, as if somebody had blown out six candles with one breath; and the mages stood with their hands up and their mouths open, reaching for a thing in themselves that had always been there and was not. One was off his feet, floating, and grabbed at the mast and missed and drifted out over the rail, and was taken out of the air below it by one of escort one's knights, who swore at him all the way back. Beyond the rail three of Osric's own, who had been in the air between the ships, dropped out of the sky with their suits dead and their hearts out, and their chutes cracked open behind them one, two, three, and they swung away down into the bank.

Osric's blade went dull. The charge was gone from its edge.

So was the temple guard's.

The old man felt it a breath after Osric did, and Osric watched his eyes go wide behind the visor; not fear, surprise, the surprise of a man who had never in forty years stood anywhere that his mana was not. Then they narrowed, because his strength was still in him, all of it, inward, where no dead zone could reach. So was Osric's. That was the thing the island had drilled into them until they dreamed it. In dead air a knight was a knight. The mages were nothing.

It was a knights' fight now, for as long as the dead air lasted, which at this height was a breath and a breath and a breath.

Osric did not try to beat him. He could not have. He let the old man's third cut drive him back, and as the old man came on, Osric's second put a rifle past Osric's hip and fired one spike round. The hardened point went through the white plate at the thigh, where the cuisse met the tasset, a thumb's depth in; and the lightning chip behind it, which on the skin of a man that full would have been brushed off like a midge, went off inside him.

The temple guard went down on the oak in one piece, rigid, his blade still in his fist.

"Flash," said Osric. "Mast."

Two flash rounds hit the foot of the mast together, a great noiseless white and then a noise like the sky falling in, and every man forward of the mast who was not a Kaldo knight with his visor down went to his knees or into the air with his hands over his face.

The dead air was going, the Band's dense mana filling the hole the way the sea fills a footprint. His knights did not wait for it. Cuffs went onto the six mages while they were still on their knees and blind, iron, a stone at the clasp, and the mages' notes went small and grey and stayed that way. Two temple guard went down to blades, wounded, not killed; Osric had told every man of his on the island that he would hang any one of them who killed a man on a deck, and they believed him. The third threw down his blade.

The anchor teams went forward to her prow, three teams of three, to the violet glass low in her bow: three panes each the height of a man, the lift stones' light behind them deep and bright and beating faintly, like a heart behind ribs. The first team clamped the anchor below the middle pane, an iron limpet as big as a cartwheel, jaws shut on her oak, and turned its stone to Heavy; and the whole hull lurched and groaned and her bow went down a yard.

Her lift stones answered. The violet flared; somebody aboard was throwing everything at the bow, and she began, slowly, to come level.

The second team put a breach stone to the middle pane and stood clear. It went off with a shove like a horse's kick, and the pane went in whole, and behind it the glow jumped and broke and went out; and the third team put lightning rounds through the hole into the next cluster, one, two, three, and that glow went out too.

She stopped trying. Osric felt it through the oak, a long slow settling like a cow lying down. Not falling: sinking, by the head, a foot in a breath, the anchor's weight on her and nothing left in her bow to fight it, going down out of the bank toward the dark fields east of the river with her people lying on her deck with their hands on her rail.

"Cut the hooks," said Osric. "Everybody back but my twelve." He looked round. The knight with the laid-open arm was by the rail with two others holding a pad to it, his face grey. "He goes across. The rest of us take her down."

"All the way down?" said his second.

"She's ours," said Osric. "We land her."



The north set talked in Rylan's voice a line at a time, with long gaps between, and Emeric listened with half his head and fought the bank with the other half.

Swimmers in. Fenn's twelve, in kit, a mile off the Claim's west end, pulling a line ashore on the tide. Boats behind the line, four, no lamps, oars muffled in sacking at the thole pins.

A sentry on the dune. One. Kesrath's. Taken. Quiet. Not hurt.

In the gap Emeric felt the fourth great hull going down out of the bank with Osric's men on her, and the burning one lower, drifting east, and escort one going after her to push her off the city with her bow if she had to.

Divers on the tanks. The rebreather pair, in the cove under the west cliffs, on the bell before anybody in Kesrath knew there was a bell there to be on. No bubbles.

The wells held. The dune drum held.

"Water," said the king, beside him, quietly. "Moving Unseen." He did not take his eyes off the set. "Ward's books. He'll be unbearable."



The flying mages came at the K Amara a little after eleven, out of the dark above the bank, eight of them, and Ward went off the forward apron to meet them with twenty knights behind him.

He had felt them leave the Lantern's rail, eight bright notes spreading like sparks off a struck flint. Men, not hulls. They came down into the bank, which was brave, found the K Amara by her guns' last bloom, and came out of the white above and to port, hovering two dozen yards off her rail on their glyphs, and the first was building lightning before Ward was off the apron.

Ward had never liked flying, and liked it less in a cloud at night with eight mages in it. He went off the apron's edge with his hearts at half and his blade's charge lit along the edge, a thin cold blue line in the fog, and the knights came after him in a wedge.

The lightning hit the K Amara's shell, which took it without a flicker. It had not been meant for the rail; it had been meant to show the mages where the rail was. The second glyph was fire, a great deal of it, and it was meant for the knights. Two went through the ring and broke it. A third was not quick enough, and the fire took him across the chest plate and he went down out of the wedge glowing dull red, and Ward felt him fall and could not go after him; and felt, a breath later, a knight come off the after apron and take him and hold him, fast and low, Bren by the way he flew. Then the mages were all round them.

Nothing on the island had been like this. A knight in a flight suit flew as a stone flew, pushed, in a line; he turned the way a horse turns, round, and a man turning round in the air was a man going somewhere he had not been. The mages did not go anywhere. They stood in the air as still as men on a floor and turned on the spot, and when a knight came at one the mage was simply a yard to the side, as easily as stepping off a path, and the knight went past into the white and had to come round. They had been doing it for four hundred years. Kaldo had been doing it for two.

But their glyphs showed. Every ring was the brightest thing in the sky, and a charged blade went through its lines like cobweb.

The knights broke four rings in the first minute and two in the second. They took one mage, whose ring was cut and who stepped aside too slowly, by both arms, and brought him back toward the apron with his robes flapping. A girl off the after apron had her arm broken by a bombard ball that came up out of the white from somewhere far off by plain bad luck, and was caught. And Ward found the good one.

He was the one in the middle, who had not cast yet; older than the rest, his note very bright and very still. When at last he drew, his ring was the size of a man's hand.

Dimmed. Ward had seen Armand do it. He had never seen it done fast.

It closed in the time Ward took to turn, and what came off it was air, a fist of it, that hit him in the chest like a falling beam and threw him thirty feet back through the white with the hearts going out along his left arm. He came round, too slowly, and the second little ring was closing. He went for the ring, not the man, and his edge went through it a hair before it closed, and a shower of nothing went over his visor.

The mage was a yard up and a yard to the side, building again.

Ward was not young, and he was tired, and he knew as he came round the second time with his left arm dark that he was going to lose. In the air the man was better than anybody Kaldo had.

So he stopped flying.

He put his thumb on the cut-off at his belt and his suit went dead, and he dropped like a stone under the third ring, which closed on the place he had been and threw its fist at nothing. And as he fell he reached up; not with a hand, but with the sense that had opened in him late, one winter in his own yard, at an age when nobody's did. Through it he found the mage's note, a yard above and going away, very still, and the next ring beginning in it, and the exact place the man was holding himself in the air.

He could not cut that. He was no mage. But he knew now where the man was, without any glyph to show him, in the dark; and he lit every node at once and went up.

He came up from below, where no man who had stood in the air for forty years had ever needed to look, and took the mage round the waist with his dead arm, and laid his charged edge flat against the back of the man's neck, not cutting, and said into his ear through the visor:

"Stop."

The mage stopped.

They hung there together in the white, Ward's suit holding both of them, Ward breathing like a bellows. Round them the other rings were going out as the knights cut them, and the rest of the mages, who had felt their best man taken, were drawing back up toward the Lantern, slowly, and then not slowly.

"You're old," said the mage, after a while, in the dry accent of Ostrel. He did not try to move.

"So are you," said Ward.

He brought him in on half his hearts and gave him to the apron for the cuffs, and sat down on the steel with his back to the coaming, and found he could not lift his left arm above the shoulder and that something in his chest moved when he breathed.

"Ribs," said Bettany, with her hands under his plate. "Two. Cracked, not broken. Sit there."

"I'm on the tower."

"You're on the apron until I've strapped them." She sat back on her heels. "Was that you? The one that fell?"

"Yes."

"On purpose?"

Ward looked out at the white.

"Yes," he said.



On the Lantern, when the mages came back seven, Orme understood it.

Two great hulls gone, one burning while black men in the air took her people off her like apples off a tree, one gone down level with men in black on her deck. Two small hulls gone into the white and not come out. The best mage he had ever had on the black ship, Ildan said, in iron. And the white lit from inside every few minutes in two places, never the same two.

"They did not hide from us," said Orme. "I thought they had hidden themselves. They have hidden us. From each other." He laid one hand on the rail. "Every hull of mine can see every other by her lamps, and none of them can see into that, and I cannot tell them anything inside it. And they can see every one of us, and talk in the dark without a lamp." He was quiet. "They are not fighting sixteen hulls. They are fighting one at a time, and we are letting them."

"Then we go in," said Orsolo.

Orme looked at his captain.

"The black one. The big one. Ildan has her; she pulls like a hill, he says. Calder has every flash she's made." Orsolo's jaw was set. "Her guns can't hit what they can't see either, so she fights close, and she shoots every hull we send out alone. Send the Lantern. She'll come to us. And when she's close, we'll be closer, and she can't hide from the hooks."

Orme thought about it for longer than a man thinks about a thing he has already decided.

"Every bleeder to the shell," he said. "And load the launchers."



Ruan felt the Lantern before he saw her.

A little after one, with the forward battery cold and the bay waiting in its red half dark, the deck stones began to tick faster, and faster, the way they did when something heavy came near; and the clerk read the duke's voice with no breath left in his own.

"The big one. Above you, coming down through the white. Two hundred yards. One fifty. Launchers..."

The first grapple hit the rail ten feet from the forward port.

Silver, thin, the hook as long as a forearm, out of the white above; it went over the rail and bit, and the cold came into the ship through it. Ruan felt it in his teeth. He was no delver and had never felt the K Amara's shell in his life, and he felt it go now, a long falling draining cold, as if the whole black length of her had been put into the river. A second line aft of the first. A third on the apron overhead. A fourth.

And the deck went soft. Not his boots; his boots held, iron to steel. The hold. The firm hand on his shoulders that he had stopped noticing a month ago went light, and his harness, trimmed for a hold that was not there, began to lift him by the straps.

"Trim up," he said, down the bay. "Boots and lines. Hook-cutters."

Every man turned the stone at his back and came down heavy on his boots and reached for the bar by his breech. Ruan had his in his fist already. He had drawn them in October and been laughed at in the yard for a week.

The Lantern came down out of the white over them.

She filled the forward port from edge to edge, a hundred yards up and fifty out, a white wall of oak and gilt with her fins going up into the fog like a cathedral's buttresses, and her dome of coloured glass so near he could see the leading between the panes. On her flank, a row of bronze launchers on swivels. From each, a silver line across the gap into the K Amara's rail.

And down one of the lines, a man.

He came hand over hand, fast, in a white surcoat over plate, a short blade at his belt and a coil of drain net over his shoulder, as if he had done it all his life; his own mana held him to the line as it would have held him to a deck. Temple guard. The K Amara's shell was the thing that stopped men like him at the rail, and it was nearly gone.

He was coming down onto the forward battery's rail, beside the hoist.

Tolliver was at the hoist.

He was clipped on. Ruan would see that for the rest of his life. Tolliver, who had forgotten his clip on the shed floor and again in October, was clipped to the jackline by the hoist with his boots locked and his trim up, reaching up with his hook-cutter for the grapple line where it came over the rail, as he had been drilled.

The temple guard came off the end of the line onto him.

Ruan was moving, six stations aft, click, catch, click, catch, one locked step at a time with his lanyard sliding behind him, no faster than a man walks; and he watched it all the way.

The man came down on Tolliver's shoulders with both feet, and the boy did not go down. He could not; his feet were iron to the deck. He went back from the knees like a tree in a wind and the jackline caught him at the waist and threw him forward again, into the man. The man was standing on the rail with one hand on the grapple line and the short blade out, a big man, looking down at the boy under him the way a man looks at a thing in his road; and he put the point to Tolliver's throat to push him aside with it, and said something short in the old accent that Ruan did not hear.

Tolliver could not get clear. He could not step back; his boots held him. He could not go sideways; the line held him. He could not go down. There was nowhere for him to be but under the man with the point at his throat, and the hook-cutter was in his hands, up over his head where he had raised it for the line.

He brought it down.

He did not aim it. Ruan was sure of that, and said so, once, years later, to the only person who ever asked him. The boy brought it down because it was in his hands, the way a man drops what he is holding when he is hit; and the hook came down past the man's shoulder and caught him behind the neck, above the edge of his plate, and the edge on the inside of the hook went in.

The man jerked back, and the hook came with him, and Tolliver pulled, because it was caught and he was frightened, once, with his whole weight, as he had been taught to pull on a line.

The temple guard came down off the rail onto the deck at Tolliver's feet in a long loose slide of white and plate. His blade rang on the steel. He moved his legs twice, and then did not.

Ruan got there.

Tolliver was standing over the man with the hook still in him and the bar still in his hands, clipped on, looking down. In the red lamp the blood on the steel was black. The boy's face was not doing anything at all.

Ruan took the hook-cutter out of his hands. He had to open the fingers one at a time.

Then he turned to the rail, to the line the man had come down, still there, taut, humming, the cold going into the ship along it, and hooked it with Tolliver's bar and pulled; and it parted like a fiddle string and went whipping back up into the white.

"Next," said Ruan, to the bay. "Next line. Cut them."



On the K Discovery, Emeric felt the man go out.

His sense had been on the K Amara since the Lantern began to come down, and every line that went into her was like a hand going round his own throat. He felt the shell draining out of her through them, faster than anything had drained it all night. He felt the boarders on the lines, bright and dense, hand over hand. He felt one reach the forward rail, among the small grey notes of the gun crew, and go white all at once, close beside a boy's note he knew; and go out.

He had felt notes go out before. He did not say anything.

"She's got seven lines in her," he said. "Her shell's going. She can't fire through her own lines, and she can't roll off with that thing sitting on her. Every launcher on the Lantern's laid on her."

Fletcher turned his head.

"Then they want something else to look at," he said, and his eyes went to the brass guard over the lever between the seats, and then to the man in the brown coat in the other seat, who was looking at it too. "Majesty. You'll want to hold onto something."

The king put one hand on the seat's arm and the other flat on the cabin wall.

"Is this it?" he said.

"Across her bow," said Emeric. "Close. Under her launchers."

"Under her launchers," said Fletcher, as if it had been his idea, which it had, and lifted the guard.

The K Discovery came out of the bank's edge on her quiet stones, nose down, and round under the Lantern's bow a hundred yards off it, a dark shape nobody on the Lantern had seen; and Fletcher pulled the lever.

The gate opened. The fire ring went down onto its seats. The iris opened.

Behind them the stern stone's roar went up, and up, past anything Emeric had heard it do on the sea trial or the night at Grier's house, into a scream he felt in the cabin's wooden walls and the bones of his face; and the K Discovery went.

She went the way a stone goes off a sling. The cabin wall came up at Emeric's back and held him, and the king's hand went white on the seat arm, and the Lantern's bow went across the forward glass faster than any ship had ever crossed anything; and in the after glass the can's lip came up from dark to dull red to the red of a poker in a forge, and the plume behind it shook the stars.

And on the Lantern's flank, every launcher swung.

Every man at every bronze swivel, laying his line on the black ship below, heard a scream go across his bow and turned to it, as any man turns to a scream in the night, and saw a red star going under the Lantern's nose much too fast, and swung after it. Three fired. Three silver lines went out after the K Discovery and fell short a long way behind her, curling, empty. And the men coming down the lines that were still in the K Amara's rail stopped where they hung and looked.

The K Discovery came down the Lantern's side, under the launchers, close enough that Emeric could see her gilt in the glare of their own can; and there were the lines, seven of them, silver, taut, going down across the gap. Two were right across their path, the after two, with men on them.

He did not lift a hand. He had his sense along both already; hemp plaited round a silver wire, wet with the cloud; and he found on each the yard of it between the men and the Lantern's launchers, and put fire into it, all at once, from inside. The wet went off in a puff of steam and the hemp after it in a puff of flame, and both lines parted a breath apart. The two men swung down on the loose ends against the Lantern's side like men on bell ropes, and hung there.

The king had been looking at the lines as they came. He had seen them catch, with nobody near them and no glyph anywhere. He did not say anything, and did not look round.

Then they were past, and Fletcher was pulling her up and away under the burn, the scream going away over the bank and the red eye with it; and in the after glass Emeric felt the K Amara come free.

Five lines left in her, every launcher on the Lantern swung the wrong way, every man on them staring after a red star; and on her forward battery Ruan's people at the rail with their bars, and Ward's knights on the apron above them with theirs. One. Two. The cold going out of her as each line parted. Four. Five. Her shell, what was left, coming back up inside her like a breath let out; and her held stones putting her over, her whole black length rolling away from under the Lantern's flank and down into the white.

Then she turned in the white and fired every gun she had into the Lantern's shell from three hundred yards.

The bank lit, and lit again. Escort one came up out of the dark from below and fired too, and the Lantern's shell lit with every round it stopped, a pale frost of light over the whole of her flank, again and again, so that from the K Discovery's after glass she looked like a lantern in truth, a white ship in a white burning cage; and every volley the cage was dimmer.

Fletcher put the lever down at four minutes. The scream died. The cabin was so quiet that Emeric could hear the can ticking, and the king breathing.

It did not stay quiet. Two small hulls came down out of the high dark at them from above, fast, close, with their launchers already swung; not for the K Amara. They had seen the red star go across the Lantern's bow, and they wanted the thing that had made it, and Emeric felt in them what he had felt in every white hull that night, the one order that ran under all the others. The gold-eyed man. Alive.

"Can's at the line," said Fletcher, looking at his dial. "It wants a quarter hour."

"It hasn't got one."

Fletcher lifted the guard again. The scream came back, higher, and the dial's needle went over into the red and stayed there, and the K Discovery climbed out from under the two small hulls with their lines falling short below her, five minutes, six, longer than the cutoff liked and longer than the potter's tiles had ever been asked for, until the strip on the can's shell lifted the fire ring off its seats for her and she went quiet again, high, alone, ticking like a stove.

"So that," said Harrold at last, to the forward glass, "is what it looked like." He took his hand off the cabin wall and looked at it. "From underneath. Going the wrong way." He put it back on his knee. "I'd have known it wasn't a falling star."



Kesrath's boats came in at a quarter to two.

Six of them, the north set said: the new landing boats Saban's man in the port had written of, forty men to a boat, rowing in along the Claim's shore with their lamps shuttered toward the beach under the dune; and on the dune Rylan's detachment in the sand with their faces blacked, and on the beach nobody at all.

Boats in the shallows. First keel on the sand.

Then a gap, and in it Emeric could see Rylan on the dune with the handset at his mouth, watching two hundred and forty men climb over the sides into the surf below him.

Lit them.

Flash rounds along the whole beach at once, and Declan's big lamps off the escort from the dune's crown, so that the sand went white as noon; and then, Emeric did not need to hear it, Rylan's ordinary voice calling down that this was the crown's beach and the crown was on it, and the first man to raise a weapon toward the dune would be the first man shot.

Nobody raised one.

Boats off. Back east along the shore. Nobody hurt.

And one more line, near four.

Tower's high mage is awake. Lamp on the tower. Watching us.



Orme broke north a little after four, with the Lantern and four.

The Lantern's shell had not broken. It had come down and down until there was almost nothing in it but the grey notes of men in her hold with their hands on her stones, and then she turned from the guns and climbed, and four climbed after her, one great hull low and labouring and three small, lamps talking all the way up. They went into the high dark in a line, a row of coloured lights over the hills, then stars, then nothing.

Nobody went after them. Nobody had the shells.

The tally came ship by ship over the fleet set, and Emeric took it himself and wrote it on the back of a bearing sheet, because Ferra would want it in his hand.

Four taken. The fourth great hull, landed whole in a stubble field east of the river with Osric's men on her and her crew sitting in rows with their hands on their heads; two small hulls anchored down into the water meadows by escort one's teams; and one small hull that came down onto the river flats of her own accord and put a white cloth out of her hatch, which her people had plainly been taught to do and never expected to need. Seven down: the burning great hull, pushed off the city by escort one's bow and down in the fields three miles east with her people off her before she touched; another great hull and five small, in the river and the fields and the marsh beyond the north road, every one a place Emeric had marked on Linus's map as it fell. Their crews in the cells on hull two and hull four and the K Endeavor.

Kaldo's knights hurt: nineteen, burned and broken, the laid-open arm, Ward's ribs; all on the K Endeavor's racks with Imani going down the rows. Mira's catch, and one of Arthur's from the burning hull's rigging, and Bren's, in the K Endeavor's book with the hour against each. The three who came down on their chutes in the dead air were all found, one on a haystack, furious about it.

The K Amara's shell stones: near dust. Seven lines had drained more out of her in ten minutes than every gun the Church fired at her all night. Escort one, forty rounds left. The K Amara, a hundred and six, by Ruan's chalk, which he read into the set himself.

The K Discovery's can: red, and staying red. Fletcher wrote that himself.

And the dead. The K Amara's clerk read her line last, and Emeric heard him stop before he read it.

None of ours.

Emeric wrote it under the rest in the same hand, and looked at it, and folded the sheet into his coat beside the leaflet. The king had gone out to the rail and was looking at the east, where the sky over the hills was not yet anything but darker than the rest.

The crews dosed by the card at five, out of Imani's racks, an ampoule each by the small Ottoline's card said and no more, and the clerks wrote down every one.



Tess had called at three, in the lull after the Lantern.

She had been on the home set all night with Ambrose, seven hundred miles south, and had not said a word since dusk that was not a relay, which Emeric would not have believed possible of her. Now she asked for Osric.

He came on from the stubble field, from the set on the landed hull, tired, in the clear.

"Her prow," said Tess. "Where the glass went. The stones behind it. Not the outside. The broken faces. What's on them?"

A long pause, a scrape, Osric breathing; and then he told her, like a sergeant and not a stone-wright. Lines. Very fine, inside the split, like the grain in a cut log. Finer ones across the grain. Places where two lots met and made a third kind running between them. Not cut, he thought. Grown, if a stone could grow. Like frost on a window. Like something written very small by somebody who didn't stop to think about it.

Tess did not say anything for a long time.

"Bring me one," she said. "A lift stone from the prow. Not the big one in the middle; the duke'll say don't touch that and he's right. In iron. Cold."

"Something wrong?"

"I don't know. I'll know when I've got it."

She had it when the first hull went home for shells, on her bench in the Motion Shop under an iron cover with the door locked and Bram's stool pulled up beside hers. She looked at the broken face through the glass for an hour, and Bram looked with her, and neither of them said anything. Then she opened SEAMS at a clean page and wrote one line across the top.

Keywork. Not the Church's hand. Not the tower's. Not the dockhand's. Not like anything I have.

And under it, smaller, after a while, the only question mark on the page.

Whose?



Ruan sat with Tolliver on the forward battery's shell locker till the light came.

The guns were cold. Most of the crews slept at their stations, clipped on, heads against the breeches; and the hold was back under them, firm and ordinary. Somebody had brought a sailcloth from the bosun's store, and the temple guard lay under it by the forward hatch, a long shape, with his short blade laid on top of the cloth because nobody had known what else to do with it. Bettany had come and lifted a corner and put it down again and gone away without a word. The steel by the hoist had been washed. It still showed a little in the red lamp.

Tolliver had not said anything. He had sat down where Ruan sat him after the last line was cut, and not moved. He was still clipped on. Ruan had thought about unclipping him, and decided it was the boy's to do.

Ruan was not a man who said things. He sat beside the boy with his hands on his knees and his own clip on the line, and waited.

Toward six the cloud at the port began to thin. The seeder had been shut off when Orme broke north, and the wind watch were asleep in a heap in the passage behind the bay, and the bank was tearing and going east with the wind at last like any cloud. Through it, far below, the capital began to show: grey, wet, its lamps still burning in their rings up the hill, the river black round it, and in the river the long dark shape of escort two.

Tolliver looked at it.

"He said something," said Tolliver. His voice was quite ordinary. "Before. With the point on me."

"I know."

"I didn't hear it."

"No," said Ruan. "Nor did I."

Tolliver looked at the port for a long while.

Then he reached down to his hip, slowly, and unclipped his lanyard from the line, and held the clip in his hand and looked at it; and clipped it on again.

Ruan sat on the locker beside him, and outside the port the east began, very slowly, to go grey.
