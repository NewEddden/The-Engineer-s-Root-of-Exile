Chapter 366 - The Card

Imani had brought the cards in a cigar box, because it was the right size, and she put it on the long room table on the fourth of September and took the lid off and turned it round so they could all see in.

They were plain buff card, the size of a hand, a hole punched at one corner for a string. Each had a name across the top in her slanted writing and a grid ruled underneath, and most of the grids were full: dates down the side, and against each date a number of hours, and a column of small marks Emeric knew from four years of reading them over her shoulder. A dash, nothing. A dot, the nose. Two dots, the gums. A ring, sent in. There were a great many cards in the box. The ones at the front were the oldest, soft at the edges, and the grids on them ran to the back.

"The Hod's hands," she said. "The K Amara's. The hulls' crews. The knights. Everybody who's ever sat the foot of the Band with the scrubber open under me." She took one out and laid it flat. It was Ned's. Two years of dashes, and before that, at the top, a long run of dots that thinned out and stopped. "That's what the wall's going to be. That's the whole question, on one card."

Ferra had the book open already. Saban had his hat. Ward was at the door. Bram had come up from the yard for it and had his apron off, which meant he thought it mattered.

"How many of us can breathe past the wall?" said Imani. "That's what I've been asked. In the thick water. Past where Zale's eyes went on Friday." She sat down. "And the honest answer this morning is: the few of us who've eaten a stone. The knights. Ned and the Band hands, and the war fleet's crews. Everybody else, I'd be guessing."

"Then they go," said Ward.

"They can't sail a convoy on their own," said Ferra, not looking up. "I don't know how many it is yet, nobody'll tell me. But Abloh's never been off the ground. Fletcher's never been off the ground. Half the people we need have never flown at all."

"Then feed the rest a stone," said Bram.

He said it the way he'd have suggested more rivets, and Emeric saw Ferra's pen stop, and Armand turn his head by the fireplace, and he said it before either of them could.

"No."

Bram looked at him.

"There aren't the stones. There are the tree's pods and what's left of the twelve, and every one of them goes through the Hearing, by name, on the Roll, and gets argued." Emeric put his hands flat on the table. "And say there were. Say we had a cellar full. I'm not making a ship's company of stone-eaters to go and meet the first people past the wall. I'm not making any more stone-eaters than the Hearing gives us. I told Ferra and Imani two years ago what that looks like a hundred years on, and I'm not doing it to sailors because it's quicker."

"The gold ones," said Ferra quietly, to the book.

"The gold ones."

Bram didn't ask. He looked at Emeric for a moment, and then nodded once, the way he did when he'd been told a weld wouldn't take and believed it.

"Then they stay aboard," said Saban. "The ones who can't. They sleep in the cabins at the mark and look out of the windows."

"For a year," said Imani. "However many it is. In a new country. Looking out of a window." She shook her head. "They'd go mad. I would. And we'd learn nothing. You don't make friends from a porthole."

"Then what?"

Imani took a second card out of the box and laid it beside Ned's. Then a third. They were Mira's and Bren's. Emeric knew them without reading the names; he'd sat at the chart table on the Hod in the winter of year twelve and written those first times down himself, while Mira held a handkerchief to her nose and went on reading gauges. Two and a quarter hours. Nearly three. And under those, on both cards, down and down, the dots getting further apart, and then the dashes.

"This," said Imani. "I've done it already. I didn't know I was doing it, but I've done it. Every hand on the Hod bled at the foot in their first year. After an hour, then an hour and a half, then two. Then they stopped. Nobody gave them anything. They just went up every week and sat in it, and their bodies learned." She tapped the cards. "That's induction. It's the same thing I did to her, a notch at a time. It's the same thing we did to the town on Hollow Night. You don't pour it in. You let it rise."

"Two years," said Ward. "It took them two years."

"It took them two years to stop bleeding at the foot with the scrubber shut. We've got seven months, and we don't need that. We need them to bleed late." She looked round the table. "And then we need to do it the way you'd climb a mountain."

They looked at her. Emeric didn't; he'd seen where she was going two cards ago, and something in him had gone quiet and pleased the way it did when somebody else got to a place first. Somewhere on the other side of everything, men had climbed mountains so high the air at the top would kill you, and they'd done it by going up and coming down and going up again, and sleeping lower than they'd climbed. He had never once mentioned it to her.

"Work high, sleep low," said Imani. "Go up in the day and do what you came for. Come down at night and let the body catch up. A notch higher next week. That's the voyage. Past the wall, everybody goes ashore by day, and swims, and trades, and walks round, and at night the ones who aren't ready sleep aboard in cabins I've set, at the mark. And every few nights I raise the mark a notch. And in a month most of them are sleeping ashore too."

"And if somebody's not ready," said Ferra, writing.

"Then they go back aboard and lie down and I have a look at them. And they've a mask, for the bad days. Spent-stone cartridge, same as the scrubber, same as the new suit's pack. That's the backstop. It's not the plan."

"Who goes?" said Saban.

"Whoever's card says so." Imani put her hand flat on the box. "That's the gate. Everybody who sails carries one of these with their hours on. And nobody sails who bleeds at the foot with the scrubber shut. Not early. Late's all right. Late I can work with. Early, they stay home."

"That's a hard line," said Ward, after a moment.

"It's your line," said Imani. "From the Hod. I'm only writing it on a card."

Ward looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked at Emeric, and something passed across his face that Emeric couldn't read, and he said, "The knights are past it."

"I know they are."

"All of them. Every man who's flown the war fleet since year twelve. Every one of Arthur's lot. They've sat the foot so long they don't feel it." Ward folded his arms. "That's not the trouble with the knights. The trouble with the knights is the other end."

"Go on."

"They hold their mana in. That's what a knight is. It pads them. And every hour they sit in thick air, it comes in, and comes in, and they don't spend it." He looked at Imani. "You told me in year thirteen what happens to a body that takes in more than it can hold. I watched nine in ten die of it on the rite field before I knew your name. I'll not have it on a beach a thousand miles from home because a man was enjoying the view."

"So they spend it," said Imani.

"So they spend it. All day. On purpose." He unfolded one arm and held his hand up, and Emeric felt it, the long low heat of the knight's way coming up through him, enhancement held and held and not let go, and then, deliberately, something else: a burning, an opening, mana going out of him into nothing at all, the way a man opens the valve on a boiler. "Like that. You hold it and you burn it, and it can't build. A valve. It'll make them slow. It'll make them hungry. But it won't make them dead." He let the hand fall. "I'll teach it at the foot. Every man. That's the last stage."

"And when they get slow?" said Ferra.

"Imani's card," said Ward. "She'll tell you the signs. Heavy. Yawning. Head. Talking slow. Then the nose." He looked at Imani, and she nodded. "Any man who goes that way stops spending and stops breathing it, and goes back aboard and lies down at the mark, and eats, and sleeps. And it's not a punishment. I'll put the next man in the brig who calls it one."

"And the ones who aren't knights?" said Saban. "They can't hold anything. They can't burn it."

"They work," said Imani. "They carry, they row, they haul. A man who's dug a trench all day isn't storing much. And they can cast through a stone, badly, the way anybody can, and it'll cost them, which is the point." She shut the cigar box. "And they sleep aboard a while longer than the knights. That's all. It's not fair. It's just bodies."

Ferra wrote. When she'd finished she ruled a line under it and wrote a heading over it, and turned the book round so they could see: The wall: acclimation. The card is the gate.

"Who starts?" she said. "The ones who've never flown. The ones we need who've got no card."

Imani took the lid off the box again. At the back there was a little stack of new cards, white, with nothing on them but a name.

"Abloh," she said, reading them. "Bettany. Dagny. Fletcher. Linnet Shaw, off the island, Fletcher's operator. A dozen hands off the yard and the galley. And Maren, if she wants to cook for us." She squared the stack. "Every week on the Hod's charging run, the whole autumn and the winter. Sit the foot under my watch. Kaldo's bread at every meal, and the induced crops, the way the town's had on Hollow Night. Seven months won't make a veteran. It'll make them bleed late, and late's enough." She put the white cards in the front of the box. "Next Tuesday."

"Somebody won't make it," said Ward.

"Somebody won't make it," said Imani. "Somebody never does."



Maren had been told to bring a coat, and she'd brought her good one, the dark red with the black buttons, because nobody had told her what kind.

The Hod went up from the yard at seven on the ninth with the racks empty and twenty-six people aboard, and Maren stood at the rail with both hands on it and watched Kaldo go down under her: the castle and the wards and the long curve of the harbour, the glass towers along the river catching the sun, the trams on the shore road like beetles. Then the whole of it, small and round, the walls in a ring, with the coast going off north and south and the sea all grey and shining. Then cloud. Then the top of the cloud, which she hadn't known had a top.

"Oh," said Maren.

It lay below them in every direction, white and heaped and still, like a field after a snow, with the sun on it so bright you couldn't look. And above them the sky went on getting darker. Not night; blue, and bluer, a blue she had never seen in her life and didn't have a name for, a blue like the bottom of a well.

"Four miles," said Ned, from the wheel. "That's the foot. Hold on to something, I'm going to stop."

He stopped. Maren held on to something. It turned out to be Fletcher.

"Scrubber," said Imani, and Ned reached up and turned a long brass handle over his head a quarter turn, and something in the pump line sighed, and that was all.

Maren waited for it to feel like something. It didn't, much. The air was cold and thin and the light was very hard, and that was the same as it had been a minute ago. Then, slowly, over the next quarter of an hour, she began to feel it the way you begin to feel wine: a warmth, behind the breastbone, nothing unpleasant, and a sort of fullness in her ears, and a feeling that she was sitting in a hot bath with all her clothes on. Arthur had said that, years ago, in the castle kitchen, about the Band. She'd thought he was showing off. She put her hand on her chest.

"That's it," said Imani, beside her, with the clock in her hand. "That's what it feels like. Sit down if you want. Don't spend anything. Breathe ordinary."

"I'm a cook," said Maren. "What would I spend?"

There were knights along the far rail, eighteen of them, Ward's, with Arthur's three at the end, and Ward walking along behind them with his hands behind his back saying the same thing to each one quietly as he passed, and they were doing something Maren couldn't see. She could feel it, very faintly. A heat off them, like standing near the bread oven with the door shut. They stood easy and looked at the cloud, and the heat came off them steadily, and every now and then Ward stopped behind one and said something, and the heat changed.

"Burning it off," said Imani, seeing her look. "So it can't build up. They've been sitting in this for years, it doesn't touch them coming in. It's the going out they've got to learn." She looked at the clock. "Watch Bren."

Maren watched Bren. After a while, he yawned. He tried not to. He put his hand over it, and then he yawned again, and leaned on the rail a little more than he had been.

Ward was beside him at once.

"Say your name," said Ward.

"Bren Tallis." It came out slow, as if Bren were reading it off something a long way away.

"Below. Lie down. Eat whatever Imani's put out." Ward said it without any weight in it at all. "You're done for the day."

"I'm all right."

"I know you are. Below." And Bren went, and Ward watched him down the hatch, and walked on along the line as though nothing had happened, and Maren saw Mira, further down the rail, look after Bren with a face like a woman who'd lost a bet and was going to collect on it later.

"That's the drill," said Imani. "That's all it is. You go slow, you go down. Nobody makes anything of it." She wrote on Bren's card: a time, and a small square, which was new, and which Maren supposed meant sent below for slow.

Maren lasted forty minutes.

She knew it was forty because Imani told her. She'd been standing at the rail with her hand on her chest feeling warm and rather pleased with herself and thinking about whether the cloud deck would look better or worse at sunset. She'd been working out, in the back of her head, whether a person could get yeast to rise up here, and how long it would take. And something warm ran out of her nose and down her lip, and she put the back of her hand to it, and it came away red.

"Oh," she said, in a quite different voice from the first time.

Imani had her by the shoulders and sitting down on a coil of line before she'd finished saying it, with her head forward and a cloth under her nose, pinching. "There. Pinch, here, that's it. Lean forward. Not back. Never back." The cloth was warm against her face, and Imani's hand on the back of her neck was cool. "It's all right. It's nothing. It's the first time. Everybody's first time."

"Not everybody," said Maren thickly. She could see Abloh on the next coil of line, perfectly dry, looking at her with concern. And Fletcher, perfectly dry. And Dagny, who had sat through it with her eyes shut and her jaw set, and was looking at Maren now the way people look at a stranger who's fallen over in the street.

"Most," said Imani.

It stopped in a quarter of an hour. Maren sat on the line with the bloody cloth in her lap and looked at it and felt, all at once and to her own great surprise, a fury so complete that she had to look at the cloud for a while until it went down.

Then she laughed. It came out a bit cracked, with her nose still thick, and she wiped her lip with the back of her wrist.

"It's the coffee," said Maren. "I had three cups. I always have three cups on a Tuesday. It's thinning the blood, that's what it is."

"It might be," said Imani, who knew perfectly well it wasn't, and wrote on Maren's white card, in her small slanted hand, the date, and 0.40, and a dot.



Ferra was in the stern with the cards on a board on her knee, writing them up as Imani passed them back, when Ilenne came up the hatch.

She'd been below. She'd been below on purpose, because Imani had wanted the first quarter hour of the scrubber shut to be a clean test, and Ilenne was the densest thing aboard but Emeric, and nobody could say what the Band would do to a body built for thick water. Imani had thought nothing. Armand had thought nothing. Ilenne herself, asked, had shrugged. But nobody knew, and Imani had put her in the cabin at the mark with the door shut and the clock running, and told her to come up only when called.

She came up the hatch at the half hour, called, and stood on the deck of the Hod at four miles over the sea and looked round.

Ferra watched her look. She'd seen Ilenne in the tank, and on the beach, and in the bay house pool, and at the wall water in the bright of her own sea, Imani had told her. She had never seen her somewhere that had no water in it at all. There was none here. Not a drop: the sky was empty of it and dark, the cloud was far below, the air was as dry as an oven and so cold it hurt the teeth. Ilenne stood in the middle of it with her hood back and her face turned up to the blue.

Something happened to her colour that she couldn't do anything about. Ferra saw it go. It wasn't any of the colours on Imani's card. It went up from her throat in a long slow brightening that kept going, past the pearl, past the brightness at the wall water, into something nearly white, the way the cloud deck was white in the sun; and her hands came up a little from her sides, open, as though she were standing in a current and feeling it go through her fingers.

She didn't bleed. She didn't yawn. She stood there in the Band as if she'd been made for it, which, Ferra thought, was a thing nobody had considered and Imani would want to write down.

Zale came up the hatch behind her.

He wasn't supposed to be on the Hod at all. He had a card, a good one, from his years at sea and his ship's runs, but Imani hadn't cleared him for the Band after his eyes at the wall water, and he'd come anyway, and said he was there to look at the louvres on the Hod's flank and had a note from Bram to say so, which he had probably written himself. He stood on the deck on his stick with his coat buttoned to the neck and looked at Ilenne in the dark blue light, and then he looked at the cloud, and then he took his coat off.

It was the long blue sea coat he wore everywhere. He held it out to her.

"It's cold up here," said Zale.

She looked at the coat.

She wasn't cold. Ferra could have told him that. Ilenne had stood in the bay at midnight in January in her shift; she had lain on the bottom of the bay house pool for twenty minutes at a time with the water so cold it made Ferra's hand ache to touch it. She didn't feel the cold the way they did. Everybody aboard knew it. Zale knew it better than anybody.

She took the coat.

She put it on. It came to her knees on him and to the middle of her shins on her, and the sleeves stopped short of her wrists, and she buttoned it, one button, the top one, with some care, and then stood with her hands in its pockets looking out over the cloud as if that were what she'd come up for.

A little colour came up at her throat. Not the white. Blue. Deep, like water a long way down.

"There," said Zale. "You see. Cold. You've gone blue with it."

"Yes," said Ilenne, gravely. "Cold."

He stood beside her in his shirt in the Band's dry air, which was colder than it had any right to be, with his stick and without his coat, and didn't say anything else.

Ferra looked down at the board on her knee. She had Maren's card on it and Bren's, and the pencil in her hand, and Ilenne's card under them both, where Imani had put it, with nothing on it yet. She held the pencil over Ilenne's card for some time.

She didn't write anything. She found she was smiling at her pencil, and she put it away.



Maren went up again the next Tuesday, and bled at forty-four minutes, and the Tuesday after that, at fifty. Imani wrote each one down with a dot. Maren said it was the coffee every time, and on the third Tuesday she didn't have any coffee at all, on principle, and bled at forty-eight, and didn't say anything, and looked at the cloud.

That night, in her kitchen, after closing, with the chairs up on the tables and the range banked, she took her recipe book down off its shelf. It was a fat green ledger, stained, nineteen years of her own hand in it and some of her mother's pasted in the front. She sat at the end of the long table with it, and a pencil, and went through it slowly, page by page. Here and there, beside a dish, she made a small mark in the margin.

She didn't tell anybody what the marks were. If she'd been asked she'd have said it was nothing. She was a cook. She was only thinking about what would keep.
