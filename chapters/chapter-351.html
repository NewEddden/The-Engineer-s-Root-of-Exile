Chapter 351 - Thin Water

On the sixth morning of the honeymoon Ferra brought the ledger to the beach, which was against the rules, and opened it at the column, which was worse.

Emeric watched her do it from the shade of the third palm. She sat on Abloh's long knit robe with her knees up and the book on them and her hair pinned high with two of the yard's marking sticks, and she ran her pen down the page without writing anything, the way she did when she was reading a sum to find out whether she still believed it.

"Go on," said Imani, without opening her eyes. She was lying on her front on the sand beside him in the high knit bottom and the palm-print shirt knotted under her breasts, with her chin on her folded arms and her hair piled on the back of her neck to keep it off the sand. "Read it out."

"I'm not reading it out."

"You brought it to read it out. Nobody brings a ledger to a beach to keep it shut."

Ferra turned a page back, which was a way of saying yes.

"First of November," she said. "Arrive. Second. Emeric to the Dust House, one hour." She looked over the top of the book at him. "Four hours. Imani to the sick bay, one hour. Five. Ferra to the stores, one hour..."

"How long?"

"It doesn't say." She turned a page forward. "Third. Emeric to the Dust House, one hour, which was six. Fourth, he was there before I woke up and I've written unknown. Fifth, Imani took a man's thumb off at the slab."

"Put it back on," said Imani. "I put it back on. He'd caught it in a chain. It's a very good thumb now, better than it was. Write that down."

"I've written it." Ferra looked at the page for some time. Under the column's head, where on the morning after the wedding there had been No work struck out and Less work struck out under it, there was now a third line, in a smaller hand, and that was struck out too.

"What was the third one?" said Emeric.

"Some work."

Imani laughed into her arms.

So Ferra had made a rule, and the rule was in the book, which in Kaldo was the same as its being in the charter, and it said that from eleven o'clock on the sixth of November there was no Dust House and no sick bay and no stores, and that anybody who came along the shore from the works with a figure for any of them would be sent back with it. She read it to him. She had read it to Fletcher, the night before, at the works pier, and Fletcher had taken his hat off.

It was a quarter to eleven. The beach ran pale and empty along the inner shore under the old grove, the fan palms leaning out over it in their long half-wild row, and beyond the palms the hills went up green to the carved face, and the falls behind it, which he could hear when the wind dropped. Out in the shallows the Cradle lay long and squared off on the water with a hull on its deck under the berth's canvas and nobody working on her, because it was the yard's rest day and Fletcher kept it, and the whole east bay was the colour of the inside of a shell.

Nobody had come along the shore with a figure in an hour. Emeric had been listening for it.

"You're doing it," said Imani.

"I'm lying under a tree."

"You're lying under a tree working out the mill's grit." She opened one eye and looked at him along her own arm. "You've got the face. The fifty-mesh face."

"It's forty."

"Ferra," said Imani.

"Eleven o'clock," said Ferra, and shut the ledger, and looked at the sun, and decided that it was.



The walk was Imani's idea. Ferra would not admit this at any point during it.

They changed into the white under the palms, out of the beach things, because Ferra said a person could not walk round a bay in a knit robe without looking like something the tide had left, and because the white had been made for this. The jackets that crossed at the front and tied. The sashes, and Ferra's belt with Abloh's chain on it that did nothing. Imani turned his sleeves back for him without being asked and then turned her own, and Ferra did up his sash again, because he had done it wrong, and Imani pulled the end straight after her, because Ferra had.

"I can tie a sash," said Emeric.

"You can tie a knot," said Ferra. "That's a different thing."

They went east along the sand, away from the works, with the sea on their right hand and the grove on their left. Ferra had her sandals in one hand by their straps before they had gone a hundred yards, because the sand was warm and the sandals had cork soles and a history, and she walked at the edge of the wet where the small waves ran up over her feet and back, holding her trousers off the water with the other hand.

"Whose idea was the walk?" said Emeric.

"Mine," said Ferra.

"It was mine," said Imani. "I said it last night. You said we'll see. That's you saying yes in ledger."

"I said we'd see if there was time."

"There's always time. You write the time."

"Then I wrote it," said Ferra, "and that makes it mine," and walked a little faster, through the wash, so the spray came up round her ankles.

Imani had his arm. She had taken it at the end of the grove, the way she had taken it on the castle hill since the summer, her hand in the crook of his elbow and her shoulder against his, and she steered him by it, which was how he came to be on his knees by a tide pool on the rocks at the bay's first point before he had noticed they'd stopped walking.

The pools lay in a shelf of dark rock the sea had left at the half-tide, each one a bowl of still water so clear that it looked like air, and in them the ordinary small life of a shore that nobody had ever walked: the animals that had never seen people, as Arthur had said the first winter, and didn't know yet that they ought to mind. Imani knelt beside him with her white trousers on the wet rock, which Abloh would hear about, and put her finger on the water without breaking it.

"Bed one," she said. "Anemone. Adult. Closed." She moved her finger. "Two. Limpet, three of them, stable, not going anywhere. Three. Crab. Small. Male, I think. Hostile."

The crab, under its ledge, opened its claws at her.

"Hostile," said Imani, with satisfaction. "Four. Whelk." She bent closer. "No. That's a whelk shell with a hermit in it, and he's outgrown it. Look at him, he's hanging out of the back. He wants a bigger house." She looked up at Emeric. "Discharge him."

"Into what?"

"There's a bigger one there. By your knee." She pointed. "I'm not touching him, I'm on holiday."

He put the bigger shell into the pool beside the hermit, and they knelt and watched the hermit consider it for some time, as a man considers a house he can't afford, and then come out of the old one in a pale soft rush and go into the new one backward and sit there in it, insulted.

"There," said Imani. "Better than half my ward."

Ferra had come back along the rocks to stand over them with her sandals in her hand.

"Star," she said, and pointed with the sandals at a pool further out, where a sea star lay orange on the bottom with one of its arms half grown.

Imani looked at it. "That one's regrowing," she said. "Look. The fifth arm. It's lost it and it's growing it back." She looked at the star with a professional sort of envy. "It doesn't need a feeding sheet, either."

"It's a star," said Ferra.

"I know it's a star. I'm saying it's showing off."

The first time they pulled him off the path it was Ferra.

It wasn't much of a path. It went up over the point through the coarse grass above the rocks, where the gulls nested in the spring and the ropes Emeric had put round the nesting ground still stood, sagging, on their stakes, and it was there, between two of the stakes, that he said something about the mill. He hadn't meant to. It was only that the grass on the point was the same colour as the dust in the forty-mesh trial and he had said so aloud. Ferra stopped on the path in front of him and turned round and took the front of his white jacket in her free hand, where it crossed, and pulled him off the path into the grass by it, and kissed him with her sandals knocking against his back.

"Ferra," he said, when he could. "The mill..."

"The mill will keep."

"The grit's..."

"It'll keep," said Ferra, "it's grit," and kissed him again, and let go of his jacket and smoothed it flat where she'd held it, and went on up the path as though she had stopped to look at the view.

The second time was Imani, at the top of the point, where the path went round a slab of grey rock and the whole of the east bay opened out below them, wide and blue-green and still to the far shore. He had stopped to look at the works pier from up there, a mile and a half back along the curve, and had begun, very quietly, to count the hulls tied up at it. Imani put her hand flat on his chest and walked him backward two steps into the lee of the rock, where the wind didn't come, and kept him there with the one hand while she took her time.

"Seven," he said, afterward. "There are seven hulls at the..."

"They'll keep."

"I wasn't working. I was counting."

"You were counting so you could work later." Imani looked at him from very close, with her hand still on his chest, and he felt two of her fingers settle where they always settled, and begin, and stop. "That's working," she said. "Ferra, he's working."

"I heard," said Ferra, from the path. She had sat down on the rock above them with her feet bare and her sandals in her lap, and was looking out to sea, and had not, by the look of her, been looking out to sea the whole time.



The seal was on a flat rock below the far side of the point, where the shore turned south toward the bay's outer headland.

It was a grey seal, a big one, lying along the rock at the water's edge with the small waves washing up round its tail end and back, fat as a rolled sail and mottled like wet slate, and it had seen them coming down the path some time before they saw it. It lifted its head and looked at them. It didn't move otherwise. It had the face of a dog that had been told something about them by another dog and didn't think much of it.

Emeric let his sense go out, as he did on every new stretch of shore, and touched it. An ordinary animal. A warm slow life, the sea's own thin mana in it and no more, the same as every seal on every beach on the island and every gull above them, nothing in it that wasn't seal. He went further, without quite deciding to, along the shore both ways and back over the point, and found gulls, and the seal, and the small cold lives in the pools, and nobody. Not a man. The works were well out of reach. Nobody was on the far shore, or on the water, or anywhere in the arc his sense could hold without hurting.

He knew that it was the only reason he said it.

"Where I'm from," he said, "sailors used to swear they'd seen mermaids."

Ferra looked at him and then at the seal. Imani, who had sat down on a dry rock to brush the sand off her feet, stopped brushing.

"For hundreds of years," he said. "Every navy, every fishing town. Half women and half fish, sitting on rocks. Singing, some of them." He looked at the seal, which looked back. "And not all the stories were about the singing. A lot of them were about what a year at sea does to a man with no women aboard. What he'd do if he met one."

"Do," said Ferra.

"Do."

"With a fish."

"With the woman half. In theory. The stories weren't very clear on the details." He sat down on the rock beside Imani. "The thing is, nobody ever found one. Not one. Not a bone. So in the end the scholars decided that what the sailors had been seeing, all those years, out on the rocks, was seals. Seals, and a sort of big slow grey thing in warm water that eats grass under the sea. Far enough off and long enough at sea and anything with a face starts to look like a woman." He paused. "And they reckoned some of the sailors had done more than look."

Ferra stared at him. Then she stared at the seal, fat and grey and whiskered on its rock, with its belly in the wash.

"Are you saying," she said, "that they did it to that?"

"I'm saying that's what the scholars said."

"That."

"Or one like it."

"The whiskers," said Imani.

They both turned to look at her. She was looking at the seal with her healer's face, the flat one, the one she used on a man who had told her how he had come to have a cooking pot stuck where a cooking pot didn't go.

"I'm only asking," she said. "As a professional. Because it's got to weigh, what, four hundred pounds? And it's got no hips. Nothing to hold on to. It's all one shape. And they bite. And it's wet, it's cold, it's always wet, there's that whole layer of fat." She held her hands up a little way apart, measuring the seal from the rock. "I just think a man would want to think hard about the logistics before he got anywhere near the whiskers."

"He'd been at sea a year," said Emeric.

"Then he'd want to think harder."

The seal heaved. It went off the flat rock all at once, in one long graceless slide like a sack going down a chute, and hit the water with a flat smack that threw spray up the rock, and was gone, and came up again ten yards out with only its head showing, looking at them.

"It heard you," said Ferra.

Imani had started laughing at the smack and couldn't stop. She put her face down on her knees. Ferra had gone red to the fringe and was laughing too, the high helpless one, holding her sandals against her chest; and the seal watched the three of them from the water with its round dark head, offended past all bearing, and sank out of sight as slowly as a seal could, so that they would see it go.



They went on down off the point to the next stretch of sand, where it ran dry and deep and white above the tide line and the grove came almost to the edge of it, and a shelf of the grey rock threw down a wedge of shade. Imani got there first, sat with her back against the rock and her legs out, and said that was the walk done.

"We've come half a mile," said Ferra.

"I know exactly how far we've come. That's the walk done."

Emeric stood at the edge of the shade and looked back along the bay and saw none of it.

"There," said Ferra.

"There what?"

"That." She had sat down with her arms round her knees and her chin up. "You've been at it since the seal."

"I've been looking at the water."

"You've been looking at her. Then at me. Then at the water, so it comes out even."

"Averages," said Imani, with her eyes shut.

"He thinks we can't see him do it."

"He thinks a great many things."

"I was working out the walk back," said Emeric.

"You were not."

"It's a mile and a half to the house."

Ferra looked at him. Then she looked down, slowly, with enormous deliberation, at the sand.

"Here," he said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You looked at the sand."

"I looked at the ground. The ground's there. I'm allowed to look at it."

Imani had opened her eyes.

He put his sense out along the shore both ways, which took a second and a half and was something he no longer decided to do, and there was nothing on the path and nothing on the water and nothing on the far shore; and the works lay a mile and a half back behind the point with Fletcher's rest day on them.

"Are you both all right with that?" he said. "Out here, I mean."

Ferra considered the bay. There was nothing on the whole of it.

"Who's going to mind?" she said. "It's not as if the fish care."

"The seal might," said Imani. "We've hurt its feelings once already today."

Abloh's white had not been built for sand, and the sand got into all of it at once. Both jackets crossed at the front and tied, and both came undone with one pull of the sash, which Abloh had also known perfectly well. Ferra came down off her rock onto her knees behind Emeric and had his over his shoulders and down to his elbows, and left it there, and held him by it, which made twice today.

"The grit," said Ferra, against his ear, "will keep."

"I didn't say the grit."

"You were going to."

He got Imani down into the warm sand above the tide line with his hand under her head, to keep the sand out of her hair, which was hopeless and both of them knew it. His locs came down round her face, which was only fair, and he knew without looking that Ferra had seen him think it. Imani hooked a heel round the back of his knee and looked up at him through his hair, with the sea-light coming in at the edges of it.

"Slower," she said. "I'm on holiday."

Ferra kept hold of the jacket. With his arms half pinned at the elbows by it, everything he did he had to do slowly, which was what Imani had asked for, and after a while it came to him that the two of them had arranged this between them somewhere on the walk, probably at the seal. Ferra's mouth was on the back of his neck under his hair. Imani had both hands flat on the small of his back under the white, holding him to her, and was watching his face from an inch away with the look she wore when a thing was going exactly the way she had said it would.

There was salt on her throat and sand on his knees and Ferra's breath on the back of his neck, and the sun on his shoulders through what was left of the white; and for a stretch that nobody could afterward put a figure to, which annoyed Ferra later, Emeric was not thinking about anything at all.

Then he was.

He went still, with his hand under Imani's head.

"Don't you dare say the mill," said Ferra.

"The fish care," said Emeric.

His sense was half out, as it always was on that shore, held in a loose ring round the three of them, and at the outer edge of the ring, out toward the bay's mouth, something came into it. Not the seal. Not anything that lived in the bay. It came in off the open water between the headlands, moving fast and low, and it was dense.

He stood up without meaning to.

It was dense the way nothing in home water was ever dense. He had felt the sea round the island every day for two summers and it was thin, thin as the land, a faint even wash with the fish and the seals moving in it like warm specks; and this went through the wash like a lit stone through a dark room. It was the size of a person. It had a person's warmth, a life's warmth, and round it and through it there was more mana than he had ever felt in anything that moved by itself, more than in Armand on the morning after the stone.

It was moving wrongly. It came in fast and then it didn't, and then it did again, in bursts, like a swimmer who was all out of breath and had decided not to stop. And it wasn't steady. The mana in it was strong and it wasn't steady at all.

"Emeric," said Imani. She had stopped laughing. She was on her feet beside him, and he saw her eyes go unfocused toward the water, and knew she had felt it too.

"What?" said Ferra. "What is it?"

"Something's come in," he said. "Off the open sea. Into the bay."

"A whale?"

"It's not a whale."

The Catch was the first thing he thought of and he put it down at once. It lay on the far side of the island off the north-west reef, and it had spent itself on a passing whale the week they came out, and it would not be full again before December; and whatever this was, it wasn't being pulled. It was swimming. It was swimming, as far as he could tell, for its life.

It came across the bay's mouth and turned, away from the works, away from the grove and the Cradle and the hulls, toward the far shore, the empty one under the outer headland where there was nothing but a strip of grey sand and the rocks; and it slowed in the shallows there, and stopped, and he felt it come up out of the water.

He saw it then, with his eyes, across the better part of a mile of bright water. A figure in the shallows on the far shore. It stood, or tried to, and went down, and got up.

"That's a person," said Ferra.

It went forward through the wash onto the sand on its hands and knees, and stopped, and lay down.

Emeric was already on the rocks. He heard Imani say his name, and Ferra say something sharper, and then he was at the water's edge, and he didn't stop there.

He had done it in the Mere and for Fletcher at dawn, and it had never once been easy. He pushed down through the soles of his feet against the water under each, the way a held stone pushed against the ground, and the bay took his weight for a breath, as ice takes a skater's, and ran out from under him, and the next foot had to be down already on fresh water before it went. So he didn't walk. He ran. A walk was too slow for a long way. A run was a long string of short ones, every one caught. The bay was flat and the swell low and long, and he ran on the rising faces of it with his knees loose and his stride short, his sandals slapping the water and the water slapping back, in a line of small white bursts across the blue, and the far shore came toward him faster than any boat could have brought it.

Halfway across he saw it lift its head.

It had been lying on the sand with its face down. It came up on its arms now, as he came, and looked out at the bay, and he saw it see him: a land man in white, running toward it across the top of the sea.

It went still. Then it tried to go back into the water.

It didn't get far. It dragged itself a yard toward the wash on its arms with its legs trailing behind it, and the legs moved, badly, in the wrong order, as though they belonged to someone else and it had only been lent them, and it got its hands into the water and stopped there, and stayed there, with its head turned to watch him come. Its skin was changing colour. He could see it from fifty yards. It had been pale when it came out of the water, a grey-white nearly the colour of the sand, and as he came on it went darker, in patches, slate over the shoulders and down the arms, as if a cloud were crossing it from the inside.

He stopped running. He stopped thirty feet out, in the shallows, where he could stand on the bottom, and let his feet go through the water onto the sand under it, and stood up to his shins in the sea with his empty hands held out from his sides where it could see them.

It looked at his hands. Then it looked down, at his feet in the water, for a long time, and back up at his face. It made a sound. It was low and short, with a hard catch in the middle of it, like a word in a language that had clicks in it, and it was a question; he could hear that it was a question, and he had no idea what it asked.

"It's all right," he said, which meant nothing to it, and he knew it, and said it anyway.

He came in through the wash slowly, and knelt on the wet sand an arm's length off, and looked at her.



She was a woman. He had been sure of that since the bay's middle and he was sure of it now and it explained nothing.

She lay propped on her arms half in the wash, a long body, longer than Ferra, lean and hard-muscled across the back and the shoulders the way a swimmer's is, with her legs out behind her on the sand. She wore something close and dark from breast to hip, slick and greenish, that wasn't cloth and wasn't skin, and nothing else. Her hair was plastered flat to her head and down her back. Her feet were wrong. He saw that first, of everything, because they were the nearest: they were perfectly ordinary feet, long, narrow, with ordinary toes, and the soles of them were as soft as a baby's and cut to ribbons. She had come four yards up the sand on them and the shells had done the rest.

The wash came up round her and went back. Every time it went back she shut her eyes.

And her mana was enormous. Up close it was like kneeling beside a furnace with its door open; it came off her in a press he could feel on his face. And it was going. It wasn't steady because it was going somewhere, out of her, the way heat goes out of an open door, and he couldn't tell where.

He heard the oars before he turned. A works skiff was coming across the bay from the point, very fast, with a girl at the oars pulling as though the boat had insulted her mother, and Ferra and Imani in the stern; and the girl was Wren.

Ferra's sash was tied the way he tied it. There was sand all down one side of Imani's hair and neither of them had a word to say about any of it, because they had both been looking at the far shore since the point.

She had been out on the bay all week in the skiff, Emeric remembered, sounding for the Dust House's new outfall with a lead line, because she'd come out in October to learn the mill and the mill's master had found that she could row. She ran the skiff up on the sand twenty yards along the shore, hard, so that Ferra went forward into the thwart, and was out over the side and up to her knees before it stopped.

Imani was out first, though. She came up the sand with her white trousers wet to the thigh and her face already changed, all the laughing gone out of it and the other thing there in its place, the ward thing, and she went down on her knees at the woman's side without stopping to look at Emeric at all.

The woman's head came round, and her skin went dark from the throat to the shoulder all at once, like ink in water.

Imani stopped. She sat back on her heels and held out her hands, open, the way she held them out to a child or a soldier who'd come round fighting. She didn't move them. She waited.

The woman looked at the hands. She looked at Imani. Then she looked at Emeric, for some reason, as though he were the one who would know whether this was allowed, and he didn't know, and kept still. She put her face back down on her arm.

"Thank you," said Imani, quietly, to the back of her head, and put two fingers on the inside of her wrist.

Ferra came up behind with the ledger, which she had brought in the skiff, which she had brought on the walk, which she had sworn on the beach she had left under the palms with her robe. She went down on the sand on the woman's other side. Wren stood a little way off with the skiff's painter still in her hand.

"Pulse," said Imani. "Slow. Very slow." She didn't say what she thought of it. "Strong, though. Breathing..." She stopped.

The woman was breathing. Her back rose and fell, ragged, through her mouth. But Imani had moved her hand from the wrist to the jaw, to turn the head a little and clear the mouth of sand, and under the jaw, at the side of the neck, where the hair lay plastered, there were cuts. A row of them, long and straight and side by side, running back from under the ear. Imani had seen them and reached for them as cuts. And as her fingers came near them the cuts opened, all together, by themselves, and showed something dark red and feathered inside, and closed again flat.

Imani took her hand back.

She knelt there on the wet sand with her hand held a little away from her and looked at the side of the woman's neck, and the cuts opened again, and closed, slow, in time with nothing, and in the wash that came up round them they opened wider and stayed open while the water was there and shut when it went back.

"Those aren't cuts," said Imani.

"No," said Emeric.

"Those are gills." Imani said it the way she would have said a man had two hearts: flatly, as a finding. "She's got gills. And she's breathing air. Through her mouth. Both." She looked at the other side. "Both sides."

Ferra had the ledger open on her knee and the pen in her hand and had written nothing. She was kneeling by the woman's other shoulder looking at the gills, and the feet, and the legs that lay on the sand behind her as if they had been put there, and the skin that had gone back from dark to grey-white while Imani's fingers had been at her wrist and was going dark again now, slowly, at the shoulder nearest Emeric.

"You said," said Ferra, not looking up, "there weren't any."

He didn't answer that. He knelt in the wash in his white with the sea going in and out round his knees, and looked at what was lying on the sand.

Magic had been real from the first day. He had built a town out of it, and a fleet; he had walked the cave under the Isle of Shards and stood in front of the golems the old races had left in the dark, and read their hands in the stone; he had stood on this island's slab with the carved face over him, and known that other people had lived here and done it first. He had known for a long time that it was another world. He'd known what sort. And not once in all that time had he let himself say the word for it, even in his own head, because it had seemed like a child's word, for a child's kind of book, and he was a man with a mill to run.

He said it now, in his own head, once. It was a stupid word. It was the right one.

"She's what my tail was," said Wren.

They all looked round. Wren stood with the painter in her fist and her face very white and her eyes enormous, eighteen years old and knee-deep in the bay, and she said it again, louder, because nobody had answered her.

"She's what my tail was supposed to be. Abloh's tail. The sea people. That's one." She pointed with the painter. "That's a mermaid. She's a mermaid and she's got legs." Her voice went up. "I said. I said if one came out of the sea it'd be stuck. I said it'd be no good on land, you'd have to be carried. I said that. She's not stuck, she's got legs, but look at her, she can't use them, she's the same as stuck..."

"Wren," said Ferra.

"...she's the same as I was..."

"Wren. Breathe."

Wren breathed. She stood in the water with the rope in her hand and did it, in and out, as if she'd been told to by the mill's master, and her colour came back a little. The woman on the sand had turned her head at the noise and was looking at her. At the girl, and the rope, and the skiff on the sand behind her. Her eyes went from the skiff to Wren and back.

"She's looking at the boat," said Wren, in a smaller voice.

"Yes," said Imani.

"She doesn't like the boat."

"I wouldn't either," said Imani, "the way you brought it in." She hadn't taken her eyes off the woman's back. "Emeric. Here."



The wound was low on her back, on the left, a hand's width above where the dark close garment ended at the hip. Two holes, side by side, about a hand apart and level with each other. Each was round and clean-edged and a little puckered, the width of a thumb, and wept a thin pinkish water instead of blood, and the skin round both had gone the colour of an old bruise and stayed that colour while the rest of her changed.

Imani looked at them for some time without touching.

"Two," she said. "Same size. Same depth, near enough. Same angle, in and slightly up." She laid her flat hand in the air above them, not touching, measuring. "One thing did both at once. Something with two points on it, and somebody behind it, putting it in." She looked at the angle again. "From behind. She was going away from them."

"A fork," said Ferra.

"Something like." Imani bent closer. "Not a hook, not a rock, not teeth. Teeth tear. That's been made."

Emeric thought of a trident with one tine gone, and a fork with two tines, and kept both thoughts where they were.

"Can you see in?" he said.

"Not here. Not without asking her, and I can't ask her." Imani sat back. "I can close the skin on the shallow one. Stop the weeping. That's all I'll do on a beach." She looked at the woman's face, which was turned toward her on the sand, the eyes half shut, the gills going slowly in the wash at the neck. "If she lets me."

She held her hand up again where the woman could see it, open, and then laid it, slowly, flat on the sand beside the woman's ribs, not on her. The woman watched it. Imani moved it, slowly, an inch at a time, and the skin under it went dark, and then, when the hand stopped and stayed, a little less dark, and Imani laid her palm on the small of the woman's back beside the wound, and kept it there, and nothing happened, and the woman let her.

Emeric felt her work. He always could, when he was this close: the thing she did, the reaching down into a wound and talking it shut. She went in at the right-hand hole, which was the shallower, and he felt the edges come together under her, slowly, and seal, and the weeping stop. She went toward the left one and stopped.

"No," she said, to herself.

"What?"

"Later." Imani took her hand away. "Not on the sand. I want her lying flat, and dry, and a lamp. Something's wrong in that one." She wiped her palm on her white trousers, which were past saving. "And I want salt water. A lot of it. Those gills are drying." She looked up, at Ferra and at the skiff and at the bay, the way she looked down a ward. "She came out of the sea. I'm not putting her somewhere dry until I know she can be dry."

"The Cradle," said Ferra.

Imani looked at her.

"It's in the water," said Ferra. "It's in the bay, it's got cells under the deck, it's got the yard's pumps. Fletcher can flood a cell to any depth you like in half an hour. And it's sworn." She had begun writing. "Nobody on the Cradle isn't sworn. Nobody walks there from the town shore. If we carry her past the works there'll be four hundred people on the pier by the time we land her and every one of them will write home."

That was how it went, after that. Emeric saw it happen and couldn't have said when it began. They were on the sand in the white in the middle of the sixth of November with nothing on any page but a tick and a struck-out line, and then they were at work, the three of them, and the work was the same as any work: Imani at the body, Ferra at the book, and he was the one who had to lift things.

"Wren," said Imani. "Bail. Fill the skiff's bottom with sea, a hand deep. She goes in it wet."

Wren went.

"Emeric. You carry her. You, not the girl, you won't drop her. One arm under the knees, one under the shoulders, and keep it off that wound. Keep her face to the side so the gills are free."

"She won't like it."

"Then go slowly. Let her see your hands first. She looks at you." Imani frowned, briefly, as if that were a symptom she'd come back to. "I don't know why she looks at you."

He showed her his hands. He knelt where she could see him and put them out, both of them, open, palm up, and then slid one under her knees, slowly, the way Imani had moved hers, and felt the skin on the back of her legs go cold and dark at the touch, and waited, and it lightened a little. Her legs were heavy. She was heavy all through, heavier than she looked, dense in the plain way as well as the other, like a body made for pressure. He got the other arm under her shoulders and lifted her off the sand, and her head came round against his chest and she made the low sound again, the one with the catch in it, very close, and her breath was cold, and she smelled of the deep sea, of cold salt and something under the salt he couldn't name.

Her hand came up and took a fistful of his white jacket where it crossed, as Ferra had done on the point, and held on.

He carried her down to the skiff. Wren had the bottom boards awash. He laid her in it on her side with her face to the thwart, in a hand of sea, and the gills opened at once and went on opening and closing, slow and wide, and the woman's whole body loosened against the boards like a fist coming undone.

"Oh," said Wren, at the oars. "She likes that."

"Row," said Ferra, getting in. "Gently."

"I'm always gentle."

"Row the way you didn't before," said Ferra, and Wren rowed.

Emeric didn't get in. There wasn't room, and the boat sat low enough as it was. He walked beside it on the water with one hand on the gunwale, which was easier than running, because the boat held some of his weight, and he looked down over the side as he went at the woman in the bottom with her eyes shut, breathing the bilge. Once, halfway across, she opened them, and they went past the gunwale to his feet on the bay, and stayed there; and she watched him walk on the sea all the way to the Cradle's edge and didn't make a sound.



Fletcher cleared the cell in twenty minutes and flooded it in twenty more, and said nothing while he did either.

He had been on the Cradle's deck when the skiff came across the bay, at the berth with his hat on and his rest day in his face, and he had watched the king walk on the water beside a rowing boat without taking his hat off at all, because he had seen it before. He had taken it off when they lifted her out onto the deck. He held it now, against his chest, at the head of the ladder, while two of his yard men ran the hose down into the cell from the deck pump and the sea came in.

The cell was one of the hollows under the Cradle's deck, the old fishers' cells, cut in the slab like the hold of a ship. This one was twenty feet by twelve and had been the yard's rope store; the rope was on deck now in coils, and the floor below had been swept, and a bench had been carried down and set against one wall, and the floor was flooding to the depth Imani had asked for, which was a foot, so that a body lying in it on its side on a canvas sling would have its neck and its gills under and its mouth above.

"Does she want it deeper?" said Fletcher.

"I don't know what she wants," said Imani. "Nobody does. I want her breathing, and I want to see her back." She was down the ladder already in her ruined white with her sleeves past the elbow and her satchel, which somebody had run along the shore from the palms for her, open on the bench. "A foot."

"A foot," said Fletcher, to the hose, and to his men. "And then off. And then you never saw this. Either of you."

The yard men looked at him, and at the cell, and at the king, and said they never had.

There were eleven of them, in the end, who had seen her: Fletcher and the two at the hose, the four who had been on the Cradle's deck when the skiff came in, two more who had come running along the shore from the works when somebody saw a man walking on the bay, the mill's master, who had come after Wren, and Wren. Ferra wrote all eleven down. She sat on the Cradle's deck by the head of the ladder with the ledger on her knees, where she could hear Imani below and see the shore, and wrote them in a column by name, with their trade and where each had been standing.

Then, under them, she began a new page.

She wrote the date at the top of it. Then a line, which she ruled with the edge of her pen case. Then she stopped, and looked down the ladder into the cell, where the woman lay in the shallow water on the sling with the lamp over her and Imani on her knees beside her in the wet, and the light coming off the water onto the ceiling.

"What are you writing?" said Emeric.

"A register." Ferra didn't look up. "She's on the island. Everybody on the island goes in a register. Name, trade, where from, who for, sworn or not." She looked at the empty line. "I haven't got any of those."

"Leave it blank."

"I don't leave lines blank." She sat with the pen over the page. Then she wrote, in her ordinary hand, and turned the book a little so he could see it.

Guest, South Sentinel. Came ashore at the east bay's outer shore, 6 Nov, about noon. Not sworn. Not asked.

"Guest," he said.

"She's on our island and we didn't ask her here. That's a guest." Ferra blotted it. "And she hasn't said she'd like to stay, so she's not anything else."

Below them in the cell the woman had turned her head on the sling toward the sound of their voices, and was looking up the ladder, past Imani, at them. Her skin in the lamplight was nearly colourless, grey-white, with a sheen on it, faint and silver-blue, that moved when she moved, the way the light moves on the inside of a mussel shell. Where the water lay against her neck the gills went slowly, wide open.

Imani said something to her, very low. Not words; Imani's ward voice, the one that didn't need words, and the woman turned her head back and shut her eyes.

"Is she asleep?" said Ferra.

"No. She's letting me." Imani had her hand flat on the woman's back above the wound again. "She's been swimming a long way, I think, and not eating. I'll tell you the rest tomorrow. I'm not saying things I don't know."

"The other hole," said Emeric.

"I said tomorrow."

He went down the ladder anyway and knelt on the bench end above the water, out of her light, and let his sense down into the cell, carefully, the way he would have reached into a box with something sharp in it.

It was still enormous. It filled the cell. Kneeling there was like sitting in the next room to a furnace. And it was less than it had been on the beach. He would have sworn to it. Not much; a little, the way a lamp is a little dimmer after an hour and you only know because you've been reading by it. And under the ordinary going there was something else, low on the left side, that he couldn't make out: a place where the note of her went thin and wrong, like a sound with a crack in it.

"You feel it," said Imani, without looking round.

"Yes."

"Then you know why it's tomorrow." She went on with what she was doing, which was cleaning the sand out of the cuts on the soles of the woman's feet with a pad and a basin of sea, one cut at a time, while the woman lay with her eyes shut and her skin darkening in a slow wave from the ankle up every time the pad touched and fading every time it lifted. "Go and tell somebody. Somebody should know who isn't us."



Word went back to Kaldo on the evening hull in a sealed pouch with Ferra's seal on the cord and nothing on the outside of it but a name.

She had written it on her knee in the last of the light, and it said nothing a man opening it by mistake could have used. Something has come ashore here that you will want to see before anyone else does. Come yourself. Bring nobody you'd have to explain it to. And her initial. The hull's master put it inside his coat and was gone before the light went off the water.

They didn't go back to the beach. Somebody from the works brought their things along the shore in a basket, the knit robe and the beach things and Ferra's hair sticks and the sandals, with the cork soles, both of them, and set the basket down at the head of the Cradle's ladder very carefully and didn't look down it. They slept on the Cradle, in the yard's watch room at the end of the berth, on two cots pushed together and Abloh's robe over the three of them, with the door open on the deck and a lamp turned low and the sound of the bay against the slab's side.

Or Ferra and Imani did. Imani went down the ladder twice in the night, and came back up cold to the knees, and lay down again without a word and was asleep in a breath, with her hand on his chest. Ferra slept with the ledger under the cot where she could reach it. Emeric lay between them and listened to the bay, and once, very late, went to the head of the ladder and stood there in the dark with his sense down in the cell.

The woman was awake. He could feel that she was. She lay in the water in the dark under the deck, not moving, enormous and leaking, and somewhere above her a land man stood at the top of a ladder doing nothing, and he had no idea whether she knew.

He went back to bed. Imani said "Hm" into his shoulder without waking, and Ferra put her cold foot on his shin, and he lay in the middle and didn't sleep, and in the morning they both looked as though they'd slept nine hours in a field.



Saban came at a quarter to ten, on Zale's ship.

They heard her before they saw her, which was new. The Endeavor's class came in over a bay with a sound like a long breath through teeth, from the engines; this one came in nearly silent, on the veins, with the air going through her flanks in a low steady rush, and she came down out of the west over the hills and the grove and settled on the water off the Cradle's end as lightly as a gull, and Zale put her alongside on a rope's length without bumping. Fletcher, at the Cradle's edge with the line, said something to himself about the paint.

Saban came over the side first, in his grey coat, with a dispatch case under his arm and his hair flat from the wind, and looked round the Cradle's deck once, at the yard men, who had all found work at the far end of the slab, and then at the three of them coming toward him.

"Ferra," he said. "Bring nobody you'd have to explain it to."

"Yes."

"I've brought Zale," said Saban, "because he's the only man in Kaldo with a ship that could get here by ten, and because I've never once in my life had to explain anything to Zale. He doesn't ask." He looked at Emeric. "He'll ask about this, won't he."

"Probably."

"Good," said Saban. "Show me."

They showed him. Imani took him down the ladder herself, and he went down it in his coat holding the dispatch case over his head as though the cell might be deeper than it looked, and stood on the bench end out of the water in his town boots, with Imani beside him in her scrubs, which she had sent for at dawn, and looked.

The woman was sitting up.

She hadn't been, at dawn. Imani had left her lying in the sling and gone up for an hour, and when she came back down the woman had got herself up out of it onto her hip in the shallow water, with her back to the wall and her legs drawn up in front of her and her arms round them, and her neck in the water at the bend so that the gills were under. Her hair had dried overnight into something dark and long that hung over her shoulders. She had watched Imani come down the ladder. She watched Saban come down it now, and her skin darkened at the shoulder, a little, and stayed.

Saban looked at her for a long time. He looked at the gills, going slowly under the water at her neck, and at the legs, and at her face, and at the dressing on her back, which Imani had put on at dawn and which had a pale stain coming through it already, on the left. His face didn't do anything. Emeric had seen Saban's face not do anything at a hanging and at a birth and once at a ball, and this was the same face, and it went on not doing it for longer than any of those.

Then Saban turned round on the bench end, carefully, so as not to get his boots wet, and looked up the ladder at the deck, where Zale's head and shoulders had appeared over the edge against the sky.

Zale had come to the top of the ladder and stopped there, one hand on the rail, too big for the hatch. He wasn't looking at Saban. He was looking past him and down, at the woman in the water against the wall, and she had looked up when his shadow fell across the hatch, and was looking back.

Neither of them did anything. Zale stood at the top of the ladder in his sea coat with the light behind him and looked at her as he looked at weather a long way off, a long, plain, considering look, and she looked back at him past Saban's shoulder with her arms round her knees.

Then her skin moved.

Emeric had watched her colour all night, the slow slate that came up under Imani's pad and went down again when it lifted. This went faster. Something passed over her from the throat across both shoulders and down her arms, light and dark and light, the way wind goes over barley, and it was done before he was sure he'd seen it. Under his sense her note jumped, once, and came down. Then the slate went out of her, slowly, back to grey-white. At her throat, where the gills went under, a colour stayed a breath longer than the rest. Blue, if he'd had to name it. The blue of deep water from a boat on a clear day.

Zale took his hand off the rail and went back across the deck to his ship, without a word, to see to his lines.

"Well," said Saban.

He had been standing a yard from her. He was still looking at her throat.

He came back up the ladder and stood on the deck in the sun, looking across the bay at the outer shore, where a smear on the grey sand still showed where she had dragged herself out of the wash.

"How many?" he said.

"Eleven," said Ferra. She had the page open. "Besides us. The last one's Wren."

"Rylan's Wren. And she's eighteen." Saban didn't say it unkindly. He said it as a figure. "All right. Here's the first rule, and it's the only one today, and I want it in your book in my words." He waited until Ferra had the pen up. "Nobody leaves this island with this."

Ferra wrote.

"Not in a letter. Not in a sealed letter. Not in a sealed letter to the king." He nodded at Emeric. "Not in their heads, either, if I can help it, but I can't, so: nobody on that list goes home. Not on leave, not on the packet, not for a funeral, until I say. They're paid double from today, Ferra, which you'll hate, and they're told why they're paid it, which is that they've seen something that will start a war if it gets to the wrong table, and the wrong table is every table." He looked at the cell hatch. "And anyone else who sees her goes on the same list on the same terms. That includes me. I'm not going home either, tonight."

"You're wanted in Kaldo on Thursday," said Ferra.

"Kaldo can miss me. It does." Saban looked down at the bay. "Who else knows on the mainland?"

"You," said Emeric. "And whoever saw you get on Zale's ship."

"Nobody saw me get on Zale's ship. I got on it at the far end of the yard at eight in the morning in the rain." He considered it. "Harrold will have to be told. And the knights. Not by letter." He turned to Emeric. "That's you. Not yet. When we know what she is."

"We don't know what she is."

"No," said Saban. "Wren does, apparently. Ferra wrote it down." He tapped the ledger's edge with one finger, where Ferra had written, at Wren's insistence, mermaid (Wren) in the margin beside the guest line, in brackets, in a much smaller hand. "I'd like to know where she learned the word."

"Hollow Night," said Ferra. "Abloh made her a tail."

Saban thought about that. "Then half the children in Kaldo know the word," he said, "and every one of them has worn it in the lane for sweets." He thought about that too. "Good. If one of my eleven gets drunk in a year and says it, nobody grown will believe him." He put his case under his other arm. "Where's Wren now?"

"At the mill," said Ferra. "Working. The master took her back up at dawn. He said she was more use there than standing on the Cradle staring."

"Was she staring?"

"Since six."

"Good man," said Saban. "I'll talk to her this afternoon." He paused. "She'll ask if she can tell her father."

"She already has," said Ferra. "I told her you'd say no."

"I'll say her father's a knight, and he'll be told, by me, and he'll be told that she kept it." Saban looked at the cell hatch again. "That'll hold her. She'll want him to hear that more than she'll want to tell him."

He went off along the deck toward the watch room, and halfway there turned round.

"And the eleven are not to call her that," he said. "Anything. Not the word. Not a name. They call her what your book calls her."

"Guest," said Ferra.

"Guest," said Saban. "That's a word a man can say in a taproom and nobody turns round."



At noon Imani came up out of the cell for the third time that day and sat on the deck by the hatch with her back against a coil of the yard's rope and her eyes shut, and Emeric sat down beside her, and they did it together without saying they were going to.

It was easier with two. He had learned that in the ward, since the sleep, when Imani's sense had come fully open and they had begun to sit by the same bed and read the same patient, each from their own side, and check each other. He let his sense down the hatch and felt hers go down beside it, a different touch, finer and slower, the way her hands were finer and slower than his. They found the woman in the water against the wall where she'd been since dawn, and lay their sense along her, carefully, the way a man lays his palm along a wall to find the warm pipe in it.

Then they came back up, and opened their eyes, and looked at each other.

"Less," said Imani.

"Than this morning."

"Than this morning. Than last night. Than the beach." She leaned her head back on the rope. "She's still more than anyone on this island. More than Armand, more than you, more than anything I've ever had on a table. She's still a furnace. But it's going out of her, Emeric, and it's not going out the way a man gets tired. A man gets tired and he sleeps and it comes back. This doesn't come back. It's going out somewhere, and it's faster on the left."

"The hole."

"Something in the hole. I can't see what. Not without her letting me look, really look, and I don't know how to ask her." She opened her eyes. "And I closed the shallow one on the beach, and it's open again. Not much. A thread. I closed it this morning, too." She looked at her hands. "Twice. It doesn't hold."

Ferra came along the deck from the watch room with a mug in each hand, and gave them one each, and sat down on the coil above them with her own, and the ledger, and waited to be told.

Imani told her. Ferra wrote it. Not the words; the figures, such as they were, the way she'd write the charge on a stone: Noon. Strong, less than dawn. Shallow wound reopened (2nd). She ruled a line under it.

"Again at dusk," she said.

"Again at dusk," said Imani.

"And then every morning and every noon and every dusk," said Ferra, "until somebody tells me why," and drank her coffee, which was the yard's, out of the yard's kettle, and only a little burned.

They did it again at dusk.

The sun had gone down behind the hills and the grove, and the bay had gone from shell to slate to something darker, and the lamps were lit along the works pier a mile and a half off, small and yellow, seven hulls' worth, and nobody on the Cradle but the eleven and the five and the woman in the water. Saban had talked to Wren and come back and said she'd held. Zale had not come off his ship since the morning except once, at four, to stand at the top of the ladder for about the time it takes to read a gauge, and go back.

Ferra sat by the hatch with the ledger open at the guest's page and the pen ready. Imani sat with her back to the rope and Emeric sat beside her, and they let their sense down together into the dark of the cell, into the low sound of the water against the walls, where the woman lay in the shallows on her side now with her gills under and her eyes, he thought, open.

The note of her was there. It was still the loudest thing on the island. It filled the cell and came up through the hatch and pressed on his face like the heat from an oven door.

It was fainter than it had been at noon.

Not much. If he hadn't sat there at noon with Imani beside him and felt it, and held it in his head all afternoon as a figure, he could have told himself it was the same. It wasn't. It was less, and the crack in it on the left was a little wider, and somewhere in the dark under his sense the woman shifted in the water, and was still.

He opened his eyes. Imani had opened hers already and was looking at the hatch.

"Write it," said Imani.

Ferra wrote it.
