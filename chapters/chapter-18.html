Chapter 18 - Held

He threw the deadzone into the seam between the two mages, and the world around them went quiet in the way only a mage could hear.

The cheap water-stone hit the ground and vented all at once, its whole small charge dumped out crooked and wrong, and the field for a stone's throw in every direction went slack and dead. Both grey-robes reached for the braided cast they had been building and found nothing to hold. Their charged crystals, the fat ready ammunition on their belts, went inert in the same breath.

Emeric had killed magic in a circle, on purpose, without casting anything bigger than a thrown rock, and for one held second the whole enemy center had no mages in it at all.

Then the deadzone did the thing he had gotten wrong.

He had tested the stones out past the empty quarters where there was nobody to hurt, and he had read their reach off that empty ground and trusted the number the way he trusted numbers.

But a field with men and charged steel standing in it is not empty ground. The null spread further and dirtier than his clean little test had promised. It rolled out past the mages and washed over the near end of Kaldo's own sortie line, and Rylan, who had pushed up too close reading the same moment Emeric had, felt every crystal in his Enhanced harness die at once.

One heartbeat he was the most dangerous thing on the field. The next he was a very good soldier in a suddenly ordinary suit, in the front of a fight, on dead gear, because his lord had misjudged a falloff by twenty paces.

Emeric saw it happen and had no time to feel it, which was its own small mercy and its own accusation.

He had stood on a threshing floor and told himself never to trust a fixed number when he could read the real one, and had written it in his own hand, and had built a foundry around it, and then had taken a figure off an empty riverbank and carried it onto a field full of men and steel and never once asked whether the ground had changed. It was the same mistake as the air-line angled for iron that wasn't this iron and the clean page that nearly cut a loyal man. The mistake was always the same mistake wearing new clothes.

He filed it, because filing it was the only thing that let his hands keep working, and his hands still had one thing left to do.

Renner sat his horse at the center of the mageless circle, rallying the charge he thought was still coming, the one man whose falling would take the coalition's nerve with it. He was across open ground and inside the dead zone, which meant no spell in the world would reach him, which was exactly the case Emeric had built the slug for.

He braced the tube on the parapet, sighted down a hundred paces of dead air, and fired.

The air crystal shoved the rifled cone off the muzzle with a violent crack that took the hearing out of his right ear for the rest of the day and put him backward into the stone behind him. After that it was not magic. The deadzone had nothing to say to it. It crossed the null as a plain fast heavy thing obeying nothing but the arithmetic of its own flight.

At a hundred paces, through a slit in a helm, Emeric did not see a face. He saw a shape on a horse stop being upright. That was all it was from the tower. A man sitting, and then a man not sitting, and a banner behind him carrying on for another two paces before it understood.

There was no flash and no sound out there that anyone would have separated from the rest of the noise, and that was the part he had built for and the part he had not thought about. He had spent a season making sure nothing about the slug could be turned off by an enemy, and had not once considered that the same design meant a man would die without ever being given the chance to know what killed him.

He lowered the tube. His hands were entirely steady, which he noticed, and which he would think about later at some length.

For one long second nobody on the road understood what they had just witnessed. Then the men nearest the horse did, and the second after that the men behind them did.

The center broke first, as centers do when the man they are watching goes down and their mages have gone silent and the air itself has turned against them, and the break ran outward.

The coalition knights, magicless, their fire dead and their leader down, did the only thing a hundred committed men can do, which is come on anyway. Out of the deadzone and up the open approach at the gate, still churning forward on the momentum of the ride and on orders given by a man who was no longer mounted and no longer relevant.

And Kaldo answered with the last thing the stale map had never accounted for.

The gate opened.

They came out in four columns, not a wall. Ward on the left flank, Declan to the right, Osric center-left, Rylan center-right. Each at the head of a tight file of five, the whole sortie no wider than four men abreast, moving fast off ground Ward had walked every morning for a season and the coalition had seen for the first time an hour ago.

Rylan was on dead crystals, every charged piece in his harness gone inert when the deadzone rolled over him, and the field did not know it and he did not intend to show it.

Ward moved first, and the way he moved was the thing that cracked the coalition's shape before the swords did. One moment he was at the edge of their sight, a black figure coming out of the gate. The next he was somewhere else, somewhere a man could not have reached in the time it took the eye to follow, and then he was there again, inside the front rank, past the second, the gravity hammer already falling.

The weightless upswing was nothing, a tap. The landing was a man going down through his own armor under a weight he had no frame for, and the one beside him had half a second to understand he was now the front rank before Ward arrived in front of him too.

Declan cut the right flank before it could wrap, moving in the same grammar, that wrong-distance blur that did not resolve into steps a watching man could count. Osric drove the center like a post through ice, not swinging wide, always straight, leaving the men beside his line for the five who followed him.

They were not elite. They had never been elite. They were men who had been drilled for a season into knowing exactly what to do in the space the man ahead had just opened, and they did it without gap, because gap was the one thing Ward had refused to tolerate since the first week.

Not heroes. A system. Four systems moving at different speeds through the same crowd.

The cast-out men were in the front of that crowd. They had come up the road telling the coalition what to expect, and the men who guide you to a place are the men you put first when you get there.

One crossed with Osric's file and had time to recognize the man directly behind Osric, a garrison regular he had ignored for two years running because the man had been quiet and paid on time and done nothing worth noticing. The recognition went both ways. It was a clean engagement and a brief one, and when it ended the file had already moved past.

A second made it twenty paces in before Declan's angle found him. He had been one of Wexler's senior men, the kind who borrowed tools from the lower garrison and did not return them, and had not thought twice about any face in that lower ward, because those faces had not seemed worth the thought. He had time to think that, and then he did not.

The rest of the cast-out men peeled away before the columns reached them, because they had spent the ride up the road knowing this would go one of two ways, and they could read, by the time the gate opened, which way it was going.

They had a map. They knew where the grain was.

The coalition did not break cleanly. It shredded, front to back, each piece dissolving the way ice goes from the edge nearest the fire, not all at once but in pieces, each one changing the calculation for the men behind it. The ones at the back had not yet reached the killing ground. They saw the front going wrong and they slowed, and then the ones behind them slowed, and by the time any of them arrived at a decision it was the same decision, which was away.

What was left of the coalition ran, and Kaldo let them run, because chasing a routed enemy off your own killing ground with two dozen men is how you turn a victory into a field of your own dead.

The last two grey-robes went with the rout, stripped of every charged stone they carried and worth nothing in a fight now and knowing it. Emeric marked it, because two mages who had stood inside a dead circle and felt a thrown rock reach into their hands and take the magic out of them were two men who would remember exactly how it had been done, and carry the memory back down the road to whoever had sent them.

He had shown the trick to survive the day. He could not now un-show it.

They had won. Emeric knew it the way he knew a furnace had lit, by the numbers going the right way, and he had exactly long enough to know it before Ward's hand closed on his arm and turned him toward the town, toward the smoke that was not the foundry's.

The granary was burning.

The rest of the cast-out men had done the one thing their stale map was still good for. They did not know the new wall's weak points or the twenty's drill or the depth of the trenches, because none of that had existed when they left. But they knew where Kaldo kept its grain, because the granary sat where granaries had always sat, and it had not moved.

While the field was being won, a handful of men who no longer had any stake in winning it had slipped to the one thing they still knew the location of and put it to the torch. The seed for next spring. The reserve against the winter. The whole cushion Emeric had built the barony toward, going up in a heat that had nothing to do with any furnace, and the men who lit it dying in the alleys around it because there was nowhere left for them to run and they had known that when they lit it.

The unarmored dead were laid out that evening, and there were more of them than there should have been and exactly as many as Emeric had known there would be when he decided who got steel and who did not.

Archers, mostly. Townsfolk who had stood behind stone in cloth and caught the arrows the good armor would have turned. He walked the row himself, because the version of him that had walked past Tam without asking his name was a man he had promised not to be again, and he made himself learn every name in the row before they were covered, and none of it brought a single one of them back, and he had known that too.

They held the vigil at the closed gate, the whole town that was left, eighteen hundred less the ones under the cloth.

Nobody cheered the victory. They counted it, the way Bram had said they counted everything. The field won and the grain burned and the neighbors' banners gone back down the road they had come up, and they set the win against the cost and did not pretend the two cancelled.

What Emeric said to them was short, because Kaldo had no patience for a speech and he had less than usual for himself that night. He said it would not happen again. He said he had built a thing worth taking and had not, until today, built the thing that would keep it, and that he had learned the difference at a price other people had paid, and that he would spend the rest of his time here making sure the ones who had paid it were the last.

It was not a speech that flattered him. He had stopped writing those.

And the town, which had counted him all season and found him wanting in the one way Bram had named, watched the man who had beaten a hundred with two dozen stand over the row of the dead and take the weight of them onto himself in front of everyone, and closed ranks behind him. Not because he had won. Because he had not lied to them about what winning cost.

Out on the road, Baron Paxton of Milford took his unspent hundred home without ever committing them.

He had come up with a strength that could have decided the day in either direction and had spent the whole of it standing still, which was going to be awkward with Grayrock and Wayford and was going to matter a great deal less than either of them thought, because Grayrock had no baron now and Wayford had forty fewer men than it had ridden out with.

What he took home was a simpler sum than admiration. Three baronies had gone at a broke plague town and two of them had come back broken by it, and the ground that had done it had been a ruin a season ago. Whatever was down there was going to keep growing, and it now had every reason in the world to remember who had ridden up the road with the others.

Paxton had thirty years of watching lords rise and had never once profited by being late to that particular decision. He went home resolved to be on the right side of it before somebody made him choose.

He did not know whose vendetta he had just spent his neighbors' men on. Neither did Emeric, quite, though he had felt it at the edge of the day, in the way three nervous barons do not muster and march inside a single week without a colder hand somewhere behind them lighting the fuse and standing well back.

He filed that with the burned grain and the row of the dead and Rylan's dead crystals and Tam's name and a sentence about a sister in Wayford, the growing account of things he understood the shape of and could not yet reach the bottom of.

That was the thing the fire had truly cost, in the end, and it did not show up in the row of the dead.

The granary had been the cushion, the margin, the whole reason the coming harvest had only ever needed to be adequate. Now the harvest did not need to be adequate. It needed to feed a barony through the winter with nothing behind it, and refill from ash everything the fire had eaten, and it needed to do it for a place that had just spent its last coin on the foundry and the wall and the steel that had saved it.

Emeric had won the field and lost the one thing that let him afford to wait. Ferra had the ledgers open before the vigil candles were cold, and the number at the bottom was the same number it had been the night the foundry lit, only worse now, and running out of time in a way it had not been that morning.

He already knew, in the way a man knows a thing he has not agreed to yet, that there was one road that put coin in a barony's hand fast enough to matter, and that the whole design had been built never to take it.

Everything he had made here he had made so that no single thing could reach in and stop it. Parallel ditches. Bins instead of a vault. A furnace that fell back to charcoal. A wall that did not need him standing on it.

A debt was the opposite of all of it. A debt was a hand on the barony belonging to somebody he could not audit, could not build a fallback for, and could not out-engineer, and it would tighten on its own schedule and not his.

So he went at the other thing first, because there was still another thing, and he intended to spend the whole of it before he let himself look down that road again.

"The foundry pours all season," he told Saban. "Find me men who'll pay now for iron they get later."
