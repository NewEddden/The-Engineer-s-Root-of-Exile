Chapter 18 - Two Sets of Books

He needed the barony's accounts read by somebody who was not from the castle, was not on his payroll, and could not be leaned on.

That was the arithmetic of the mill road. Whoever had reached Tam had reach inside the keep, and the books lived there, and so did every clerk who could read them.

That was three conditions and they eliminated everybody in Kaldo except one person.

She kept the forge's books. She had corrected his arithmetic to his face at her father's table. And she did not care about him even slightly, which was the condition that mattered and the only one he could not buy.

He went to Bram to ask permission.

Bram did not stop hammering. He finished the stroke, and turned the bar, and looked toward the high desk at the back of the forge, where nobody was sitting.

"Ask her."

"I'm asking you."

"I know. Ask her."

It was the right answer and Emeric should have arrived at it on his own, and said so on the way out, and Bram did not disagree.



Her terms took about forty seconds.

She heard him out at her own kitchen table with her pen in her hand and did not write anything down, which he took, correctly, as a bad sign.

"Paid," she said, when he had finished. "Properly. Not in soap."

"Yes."

"I'm not a servant. I don't come up to the keep and stand in a corner waiting to be told."

"No."

"I work here. At this table. You bring them to me." She put the pen down, square to the edge of the page. "And the castle never learns my name. Not Hale, not the baron, not the boy who brings the wood. If I hear my name in that house, I stop the same day, and you can find somebody else."

He thought of Tam's name, and how long it had taken him to learn it.

"Yes," he said. He agreed to all of it in about four seconds, and three of them went on wondering why she thought she had to ask.

She looked, briefly, as though she regretted not asking for more.

She named a wage. It was fair, which made it harder to argue with than if it had been too high, and he did not argue with it.



Getting the books out of the keep turned out to be easy, which bothered him more than if it had been hard.

He went to Hale and asked to see what the barony had spent on water, for the well order, going back as far as there were records. Hale considered him, then took a ring of keys off his belt and went down to a vaulted room under the old hall, and came back with Rylan and Osric behind him carrying crates. Thirty years of ledgers. And on top, without being asked for, the current year's daybook, in Hale's own hand.

"The baron keeps the ledger," Hale said. "I keep the daybook. What's paid goes in when it's paid." He set it down with the others. "You'll want it."

He did not ask what for. Emeric thanked him and did not ask why not.

They went down the hill to Bram's house in a handcart under sacking, Ward walking alongside as if it were nothing to do with him.

The accounts were thirty years deep and they were a disaster.

Three hands, Ferra said, touching the spines in turn: one from before the baron, one middle, and then Hale's, from about eleven years ago. No index. No running totals anywhere. Entries that referenced other entries by phrases like as before and the usual, and in one register a whole year in which the clerk had stopped writing the year.

Ferra could do it. Ferra could do all of it and was not intimidated by any part of it.

Ferra estimated four months.

They did not have four months and both of them knew it before she finished saying it.

What they had was a room where an hour cost about a minute, and a woman who had stood in her own kitchen and counted the minute.



She agreed to try it, on the terms she had already set for the two men. Somebody who knew what it was stayed awake in the house, which meant Bram, at the hearth, with a hinge and a file and no real interest in either. The book stayed open at her elbow. And she would write the times herself.

Emeric did not question the last part. He should have.

The first attempt failed.

A page on the table between them. His mother's stone in his left hand. Two of Ferra's fingers on the inside of his right wrist, where a healer lays them to take a pulse, because if she was going to be touching him for a reason she preferred it to be a useful one.

He went under the way he always did, and wanted her in, the way he had wanted her father to see the furnace. There was nothing to take hold of. He came up to the scratch of her pen. First attempt. Eighty-one. Nothing.

The second attempt was her father's evening, as near as Emeric could rebuild it. The better part of an hour with both of them in the same register, on the same lines, until neither was thinking about anything else. Then contact. Then nothing, and the pen again. Second attempt. Sixty. Nothing.

The third time, he leaned.

He had not known there was anything to lean on until he found it. At the edge of the room, where the first two tries had turned up nothing at all, there was something shut, and something on the far side of it keeping it that way.

He pushed. It gave, about the width of a hair.

Then it pushed back.

He surfaced with a headache like a thumb pressed in behind one eye. Across the table Ferra had the heel of one hand pressed to her temple.

"That hurt," she said.

"Yes."

"You did something."

"Yes," he said. "I'm sorry."

She was already writing. The pen was not as steady as her pen usually was, and she finished the entry anyway and underlined it. Third attempt. Thirty-three. He pushed. Headache, both.

Then she observed that if this was how the prince usually solved problems, she could see why the town had taken three months to come round.



The variable was embarrassing in retrospect. He had spent three attempts looking for it on his own side of the table, and he found it on the walk back up the hill, and it had been sitting in her book the whole time, in her own handwriting. Three sets of times, every one of them written by the person who was supposed to be going under.

She had been holding on, all three times, and she was very good at holding on.

That was the embarrassing part. The frightening part was that the thing had given, and he was fairly sure that with enough weight behind it, for long enough, he could have had her through in an afternoon, holding on or not.

He did not like how sure he was. One small lean had put a headache in both of them. In another life he had filled in a form every week with eleven questions on it, all of them symptoms and none of them capability, and the first question had been headaches, because the people pressing things into his head had known where trouble shows up first.

He did not sleep much.

In the morning he asked her what the third try had felt like from her side.

Ferra considered it for long enough that he knew she was going to answer it properly.

"Like dropping off in church," she said. "The moment you catch yourself. Over and over, as fast as it came."

"And you caught it every time."

"Of course I did."

"That's what's stopping it."

At the hearth, Bram's file stopped moving.

"It isn't failing on my side," Emeric said. "It found you. It can't bring you in unless you let it."

"I sat down three times."

"You sat down with two fingers on my pulse and the pen in reach, and you wrote the count every time. You can't keep the count and leave the room."

She did not like it. She did not argue with it either, because the book was open in front of her, in her own hand, and the book agreed with him.

"Your father didn't know it was coming," he said. "He reached for my shoulder and there was nothing in him to hold on with. You knew what you were sitting down to. And it isn't my room you'd be walking into. With two of us in it, it's built out of both. It's nearer to sharing a head than visiting one."

"I don't care for that."

"I wouldn't either. But I can build my half and I can't build yours. You'd have to want it. Or stop minding it. I don't know which. I have one example, and he's sitting by the fire."

She was quiet for some time.

"I'm not frightened of going," she said. "I'm frightened of not being able to count it."

Then, because she was who she was, she went straight for the practical answer.

"It gave. You said so. A hair." She put the pen down. "Then lean."

"No."

She waited. She was owed the reason and they both knew it.

"Nothing I've ever worked on likes being forced," he said. "Make a thing do what it doesn't want to and it pushes back, and the push has to go somewhere." He looked at Bram. "Take a bar past what it'll bear."

"It stretches," Bram said. "For a bit." He turned the hinge over in his hands. "Then it doesn't."

"And it doesn't tell you which pull was the last one." Emeric turned back to her. "Push water apart fast enough and it won't stay apart. It comes back all at once and crushes whatever's standing in the gap. Pumps get eaten like that, from the inside. I don't know how a mind is built. I'd be guessing. But I can't think why it would be the one thing in the world that doesn't push back."

"And if you'd leaned properly."

"Then I think you'd have come through. And I think something would have given, in you or in me or in both of us, and I don't know which, and I don't know if it mends."

"What would it look like. From the outside."

"I think it would look like the reason you were going for Imani."

Nobody said anything for a while. Bram set the hinge down on the hearthstone and left it there.



Ferra took a clean page and wrote, and then read her terms to him in the order she wanted them kept.

"You never lean. Not a hair. Not if I ask you to. If you feel yourself starting, you come up, and that's the end of the day."

"Agreed."

"He keeps the book." She did not look at her father. "Not me. He counts. Past a count he's comfortable with, he fetches Imani, and nobody gets a vote."

"Agreed."

"And whatever's in there that's mine stays where it is." Now she looked at him. "Unless I put it on the table myself."

"Agreed."

He owed her the rest of it. "I don't know what the room will show. I won't tell you I do."

"I didn't ask what it'll show. I asked what you'll open."

"Nothing you haven't put in front of me."

She weighed that the way she weighed a column somebody else had added, and kept it.

Then she closed the book, carried it to the hearth, and put it in her father's hands with the pen on top. Bram had never once been handed that book. He held it a moment with one big hand flat across the cover, and then opened it to the right page without being told.

She chose the page herself, a leaf from the register for year nineteen, and sat down, and laid her hand flat across his forearm, above the wrist.

Not on the pulse.

He went under and wanted her in, and did not lean, and waited.

There was nothing to wait against. Whatever had been shut the day before was not shut. It had not been opened for him either, in any way he could feel. It had simply stopped being held.

She came through the way her father had, with no transition in it whatsoever. She was standing beside the table in the room as if she had been there all along, looking down at her own hand where it had been on his arm, and she looked faintly annoyed that it had been so simple.

It worked the first time she let it.

Then she looked round, and stopped being annoyed.

The room was holding the paper.

Not a copy. Every sheet either of them had looked at was in there at full fidelity, on shelves that ran back further than the room was wide, including pages Ferra had turned at her table and Emeric had never opened, and the smudges, and a thumbprint in candle grease on a page from year nineteen.

The forge's own books were in there too, stacked at the far end, because she had looked at them every working day of her adult life. He did not go down that end.

Ferra took this significantly better than her father had, because she spends her life wishing she could have two ledgers open at once and had just been handed thirty.



Then he built her a grid.

He could not explain a spreadsheet. There was no sentence available to him that did not sound like a man describing a dream. So he made one instead, in front of her, on a table that grew to fit it, and told her what to imagine while he did it.

Rows. Columns. Date, amount, payee, what for, book and page. A header line that stays where it is when the rest of it moves.

"Why would the rest of it move."

"Watch."

He sorted the first three years by payee, nine hundred and some entries, and they reordered themselves in front of her in a breath, each name's payments together in one block in date order, and she did not believe it, and made him do it again with a register she had chosen herself.

Her reaction was not wonder. It was outrage.

The winter before last her father had lost a payment from a carter, and she had sorted two years of the forge's books by hand looking for it, on the floor, in piles. "Eleven days," she said. She wanted to know, with some heat, why nobody had ever thought of this, and she was not asking rhetorically and did not accept the first two answers he gave her.

Then filtering, which she understood before he finished the sentence.

Then a running total in a column that updated itself as the column changed, and she went quiet for some time.

Then she improved it.

His single flat table was wrong for this job, she said, and it was wrong because the barony's books were not one continuous record. They were one register a year with a carry line between them. What he actually wanted was one sheet per year with the carries linked, so that a correction made in year eleven walked forward to year thirty on its own without anybody touching the years between.

Emeric, who had just been asked for linked sheets by a woman who had never seen a spreadsheet, said yes and built it.

Bram had corrected his metallurgy. His daughter had corrected his data model. Neither of them had ever read a page of anything he had learned it from.

Somewhere in the second day it occurred to him that he had been arguing with her about columns for six straight hours and had not once wanted to be anywhere else. One red strand had come loose over her ear, and she kept putting it back without seeming to know she did it. He put the six hours down to the general good humor of solving a problem well.



Her clock did not find a constant, and that was a result and she treated it as one.

Bram shook them out of it the first time at a hundred and fifty, by both shoulders, hard, the way you would shake a man asleep on a cart.

"A hundred and fifty," he said. He had the book open. "Your number."

"It felt like a quarter hour," Ferra said.

Emeric had thought nearer an hour and a half.

Over the next fortnight the ratio scattered. It tracked depth and it tracked tiredness and it did not track the hour of the day at all, and both of them, every time, came out believing less time had passed than had. Nothing else in the book annoyed her half as much.

So she set a hard ceiling anyway, on no theory whatsoever, and enforced it by having Bram wake them, which he did by the least gentle method available to him, mid-sentence if necessary, and it worked every single time.

Coming out, then, could be forced. Emeric did not believe going in was the same problem, and did not intend to find out.

He wrote his own rule down that week in the back of Armand's notebook, and his had a theory under it.

Contact. Consent. Nothing forced. Not ever, and not to save time. No strangers, because a stranger has no reason to stop holding on. No enemies, for the same reason with more behind it.

It held, and he took the holding as proof that it was complete.

Both people it had worked on had been awake and sitting at a table. He did not write that down, because it did not look like a condition. It looked like the furniture.



At night, in his own room, he went on with the other work.

Somewhere in the long hours in the room the coin had stopped being a problem; he could hold it now while thinking about something else. So in the second week of the books, with the shutters closed and Ward's cloak pinned over the gap under the door, he tried himself.

He was only taking the weight away, and once it was gone, holding it gone cost far less than he had feared. The glyph opened small and pale at his feet, and his heels left the boards, and then his toes, and he hung a hand's width off the floor with his arms out like a man on ice, waiting for something to go wrong.

Nothing did. His nerve gave out long before his mana.

The next night he tried to turn.

Gravity went up and down. Armand's notebook said so in three places. But a man was not a coin. Take the weight off his feet and leave it on his shoulders, and he had one push up and one down at opposite ends of the same body, and a pair like that would turn him.

Holding two at once was another matter. Three times the half at his feet let go the moment he reached for his shoulders. The fourth time both held, wobbling, two small glyphs flickering like a lamp in a draught, and he tipped forward, slowly, head over heels, and kept going until the bedpost hit him in the back.

He lay on the floor and laughed without a sound, and stayed there, because he could not have stood up if he had wanted to.

The single hover cost him about what the candle did. Two holds pulling against each other emptied him like a long night in the room, and the headache after it sat behind his eyes until morning.

But lying there, he kept turning the other thing over.

Sideways, a mage needed air, and air cost him three times what his own elements did. But fire was his, and when he pushed the candle flame hard away there was a faint shove back against the palm, so slight he had taken it for nothing. Hot gas going one way. A hand going the other. Every rocket he had ever read about came down to that sentence.

Hang in the air on gravity. Push with fire.

He was too tired to try it. He thought he could, though, some other night, somewhere no glyph could be seen.

In the back of the notebook, under the night's counts, he wrote Fire for thrust? and left the question mark where it was.



The better part of a fortnight of real time, and a good deal more than that inside, and then they had it.

Wexler had been taking money out of Kaldo for years.

Not crudely. Repairs invoiced and half done, with the other half invoiced again later. The drawbridge chain had been paid for in full, twice, eight years apart, and Emeric had ridden in under the rope that replaced it. A grain contract at a price no grain has ever cost in the history of grain. Sixteen men on a garrison roll that has not had sixteen spare men in it since the war and could not feed them if it did, drawing a wage every quarter and existing nowhere else in any book.

And then the thing Emeric had not expected, which Ferra found first and stated without any drama at all.

"It isn't enough to live on."

"It's theft."

"It's theft," she agreed. "A man stealing to live well steals more than this, or he steals smarter."

The sums were wrong for a man enriching himself. They went out again almost as fast as they came in. And a good deal of it went back into the barony through doors that were not the front one, in amounts too small and too frequent and too dull to be anything but somebody quietly keeping a wall standing.

Wexler had been robbing Kaldo to pay for Kaldo, and skimming a comfortable and entirely unremarkable amount on top.

Emeric sat with how much harder that was to hate and did not arrive anywhere with it.



The same pass turned up draws out of the garrison's share.

Small. Regular. Going back nine years. Taken by a guard, by name, with the name in the same hand every time.

The name was Corliss.

Emeric read it as exactly what it looked like, which was a man skimming, and was annoyed rather than shocked.

"Look at that first," Ward said, when Emeric told him. "Before anything else. He's been in a room with your prisoner."

Ward did not say he disbelieved the ledger. But a ledger records what moved and never records why, and Ward had been in enough barracks to know which of those two usually matters.

And Emeric did look into it.

He did not forget it and he did not put it off. He ran every check that thirty years of accounts allowed him to run, and he ran them himself, at night, over two nights: the garrison's own book; the quartermaster's slips, which he had Rylan fetch without saying why; the coin the slips said had gone out against the coin the box said was there; Corliss's signature on the slips beside the mark on his muster. The name did not change once in nine years, and by the end of the second night he had four independent confirmations of the same fact.

Then he went to Corliss privately, and found him in the garrison yard, alone, oiling a buckle.

"Did you take it?" he said. "From the garrison funds. For nine years."

Corliss looked up at him. There was something in his face that Emeric had seen once before, outside Petram's door: a man who has been waiting a long time to be asked something, and is ready.

"Yes," Corliss said.

Emeric waited for more. Corliss did not offer it. He only went on looking at Emeric as though there were a second question, and he was waiting for that one too.

And Emeric stopped, because a confession closes a question. He had come for an answer and he had it, and he came away satisfied that he had been thorough.

He did not act on it, either, and the grounds for that were sound as well. He was not going to move on a garrison matter until he actually held the garrison, and he did not.

In the same fortnight, sorting the year's outgoings by payee, Ferra turned up a block of purchases from the coffin-maker on Cooper's Row. A dozen or so a year, going back years, under no family's name. Just box, and a sum. She noted them in her column with everything else. Emeric read the line and moved on to the next. He was looking for an assassin.



Then the name came up in the books and everything else stopped.

Cobb, south road, and a sum, every quarter, as regular as rent, going back years. Ferra had sorted the whole run into one block on the table in the room, and Emeric stood looking at it.

At the bottom of the block, one entry was larger than all the rest, several quarters' worth at once, on a date Emeric did not have to look up, because he had spent that date lying in the dirt at the edge of a fire with his jaw going numb.

After it, nothing. The quarters stopped.

"There," he said.

Ferra told him he was wrong immediately and without softening it, because that is what she does.

"It's a discharge. Not a payment for services." She had her pen on the line. "Same sum, every quarter, eleven years. Not in the plague, not in the bad winters, when every other line in these books moves. That's a retainer. And then this settles it. You don't discharge a debt for a thing that hasn't happened yet."

"It's the date."

"It's a date. The rhythm doesn't bend round it. When you hire something done, money goes out early. Here nothing changes until it's over." She sat back. "He paid people who were already coming."

Then the honest version, which she gave him because she thought he could use it.

"It's worth pulling on. It is not what you think it is."

He overruled her.

Not rudely. He thanked her, and told her she was right to flag it, and then explained why this one was different.

And every sentence of the explanation was a sentence he would have taken apart inside a minute if somebody else had said it to him.

He had a name. He had a date. He had a nineteen year old in the ground four hours after the name was spoken out loud in a room in that man's house. Petram's purse in a stable yard, the road, Garrin, the fire: the shape of it was so whole that he could see every piece from where he stood.

"It's Wexler," he said. "He paid for the road."

The conclusion arrived already assembled and he walked straight into it.

When they came up, Ferra did not argue. She took her own book back from Bram's side of the table long enough to write in it, in her own hand, because that is what she does with things she has been overruled on. He read it upside down as she wrote.

Cobb account. A discharge, not a commission. Retainer unbroken eleven years. Said so. Overruled.

She dated it, and said nothing more about it.

Then she wrote one more line under it.

Grain contract, Cobb retainer, Hale made steward, last magistrate gone. All begin the same year. Eleven years back.

He saw it. She did not explain it, because she did not know what it meant and would not guess past her data, and he did not ask, because he had a prisoner in a cell under the keep and a name to put to him.

He had what he needed to take Wexler down. What he believed he had was a good deal more than that.

He went back up the hill. At the head of the stair Declan stood aside, and Emeric took the stair down to Petram with the lamp in his hand.
