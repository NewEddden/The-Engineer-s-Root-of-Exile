Chapter 18 - Two Sets of Books

He needed the barony's accounts read by somebody who was not from the castle, was not on his payroll, and could not be leaned on.

That was three conditions and they eliminated everybody in Kaldo except one person.

She kept the forge's books. She had corrected his arithmetic to his face at her father's table. And she did not care about him even slightly, which was the condition that mattered and the only one he could not buy.

He went to Bram to ask permission.

"Ask her."

"I'm asking you."

"I know. Ask her."

It was the right answer and Emeric should have arrived at it on his own, and said so on the way out, and Bram did not disagree.

Her terms took about forty seconds.

She is paid, and paid properly, and not in soap. She is not a servant and will not be addressed as one. She works at her own table in her own house. And nobody in the castle learns her name, not the steward, not the guards, not the baron, and if that becomes impossible she stops the same day.

Emeric agreed to all of it in about four seconds.

She looked, briefly, as though she regretted not asking for more.

The accounts were thirty years deep and they were a disaster.

Three hands. No index. No running totals anywhere. Entries that referenced other entries by phrases like as before and the usual, and in one register a whole year in which the previous clerk had stopped writing the year.

Ferra could do it. Ferra could do all of it and was not intimidated by any part of it.

Ferra estimated four months.

They did not have four months and both of them knew it before she finished saying it.

The first attempt failed. So did the second.

Contact and concentration and a page on the table between them and nothing at all, twice, and then a third time with a headache on the end of it, and Ferra observing that if this was how the prince usually solved problems she could see why the town had taken three months to come round.

The variable took him until the following morning and was embarrassing in retrospect.

Bram had been pulled in while both of them were looking at the same thing. Not touching the same thing. Attending to it. An hour on one drawing, both minds in the same object, and then contact.

So the rule wrote itself, and he wrote it down, and it never changed afterward.

Contact, plus a subject both people have genuinely looked at. No strangers. No enemies. Nobody who has not sat down with you over the same object first.

They tested it on a single page of accounts and it worked on the first try.

The place was holding the paper.

Not a copy. Every sheet either of them had looked at in the real world was in there at full fidelity, including the ones Ferra had read three weeks earlier and Emeric had never opened, and including the smudges, and including a thumbprint in candle grease on a page from year nineteen.

Ferra took this significantly better than her father had, because she spends her life wishing she could have two ledgers open at once and had just been handed thirty.

Then he built her a grid.

He could not explain a spreadsheet. There was no sentence available to him that did not sound like a man describing a dream. So he made one instead, in front of her, and told her what to imagine while he did it.

Rows. Columns. A header line that stays where it is when the rest of it moves.

"Why would the rest of it move."

"Watch."

He sorted nine hundred entries by date and they reordered themselves in front of her, and she did not believe it, and made him do it again with a register she had chosen herself.

Her reaction was not wonder. It was outrage.

She had hand-copied ledgers into date order four times in her life. One of them had taken her eleven days. She wanted to know, with some heat, why nobody had ever thought of this, and she was not asking rhetorically and did not accept the first two answers he gave her.

Then filtering, which she understood before he finished the sentence.

Then a running total in a column that updated itself as the column changed, and she went quiet for some time.

Then she improved it.

His single flat table was wrong for this job, she said, and it was wrong because the barony's books were not one continuous record. They were one register per year with a carry line between them. What he actually wanted was one sheet per year with the carries linked, so that a correction made in year eleven walked forward to year thirty on its own without anybody touching the intervening twenty.

Emeric, who had just been asked for linked sheets by a woman who had never seen a spreadsheet, said yes and built it.

Bram had corrected his metallurgy. His daughter had corrected his data model. Neither of them had ever been off this island.

Somewhere in the second day he noticed he had been arguing with her about columns for six straight hours and had not once wanted to be anywhere else, and filed it under the general good humor of solving a problem well.

Her clock did not find a constant, and that was a result and she treated it as one.

She had been timing them on paper for weeks. The ratio scattered. It ran further out of true on long sessions than on short ones. It tracked depth and it tracked tiredness and it did not track the hour of the day at all, and both men had consistently reported low.

So she set a hard ceiling anyway, on no theory whatsoever, and enforced it by having Bram wake them.

Bram did it by the least gentle method available to him and it worked every single time.

Three days of real time and a good deal more than that inside, and then they had it.

Wexler had been taking money out of Kaldo for years.

Not crudely. Repairs invoiced and half done, with the other half invoiced again two years later. A grain contract at a price no grain has ever cost in the history of grain. Sixteen men on a garrison roll that has not had sixteen spare men in it since the war and could not feed them if it did.

And then the thing Emeric had not expected, which Ferra found first and stated without any drama at all.

"It isn't enough to live on."

The sums were wrong for a man enriching himself. They went out again almost as fast as they came in. And a good deal of it went back into the barony through doors that were not the front one, in amounts too small and too frequent and too dull to be anything but somebody quietly keeping a wall standing.

Wexler had been robbing Kaldo to pay for Kaldo, and skimming a comfortable and entirely unremarkable amount on top.

Emeric sat with how much harder that was to hate and did not arrive anywhere with it.

The same pass turned up draws out of the garrison's share.

Small. Regular. Going back years. Taken by a guard, by name, with the name in the same hand every time.

Emeric read it as exactly what it looked like, which was a man skimming, and he was annoyed rather than shocked, because a barony run this badly for this long was always going to have one.

Ward told him to look into it before he did anything with it.

Not because he disbelieved the ledger. Because a ledger records what moved and never records why, and Ward had been in enough barracks to know which of those two usually matters.

And Emeric did look into it, which is the part that matters.

He did not forget it and he did not put it off. He ran every check that thirty years of accounts allowed him to run, and he ran them himself, at night, over two days.

The amounts were right. The dates lined up against the garrison's pay schedule. The name did not change once in nine years. Nothing anywhere in thirty years of paper contradicted any part of it, and by the end of it he had four independent confirmations of the same fact.

Then he went to Corliss privately, which was what he understood Ward to have asked for, and put it to him.

He asked whether he had taken the money.

"Yes."

And Emeric stopped, because a confession closes a question.

He never asked the second one. He never asked what the money was for. The first one had been answered, and an answered question is a finished question, and he came away satisfied that he had been thorough.

He had been. At the wrong thing.

He did not act on it, either, and the grounds for that were sound as well. He was not going to move on a garrison matter until he actually held the garrison, and he did not hold the garrison and would not for some time.

So he carried a settled, checked, four-times-confirmed conclusion, and every month that passed made it more certain rather than less, because nothing ever arrived to disturb it.

A verified wrong answer is far more durable than an open question.

The same pass showed burial fees paid to the parish for people who had nobody to pay them.

It was the figure Doran had mentioned in passing, sideways, at the end of an evening about ravines and lime, and which was sitting in Emeric's own notebook in the margin of a page he had not opened since.

He did not put the two columns next to each other. Different page. Different register. Different purpose. And he was looking for an assassin.

Then the name came up in the books and everything else stopped.

Payments to Cobb, going back years, at intervals regular enough to have a rhythm.

And one entry, larger than the rest, on a date Emeric did not have to look up, because he had spent that date lying in the dirt at the edge of a fire with his jaw going numb.

Ferra told him he was wrong immediately and without softening it, because that is what she does.

"It's a discharge. Not a payment for services." She had her finger on the line. "It settles against an account that's been running eleven years. You don't discharge a debt for a thing that hasn't happened yet."

"It's the date."

"It's a date. He's on a retainer rhythm and the rhythm doesn't bend round it. Look at the spacing before and the spacing after, it's the same. A bigger payment means a longer errand or a heavier bag." She sat back. "Which means he paid people who were already coming."

Then the honest version, which she gave him because she thought he could use it.

"It's worth pulling on. It is not what you think it is."

He overruled her.

Not rudely. He thanked her, and told her she was right to flag it, and then explained why this one was different.

And every sentence of the explanation was a sentence he would have taken apart inside a minute if somebody else had said it to him.

He had a name. He had a date. He had a nineteen year old in the ground four hours after the name was spoken out loud in a room in that man's house.

The conclusion arrived already assembled and he walked straight into it.

Ferra wrote her objection in her own book, dated, in her own hand, because that is what she does with things she has been overruled on, and she did not mention it again.

He had what he needed to take Wexler down.

What he believed he had was a good deal more than that, and the difference between those two things was going to cost him years.

He went down to the cell to put it to Petram.
