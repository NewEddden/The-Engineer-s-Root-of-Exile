Chapter 18 - The Ear He Didn't Trust

Ward waited nine days, which Emeric understood afterward was not hesitation. It was collection.

He came up to the long room on a wet evening with his hat in his hand and water off his shoulders, and he did not sit, and he said:

"Rylan's wife says Bram's been strange for a week and a half. The chandler says Ferra's had the office door shut in the afternoons, which she's never done in four months, and that she came out of it once at four in the afternoon and asked him what day it was. The girl who does your fire says you slept nine hours."

"That last one's true."

"I know it's true. That's why I'm standing here." Ward turned the hat over. "You've not slept nine hours since you got off the cart. So either you've stopped being frightened, which I'd have noticed for other reasons, or something's changed and I've not been told."

Emeric had known this conversation was coming and had prepared for it and discovered, in the first three seconds, that everything he had prepared was for a different conversation, the one where Ward arrived angry.

"Sit down," he said.

"I'd rather not."

"Ward, sit down, because it's going to take a while and you standing there dripping is going to make me talk faster than I should."

Ward sat.

Emeric told him all of it. He had decided some days earlier that when this happened he would tell all of it, in order, without shaping it, because shaping it was what he did and because Ward could smell shaping the way Bram could smell a bad weld.

He told him about his mother and the hollow crystal and being six. He told him about sixteen years of thinking she had only been being sentimental. He told him what it was for and what it was not for, and he read him the three rules off the sheet, and then he told him the thing he had been dreading, which was the order of it: that Bram had gone in without being asked, and that Ferra had been asked an hour later, and that both of those facts were his.

He did not tell him about the easy descent. He noticed himself not telling him.

Ward listened the whole way through with his hands on his knees, and when it was finished he said:

"How long since you knew you could do it to a person?"

"Eleven days."

"And how long since the first time you did it to a person?"

"Eleven days. It was the same evening. I found out I could by doing it."

Ward nodded slowly.

"Good," he said.

Emeric waited.

"That's it?"

"What did you want?"

"I wanted, I think, to be shouted at. I've been arranging the room for it for nine days." He heard how that sounded and did not take it back. "You've spent four months telling me every time I do something clever without telling you. This is the largest thing I've ever not told you."

"Aye," Ward said. "And you told me eleven days in, and the reason you didn't tell me on the first night is that you were sat on Bram's anvil at midnight not yet sure what had happened to you, and the reason you didn't tell me on the second is that you'd have been guessing, and you don't bring me guesses, you bring me the thing when you know its shape." He set his hat on the table. "You've told me before you'd finished being sure. That's more than you did with the tenants and more than you did with the wall. So no, I'll not shout at you. You've done it right this once and I'll not punish the once because I'm sore about the rest."

It was, Emeric found, considerably worse than being shouted at.

Because Ward was not conceding. Ward had listened to a man describe a private room that no one else in the barony could enter, that stripped a sleeping man's guard off him, that had been used on Bram without asking, and had judged the disclosure adequate. He had accepted the reasoning. And accepting the reasoning meant that the ear Emeric had installed specifically to be un-persuadable had just been persuaded, by Emeric, about Emeric, on the largest question there had ever been.

"Ward. I'd like you to argue with me about it."

"I'm going to. I've not started yet." Ward leaned back. "You've been waiting for the shouting and I've been waiting for you to stop apologising, because the apology's the part I don't need. Here's the part you'll not enjoy."

"Go on."

"Four months ago you put your terms in a candle shop." He said it flatly. "You said it to my face and you were pleased with yourself and you were right. Register in the town, not the castle. Three tallies, one of them held by the man who trusts you least. A clause that costs you money so they can see it cost you. I've watched you build the whole thing on the same idea and I've heard you say it a dozen ways: they shouldn't have to trust you, they should be able to check you."

"Yes."

"Then tell me who checks this."

Emeric did not answer, because there was no answer, and because Ward was not asking rhetorically. He was waiting, the way he waited on a wall.

"Bram," Emeric said eventually. "Ferra."

"They're inside it. A man inside a thing can tell you what it's like in there. He can't tell you whether he's been changed by it, because the thing that would notice is the thing you're asking." Ward's voice did not rise. It never did. "Say you went down tomorrow and came back up wrong. Not mad. Wrong by a degree. Who in this barony would know? Not Bram, he's had his hand burned by a stone that wasn't there and he's still going in. Not Ferra, she's better at it than you are inside a week and she'd not give it up for a bad feeling. Not me. I've never been in it and by your own rule I'd have to be asked in to see it, and you'd be the one asking, and if you'd come back wrong then the man doing the asking is the man I'd be checking."

"You've thought about this."

"Nine days." Ward picked a thread off his knee. "You built a barony that works when nobody's watching. Then you found the one room in it nobody can watch, and it's the room where you're best, and you've written three good rules for it, and every one of those rules is kept by you."

They sat in it a while. Rain on the long room's shutters.

"I don't have a fix," Emeric said.

"No."

"I could tell you the rules are on paper and the paper's dated and Ferra keeps it."

"You could. And if you came back wrong you'd rewrite the paper and Ferra'd date the new one." Ward shrugged. "There isn't a fix. That's what I came to tell you. I've turned it over nine days looking for one and there's no arrangement of men that puts a check on this, because the checking wants a man outside it and there is nobody outside it who can see in." He looked up. "So it's not a system, and you can't make it one, and I want you to stop trying to for a minute and hear the only thing I've got instead."

"Which is what?"

"It's me watching you. Not the work. You." Ward held up a hand before Emeric could say anything. "I've known you since you were nine. I know how you stand when you've had a bad week and how you stand when you've done something you'll not say. I've got no way to check the room and I've a very good way to check the man, and it's not a good way, it's just the only one, and it'll fail eventually because a man who's changed by degrees changes the man who's watching him too. I'd rather have a bad check that exists than a good one that doesn't."

"That's not enough."

"No. Write that down as well."

Emeric wrote it down.

"Two conditions," Ward said. "Then I'll leave you be about it."

"Name them."

"One. I don't go in. Not once, not to see it, not because it'd be faster." He said it before Emeric could offer, which Emeric realised was the point. "You'll want to bring me in eventually, because you bring everyone in, it's the way you're made. If I've been in it I'm inside it, and then there's nobody at all standing where I'm standing. So I stay out. That's not fear. That's the job."

"I'm going to write that down with today's date on it."

"Why the date."

"Because Ferra dates everything and she's right to. And because in two years I'll want to know what you promised and what you promised it about." Emeric heard the second half of his own sentence arrive a beat behind the first. "A man agrees to a thing as it stands on the day. The thing doesn't hold still."

Ward considered that for longer than Emeric wanted him to.

"Put the date on it," he said.

"Agreed. That costs us. You'd be good at it."

"I'd be adequate at it and we'd both lose the only man in Kaldo who can tell you you've gone strange." Ward turned his hat back over. "Two. When it goes wrong, and it will, because everything you build goes wrong once before it goes right and this is the first thing you've built that I can't inspect, you tell me on the day. Not when you know the shape of it. On the day. You've earned eleven days on the good news. You get none at all on the bad."

Emeric thought about the easy descent, and the way it had got easier the further down he went, and the breath in which he had been noticed by nothing at all in a dark field.

Eleven days ago.

"Agreed," he said.

He heard himself say it and he did not correct it, and there was a moment, small and entirely private, in which he understood exactly what he had just done and let it stand anyway, because the alternative was handing a man who had just refused to enter the room a reason to think its owner should not be in there either.

He filed it, the way he filed things.

"Then that's the whole of it," Ward said, and stood, and picked up his hat, and stopped at the door. "One more, and it's not a condition, it's an observation, and you'll want it because you've not had it from anyone."

"Go on."

"There's three of you now. You, the smith, the woman who keeps the books. That's not a secret, that's a body." He put the hat on. "Bodies want a name eventually. Men give them one whether you do or not, and if you leave it to the town you'll get whatever the town's afraid of. And bodies want to know who's in charge of what, or they find out the hard way in the middle of something."

"There's no titles in it."

"Then say what there is instead, out loud, to the two of them, this week." Ward opened the door onto the rain. "Because I'll tell you what it looks like from where I stand, and I'm the friendliest man who's going to be looking. It looks like the prince and two people who can do a thing nobody else can do, and no one to say what it's for."

He said it that week, in the office with the door open, which he did on purpose.

He did not call it anything. He had thought about it and had decided that naming it made it a thing that could be joined and a thing that could be excluded from, and that he would rather it stayed inconveniently unnamed for as long as the town let it.

What he said instead was what each of them held.

Ferra held the numbers and the record, and nothing came out of the room without going into her book in daylight, and she could refuse to write a thing down, and if she refused it did not exist.

None of which was on paper. It was three people in a forge yard agreeing out loud, which was the weakest instrument Emeric had ever built anything on, and he said so. Ferra said that would do for now, because the only man it protected them from was standing in the yard, and she did not say what it would have to become later. He worked that part out on the walk up the hill and did not enjoy it.

Bram held the material, and his hands were the test, and if he said a shape would not survive real iron then the shape was wrong regardless of what it had done in a place with no physics in it, and Emeric would not overrule him on it in front of anyone or in private.

Emeric held the room, and the cost of it, and the rules, and the responsibility for every person taken into it, and he said the last part in a way he could not take back later.

"And when you two decide I've gone wrong in there," he said, "you go to Ward, not to me. He's not in it and he's never going to be, and that's the arrangement, and I'd like you to know I asked for it rather than agreed to it."

Ferra wrote it down. Bram said nothing and looked at his hands and eventually nodded once.

"Domains," Ferra said, ruling the column heading. "Not offices."

"Domains. Same as the field. Halloway answers to me on the water and to them on the count, and everyone knows which is which, and nobody has to guess in the middle of something."

"It works better in the field," she said, "because in the field the count is nailed to a door."

"I know."

"I'm going to keep saying it."

"I'd like you to."

He was still in the office an hour after they had gone, with the three rules on the sheet in front of him and Ward's fourth thing written underneath in his own hand, which was: a bad check that exists beats a good one that doesn't.

Under it, in the list, in the space under the space, he wrote the thing he had not said to any of the three of them.

I have agreed to tell him on the day, and I did not tell him about the eleventh day, and I have now let that stand twice.

He looked at it for a while. It was, at least, written down where he would find it.

Then he blew out the candle, because the alternative was sitting there being honest with a piece of paper all night, and there was ore to cost in the morning and a merchant coming up from the ford whose name he had been given three times in a fortnight, always in the same slightly grudging way.
