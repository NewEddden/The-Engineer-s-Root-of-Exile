Chapter 18 - A Real Ward

He went to Imani's door the next morning, and she let him stand on the step.

"You said the soap got a room first," he said. "You were right. I want to give the sick one."

She took her time answering. Behind her, in the front room, the second chair still sat by the fire with its cushion smooth.

"Then here's what it costs you," she said.

She told him on the step, the way Ferra had told him at her table, and he understood that she'd had it ready for some time.

It would be her ward. Her register at the door, in her hand, and nobody else's. Her rules about who came in and who went out and when. She'd choose who worked in it. And he would not walk the floor of it, not once, not to look, not to help, without asking her first and being told yes.

"Yes," he said.

"I haven't finished."

He waited.

What she needed, she named herself, counting it off on her fingers like a woman ordering from a stall. A ward that could be kept clean, which meant a floor that could be scrubbed and walls that could be washed. Laundry that was boiled, not rinsed, every sheet and every cloth, every time. And a room apart, with its own door, for the ones who were catching. So that a woman who came in with a broken arm didn't go home with the flux.

"That's all," she said.

It wasn't, and they both knew it. But it was the start.

He could have told her more. He knew more about a sick ward than anyone in the kingdom, every reason for every rule, the way he knew the angle of a stitch. He could have walked her floor with her and set it out from the first bed to the last. He didn't. It was hers. She'd kept this town alive with a theory that was wrong about every reason and right about every house, and she'd do better with a building than he would with a lecture. And he had a barony to run.



The building was one of the two dark market halls.

He'd walked past it on his second day, chained and shuttered: the smaller of the two, two streets above Cooper's Row, close enough to the lower ward that nobody would have to be carried up a hill to reach it. He had Hale find the key. Imani walked it with him on a wet afternoon, end to end and back again, with Ward at the door and the rain coming through the roof in three places.

She didn't say one good thing about it.

The floor was beaten earth and would never be clean. The roof leaked at the north end, there, and there. The windows were too small and too high and you couldn't open them. There was one door, and it opened onto the street where the carts went. A drain ran under the back wall and she could smell it from the middle of the room. Rats. Cold. And no water nearer than the north well, which was a fair walk with two buckets and a sick child waiting.

Behind the main hall there was an old weigh-room with its own door onto the back lane. She stood in it, and looked at the door, and said nothing about it at all, which he took to mean it would do for the catching room.

Standing in the wet, he found he'd rather have her list than anybody's praise. A list was something you could fix.

"Flagstones," he said. "And lime on the walls. The roof's a week for a carpenter. The drain can be moved." He looked at the door to the street. "Another door at the side."

"And the water?"

That was the one he didn't have yet.



The clean water was the Mere's, and the Mere's water came down the aqueduct on its arches and ran out into the sand where the span had fallen, a mile and a half outside the wall.

He walked out to the gap with Bram and a mason from Ferra's census. The mason was one of the two who'd been digging Doran's graves all summer for want of any stone to cut. A grey, square, quiet man.

The span had come down whole, or nearly. Its stones lay in the field below in a long heap, half grown over. On either side the arches stood up thirty feet, and the channel ended in the air on one and started again on the other, and between them the Mere's water poured out of the near end in a clear sheet and vanished into the ground.

"Stone's all there," the mason said at last. "Most of it." He walked along the heap, kicking at it. "Spring. With twenty men and a frame. Not before. You'd lose it in the frosts."

"And before spring?"

Bram had already worked it out. He was measuring the gap with his eye, the way he measured a bar.

"Wood," Bram said.

It took them eleven days. A timber trough, long enough to cross the gap, set on trestles built up from the field, the carpenter off Ferra's pile cutting the joints and the mason setting the feet. They lined the trough with lime plaster from Emeric's kiln so it wouldn't rot through in a winter. It was ugly. It sagged a little in the middle, and Bram swore at it, and propped it.

On the twelfth morning they knocked out the board that had been holding the water back at the near end, and the Mere came down the channel and into the trough and along it, and out the far side, and on toward Kaldo over arches it hadn't crossed in years.

The mason stood under it with his hand flat against the trestle and felt it shake.

"Spring," he said again, to nobody. "Stone."



At the wall, before the water reached the keep, they tapped it.

That had been Emeric's idea, and the only one in the whole business he could take any credit for. The aqueduct had only ever fed the keep's cistern. Now a stone cistern went in at the foot of the wall where the channel came through, and from it a run of bored elm pipe went down the slope, under the lanes, to a second cistern and a public tap at the top of the lower ward, two streets above the wells that had been killing people since the rows went in.

The filter he took from a book. A slim grey municipal handbook he'd read once in a library on a wet afternoon in another life, for no reason except that it was there. He could see every page of it now. A deep box, and in it gravel at the bottom, then coarse sand, then fine, so the water went down through it slowly and came out the bottom clear, with the silt and the worst of what the Mere carried left behind in the sand. The book had diagrams. It had numbers for how deep each layer should be and how fast the water should go through.

The book was written for sand you could order by the cartload. The mason took one look at the river sand Emeric had had brought up and said it was too fine and too muddy and it would choke the box in a week. The sand from the old quarry on the Milford road, he said, was sharp and clean, and his father had used it for mortar. They used the quarry sand. Emeric wrote it in the margin of the page in his head.

Imani came to look at the cistern on the afternoon it was finished. She walked round it once, and then she asked what he'd known she would ask.

"Where does it go?"

"The water?"

"What doesn't get drunk." She pointed at the overflow lip. "When the cistern's full. And when the ward's running, all the water off the floors, and the laundry, and the slops. Where does it go?"

It was her own question from the foundry, and she was asking it on purpose, and he'd been ready this time, mostly.

The overflow from the wall cistern would go into the canal ring around the keep, which hadn't moved in years and stank in summer. With water coming in at one end, they could open the old sluice on the east side and let it run out to the river, and the canal would be a canal again instead of a pond. The ward's waste was harder. He'd thought of a soakaway behind the hall. She asked where the soakaway drained, and he told her, and she asked what was below it, and he had to admit it was the top of Cooper's Row.

"No," Imani said.

So the ward's waste went into a covered drain, down the side of the hall, down the slope, out under the east wall and into the river below the bridge, below anywhere in Kaldo or the dock quarter that drew water. It added nine days and a great deal of digging. Imani wouldn't let a stone of the ward be laid until it was dug.

Ferra priced all of it against the shop's takings. The trough. The pipe. The cisterns. The drain. The flagstones and the lime and the carpenter. She worked it twice and showed him, and it fit, the way the exchange had fit: just.

"Soap pays," she said.

"Soap pays," he agreed.

And soap went in the door with the first sheets, a crate of Abloh's plain bars stacked by the laundry kettles, which was the other thing Ferra wrote in the margin.



The laundry was boiled.

Bram made the kettles, three of them, big enough to take a sheet folded once, with lids and a lip for pouring. They stood in the yard behind the hall on a hearth the mason built. There was a rota, in Imani's hand, nailed to the door: who stoked, who stirred, who wrung, who hung. Two of the north-well women took the first week, and Sela sent a third.

The ward opened at the end of October. There was no ceremony. Imani put her register on a desk inside the new side door, and opened it to the first page, and wrote the date, and that was the opening.

Emeric came because she'd said he could, that one morning, to see it. He stood just inside the door, where she'd told him to stand, and looked down the length of the hall: the flagstones, the limed walls already starting to dry white, eleven beds in a row with clean sheets on them, the light from the new windows. A woman was sitting on the end of one of them with her arm in a sling, waiting to be seen.

Imani was at the desk. She'd tied her hair up higher than usual, out of the way, and a piece of it had come down anyway against her neck, and she was writing the woman's name with her head bent, and he realized he'd been watching her write for longer than it took to write a name.

She looked up. Her face said don't.

He looked at the beds.

Ward had come down the hill with him, the way Ward came everywhere now. He stopped on the step outside the side door. Imani glanced at him. Ward had never once in six months come through her door on his own account, and Emeric watched him decide not to start now. He stood on the step with his back to the lane, where he could see the street and the door both, and didn't come any further in.

Imani looked at him a moment longer and went back to her register.



Doran found him on the parish step a week later.

It had taken most of a season, the way Doran had said it would in July. One family at a time, one kitchen at a time, never from the pulpit. Emeric had kept away from all of it by Doran's rule, and hadn't asked, and had only known how it went from the fresh earth in the hollow beyond the south-east wall, a few more stakes every week.

Doran came out of the church as Emeric was passing, and stopped on the top step, and Emeric stopped below him.

"The last family moved on Tuesday," Doran said. "The new ground was consecrated this morning."

That was all. Two sentences. They were the first thing Doran had said to him since the day he'd come up to the keep in his good cassock and asked whether they would have died anyway.

Emeric thanked him.

He didn't ask what Bram had said to him in his kitchen on the night of the map. Doran didn't offer. He nodded once, and went back into the church, and Emeric went on down the hill.



The tap ran in the lower ward on the first of November.

There was no ceremony for that either. The mason turned the valve, and stood back, and water came out of the spout, clear, into the stone trough under it, and ran over the lip and away down the gutter. A few children came to look. Then a few more. Somebody brought a cup.

Emeric watched from the end of the lane, against the wall.

Around noon an old woman came down the street with two buckets. He knew her. He'd seen her on his second day in Kaldo, at the lowest well by the south-east wall, filling those same buckets from the same water that had been draining out of the rows into the ground for four years.

She stopped at the tap and eyed it, as if she didn't trust it. Then she put the first bucket under the spout.

She was four hundred feet nearer the clean water than she'd been in April.

She filled both, and lifted them, and three steps up the lane she stopped and set them down again.

He went over and picked one up. She looked at him, at his eyes, and then at the bucket, and didn't argue. They carried them up the lane together, one each, without a word, as far as a door with a broken latch, and on the step she took the bucket back from him.

"Mind the gutter going down," she said. "It's slick."

Then she went in.
