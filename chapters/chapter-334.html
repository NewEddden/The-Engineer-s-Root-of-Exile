Chapter 334 - Grier

Imani took the strap off on the third of January, in the K Endeavor's hospital hold, at the bench under the forward lamps where she did everything she did not want an audience for, and the whole hold watched her do it anyway.

She unwound it and took the splint away, and the hand lay on the bench between them, white and a little shrunken and very clean, with the two knuckles back in their row where knuckles ought to stand. He looked at it as if he had been asked to identify it.

"Make a fist," said Imani.

He made one. It came slowly, like a door that had been painted shut, and it hurt in a dull complaining way all down the back of the hand, and it closed.

"Now open it."

He opened it.

She put her thumb over the knuckles and shut her eyes; and when she opened them she did not say anything clever, which was how he knew it was good.

"Straight," she said. "You'll write like yourself. Nothing heavy for a fortnight, and don't hit anybody, and if you hit anybody, hit them with the other one." She began to coil the strap. "That's medicine, and it's also me asking."

"I know."

"You don't, but you'll learn it the way you learn everything." She put the strap in her bag, which was the first time in thirteen years he had seen her keep a used one. "Go and do your sum with it. You're dying to."

On the evening boat he went up to the K Amara's chart table and wrote the day's date on a slip with his right hand, in ink. It leaned a little more than it used to and the tail of the nine shook. When he came back from supper somebody had written under it, in Ward's square capitals, GOOD.



The capital had no cardinal now.

It had not had one since the end of December, when a man in white and gold had ridden north up the Ostrel road with the second cardinal at his knee and two canons behind, at a walk, with both his hands on his reins; and Saban's man on the north road had written that down without knowing what he was writing. Emeric knew. Imani knew, and Ferra by cipher; Armand knew the half he had felt, and had not asked for the rest. The nosebleed had stopped by morning, and his hands had been cold for a day.

The rest of the city knew only what it had seen: the cathedral a field of cut stone on the summit, the towers walked out, the lay brothers gone north in carts. In the lower town a woman who had sold candles on the cathedral steps had painted out the Eye on her board and painted a lamp, and did a better trade with the lamp.

And in the chancery by the drapers' street, in the rooms over the council stair, the crown's Lieutenant sat behind thirty knights of his own household and wrote letters nobody carried, to nobody left.

"He's bought candles," said Saban, on the fifth.

He laid the slip on the chart table and sat down and blew his nose.

"Long ones. Sixty-four. Not a box of a hundred." He turned his hat. "The same man at the same chandler's as in August, and the same face on him when he paid."

"He's calling the council," said Ward.

"He's not calling anything. The council calls itself at the turn of the year, by a law older than the chancery's roof; it sat through the plague year, and the winter the dean said the prayer in a fur hat." Saban squared the slip. "The twelfth. He can't stop it, so he's bought the candles himself, to look as if he meant it."

His father was on the bench under the chart rack, where he had sat every evening since the twentieth of December with his collar up. He had not spoken since the boat. He did not speak now.

"Keller," said Emeric.

"On the mail hull. The seventh." Saban put a second slip beside the first. "He asked whether there'd be room for a box of papers. Fletcher said how big. He said about the size of a coffin, and Fletcher's mate asked whose, and Keller said nobody's yet."



Keller came up the K Amara's after ladder on the evening of the seventh with his box behind him on the shoulders of two of Fletcher's crew, and stood on the apron in his black coat with a hand on his hat, looking down at the city a mile below as a man looks at a field he has ploughed once already and will have to plough again.

"I don't care for heights," he said, when Emeric came to him. "I've told nobody that in thirty years. I'd be obliged if it went no further." He took his hand off the hat. "Good evening, Your Grace. I'm not certain yet what to call you. I'll know by the twelfth."

He would not sit at the chart table. He sat on a crate by the bulkhead with his hands folded on the box's lid and gave them the law in the dry small voice, and looked at nobody while he did it.

The crown's table had the right, older than any of them, to lay a petition before the first sitting of a year: that the finding of April be read again on new matter. The Lieutenant would have to hear it. "Then it comes to the matter," said Keller, "and the matter is a man."

"Two men," said Saban.

"Two. I have the one. He came to me." Keller looked at his hands. "The under-purser of His Highness's household wrote to my clerk on the thirtieth of December from a farm of the household's in the river country, where he has been kept since June for his health. He asked what became of a man who had carried a case and not opened it. I wrote back that nothing became of him, if he said so in council. He is sleeping in my clerk's mother's back room and eating a great deal." He paused. "He corrects porters."

"And the other," said Ward.

"The other is Lord Grier."

Nobody at the table said anything. Keller waited, as if somebody might, and when they did not he unfolded his hands.

"I'll not call him," he said. "If I call him and he says what you believe he will, every lord on those benches will ask himself what His Grace of Kaldo has in his pocket that made a man stand up against his own name, and the answer they give themselves will be worse than whatever it is." He looked at Emeric for the first time. "And if I call him and he doesn't, I've lost twice on the same voice, and deserved to."

"Nobody's going to his house," said Emeric.

"No."

"Nobody's sending a letter."

"Nobody is to send him so much as a ham." Keller turned a little on his crate toward Saban. "Including at the quarter, Master Saban, which I understand is your habit."

"It was a very small ham," said Saban.

"Then it was a very small bribe, and we'll do without it." Keller folded his hands again. "Rudd can say what he did. Only Lord Grier can say why Lord Grier voted as he did, and only if he chooses to. If he doesn't, I'll put Rudd up anyway and lose properly, and write it down."

"You said in April you'd won the law," said Emeric.

"I had." Keller looked at the bulkhead. "Law isn't what the council lacked in April. It lacked a reason to be ashamed in public, and it had a cardinal to tell it not to be." He was quiet. "It has no cardinal now. That's all that's changed. A great deal hangs on very little, as a rule."

From the bench under the chart rack, his father said, "Will he stand?"

It was the first thing he had said all evening. Keller looked at him, and the magistrate went out of his face, and what was left was older and a good deal more tired.

"I don't know, sir," he said. "I'd like to."

Emeric did not say what he thought: that a man in a cold library in May had put his hand out palm up on a chair's arm and asked whether they would know, up there, that he had. Not from us. He had promised that. He had promised nothing about the man.



Matthias Brey had a cabin of his own aboard the K Endeavor, aft of the hospital hold, a stores room that still smelled of lamp oil; a cot and a bucket and a stool and a knight on the door, and a slate, because he had asked for one every hour of his first morning until Imani, who could not stand a man asking for the same thing twice, found him one.

When Saban went in on the eighth with Emeric behind him, the slate was full on both sides and propped against the bucket to dry.

"You'll want this," said Brey.

He was a narrow little man in plain black with a beard he had not meant to grow, and he sat on the cot with his knees together like a boy outside a schoolmaster's room, and looked at Saban the way he had looked at everything since the cookshop step: all of it at once, with nothing on his face.

"Everybody who came to the house above the falls between the night we came down from Ostrel and the fifteenth of December," he said, "who wasn't of the house. Day and hour where I had the hour. I've put the weather in. You'll want to know who came in the rain; people don't, as a rule, unless they've reason." He nodded at the slate. "I asked for paper on the second day and was told paper would be considered."

Saban picked the slate up and turned it to the porthole's light.

"The second cardinal," Brey said, before Saban could read it. "Came down from Ostrel with us in the carriage, with his books behind on two mules, more than the mules liked. Stayed. The two canons with him; they're nobody; one snores, and the other doesn't know the first one snores, which tells you how often they talk. A bishop out of the north twice in November, both times with a cold. The Kesrath envoy's secretary three times, a long young man with good boots. The chancery's rider every Friday with the week's sheets, until the second of December. After that nobody but the baker's boy at seven and a woman from the lower town with eggs, because His Eminence paid for eggs in silver and nobody else in the city was paying for anything."

"You're very thorough," said Saban.

"I'm very bored." Brey looked at the bucket. "And I've had the soup."

Saban did not look up from the slate. "The soup's Mistress Imani's."

"Then I'll be careful," said Brey. "I'll say only that it's a soup with a great deal in it, all of it boiled until it apologised, at eleven and at six, for seventeen days, and I could draw you every barley grain." He considered. "It's better than the house's. The house had a cook who came up on the Tuesday and made a pot of something brown that we ate until the Friday, and on the Friday a pot of something else brown that we ate until the Tuesday. His Eminence had bread and water and never asked what was in the pot. I asked once. The cook said it was soup, as if I'd asked whether the river was wet." He rubbed his beard. "Nine years of Tuesday soup and Friday soup. You'd think a man who could read your mind across a room would want to know what was in his own kitchen."

"He didn't read yours," said Emeric.

Brey looked at him. It was the first time he had, since the door.

"No," he said. "He didn't need to. I was the man who walked behind. You don't read the man who walks behind; you tell him which side of the road." He looked down at his hands. "I'll tell you one thing that's not on the slate. He didn't ride, not if he could help it. Twice in nine years, and both times with the reins in his left hand and the right under his cloak, like a man carrying a tray. He walked, or he took the closed carriage, or he stood still and let the world come to him, which it did. The bay was mine. He'd put his left hand on her neck and say she thought too much." He was quiet. "Your man says he rode north."

"With both hands on the reins," said Saban.

"Both hands," said Brey.

He did not say anything more about it. He looked at the bucket, and his face did nothing, as it had done nothing on the cookshop step; and Emeric felt his note go small and careful, the way a man goes careful who has put his foot on a stair in the dark and found one step fewer than there should be.

"Where's the bay?" said Brey, to the bucket.

"In the cookshop's yard," said Saban. "The owner's feeding her. He says she's eating him out of oats."

"She would." Brey nodded once. "She thinks too much and eats too much. She was always the better of the two of us." He looked up. "Do I get a better soup, if there's more?"

"No," said Saban.

"I'll tell you anyway," said Matthias Brey. "I've nobody else to talk to, and your knight on the door won't. I've tried him on the weather. He just looks at it."



The council hall was at the top of the chancery's council stair, long and high and cold, the temporal benches down the left and the spiritual down the right and the gallery over the right-hand benches on its old black brackets; and at the far end, up three steps, the dais and the council chair, at the head of the long crown's table that ran down the hall between the benches like a road between two fields.

Sixty-four candles in the iron crowns overhead. Emeric had counted them coming in.

He sat at the gallery's end with Ward beside him. The door wardens had let them up without a word, and the gallery had emptied round them. He wore a plain dark coat and no badge and kept his right hand in his lap, and the lords below looked up at him as they came in, and looked away, and looked again.

Of the nineteen spiritual seats, five had men in them: the dean of the chapter, a very deaf abbot from the river country, and three bishops of the south whom nobody had sent for. The cardinal's carved chair at the head of their benches stood empty under its canopy, with the Eye on its back, and every lord who came in looked at it on his way to his seat.

The temporal benches were full but for Aldane's seat on the first bench, because Aldane was in the north with his horse. On the end of the fourth bench a big grey man sat by himself with a space either side of him that nobody filled, in a black coat brushed very hard, his face fresh-shaved with two cuts on it, holding his hat on his knees.

Grier did not look at the gallery. Emeric did not look at Grier for long.

The dean said the prayer. It was a short one, and he said it quickly, as if he would rather have been somewhere warm.

And the Lieutenant came in by the door behind the dais.

Corbin came in the way he came into every room, as if the room had been waiting for him and had been a little late getting ready. He wore the crown's red, plain and very well cut, and no circlet, and stood by the council chair with one hand on its back and looked down both benches, and at the gallery last, for exactly as long as courtesy asked.

He looked rested and pleasant and at his ease, a man who had sat six weeks over a stair with nowhere to go; and Emeric, who could feel the note under the face, knew it the way you know a fiddle string is wound one turn too far, by the sound it makes when nothing is touching it.

"My lords," said Corbin, and sat.

The first business was the turn of the year: the rolls, the seals, the custodies, a realm telling itself it still existed. Corbin took it easily and made a small joke about the candles, which the benches laughed at, not quite enough. Then the clerk of the council said that the crown's table had laid a petition, and Corbin said, "We'll hear the crown's table," as a man says he'll take the soup.

Keller stood up.

He stood at the crown's table below the dais with a single sheet under his folded hands and read the petition in the old form without raising his voice: that the finding of the fourteenth of April last, touching His Majesty's oath, be read again on new matter; that the new matter was a man of His Highness's own household who had carried a thing to a lord's door in April and was ready to say so; and nothing else.

"New matter," said Corbin.

"New to the council, my lord."

"A man of my household." Corbin smiled a little. "I'd no idea I kept men of such importance."

"Nor had he, my lord," said Keller. "He wrote to say so."

That got a laugh too, a better one, from the back of the temporal benches, and Corbin let it go by him as a man lets a cart go by, and turned his hand over on the chair's arm.

"Then call him, Master Keller."

Keller turned toward the hall's west door, where his clerk stood with a hand on the latch.

And on the end of the fourth bench Lord Grier stood up.

He stood up badly. He put his hand on the back of the bench in front and pushed, and came up all at once, a big man who had never stood up in a full hall without a cup in his hand; and every head on the temporal benches turned, one after another down the rows, like wheat when the wind goes over it.

"My lord," said Grier. His voice was too loud. He heard it, and brought it down. "My lord, I'd like to be heard."

"Lord Grier." Corbin did not move. "The crown's table has the floor. You'll have your turn."

"I've had my turn." Grier looked at his hands on the bench back. "I had it in April. I'd like it back."

Beside Emeric, Ward went perfectly still, which was the most Ward ever did.

Corbin could have stopped him. Emeric watched him think it, and look along the full benches, eleven of them men who had buried a son off the tower's floor, and decide not to; because a Lieutenant who would not let a lord speak in council in the old form was a Lieutenant with nothing left but thirty knights on a stair.

"Then be heard, my lord," he said, as kindly as a man could say it.

Grier did not look at him, or at the gallery. He told the council what he had done in about the time it takes to drink a cup of wine, and not well; he went back twice and lost his place once.

That he had owed the vintners eleven hundred and his saddler eleven gold for years, and every lord on the benches had known it and some had dined on it. That in the first week of April all of it had been paid, by nobody he knew. That a man had come to his house the week before the sitting, at night, in the rain, and told him friends of the crown hoped he'd see where the crown's good lay. "I thought it was the Thursday," Grier said. "I've been told since it was the Tuesday. It rained all that week." That he had voted against the Lieutenant for six years, and in April voted with him, sober, for the first time in his life in either; because he had been ashamed of being poor for longer than of anything, and had not known until April there was anything he would be more ashamed of.

"My father held the ford at Carrow for a day with forty men," said Grier, to the table. "The old king knighted him in the water. I'd have liked to be able to say I was his son in this hall. I can't. I sold it." He took a breath that shook. "I take back my voice. It wasn't mine to give. I'd sold it already."

And he sat down, as badly as he had stood up, and put his hat back on his knees, and looked at it.

The space on either side of him stayed empty. But an old lord on the bench in front, with white hair to his collar, turned round in his seat and looked at Grier a long while, and then reached back and put his hand on Grier's arm, once, the way a man puts his hand on a horse that has been badly frightened, and turned back.

Emeric had not asked him. He sat with that in the gallery, his hands in his lap. Not because I asked. He had said it in a library in May without knowing whether it was true.

"Master Keller," said Corbin, after a while. His voice had not changed. "You'd a man to call."

"I have, my lord."

Rudd came in by the west door behind Keller's clerk.

He was what Grier's memory had kept: a man of forty or so in a long grey coat, plain and good, a thin clean-shaven careful face, the narrow hat in his hand. He walked up the hall to the barrier and bowed to the dais the way an upper servant bows to the house he serves, and Emeric saw him not look at the man in the chair.

Keller asked him four questions, and he answered four.

His name: Josiah Rudd, under-purser of His Highness's household these eleven years. Whether on the Tuesday evening before the April sitting he had gone to Lord Grier's house on the household's warrant: he had, at seven, on his own cob, and come back before eleven with the cob lame on the near fore, as the groom had written in the stable book, which Keller held up. Whether he had carried anything: a case, with a sheet in it. And whether he knew what the sheet was.

"I don't open cases, sir," said Rudd. "I carry them."

"Who gave you the case?"

"The household's purser, sir." He paused, and corrected himself exactly, the way a man corrects a porter. "The purser gave it me, on a warrant. Whose warrant I couldn't say. I'm under-purser. I read what I'm given."

He did not look up at the dais. He had not looked up at it once.

"Thank you, Master Rudd," said Keller, and Rudd bowed again to the dais and stood where he was, with his hat in his hand and the ink on the side of his second finger.

Corbin had listened to all of it with his chin on his hand. When it was done he sat back.

"A man who owed his saddler," he said, gently, to the hall, "and a clerk who doesn't read. It's very affecting." He turned his head and looked down at Keller. "I'll ask what any lord here will ask, Master Keller, and then we can all go to our dinners. Whose money? It went somewhere. It came from somewhere. Debts aren't paid by the weather." He spread his hand on the chair's arm. "You found a man. You didn't find a book."

In the gallery Emeric sat very still.

He had last heard that sentence in a wine store in Kaldo, in a woman's voice, her gloved hands folded, on the night she said she would say a thing once. He had not expected to hear it in this hall, pleasantly, in his brother's mouth. Beside him Ward did not turn his head. Below, Keller did not either.

"No, my lord," said Keller. "The council hasn't been asked to read a book." He unfolded his hands and laid one of them flat on his single sheet. "It's been asked to count."



Keller had been frightened in every court he had ever stood in.

He had been frightened at twenty-six, when his voice went up at the end of a clerk's will like a boy's; and at thirty, when he sent a man to the rope for the first time and walked home the long way so as to be sick in the river and not in his own street; and thirteen years ago, across a table from a prince of the blood who was supposed to be a drunk and was not; and in April, all day, losing by one voice. He had never once let it reach his hands. That was what the file was for, and the folded hands on it. He had learned it from his first master, who had been frightened in every court for fifty years and died of it, people said, though Keller thought it more likely the port.

He was frightened now. He did not look at the dais.

"The finding of the fourteenth of April," he said, "was made by forty-three voices of sixty-four. The clerk of the council will have the roll."

The clerk had the roll. He was an old man with a wen on his jaw who had kept it twenty-two years and had once been sick on the chancery steps in Keller's presence, after a sitting about the price of bread, and neither of them had ever mentioned it. He read the April count in his thin exact voice: forty-three for, twenty-one against.

"A finding against the crown's oath," said Keller, "is good by two parts in three of the council, and not otherwise. That's the old law; I only read it. Two parts in three of sixty-four is forty-two voices and two-thirds of a forty-third. The finding had forty-three, and was good by that two-thirds of a man and nothing over. I said so at the time."

"It's in the roll," said the clerk, who had written it.

"A voice given for money is no voice. That's older still, the bench law of the first council; the chancery's copy is the third, and the rats have had the margin." Somebody on the temporal benches laughed, and stopped. "A lord of this council has stood in his place and said his voice in April was bought, and taken it back. A man of His Highness's household has said he carried the payment to that lord's door. The council has the stable book. It needs nothing else." He let a breath go. "Forty-two."

The clerk wrote it. Keller heard the pen.

"Forty-two is not two parts in three of sixty-four," said Keller. "It falls short by two-thirds of a man. So there was no finding. Not a finding since undone: none. What the council did on the fourteenth of April it did not do."

He had written the next part three times since December and torn it up three times. He did not need it. The four lines he had sent south at the escheat, each holding by the one above, had been in his head since August; and now he took them down again in the same order, the way a man takes down a wall he built himself.

"There being no finding, there was no keeping. His Majesty was committed in April to the keeping proper to a soul in question by a finding that was not made. He was held in his rooms, and on the close, and on the river road, by nobody's lawful order. He was a king in council's keeping by no council's voice."

"There being no lawful keeping, there was no treason in the taking. The council found in August that His Majesty's person was taken by force on the night of the second of July from the council's keeping, at the crossing of the river road, by men of the house of Kaldo; and found it treason if and only if the keeping was lawful. Those were the words the crown's table certified. The keeping was not lawful. Men who take a king out of nobody's keeping and carry him home have done what the old law would have called a kindness, and had a name for. I'll not trouble the council with the name. It's in the old tongue, and long."

A sound went down the temporal benches that was not quite a laugh.

"There being no treason, there is no escheat. The duchy of Kaldo, with Grayrock and Milford and Wayford, every right and rent and office and court in it, was declared forfeit from the date of a finding that was not made, for a treason that was not done. The instrument is void for want of its cause. The patent stands as it stood, and every oath sworn to its holder binds as it bound." He took a breath. "And the realm being governed by its Lieutenant by custom only while its king cannot govern it, and its king being in nobody's keeping, the custom has no ground to stand on. His Majesty is His Majesty. He was the whole time."

He sat down. He found he had sat down; his hands were on the sheet, folded, and under the table his knees had begun, quite quietly, to shake.

Nobody said anything for a while. Grier looked at his hat. Rudd looked at the floor. At the gallery's end the Duke of Kaldo, as he was again as of about a quarter past three by the hall clock, sat with his hands in his lap and did not move, and Keller understood without looking up that he had sat exactly so all afternoon, so that nobody in this hall could say afterward that he had leaned.

The clerk of the council cleared his throat.

"My lord," he said to the dais. "The council will want the question put. Whether the count stands."

"Put it," said Corbin.

The clerk put it. Did any lord dispute the count of forty-two. Nobody did. A lord of the south opened his mouth, looked at the empty chair under the canopy, and shut it. The deaf abbot asked his neighbour what had been said, was told loudly, said "Good," and went back to sleep.

Then Keller looked up at the dais, because by then he had to know.

Corbin sat in the council chair with his hand on its arm and looked down the hall, perfectly pleasant, as he had at the start; and Keller, who had built a career on the half breath before a man decides what to say, saw that the pleasantness had stayed on a little too long. That it was still there because nobody had told it to go. That for perhaps two breaths the crown's Lieutenant had forgotten he was wearing it.

Then he remembered, and stood up out of the chair.

"Then the council has its king," said Corbin, "and nothing before it. My lords, I'll not keep you from your dinners." And he went down the three steps and out by the door behind the dais without looking back at the chair or the gallery, unhurried, as if he had remembered a letter.

The council rose untidily, the way a hall rises when the man meant to tell it to has left. Keller sat with his hands folded on his sheet until most of them had gone, because he did not trust his knees; then he gathered up his sheet and the stable book and went down the council stair and out into the January dusk, and walked home.

He had two rooms over a bookbinder's in the lane behind the drapers' street, and had had them twenty-nine years. His landlady had left a cold supper under a cloth. He looked at it. He hung his hat and coat on their peg, in the right order, and went into the back room and knelt by the washstand and was sick into the basin, thoroughly, for some time.

Then he lay down on the bed in his clothes, which he had not done since he was a student, and was asleep before he could take his boots off.

He woke at five, in the dark, with his boots still on and his collar cutting his neck, so hungry that he thought at first he was ill again.

The bookbinder's kitchen was warm, and his landlady was up, raking out the range, as she was up at five every morning of her life; and she looked at him in his slept-in clothes and at his face, and said, "Well?"

"Forty-two," said Keller.

She did not know what it meant. She knew him, though, after twenty-nine years. She put the pan on.

He ate four fried eggs, and a cold mutton pie, and a slab of the ham she had been keeping for Sunday, and two rounds of fried bread, and a whole loaf with butter; he drank a pot of tea, and most of a second she made without being asked; and when she put down a piece of seed cake that had certainly not been meant for breakfast, he ate that too, and sat back with his collar undone and his hands folded on his stomach, and looked at the range.

"You've won something," said his landlady.

"I've won," said Keller, "the law." He thought about it. "Again. They were listening, this time."

He would need a clean collar. The king sat at nine.



Ward had been at the foot of the council stair at six the evening before, with Osric and four of his people, and Emeric a little apart in the square, because if he was to be anywhere it was not in that door.

The clerks came out at six, because nobody had told them to stop: a long untidy file of black coats and satchels, Saban's old copying clerk among them, who did not glance at Emeric. Then the stair was empty, with the lamp at its head and the household's knights on the landing under it.

Then Corbin came down.

He came down with his captain a step behind him and stopped on the last stair, so that he stood a step above Ward and could look down at him, which he did.

"Captain," he said, without turning his head. "Stand the household down. Home tonight, those that have homes, the palace barracks for those that don't, and without their arms." He looked at Ward. "I assume that's what you're here to ask."

"Yes, Highness," said Ward.

"Then you needn't." Corbin came down the last step. "I'll walk up to my own rooms. I assume I'm to be watched doing it."

"Yes."

"Good," said Corbin, pleasantly. "I'd think less of you if you said anything else."

It was Sabina's sentence, word for word, the one she had said to Emeric in the counting room under the hill; and Emeric did not think Corbin knew he had said it.

The captain went up. In a while the household's knights came down, thirty of them, in twos, without their swords, and went out across the square into the dusk; none of them looked at Ward, and most of them looked at Emeric. Osric's people let them go.

Corbin stood on the chancery's steps when they had gone and looked up over the roofs at the sky above the hill, where there had been a spire all his life, and there was nothing now but the dark and the hulls' lamps. His face did nothing while he looked. Not on purpose: it simply was not doing anything, the way a face looks when a man has forgotten there is anybody in the world to see it.

Then he remembered, and looked across the square at Emeric, once, for about as long as courtesy asked.

He did not say anything. He went up the hill toward the palace and his own wing of it with two of Osric's people twenty yards behind him, walking well, his hands behind his back.

His father was on the bench under the chart rack when Emeric came up the K Amara's ladder. Keller's clerk had taken the sheet to the drapers' street house at half past four and the attic set had sent it up before five, so that the king had read his own restoration on a slip of the set room's paper, and was holding it.

"Forty-two," said Harrold.

"Yes."

"And two-thirds." His father looked at the slip a while longer, and folded it, and put it inside his coat. "I'll sit in the morning. Keller will want a clean collar." He stood up. "Is your brother in his rooms?"

"Walking up to them. He stood his household down himself. He wasn't asked."

Harrold thought about that.

"No," he said. "He wouldn't wait to be." And went to his cabin.



The king sat at nine.

He came into the council hall on the thirteenth by the great west door, not the door behind the dais, in the crown's red and the thin gold circlet, and walked the length of the hall between the benches, slowly, alone, because he had asked Ward to wait at the door; and the benches stood up as he passed them, row by row, until the whole hall was on its feet behind him.

Emeric stood at the foot of the dais steps on the left, in the place the Holder of Kaldo stood at council, which he had never once stood in before.

His father went up the three steps to the council chair.

It was an old chair, black oak gone nearly to iron, high in the back and broad in the seat, made for some king nobody remembered who had been very big; and since the year his father came home from his hunting there had been a footstool under it again, a low black oak thing with a cushion of crown red worn pale in the middle, set where a man's feet would go if they did not reach the floor. His father's feet had always reached the floor. Nobody had ever used it that Emeric had seen. It had stood there through every council of his boyhood, as much a part of the dais as the steps.

It was not there.

On the boards in front of the chair there were four small squares paler than the oak round them, each the size of a thumbnail, worn in by its four feet standing in the same place for years. Nothing else. Somebody had taken it away and put nothing in its place.

His father stopped by the chair with his hand on its arm.

He looked down at the four pale marks. Emeric saw him do it: a very little turn of the head, no more than a man makes to see whether he has a step to mind; and then he stayed so a breath longer than a man needs to see a step.

He did not say anything. He did not look round to see who had seen. His face did what it did in a room full of eyes, which was nothing; and the hall, looking at the king and the circlet and the chair, saw nothing, because there was nothing to see. Only Emeric, at the foot of the steps, saw the head turn and stay.

He had a man's voice in his head, his other brother's, by a fire years ago, making a face over a cup. They brought him a footstool and he had them take it away again. And his own, answering: he took it away himself.

His father sat down in the council chair, and set his boots flat on the bare boards, either side of the four pale marks, and looked down the hall.

"My lords," said Harrold. "Sit down. I'm told I've been away."

They sat. Somebody near the back laughed, and was not hushed.

He did not make a speech. He said that the council would sit again at the first of the month, as it always had; that he would take it kindly if the chancery bought its own candles in future; and that the morning's business was the crown's court, which he would hear from the chair, as his grandfather had, so that nobody need walk anywhere in the cold. He nodded to Keller, and Keller stood up.

The court took less of the morning than the candles. Dace Callis, flown up from Kaldo's keep on the mail hull with a fresh dressing on his head, stood at the barrier in his forester's coat, and Lord Lisle, who had come into the capital in December under the Lieutenant's protection and found that morning he had none, stood beside him and would not look at his own forester. Keller read the charge: the raid on Kaldo's South Gate before dawn on the second of December by Lisle's household men and a company of the Wayford road, with two hired grey mages, under no lawful order; and the stone forced under the culvert at Fallow Bank. Lisle said he had acted on the Lieutenant's word; Keller said the Lieutenant's word had stood on a custom with no ground under it. Lisle's household was forfeit to the crown, house and woods and rents, and he kept his life and his name and went to his wife's people in the east. Callis was a hanging matter and knew it. He asked to be heard, and gave the court for his life the whole of the Lieutenant's dispatch route on the Wayford road and past it: every post house and change of horse, every house that took a packet in and sent one on, and the names on the covers. Ferra, when she had it by cipher, wrote it down and spelled his name right.

The king rose at noon and the hall rose behind him.

He went out by the great west door the way he had come in, and behind him the old black chair stood empty on the dais with the four pale marks in front of it, where nobody would notice them who did not know they were there.



Saban retired his double seller that week with a pension and a story, the story being the one the man was to tell in every tavern in the capital for the rest of his life, about how he had sold the Duke of Kaldo's own plans to the Lieutenant's people all summer and never once been caught; Saban wrote it for him himself, and made him say it back twice.



Ferra was on the set at eleven.

She had sent Halse for tea, she said, which was the first thing she said, and meant that there was nobody in the line room but herself and the lamp and the window with the loft in it; and Emeric had sent the K Amara's clerk to his supper and sat in the little iron room alone with the horn.

"You have your duchy," said Ferra, across seven hundred miles of dark with the hiss under it, precise and a little dry, as if she had the paper in front of her, which she did.

"I'm told I had it the whole time."

"Keller's very good." The pen. "I've had it in the book since August as written off."

"Written off."

"In pencil," said Ferra.

He laughed, and the hand that had held the horn all evening without his thinking about it remembered it had two new knuckles and complained.

"Why pencil?"

"Because I'm not a fool, Emeric." He heard her turn a page. "I'll rub it out in the morning and ink it back. Four baronies and every parish by name. I spelled Wayford's bridge ward wrong the first time in August and had to do the page again, and I couldn't bear it."

"Is there anything in the book you didn't write off?"

The set hissed.

"The fleet," said Ferra. "I never wrote the fleet off. Or Ward, or Imani's hospital hull, or Armand, or the cocoon, or your mother, since you sent her." A pause. "Or you."

"Not even in pencil."

"I've never once had you in pencil," said Ferra, as if it were a figure, and turned another page, and he heard her pen stop, and not go on for a while. "Halse is coming back with the tea. Your hand."

"Out of the strap. I wrote the date with it."

"Ward told me. He said it leans." The pen again. "Bring it home. I want it on the manifest."



When she had gone off the set he went up to the chart table and sat there with the lamp turned low and the ship asleep round him, and nobody on the bench under the chart rack for the first time in three weeks.

Then he took a fresh stack of the chart room's paper out of the drawer and squared it, and uncapped the pen with his right hand, and began.

He had known since the end of December that he would, the way a man knows in October that there will be snow. On another world men had built a thing like it and given it an ugly practical name. You couldn't make yourself unkillable. You could make killing you cost more than it bought. He had a word for it in his head that nobody in this world had ever heard, and he did not write the word. He wrote the cover first, the way Augustine had written covers for twenty years: the words that put a letter into the hands of a chamberlain who answered to one man only, and the seal that went past every cardinal on the way. Then he wrote the page.

It was not a long letter. When it was done he read it through once, and it was cold and plain and exact and said what he meant, and it was the most dangerous thing he had ever written down.

He could send it. Saban had roads north that would take a sealed cover to the edge of the Empire without asking what was in it, and past that the cover would carry itself.

He did not. He knew what Ferra would ask, the first thing, before she had read to the foot: the question he had not asked it himself, because he had been thinking about the man at the other end and not the people at this one. She would put her finger on one line and ask whom it hurt, and want the answer in figures. She would be right to.

He left the page unfolded on the top of the stack and laid the stack in the chart table's drawer under the river charts, and turned the small brass key, and buttoned the key into his shirt's breast pocket, where for a few days in December there had been a stone.

Then he turned the lamp out and sat a while in the dark, with the city a mile below him sleeping under no towers and no spire, and his father's note quiet across the passage, and in the north, at the far end of a thread nobody else could feel, nothing at all.
