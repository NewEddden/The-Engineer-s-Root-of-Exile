Chapter 275 - Burning Water

Zale came up to the castle on the first night of September with his sea bag already on his coaster's deck, and asked Emeric to come down to the inn and drink with him, because he was sailing on the morning tide and he did not like to sail from a place without drinking in it first.

"It's a rule," he said, at the hall door, in the dusk.

"Whose?"

"Mine. I made it when I was nineteen. I've broken most of the others." He looked past Emeric into the hall, where Ferra and Imani were at the long table with Ferra's book between them and their heads together over it, and Bram at the hearth end with his boots off. "Just you. If that's all right. I've things to tell you, and I'd sooner tell them once."

So they went down through the lower town in the warm dark together, the big sailor and the duke, with the arc lamps humming on their poles over the market square and the new street lamps on the river road beyond, and a dog following them half the way for no reason; and in the inn on the quay Zale took the corner table under the stair, where the lamp didn't reach, and bought two pots of the brown and set one in front of Emeric, and drank half of his own straight off, and wiped his mouth, and said:

"I went past the last islands."



He told it the way sailors tell things, plainly and in the wrong order, going back for what he had missed.

He had taken the chronometer west in the spring, the one Emeric had given him at midwinter two years back, past the islands where he traded, past the islands where he had been once, to the last ones: a scatter of rocks and reef and three green humps with people on them, a long way out, where the charts he had bought on the coast simply stopped, the ink running out at the edge of the paper as if the man who drew them had stopped believing in the sea.

"Nobody goes further," said Zale. "I knew that. Everybody knows that. I went a day further." He turned his pot. "With your clock in a box in my cabin, wound twice a day, telling me where I was to a mile. First time in my life I've known where I was out there to a mile. I thought, I'll go a day, and I'll come back, and I'll know how far a day is." He drank. "I went a day. And then I went half of another. And then the water changed."

"Changed how?"

"Darker." Zale looked at his pot as though it were the sea. "Not deeper. I know deep. It was the colour, as if there was more in it. And slower. You put your hand over the side and it went round your hand like oil, not like water, and it was cold in a way water isn't." He was quiet. "And at night there was something under us."

Emeric waited.

"I didn't see it. Nobody saw it. You couldn't have seen it, at night, under that. But every man aboard knew it was there, the way you know somebody's come into a room behind you. It came up under the keel in the middle watch, and stayed there. Under us. A long time." He looked up. "Longer than the ship. I don't know how I knew that either, but I knew it. It was longer than the ship, and it stayed under us for most of an hour, and then it went down again." He drank. "It let us go. That's what it felt like. Like something big had looked at us and decided we weren't worth the trouble."

The inn was loud round them; a crew off a Grayrock barge at the long table singing something about a girl in Milford with no shame at all. Zale did not seem to hear it.

"I turned round in the morning," he said. "On the clock's word. I had it worked out to the mile where I was and how far it was home, and I thought, I've seen enough, and that's a thing I've never thought in my life at sea. And on the way back, a day out, my boy goes to light the binnacle, and the binnacle lamp's a stone. A lamp stone. I've had it eight years. It was nearly done when we went past the last islands; I'd a new one in the locker to change it. And it lit up like the day it was cut." He put his pot down. "Full. A stone that was nearly run down. Came back full, out there, on its own, with nobody touching it."

He looked at Emeric, and Emeric did not say anything, because he knew exactly what that meant, and he could not say it to Zale.

"You know what that is," said Zale. "Don't you. I can see it on you."

"I've a guess."

"Keep it, then. I'm not asking." Zale finished his pot and signalled for another. "I'm telling you because you've a clock that can find its way back, and ships that come down out of the sky, and I've a feeling you'll be wanting to know about the edge of the sea one day." He took the new pot. "I asked the islanders. When I got back to the last ones. They've stories about it, all of them. Every generation, they say, somebody goes west from there. Princes' ships. Merchants. A whole fleet of ships once, in their grandmothers' day, painted, with flags, going to find the end of the world. And none of them came back. Not one. Not a plank." He drank. "They've a saying for it. For a man who's going west. They say it to him on the beach before he goes, as if it's a blessing." He set the pot down. "Past the last island the water gets thick. And then it gets hungry."

Emeric said it over to himself. Thick. And then hungry.

"And I bought this," said Zale, and put his hand in his coat, and took out something wrapped in a bit of sailcloth, long, and laid it on the table between their pots, and unwrapped it.

It was a tooth.

It was longer than Emeric's forearm from the elbow to the wrist, and curved, and white going to yellow at the root where it had been in a jaw, and smooth, and at its point still sharp enough to cut. It lay on the table between the beer rings, and a man at the next table, an old man with a fisherman's hands, put his own pot down and stared at it.

"One of the islanders had it," said Zale. "In his hut, hanging from the roof beam. His grandfather found it on the beach, he said, after a storm, the year the painted fleet went. I gave him a knife for it and he thought I was robbing myself." He pushed it toward Emeric. "Pick it up."

Emeric picked it up.

And nearly dropped it.

It was full. That was the first thing; not full like a charged stone, but full the way a living thing is full, all through, dense with mana the way Armand was dense, or Ward with his tracery lit, but denser, much denser, denser than anything living he had ever touched in his life. Denser than his own note since the pod. It sat in his hand like a coal sits in the hand, heavy with something that wanted to be out.

And it was getting out. That was the second thing. He could feel it going. Out of the tooth's surface, all over it, into the air of the inn, into Kaldo's air, which was the thickest air in the four baronies and was nothing to this; a slow steady leak, like heat off a stone taken out of the fire, like breath going out of a man who has been holding it a long time. He could feel the tooth getting lighter in his hand, not in its weight, in the other way. In an hour it would be a little less than it was. In a year it would be bone.

It could not live here. Whatever this had been in, it could not have lived here. It had come from somewhere so thick with mana that this air, his air, Kaldo's air, was a vacuum to it; and the tooth was bleeding out into it, slowly, the way a fish bleeds out on a deck.

"What is it?" said Zale. "You've gone white."

"I don't know," said Emeric, which was true. He put it down on the sailcloth very carefully. "But I'd like to keep it. If you'll sell it."

"Sell it." Zale laughed. "It's yours. I bought it for you. I bought it the minute I saw it, I thought, he'll know what to do with that." He wrapped it up again and pushed it across the table. "Don't tell me what you do with it. I've had enough of knowing things this summer to last me."

The old man at the next table had not taken his eyes off it.

"West," he said.

They both looked at him. He was very old, brown and folded as a walnut, with a fisherman's jersey and a fisherman's hands round his pot, and Emeric knew his face from the river without knowing his name.

"That come from the west," said the old man. "I heard you. Past the islands." He nodded slowly. "We've one of those here, you know. Not west. The other way." He jerked his head at the wall, at the dark beyond it, south and east. "Out past the bar. Sou'-east. Nobody goes out sou'-east past the bar, not from Fletcher's, not from here. Never have." He drank. "Bad water out there. It takes you. You'll be fishing, and you'll look up, and you're further out than you was, and you never felt the current. And there's land out there that's there one tide and gone the next. The drowned land, my father called it. You'll see it in the morning, low, in the haze, and you'll look again and it's gone."

"Have you seen it?" said Emeric.

"Once." The old man looked at his pot. "My brother Davin went into the bad water. Forty-one years ago this spring. In his own boat, after mackerel. We saw him go. We were three boats over, my father and me, and we saw his sail going out, and out, and him at the tiller pulling against it and not getting anywhere, like a man rowing in his sleep. And we couldn't get to him. Every time we turned toward him the water turned us." He was quiet. "And we never saw him again. And the next morning, there was the land, low, in the haze. And then there wasn't." He finished his pot and stood up. "Wicket," he said, to Emeric. "That's my name. Everybody on the quay knows it. If you're ever going out there, Your Grace, which you won't be, you'll not find anybody in Fletcher's'll take you."

He nodded to Zale, a sailor's nod, and went out.

Zale watched him go.

"Every sea's got one," he said. "Every port. A place you don't go, and a man at the next table whose brother went." He picked up his pot. "Drink, Emeric. I sail at dawn."



Finn was on the quay at dawn.

Emeric had not known he would be. He had walked down from the castle in the grey with the tooth wrapped in its sailcloth inside his coat, because he had wanted to see the coaster go, and found the boy already there at the end of the quay by her bow, in his jacket with his hands in his pockets, waiting, as if he had been there an hour, which he had.

Zale came up from below with his hair wet. He saw Finn, and stopped at the rail.

"Well," he said.

Finn took his hands out of his pockets. He had a length of line in one of them, old line, the tarred line Zale had given him in July: and he held it up, and then, without a word, he shut his eyes, and held the two ends behind his back, and tied it. A bend. The bight in the one end and the other end through and round and under itself. It took him perhaps four heartbeats. He opened his eyes and held it out, over the gap between the quay and the rail.

Zale leaned over the rail and took it, and looked at it, and pulled on it, hard, both ways. It held. He turned it over and looked at the back of it, the way you look at the back of a piece of joinery.

"Sheet bend," he said.

"You said when you were back," said Finn. "You weren't back. You were going. I didn't want to wait."

Zale looked at him a while. Then he untied it, carefully, and leaned over the rail and gave it back to him, loose.

"Keep that," he said. "It's yours now." He straightened up. "Next time I'm back, it's the rolling hitch. Under load. In the dark." He looked at Emeric over Finn's head. "Your man Rylan's boy," he said, as if Emeric did not know. "He'll go to sea."

"His mother'll kill you," said Emeric.

"His mother'll have to catch me." Zale laughed, and went aft, and shouted at his crew; and the coaster came off the quay on the ebb with her patched brown sail going up, and drew out into the river toward the north-east arm and the marsh and the sea, with Zale at her tiller; and Emeric felt the deep note at her stern go out across the water, and the sail fill, on a morning with no wind at all.

Finn stood at the quay's end with the tarred line in his hand until she was round the bend. Then he put it in his pocket and walked back up into the town, and did not say anything to Emeric about it, then or later, which Emeric thought was probably the most seamanlike thing he had ever seen him do.



The refinery stood two miles downriver from the walls, on the town bank, below the bend, where the wind took its smell away from Kaldo nine days in ten: a long brick shed and a yard and a tank farm behind a fence, and in the middle of the yard, rising out of it like a church tower somebody had built in iron, the column.

It had grown out of the still. Two years ago the still had been a copper pot over a grate of fire stones and a coiled pipe, turning a cask of Duvrenne crude at a time into lamp oil and grease and a black tarry end that went on the roads; and then a first column, short, fed from the Isle's oil by the drum, in a yard nobody was let into. Now it was a column thirty feet high, iron, riveted, lagged in sacking against the heat, with trays inside it one above the other where the vapour climbed and cooled and dropped what it carried, each tray a little cooler than the one below; and off its side, at every height, a pipe running out to a cooler and a tank. At the top came off the lightest of it, the gas that would not go liquid at all unless you squeezed it, which went into Bram's compressor and came out in iron bottles; then gasoline, which nobody in Kaldo wanted yet; then kerosene, which every farm village in the four baronies wanted at once; then the light oil; then diesel; then the heavy oil; and at the bottom, out of the pot, slow and black, the tar that would be roads.

It ran off stones. Six fire stones in a ring in the firebox under the pot, charged by Orrin and Vesk on the days they could spare, held at the heat Tess had found was right by a valve on the column's side that she had made herself and called the governor and that everybody else called the clock, because it ticked.

And on the fourth morning of September, at the top of the run, with the column full and every tray working and Bram on the gallery at the twenty-foot tray with his hand on the pipe, one of the six stones began to go.

Emeric felt it before anybody knew. He had his sense open in the yard as he always did now, wide and low, and he felt the ring under the pot falter: one of the six notes in it going thin, and thinner, as a stone does at the end of its charge when the man who filled it filled it short. And the heat under the pot sagged with it. Not much. Enough. The column felt it; the trays felt it, from the bottom up, one after another, like a man missing a step on a stair.

"Bram," he said. "The firebox."

Bram had felt it too, through the pipe under his hand, and was already coming down the ladder.

A stone swap was a minute's work. Draw the firebox's slide, lift the spent stone out with the tongs, drop a charged one in its place, close the slide. A minute; and in that minute, with one stone gone out of the ring and the slide open on the cool morning, the pot would drop forty degrees, and the column would lose its trays one after another from the bottom up, and spend the rest of the morning getting them back, and everything that came off it until noon would be muck.

So when Bram pulled the slide, Emeric put his hand flat on the firebox's iron beside it, and held the heat.

He did not make any. That would have been conjuring, and expensive, and too much. He took the heat that was there, in the iron, in the pot, in the five stones still burning, and held it where it was; the way you put your hand round a candle flame in a draught. The air from the open slide came in cool, and he would not let the heat go out to it. He felt the iron under his palm try to cool, and kept it from cooling. It took all his attention. It was like holding a door shut against a wind with one finger: easy, if you never let your mind go anywhere else, not for a breath.

Bram had the dead stone out in the tongs, grey and spent, and dropped it in the bucket; and the new one in. The slide closed.

Emeric took his hand off the iron. There was sweat running down the inside of his shirt, and the column's trays had not felt a thing.

Bram looked at him. Then he looked at the firebox.

"Right," said Bram. "That's the last time that happens."

"It won't happen often."

"It happened once. That's often." Bram went to the side of the firebox, where there was a space under the pot's iron cradle that the stones did not use, and stood looking at it with his head on one side. "There's room here for a grate. Coal. A coal burner under the stones, banked low, that just sits there ticking over all day doing nothing, and the minute a stone goes thin it takes up the load." He looked at Emeric. "And you don't stand here with your hand on a hot box for a minute, and nobody else has to be you."

It was a week's work. They built it. By the next Saturday there was a coal grate under the pot with a hopper and a slow feed, banked, burning a shovel an hour and doing nothing but keep itself alight; and on the Monday when Bram pulled two stones out of the ring at once on purpose, with Tess on the gallery with a thermometer and Ferra at the yard gate with her watch, the grate's draught opened on Tess's clock and the column dropped six degrees and climbed back in four minutes, and nobody had their hand on anything.

"Better," said Bram.

"Better," said Emeric.



People came to look at the column. They always came to look at anything new that was tall; and that week there were the stewards from Milford about the kerosene, and two of Vantic's clerks about the price of it, and a man from the Grayrock barges who wanted to know what the bottles were and whether they'd burst, and on the Thursday half of Sela's top class, walked down the river road in a line by Sela with their slates; and Emeric found himself, on most days that week, standing in the refinery yard with his hands in his pockets explaining the column to whoever was standing there.

He did not mind. It was a good thing to explain. You heated a soup of a thousand different things, and each of them turned to vapour at its own heat, and as the vapour climbed the column it cooled, and each thing dropped out of it at the height where it was cool enough to be liquid again; so the column sorted the soup into its parts, by heat, the lightest at the top and the heaviest at the bottom.

"It's a list," he said to the stewards from Milford, on the Tuesday, and then again on the Thursday to Sela's class. "That's all a tar is. Any tar. Black muck. And the muck's a list of things, all mixed up, and if you heat it right it comes apart into the list." He nodded downriver, where, two miles off past the bend, the coke ovens' chimneys stood against the sky, smoking. "The coke ovens make a tar too. They've made one every day for ten years, and we burn it, or sell it to the barges for pitch. And it's a richer list than this one." He looked at the column. "This is fuel. Mostly fuel. That one isn't fuel." He was quiet. "That one's colour."

"Colour?" said one of Sela's girls.

"Dye. Blues. Reds. Anything." He watched the coke-oven smoke a moment. "There's a thing in it called aniline. And there's a blue in it. A blue you'd never get out of a plant." He shrugged. "That's a year's work, and it's nobody's job, and it isn't tonight." He took his hands out of his pockets. "Who wants to see the bottles?"

Everybody wanted to see the bottles. Nobody asked about the blue again, not that week.

Only Abloh, who had come down with the Milford stewards because he went wherever cloth was talked about that summer, stood a moment longer at the yard gate looking downriver at the coke ovens' smoke, with his hands behind his back, and the face of a man who had spent the spring making nurses' scrubs the colour of porridge.



In the forge, at night, the column's light oil paid for itself twice.

Once in the transformers. The west half of the town had run on alternating current for a year, off engine-driven alternators and dry transformers, air-cooled, that hummed in their brick huts and ran hot in summer and could not be made any bigger without burning; and now there was oil, the light cut, clear as water and slippery as soap, that would not carry a current and would carry heat away; and a transformer sat in a tank of it ran cool at twice the load. Bram's shop built the first oil-cooled step-up that month, for the line to the coal ground, and the first substation on the east road to step it down again; and the east half of the town would come over from its old direct current by the spring.

But the transformer cores wasted power as heat, as all iron cores did, and wasted a great deal; and on the second Sunday in September the forge ran one ingot of silicon steel to a declared recipe, about three parts silicon in a hundred, with a long slow anneal at the end of it, so that the rolling mill had something to copy. Tess had made the ferrosilicon in her arc furnace for it. The sheet that came off the mill on the Tuesday was grey and plain and looked like any other sheet of steel in the world, and Bram's coil men wound it into a core and found it ran a third cooler.

And from the same silicon, on the same Sunday night, another run, which nobody had asked for.

A bar of it. The purest silicon the arc furnace could make, laid in a long boat in the forge's tube; and a ring of heat, a narrow one, that came up round one end of the bar and melted a short band of it, and then moved, very slowly, along the bar toward the other end, the melted band going with it like a bead of water going along a wire; and behind it the bar froze again, clean. The impurities went with the melt. They liked the melt better than the solid; so the band of molten silicon carried them along the bar as it moved, and left behind it, as it went, silicon cleaner than before. Five passes. Seven. Ten. By morning the dirt in the bar was all crowded into its last inch, which Emeric cut off and threw away; and the rest of it was the purest substance in Kaldo, purer than anything anybody in the world had ever made, a dull grey bar the length of his hand, which he put in a glass jar, and sealed, and put on the shelf in the forge room behind the gravity-stone door.

Ferra wrote the run up in the forge book on the Monday, as she wrote all of them, with the date and the stones it had cost and the reason. She looked at the jar on the shelf.

"What's it for?"

"Nothing yet."

She wrote that in the reason column. Nothing yet. And then she sat with her pen over it for a moment, and drew a little line under it, very neat, and didn't say why.

"You've done that before," said Emeric.

"Twice." She shut the book. "The last two times you said it, it turned into something by the spring. I'm keeping a count." She went out.



The dragon's scale would not take a mark.

He had not called it that to anybody. It was the scale, or the plate, or the thing from the cave; and on the Wednesday he took it down to Bram's shop wrapped in a cloth and asked Bram, without saying where it had come from, which Bram did not ask, to scratch it.

Bram took his best scribe. The point was Forge-hardened, charged while it formed, the hardest point in the shop; he had cut glass with it, and hard steel. He drew it across the scale's ridged face, firmly, the way he scratched a test mark on a casting.

Nothing. Not a line.

Bram looked at the point under his glass. It had gone blunt.

He looked at the scale. Then he looked at Emeric, and back at the scale, and drew the scribe across it again, much harder, with his weight behind it. The point skated, and chattered, and broke off at the tip with a tiny sound; and the scale lay there on the bench in the shop's light, ridged and dark and very light, without a mark on it.

"Tess," said Bram.

Tess came over in her chair, and looked, and took it to her arc. She held it in the tongs a hand's breadth under the carbons and struck the arc, and the shop went white, and everybody turned their faces away from the light as they had been taught; and she held it there, in the arc's heat, which melted steel like butter, for a count of twenty, and then took it out and put it on the bench.

It had gone black on the face that had been toward the arc. Soot. Bram wiped it off with his thumb, and under the soot it was the same as before, dark and ridged and whole, and when Emeric put his hand near it, it was barely warm.

Nobody said anything for a while.

"Where did you get that?" said Tess.

"I found it," said Emeric.

She looked at him the way she looked at a relay that was lying to her, and then she let it go, because it was his and he had not asked her to do anything else with it; and he wrapped it in its cloth and took it back up to the castle, and put it in the drawer in his study where the tooth was, wrapped in its sailcloth, the two of them side by side.

The tooth was a little less than it had been. He could feel that through the drawer. Going, slowly, into his air.

The scale was nothing at all. It had gone as far as a thing could go. It sat in the drawer beside the tooth, empty, and could not be marked, or burned, or broken; and he took a slip of paper and wrote on it, drained, and underlined it, twice, and put it in the drawer on top of them, and shut the drawer.



"Again," said Ward. "Less wind."

It was the river below the refinery, at night, in the middle of September, under a moon two days off full; and out on the water fifty yards off a skiff sat at anchor in the stream with Declan in it, white as a sheet in the moonlight, in a white shirt Ward had told him to wear, so that they could see whether he could be seen.

He could. Very well. Emeric had put up the veil the way he had worked it out in his head a dozen times: heat off his hands into the river's surface, a long low lift of water off the top of it as vapour, and then air, a slow push to spread it out between the bank and the boat, a white wall of steam lying on the water in the moonlight. It had worked for exactly as long as it took to be beautiful. He had got the steam up thick and white and rolling, and then put the air into it, and put in a great deal too much, because air was easy and he was tired and the steam was heavy; and the whole veil had gone away downstream in one long smooth sheet, like a sheet off a line in a gale, and was a quarter of a mile off and thinning over the reeds before he could stop it; and there was Declan in the moonlight in his white shirt, sitting in his skiff, waving.

"Again," said Ward. "Less wind."

"I heard you."

"I'm saying it again so you'll hear it in your hands." Ward did not take his eyes off the boat. "You put in wind as if you were paying for it by the barrel and it was cheap. It isn't the wind that hides him. It's the steam. The wind's only to lay it out." He was quiet. "Steam's heavy. Let it lie. It wants to lie on water. Give it a breath, not a gale."

Emeric put his hands out again over the water.

He got the steam up. He got it thick. He put in a breath of air, a very small one, the smallest he could make, and felt the steam move under it, and spread, and begin to lie out along the water toward the boat in a long low bank; and it was going. It was working. It went out ten yards, and twenty, white, rolling, lying close; and then he felt it begin to rise, because it was warmer than the night and warm things rise, and he gave it a little more air to hold it down, and it went downstream again, not all at once this time but in a long slow slide, peeling off the water in rags, until there was Declan again, in the moonlight, a little blurred.

"Better," said Ward.

"He's standing there."

"He's blurred. Last time he was waving." Ward looked at the water. "That's enough for one night. You'll get it. Not this month." He turned to go up the bank. "Steam's like men. It wants to go where it wants to go. You don't push it. You give it somewhere it'd rather be."

"Did you get that from a book?"

"I got it from a lifetime of trying to keep men in a line." Ward went up the bank. "Bring Declan in. He'll have caught his death in that shirt."



The six sat late at the long table in the hall that night, after Emeric had told them about the inn: Zale's water, darker and slower; the thing under the ship that let them go; the lamp stone come back full; the islanders' saying. He had brought the tooth up from the drawer, in its sailcloth, and it lay on the table in the lamplight, and every one of them had picked it up and felt nothing, because none of them had his sense; except Armand, who had picked it up and put it down again very quickly, and sat for a while afterward with his hand flat on the table.

"So that's the wall," said Ward.

"It's a wall," said Emeric. "It's somewhere past the last islands. The water gets thick. Things live in it that can't live here. And nobody comes back."

"And nobody goes," said Saban.

He had the tooth in his hands again; he had kept going back to it all evening, as though he were trying to price it. "That's the thing, isn't it. That's the clever thing." He set it down. "Think who'd go. Think who could. The poor can't. They haven't a ship, and they've a harvest to get in, and a lord who'd hang them for leaving it. So they don't go. The rich could, and they don't, because why would they? They've everything here. Their land, their houses, their seats at court. Nobody rich ever went anywhere they didn't have to." He turned the tooth on the cloth. "So who's left? The rich who want to. The odd one in a generation. The prince with a painted fleet and a head full of the end of the world. And he goes." He let go of the tooth. "And the sea keeps him."

"It's a filter," said Ferra.

"It's a sieve," said Saban. "With every hole the right size. The poor can't get through it, the rich won't, and the ones who will are taken. And nobody has to stop anybody. It runs itself."

"The Church never had to raise a hand," said Ward.

They all looked at him.

"Think about it." Ward had his hands on the table. "If you didn't want anybody leaving. If you'd taken a thing out of the world, as Vey said, and you wanted it to stay out. You'd want a wall. And there's the wall. And it costs you nothing. You never have to post a guard on it, or pay anybody, or hang anybody. The sea does all of it." He looked at the tooth. "The Church never had to raise a hand. All it had to do was call the land we live on the world, and tell everybody there was nothing past it."

"Ostrellin," said Armand quietly. "Their name for it. The whole of it. After their own city." He did not look up from his hand on the table. "I never once thought about that."

It was very quiet.

Then Ferra said, the way she said a figure at the bottom of a column that nobody wanted:

"What would it cost? To go anyway."

Emeric did not answer straight away. He sat with his hands round his cup and looked at the tooth on its sailcloth in the lamplight, leaking, slowly, into the air of the hall.

He knew what it would cost. He had known since the inn. Not a ship: a fleet. Ships that did not need the sea to carry them, and could carry enough to come back. People who could breathe that air. A way to mend on the far side what broke there. And something in the hand, besides; whatever had looked at Zale's ship from under it and let it go might not let the next one go. A force. Not an expedition. A force, built for it, years of building, and he could see the shape of it from here, the way you see the shape of a building from its footings.

He did not say any of that.

"I'll tell you," he said, "when I know."

Ferra looked at him a moment longer, and then wrote something in her book; and he did not ask her what.



Imani was waiting for him in her surgery.

He had not known she would be. It was past midnight when he came down from the hall, and the hospital's windows were dark except the one at the end of the long ward where the night nurse sat; but there was a light under the surgery door, and when he went past it the door opened, and she was standing in it with her sleeves rolled up and her hair tied back as if it were morning.

"In," she said.

He went in. She shut the door.

"Your hand," she said.

"Which one?"

"The one you held the tooth in. In the inn. And again tonight." She took it before he could give it to her, and turned it over under the lamp, and looked at the palm; and then she put her own palm flat against it, and he felt her look: the hollow view, as she had first found it in April with her hands on him through the pass-box, going in through the skin and along the channels, careful, a nurse's look. He let her.

"It's still in you," she said. "A little. Like heat in a stone."

"It'll go."

"I know it'll go." She did not let go of his hand. "I'm not asking about the tooth."

She was quiet a moment. He could feel her thumb against the inside of his wrist, where she always put it, and knew she was counting; she counted when she was nervous, and she was nervous, which she almost never was, and he did not know why.

"You told me on Friday," she said. "In the garden. With your face washed, in daylight, like Ferra told you to, because she told me she'd told you." She almost smiled. "That your side was ahead. Ahead of itself. That you used things there you couldn't make. And that the people on the wall were stories, there, and real here." She looked at his hand. "And that you'd like the children to hear about them first. Before they meet them."

"Yes."

"And tonight you sat at that table with a tooth that's bleeding to death in our air, and Saban told you nobody comes back. And Ferra asked you what it would cost." She looked up at him. "And you went somewhere. Behind your face. Like you do. And you came back, and you said you'd tell her when you knew." Her thumb was still on his wrist. "You already know."

He didn't say anything.

"I'm not asking that either," said Imani. "Tell her when you tell her. I'm asking something else." She took a breath. "You ate a stone in April. You were in agony for a week and I watched every hour of it. You flew. You walk on walls. You can't be touched in the yard. You held a still's whole heat in your hand on Tuesday like holding a door shut, Bram says, and came away soaked through." She was holding his hand quite hard now. "And tonight you held a thing in your hand that came out of a sea so thick our air is nothing to it, and you went white again at that table when you picked it up. And you're going to go there. One day. I know you are. I can see it on you like a rash." Her voice did not change at all. "So I'm asking you, as your doctor. What is it doing to you? All of it. The stone. The flying. The thick air you keep wanting. What is it doing to you, and do you know?"

He looked at her for a long time.

"No," he said. "I don't know."

She waited.

"I don't know what it's doing to me," he said. "Nobody's ever done it. Nobody I can ask. Armand doesn't know. The tower doesn't know. There isn't a book." He found that his voice had gone strange, and he could not do anything about it. "Every month there's more of me. More than there was. I can feel the town at night, all of it, every note, lying in bed. I can feel you across the yard. I felt that tooth before I'd touched it. And I don't know where it stops." He stopped. "I don't know if it stops. And I don't know what I'll be when it does."

"Are you afraid?" said Imani.

"Yes," he said.

He had not said it before. Not to anybody. Not to Ferra, not to Ward, not to himself in so many words. He heard himself say it in the surgery under the lamp, plainly, the one word, and it was true, and it had been true for a long time.

Imani let go of his wrist.

She reached up past his shoulder to the lamp on its bracket, and turned it down, and down, until it went out; and the surgery was dark, the dark of a room at the end of a sleeping hospital, with the faint grey of the window and nothing else.

Then she put her arms round him.

She did not say anything. She held him, hard, in the dark, with her face against his neck and her hands flat on his back, the way she held a patient who was shaking and did not want to be seen to; and he stood there in her surgery with his arms round her and his face in her hair, which smelled of carbolic and of her, and found that he was shaking, a little, and that she was not going to mention it.

They stood like that a long time.

"Good," she said at last, into his neck, very low. "Good. Now you've said it, I can do something about it."

"What can you do about it?"

"Measure it." She did not let go. "Every month. All of it. Your note, your pulse, your capacity, your sleep. Armand's numbers and mine. A chart. On my wall." He felt her breathe. "If I can't tell you where it stops, I can tell you where it's going. You won't be in the dark with it. I'll be in the dark with you. With a lamp and a pencil."

He laughed, very quietly, into her hair. She held on.

"And you'll come and see me," she said, "when it gets bad. Not when it's over. When it's bad. You'll come here, at night, like tonight, and say you're afraid. To me. Not to the wall in your study."

"Yes."

"Promise."

"I promise."

She lifted her head then, in the dark, and kissed him, slowly, the kind of kiss that is not a beginning of anything but an ending of something, a full stop at the end of a long hard sentence; and then she put her head back on his shoulder, and they stood there in the dark a while longer, and did not say anything else at all.



Ferra heard it from both of them by morning.

From Imani first, at six, at the ward's back door, where Ferra had come down early with the week's linen count: two sentences, low, while the night nurse handed over. And from Emeric at seven, at breakfast, in the hall, across the table, when she looked at his face and said, "Well?" and he told her, in fewer words than Imani had used.

Ferra buttered her bread.

"Good," she said. "Term two." She bit into the bread. "Imani's chart goes on her wall. I want a copy for mine." She chewed. "And the next time you're afraid at midnight, you can come up two flights of stairs as easily as one, and I'll have my lamp out too."

Imani, beside her, did not look up from her porridge; but she put her foot on Emeric's under the table, and left it there.
