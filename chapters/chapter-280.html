Chapter 280 - Clear Water

The fourth tank was Declan's fault, and Declan was very pleased about it.

The treatment works had stood on the river bank below the town for two years by then, since the September of the ninth year, and done what Emeric had built them to do: the town's sewage came down the great brick trunk under the river road into three long settling tanks, where it sat and was still and let fall what would fall; and then out over the two round beds, sixteen paces across, walled in brick and full of broken stone, where the arms went round and round on their pivot dribbling it over the stone, and the stone's slime ate it; and out at the bottom into the river cleaner than the river was above the town. Imani tested it every week. It had been one of the proudest things in her ward's books for two years.

And every winter it slowed.

The slime on the stones did not like the cold. Nothing alive did. From November to March the beds worked at half their pace, and the water at the bottom came out greyer, and Imani's figures in her book went the wrong way week by week until the spring; and this year, with the town grown by a third since the beds were built and every house on the main, the beds had begun to go grey in October, and Imani had stood on the bank with her book on the last Monday of the month looking at the outfall, and said a word Declan had never heard her use.

So Declan had done a thing.

Declan could read a level. It was the thing he was known for, in the garrison and out of it: give him a spirit level and a straightedge and a stretch of ground and he would tell you to a hair which way the water would go, and he would be right. He had laid the treatment works' fall with Emeric two years ago and never forgotten a figure of it. And he had looked at the three settling tanks, and at the fourth, empty, that they had built for when the town grew and not yet needed; and thought, and gone to Imani.

"The beds are slow because the slime's cold," he had said. "It's cold because it's lying out on the stones in the wind. What if it wasn't on the stones? What if it was in the water? Swimming about in a tank, in the warm, under a roof?" He had shrugged. "Water keeps its heat better than stones do. And if it's stirred, it all gets at the muck at once, instead of only what trickles past it."

Imani had looked at him for a long time.

"How would you stir it?"

"Bubbles," said Declan. "From the bottom. Air. It wants air, doesn't it, the slime? That's why the arms go round, to give it air." He shrugged again. "So give it air. Lots. All the time. From underneath."

And because there was no blower at the works yet, and Imani wanted to know before she asked anybody to build one, Emeric had gone down to the fourth tank on the first Wednesday in November with them both, in the cold, and filled it from the first settling tank, a hundred tons of grey water, and stood at its edge with his hands out over it.

Air was the easiest thing he had. He did not have to make it; it was all round him. He reached down through the grey water to the tank's floor, the way he reached into the river for Hedda's lessons, and found the floor, and put air there. Not a gust. A breath, broken into a thousand smaller breaths, all along the floor, rising. The tank's surface began to seethe, gently, everywhere, like a pot on the edge of the boil; and stayed seething, all the time he held it.

He held it for an hour. Then Declan took over, with a hand pump and a length of perforated pipe along the bottom that the works' fitter had made in the morning, and pumped, swearing, in turns with the fitter, all that day and the next; and on the Friday Bram's men brought down a blower, an electric fan in an iron scroll that the works had built for the blast furnace and not used, and bolted it to a pipe, and it ran all night and every night after.

And the tank changed.

By the end of the first week the grey water had flecks in it. Small brown flecks, like tea leaves, drifting in the bubbles. By the second, the flecks were clumps, soft, brown, the size of a fingernail, a whole tankful of them turning over and over in the rising air like snow in a shaken glass. And when Declan turned the blower off for an hour on the second Friday to see what would happen, the clumps sank. All of them. They went down through the still water like leaves through a pond, and left above them, in the top two thirds of the tank, water that was clear.

Not river clear. Clearer than the beds made it. Clearer than the beds had ever made it, in summer, at their best.

Imani stood at the tank's edge on the second Friday with her sample jar held up against the low grey sky, and looked through it for a long time.

"It's the clumps," she said. "The brown stuff. It's eating it." She lowered the jar. "And it's eating it faster than the slime on the stones does. Because it's all in the water at once, and it's all getting air, and it isn't cold." She turned the jar. "And when you stop the air, it lets go of the water and sinks. So you can take the clean water off the top."

"And put the brown back in," said Declan, "with the next lot."

Imani looked at him.

"It's alive," said Declan. "The brown. It's the slime, but swimming. So you don't throw it away, do you? You'd put some of it back in at the start, with the next lot of muck. Like you keep some of the dough back for tomorrow's bread." He was very pleased with himself, and did not hide it. "Then it starts hungry."

Imani looked at him for a while longer. Then she wrote it down.

Tess was at the works that afternoon because Tess was at everything that had a blower in it that month; and she had been sitting in her chair at the tank's edge through all of this with her chin on her fist, looking not at the tank but at Emeric, who was standing a little behind Imani and Declan with his hands in his pockets, saying nothing.

"You've got the face," said Tess.

Everybody looked at him.

"That face," said Tess. "The one you had at the valve. When I worked out it went one way." She pointed at him. "You knew. Didn't you. You knew the brown would come. You knew Declan's tank would do that."

Emeric said nothing.

"You knew," said Imani slowly, "and you let us find it."

"Declan found it," said Emeric. "I just bubbled it."

Declan looked at him, and at the tank, and back; and something went across his face, and then went away again, and was replaced, after a moment, by the look of a man who has decided that he found it anyway.

"Activated sludge," said Emeric. "If you want a name for it. The brown." He looked at the tank. "Every town on my side that was any size did it that way, in the end. The beds were first. Then somebody stirred a tank."

"Somebody," said Imani.

"A man called Declan, probably," said Emeric; and went off up the river road toward the town, and left them there, Declan looking very pleased again, Tess shaking her head, and Imani writing in her book, and then stopping, and writing something else under it, smaller, that he did not see.



The forge ran the nitrogen converter's closure on the second Sunday in November, and the catalyst after it.

It had been coming for a year. Milford's fields ate nitrogen; every field on earth did, Emeric had told Hollis, every crop took it out of the ground and something had to put it back, and for two years at Milford that something had been the white salt off the Isle of Shards' nitrate beds, carried home in the K Endeavor's pods under the oil. And the Isle was not Kaldo's. It was a secret, and the secret could be lost on any night a Kesrath lamp moved along the ridge in the wrong direction; and if it were lost, Milford's yields would fall by a third in two years, and the four baronies would go back to buying grain.

So the forge made the hard parts of a machine that took nitrogen out of the air.

Out of the air. Ferra had made him say it twice. The air was four parts in five nitrogen, he said; every breath you took was mostly the stuff Milford needed. The trouble was that it did not want to be anything else. It was two atoms held together tighter than almost anything in the world, and to make it let go and take up hydrogen instead you had to squeeze it, very hard, a hundred times harder than a boiler squeezed its steam, and heat it red, and pass it over iron that had been made in a particular way, so that the iron would hold the atoms apart for the instant they needed. And then it came out the other end as ammonia, the sharp stuff in smelling salts, and the ammonia went into the fields.

The squeezing was the trouble. Nothing in Kaldo had ever held that kind of pressure, and the forge's first run was the closure: the great steel lid of the converter, and the seal round its rim, and the bolts, machined to a tolerance that made Bram go and sit down. The catalyst was the second: iron, fused with a little of this and a little of that, poured and cooled and broken into lumps the size of peas, in a particular way, because the way it broke was the whole of how it worked.

The nitrogen would come from the liquid-air plant, which already made liquid air for the cold rooms and boiled off nitrogen as a waste it vented to the sky. The hydrogen would come from the coke ovens' gas, which was half hydrogen already and went into the town's cookers. The vessel itself, the great thick steel bottle, would be rolled and forged at the steelworks over the winter, from the forge's masters, by Bram's men, who would build every one after it the same way with no forge at all.

"By the spring?" said Ferra, at the forge book, writing.

"The first one by the spring. Small. A ton a week, maybe, if it holds."

"And Milford stops needing the Isle."

"Milford stops needing the Isle."

Ferra wrote that in the reason column. Then she looked at it for a while; and then she wrote, under it, small, in her own back-page hand, a date in the spring, which was her guess for when the Isle's nitrate beds would stop mattering to anybody in Kaldo at all, and drew a line under it.

"Haber-Bosch," said Emeric, mostly to himself.

"What?"

"Two men. On my side. Who did this." He looked at the closure on its trestle, still warm. "One of them worked out how. The other one worked out how to do it big." He was quiet. "One of them also used what he knew to make poison gas for a war. That's the other thing about it."

Ferra looked at him for a long moment.

Then she turned to the back of the forge book, to a page he had never seen her use, and wrote something on it, and shut the book before he could see what.



The first snow came on the twelfth of November, in the night, and the Proving class rode north in it at dawn.

Sixty-two of them. The sixty-one Ward had taken on in July, and Joss, who was not a candidate for anything and was going because Imani had said that his arm would only mend all the way with the class's work, and Ward had said yes at supper and not taken it back. They had been at the base four months. They had lain in the tank, every one of them, at the start and in October, and their scans were in Ottoline's drawers with their names on the cards. They had done the cold water and the bands and the gravity hall and the long runs and the square, two by two on the oak at half again their weight, and the stone drill, and they had grown, every one of them, the slow way: Imani's figures said a fifth more in four months for the lords' sons and a quarter more for the crown's men in grey, who had worked harder, and a third more for the girl from Grayrock who had walked to Kaldo in July, who had worked harder than any of them.

And now the rite season had opened in the capital, at first snow, as it did every year, and they were riding to it.

Ward saw them off at the base gate, in the dark, in the snow, on his horse. He did not make a speech. He had made his speech in July, and he did not believe in making two. He sat on his horse by the gate with the snow on his shoulders and watched them ride out past him, two by two, lords' sons and crown men, the two from the Narrow March, and the miller's son and the smith's boy and the girl from Grayrock; and to each of them, as they went past, he said their name. That was all. Their name, and a nod. Sixty-two times, in the dark, in the snow, without once looking at a list.

They had Abloh's tents. Forty of them, packed in their canvas bags on the pack horses, with the frames and the cots and the liners and the little tin stoves; and the class would sleep in them on the road north, in the snow, five nights, warm, with the stoves going and the liners laced, and come into the capital in the second week with their heads up.

Abloh had come out to the base to see them go. He stood by the gate in a great coat, beside Ward's horse, and watched his tents go past on the pack horses' backs, and said nothing; and when the last pack horse had gone, Ward looked down at him from the saddle.

"Where's the rug?" said Ward.

"What rug?"

"The four-pound rug. Red and blue. Squares."

"It is on the shelf in my loft," said Abloh, "where you told me to put it."

Ward looked at him for a long time.

"There's a lump in the sergeant's pack," he said. "The crown man's. The one who's been cold every night since July and said so every morning." He did not take his eyes off Abloh. "A flat lump. About four pounds."

Abloh looked up the road north, into the snow, where the class had gone.

"Then he will have warm feet in the mornings," he said, "and he will think of me." And he turned and walked back toward the town through the snow, with his hands in his coat, and did not look back; and Ward sat on his horse by the gate and watched him go, and then, to nobody, alone, in the dark, laughed.



Imani started her tally that week.

She did it the way she did everything that she meant to keep doing for years: on a fresh card in a fresh drawer, ruled. The works families, and the port families: every child who came into the ward with a cough, and every grown man, and how long it lasted, and where they lived, and which way the wind had been that week from the coke ovens and the coal ground and the steelworks' chimneys. She did not say why. She told Ferra it was for her ward's books. She told Emeric nothing at all; but on the Friday, at supper, she said, to the table, without looking up, that the children in the lane below the coke ovens coughed twice as long as the children up the castle hill, and that she would like to know why, and that she thought she knew.

Ferra wrote it down. Emeric put down his fork, and did not pick it up for a while.



The box was walnut, with brass corners and a brass clasp, and a rose inlaid on its lid in pale wood, and it was the most beautiful thing Emeric had ever had made for anybody; and he was more nervous handing it over than he had been in the dome with the stone in his mouth.

He did it on the Sunday, in the afternoon, in Ferra's room, because it was the warmest room in the keep that week: Bram had been running the new steam pipe up the castle hill all week and had brought the first branch into the keep through Ferra's floor, on the grounds, he said, that her room faced north and was always the coldest; and Tess, hearing this at supper, had said, "That's not why," and been told to eat her supper. The pipes were laid but not yet live. The room was warm anyway, from the fire, and from the afternoon sun coming in low through the west window; and the two of them were sitting on the window seat in it with Ferra's book between them and a pot of tea, doing the quarter's ward figures, when he came in with the box under his arm.

They looked up.

They looked at the box.

And he watched both of their faces do the same thing, at the same time, the thing he had been afraid of: a kind of soft, careful, polite dismay. The box was beautiful. It was walnut and brass and inlay. It was, very plainly, a box for jewellery. And he had already given them a great deal, the two of them, over the years; and neither of them had ever worn a jewel in her life that she had not been made to wear, and neither of them wanted one now, and both of them, he saw, were already preparing to love it anyway, because he had made it.

Ferra was pricing it. He could see her do it. The walnut, the brass, the inlay; Abloh's time, or whoever's; she had the figure to a Copper before he had crossed the room. Imani was already deciding where she would put it, somewhere safe, somewhere she would see it every day and never open.

"It's for both of you," he said. "There's one each. Inside." He put it on the window seat between them, on top of the ward figures, and stepped back. "Open it."

Ferra opened the clasp.

It was lined in grey felt. And in it, side by side, folded, there were two things that were not jewellery.

Ferra lifted one out.

It was linen. Milford linen, the finest the mill had made, soft from Abloh's washing, the colour of cream; and it was a shape that had never existed in the world before, and she held it up by the two narrow bands at its top and turned it in the window's light, and looked at it, with a small line between her brows, as she looked at a ledger in a hand she did not know.

Two shaped cups of linen, cut and darted and stitched, each one lined, joined at the middle by a narrow band. A wider band that went round. Two narrow straps that went over. A row of tiny hooks and eyes at the back, three of them, in brass, so small they must have been made in the forge.

Imani took the other one out of the box, and held it up too, and turned it round.

They both looked at them for some time.

He watched them work it out. He did not say anything. He had promised himself on the stair that he would not say anything, and he did not, though it was the hardest thing he had done all year; he stood by the fire with his hands behind his back and watched them turn the things in the light and look at the cups, and the shape of the cups, and the bands, and then look at each other, and then, slowly, both at once, look down at themselves.

Ferra went red.

It started at her collar and went up, all at once, the whole of her face and her ears and the freckles on her nose, scarlet, until she was the colour of her hair; and she held the thing up in front of her in both hands and did not move and did not say anything at all.

Imani started laughing.

She did not mean to. He saw her not mean to: saw her press her lips together, and hold the thing very steady, and look at it, and then at Ferra's face over the top of Ferra's, and lose it. She laughed until she had to put her free hand flat on the window seat to hold herself up, with her head down, shaking, the linen thing in her other hand held out to one side as if it might go off.

"It's for support," said Emeric, to the fire.

That made it worse. Imani made a sound like a kettle. Ferra put her face in the linen.

"It's called a bra," he said. "On my side. Every woman wore one. Most days. It..." He stopped. "It holds things. So you don't have to. Under your clothes. All day. It's..."

"We can see what it does," said Ferra, from inside the linen.

"Abloh made them," he said. "To my drawing. He didn't know what they were. I couldn't tell him." He looked at the fire. "He went to Maren about it. He said I'd asked him to make two small sails and he couldn't find the wind." He was quiet. "Maren told him to make the sails and not ask."

Imani laughed so hard she slid off the window seat onto the floor, and sat there against it with her back to the wall and her knees up and the bra in her lap and her face wet.

Ferra lifted her face out of hers.

She looked at the thing in her hands. She looked at the cups. She looked at them very carefully, with the expression she used on a figure she suspected of being right. Then she looked up at him across the room, with her face still scarlet, and raised one eyebrow. Just one. Very slowly.

He knew exactly what it meant. Nobody had measured anybody.

"I've a good memory," he said.

"Apparently," said Ferra.

Imani, on the floor, held hers up against her own chest in its scrubs, with her chin tucked to look down at it, and then looked up at him, and her face went very still, and then she put her hand over her mouth and started again.

"Turn around," said Ferra.

"What?"

"Turn around." She had stood up off the window seat. She was still red. She held the bra in both hands in front of her like a bill she was about to present. "We're going to put them on. Turn around."

"Ferra." He looked at her. He looked at Imani, on the floor. "We're well past that."

"Turn around anyway," said Imani, from the floor, getting up.

"I've seen you both..."

"Turn around," they both said; and he looked at the two of them standing by the window in the low gold sun with the linen things in their hands, red and laughing respectively, and did as he was told. He turned round and faced the wall, by the fire, with his hands behind his back, and looked at the plaster.

Behind him, cloth.

A good deal of cloth. Buttons; the small sounds of buttons, a great many of them, because Ferra wore a great many buttons. The soft slither of something coming off over a head. Imani saying something low that he didn't catch and Ferra saying "Don't," sharply, and then laughing, which she almost never did, a short helpless sound. A silence. The tiny click of a brass hook finding a brass eye, and another, and another. Another silence, longer.

"Oh," said Imani, quietly, in a quite different voice.

He did not turn round. It was very nearly the hardest thing he had done all year as well.

"It doesn't pinch," said Ferra, in a voice he had never heard her use, wondering, as if she were reading a figure that came out right first time. "Anywhere. It's..." She stopped. "How does it not pinch?"

"Abloh," said Emeric, to the wall.

"Abloh didn't know what it was."

"Abloh's been fitting sails to masts for thirty years. A thing that holds weight without chafing is a thing that holds weight without chafing." He was talking too fast, to the wall, and knew it. "Can I turn round?"

"No," said Ferra.

And then there were hands. On his back, both his shoulders, one each; and then arms, round him from behind, both of them, one from each side, and two bodies against his back, warm, in nothing much but cream-coloured Milford linen and whatever they had left on below it, which he could not see and could feel was not a great deal; and Imani's mouth by his left ear, and Ferra's chin on his right shoulder.

"You can turn round now," said Imani, very low, in his ear.

He turned round.

He was not, afterward, able to describe what he saw, except that the sun was behind them and low and gold and the room was warm and they were very close; and that the things he had drawn for Abloh on a sheet of paper at midnight in the study had been the right shape, both of them, exactly; and that the two women in front of him knew it, and knew that he knew it, and were standing there in the gold light with their hands on his chest and their heads on one side looking up at him, with their mouths doing something that was not quite smiling, the way Ferra looked at a ledger she had already won, and the way Imani looked at a patient who has just been told to take his shirt off.

Ferra pulled him down by his collar and kissed him, slowly. Imani kissed his neck while she did it. Then they changed over.

He had his hands on their waists, both, on bare skin above the waistbands of whatever they had on below, and he could feel the linen's lower edge against his wrists, and the warmth of them both through his shirt, and the room going round very slightly. Ferra had her fingers in his locs. Imani had begun, without any hurry, to undo his shirt.

"All this work," said Ferra, against his mouth, in a voice like warm honey. "The mill. The linen. Abloh up three nights. Your drawing. The hooks, in the forge." She bit his lip, very lightly. "All that, to make a thing you're only going to take off us anyway."

He laughed. He couldn't help it. He stood there with two half-dressed women against him in the sun and his shirt coming undone, and laughed into Ferra's hair.

"Well, yeah," he said. "But it's for support. So it serves its purpose all day, until I come round." He kissed her ear. "Then it can have a break from its daily job." He turned his head and kissed Imani's temple. "The bra and I are both very busy."

There was a silence.

Then Imani laughed so hard she had to let go of his shirt, and lean her forehead on his chest, and hold on to him to stay up; and Ferra, in his other arm, with her face against his neck, made a sound that started as outrage and ended, helplessly, as the longest laugh he had ever heard out of her, high and breathless and going on and on, until she was crying, and so was Imani, and so, after a while, was he.

They stood there in the low sun, the three of them, in Ferra's warm room, holding each other up, laughing, until the sun went down behind the west wall; and that was all, that afternoon. The joke had finished it. It had been the best joke he had ever told, and they all knew it, and none of them wanted to spend it on anything else.

Ferra wore hers down to supper that evening, under her grey wool, buttoned to the throat. So did Imani, under her scrubs.

Nobody at the table knew. Bram talked all through supper about the steam pipe. Emeric could not, at any point in the meal, look at either of them.

Abloh, the next morning, received a note in Ferra's square hand, delivered by the printer's boy to the sail loft over the boat shed. It said: Six more. Each. In the same. And whatever else he's drawn that you haven't made yet. F. And under it, in a different hand, round and quick: Some in the blue, when there's blue. I.

Abloh read it, and folded it, and went down to Maren's kitchen, and stood in the door of it until she looked up.

"Twelve more small sails," he said. "And they want them in blue."

Maren looked at him for a while.

"Then you'd better find out what blue is," she said, and went back to her pastry.



"I've got it," said Emeric, to Bram, at the forge-room door, on the Thursday, at one in the morning.

Bram looked up from the bench, where he was going over the converter closure's drawings with a glass for the sixth time.

"Got what?"

"The stone. The golem's." He came in and sat down on the stool across the bench. He had come straight up from the gallery under the hill, and his eyes were bright, and his head was aching the way it always ached after the stone now, a thumb behind each eye. "I can read it. Not all of it. Enough. I've got every joint, and the balance, and the stand and the step and the turn and the strike, and the door at the top, where the rider sits." He spread his hands on the bench. "I've got the grammar. I can write in it."

Bram put down his glass.

"Write in it," he said.

"A routine. A small one. In their own hand, in their marks, the way they'd have written it. I could change the step. Make it shorter. Or the strike, make it soft." He looked at Bram. "I want to try. I want to fill its tank, the glass port, with liquid mana, and wake its joints, and put my hand on the stone, and change one thing, and see if it does what I wrote."

Bram looked at him across the bench for a long time.

"Where?" he said.

"Not in the gallery. Not near anybody. The old quarry, on the hill, above the gallery. Nobody goes up there at night." He thought. "Ward with a dead-zone stone, standing off, in case. And you, if you'll come. Because you know every joint of it from the drawings better than I do, and if something's wrong with it mechanically you'll see it first."

"And if something's wrong with it that isn't mechanical?"

"Then I'll take the stone out." Emeric smiled, tiredly. "I've read every line in it, Bram. Every line that does anything. It's a body that knows how to walk, and a door for a rider. There's nothing in it I don't understand."

"Except the ornament," said Bram.

Emeric looked at him.

"You told me. At supper. On the Sunday. About the little bits that don't do anything." Bram picked his glass up again. "The scrollwork."

"They don't do anything."

"Hm," said Bram, and bent over the drawings again. "Next week. When the moon's down." He made a mark. "I'll bring a lamp."



The steam came up the castle hill on the twentieth of November.

It came from the station, where the turbines' exhaust steam had gone up into the sky every hour of every day since the station was built, all that heat poured into the clouds; and now it went, instead, into a pipe. A great iron pipe in a trench, lagged in felt and slag-wool and laid in a culvert of Kaldo brick under the street, that went from the station at the port up the port road and in under the walls and along under New Kaldo's streets, branching as it went: to the school, and the hospital, and the first blocks in ward three, and the new houses on the hill; and up the castle hill, at last, the last branch, the one Bram had laid himself.

It reached the school first, on the eighteenth, and the children came in on the nineteenth to rooms that were warm before anybody had lit a stove, and would not go home. It reached the hospital on the nineteenth, where the pipes had been waiting in every room since September; and Imani went round every ward that evening with her hand flat on every radiator, one after another, as if she were taking their pulses. And it reached the castle on the twentieth, in the afternoon, and Bram opened the valve at the hill's foot himself and stood beside it with his hand on the pipe for an hour, and then walked up the hill, slowly, with his hand on every flange he passed.

The first floor it reached in the keep was Ferra's.

Not the walls, as in the hospital. The floor. Bram had laid it that way on purpose and not said so: a coil of copper pipe, thin, in a bed of sand under the boards of her room, going round and back and round and back from the window to the door, so that the steam's heat would come up through the oak from underneath, evenly, all over, the way the warmth comes up out of a hearthstone in the morning. He had done it in a week, alone, on his knees, with the boards up; and when Ferra had come in on the fourth day and found her father on his knees in her room with her floor in a pile against the wall, he had said it was a trial for the castle's other rooms, and her room was the coldest.

"It isn't the coldest," Ferra had said.

"It's the coldest," Bram had said, and gone on laying pipe.

And on the twentieth, at nine at night, Emeric came up to Ferra's room because she had asked him to, at supper, without looking at him, and Imani had looked at her plate; and opened the door; and stopped.

The floor was warm.

He felt it through his boots in the doorway. The whole of it, from wall to wall, warm, like the deck of the K Discovery's saloon the night he had put a fire stone under her water tank, two years ago, a mile up over the sea. And on it, in front of the fire, which was not lit, because it was not needed, there were quilts. All of them. Every quilt from Ferra's bed, and Imani's, which she must have carried up from her own house through the snow, and two from the linen press that he recognized from the castle's guest rooms; laid out on the warm floor, overlapping, thick, in the light of one lamp turned low.

And the two of them, sitting on the quilts, in their nightgowns, with their backs against Ferra's bed and their bare feet on the warm floor, looking up at him.

"Close the door," said Ferra. "You're letting the heat out."

He closed the door.

"Boots," said Imani.

He sat down on the edge of the quilts to take them off, and Ferra leaned forward and stopped his hands, and did them herself: unlaced them, both, with her head bent, the way he had knelt on the quilts in the K Discovery's saloon and unlaced hers when the knots had pulled tight in the cold; and pulled them off, and put them by the door, side by side; and then put her hand flat on the floor beside him.

"Feel it," she said.

He put his hand down beside hers. The oak was warm under his palm, the warmth of a living thing, coming up through the wood from underneath, steady, from three miles away, from the turbines turning in the station on the river, from the coal and the steam and the pipe her father had laid on his knees.

"He did this for you," said Emeric.

"He did this for me," said Ferra. "He says it's a trial for the castle." She looked at the floor under their two hands. "He'll never say it. He'll be dead before he says it."

"Duchess Ferra's floor," said Imani, from the bed's foot, softly.

Ferra looked at her.

"You stopped it. On the leads." Imani had her knees drawn up, her arms round them, her chin on her knees, and she was looking at Emeric. "You said not yet, Your Grace, and he dropped it. He hasn't said it since. Three weeks." She tilted her head. "I want it back. I like it. It's ours." She looked at Ferra. "It's a joke he makes because he can't make the real one yet. I'd rather have the joke than nothing."

Ferra was quiet a moment.

"So would I," she said.

They both looked at him.

"Duchess Ferra," he said. "Duchess Imani."

"Better," said Imani; and reached up, and turned the lamp down until it was nearly out.



It was not like the afternoon of the box.

There was no joke in it. There was no hurry in it either. There was the warm floor under the quilts, and the warm dark, and the low lamp, and the snow against the window, and the whole long winter night in front of them with nothing in it to call any of them out of the room: no ship, no wire, no fire, nothing that needed any of the three of them until the morning, or past the morning, as it turned out. And the three of them knew it; and it made them slow.

He had not known, until that night, how much of every night before it had been spent listening. For a bell, a step on the stair, a boy at the door with a message, a dead zone's wind, Ward. Every night of his life in Kaldo, with half his sense open on the town. And that night, on the warm floor, with Ferra's mouth on his and Imani's hands slow on his back, he closed it. He felt himself do it. He drew his sense in off the town, off the walls and the river and the station and the gallery under the hill, all of it, inward, until it reached no further than the edges of the quilts; and there was nothing in it but the two of them. Two notes, very close, very bright, and his own between them.

Imani felt him do it. She lifted her head.

"Oh," she said. "You've gone quiet."

"There's nobody here," he said. "But you."

She looked at him in the last of the lamplight for a long moment. Then she put her hand flat on his chest, as she always did; and this time she did not count.

The nightgowns went first, over their heads, one and then the other, and the linen under them, the cream Milford linen, Abloh's two small sails, with three brass hooks each that he undid himself, slowly, one by one, with his fingers clumsy, while Ferra laughed under her breath at him in the dark and did not help. Then his own shirt, which Imani had started on in the afternoon and finished now. Then everything. And the floor's warmth came up through the quilts into all three of them, steady, so that nobody shivered, nobody reached for a blanket; skin on warm cotton, and skin on skin, and the snow ticking on the glass.

He was careful with them. He was always careful with them. He had their weights in his hands and he knew them, and he moved them as he wanted them, easily, by plain strength, a hand under a hip, an arm under a back, turning Ferra on the quilts to face him and drawing Imani in behind her, as if neither of them weighed anything at all; and they let him, and then they did not let him, and took him instead, because they were not careful with him and never had been. Ferra pushed him down onto his back on the quilts with both hands flat on his chest and held him there, and looked at him in the dark, and he let her. Imani's mouth was on his throat, then lower, then lower still, unhurried, a doctor who knew every inch of him and had decided to prove it.

He gave up, somewhere in the first hour, trying to think about anything.

When he was over Ferra, later, with his weight on his forearms and the quilts rucked under her and her red hair spread across them, his locs came down round her face, all of them, a curtain, so that inside them there was nothing but her face and his and the dark and her breath; and she put her hands up into them, both hands, and held on, and looked at him from inside them with her grey-green eyes wide open and not one figure left in them, and said his name. Not a duke's name. His. Once, and then again, and then not words at all.

Imani, beside them, with her cheek on Ferra's shoulder and her hand on his back, between his shoulder blades, flat, felt his heart through it, racing; and did not count it. He felt her not counting. He felt her decide not to, deliberately, with her hand there, and keep not counting it, the whole long time, and it undid him more than anything else she could have done.

And later, with the lamp out entirely and only the snow-light grey in the window, it was Imani; Imani over him, slow, with her long hair loose for once, all of it, every curl, falling round both of them like a dark warm tent, and her hands on his chest, and her strong brows drawn together in the faint grey light in a look of such intent concentration that he would have laughed if he had had any breath to laugh with; and Ferra beside them on the quilts, propped on her elbow, watching Imani's face with an expression he had never seen on Ferra's face before, soft, unguarded, as if she were reading something she had not known was in the book.

"Look at her," said Ferra, very low, in his ear.

He looked. He did not stop looking for a long time.

It went on. It went on a long time, and then it stopped, and they lay on the warm floor in a heap with the quilts round them and the snow ticking; and then, after a while, without anybody deciding it, it went on again, slower, different, because the night was long and the floor was warm and nobody had to be anywhere, and it did not have to be one thing. It was every kind of thing, that night. Some of it was fierce. Some of it was so gentle it was hardly anything at all, a hand on a hip in the dark for the length of a long breath. Some of it was funny: Imani's elbow, at one point, in his eye, and her apologising so seriously that Ferra had to put her face in a pillow. Some of it, near the morning, was only the three of them lying very still in the grey light, tangled, with Ferra's head on his shoulder and Imani's on his chest and his hands in both their hair, not asleep, not talking, listening to the snow and to each other breathe, and to the faint far tick of the pipes under the floor as the steam came up the castle hill from the river and kept them warm.

They slept, at last, all three, sometime after the window went from grey to white.



At eleven there was a knock on the door.

Nobody moved.

The knock came again. Heavy. A smith's knock.

"Ferra." It was Bram's voice, through the oak, perfectly ordinary. "I want to look at the floor. The coil. Check it took the night."

Ferra opened one eye. She was lying with her head on Emeric's shoulder and Imani's arm across both of them and every quilt in the keep on top of the three of them, and her hair was everywhere, and she did not lift her head.

"It's warm, Father," she called.

A pause, through the door.

"All of it? Window end and all?"

"All of it." She shut her eye again. "It's very warm. It's the warmest floor in the kingdom. It took the night."

A longer pause.

"Hm," said Bram, through the door.

They heard him stand there a moment longer. Then they heard him go away down the stair, slowly, one heavy step at a time; and about halfway down, faintly, through two doors and the turn of the stair, they heard him begin to whistle.

Imani put her face in Emeric's chest and laughed until she shook.

Ferra did not open her eyes. She lay with her head on his shoulder and her face scarlet to the ears, and said, to nobody, very quietly, "He knows."

"He's known for years," said Emeric.

"He didn't have to whistle," said Ferra, and pulled the quilt over her head.

They did not get up until noon.
