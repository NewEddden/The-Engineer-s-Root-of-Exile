Chapter 110 - Off the Water

Back in the long room, Ferra settled it at the table in one sentence, before anybody else had finished arguing.

"I sign for her," she said. "So I ride in her."

There had been a good deal of arguing before that. Imani had wanted nobody aboard but Ward, since Ward was the only one who could work the levers, and had been told that a ship with only a man at her levers had nobody to read her stones, and had said that was the point. Armand had not argued, because Armand was not going, and had said so first, before Imani could. Saban had wanted to go, and had then raised the two keys, as he had raised them before the river last spring: the whole of the relay key in two heads, and both heads about to be in one hull. Ferra had entered it in her book as a risk accepted, with the date, the same as the river, and Armand would hold Kaldo with the sealed letter, the same as the river. But a risk accepted with two of the six aboard was not going to be accepted with three, and Saban had seen the sense of that and sulked for a day. Bram had wanted to be on the steamboat under her, at his own engine, and nobody had argued with Bram.

So it was four. Emeric, to read her. Ward, at the levers. Fletcher, because she was his, and because there would be a moment when she came down onto the water and somebody would have to know by the feel of her deck whether she had come down right. And Ferra, who had written the ninth clause's form herself and never used it, and who had written her conditions in a margin in the summer, and sent the sea trial back with the margin empty and three words on a slip, and who now wrote nothing anywhere at all and simply put her name on the list.

"You don't have to," he said, after, on the gallery.

"I know." She did not stop walking. "I'm not going because I have to. I'm going because if it drops, I want to have been in it. I'll not sign for a thing and stand on a beach and watch it."

He did not have an answer to that, so he did not make one.

The where had been settled before the who, in Fletcher's cottage on the night after the first trial, and nobody at the table had tried to unsettle it. Out past the bar, and on past the crab pots and the lane where the salt boats ran, until the salt-house chimney went down into the sea astern. When you can't see them, Fletcher had said off the bar, and rung down to stop her, and it had gone into the plan as a line of its own under Ferra's initials, in his square hand. Nothing that went up would go up anywhere a man on the shore could turn his head and see it.

The steamboat had gone into the plan as well, with a condition of Fletcher's beside it.

"She's a river boat," he had written. "She'll go a dozen miles out on a sea like a pond and come home again, and I'll not have her a mile past that, nor out at all on any day I'd not take a skiff."

Vantic was in Kaldo for the autumn quarter, as she was every quarter, and he asked her.

He did it on the evening before they went down the river, at the foot of the keep stair, where she was going out with her book under her arm after the day's service. He had not asked her in the thaw. He had thought about it for most of a week then and not asked her, and had not been proud of the reasons, and he had kept thinking about the reasons since.

"We're going down to the lower water in the morning," he said. "There's a thing out there I'd like you to see. And there's a thing I'd like to ask you first, because it isn't fair to ask it after."

Vantic looked at him for a moment with her book held flat against her.

"Your Highness has not asked me for anything before," she said.

"No."

"Then I had better hear it."

"What you see out there," he said, "I'd ask you to keep out of your report. Not what she cost. That's in the books, and you've read the books, and your House has had her at cost since her keel went down. What she does."

Vantic did not answer at once. She looked past him up the keep stair, at nothing in particular, and he had the sense of a column being run down and added before anybody was allowed to see the total.

"My House lent against this barony's revenues," she said, "and against its works, and the stock in its yards, and it keeps me here so that it may know those are what the contract says they are. It knows your ship's hull to the Rand. It knows her engine and her fitting-out, because I sent them." She moved the book from one arm to the other. "It has never asked me what your steamboat does on the river. It will not ask me what your ship does at sea. What a thing does out of sight of land is not a figure, Your Highness, and my House does not lend against anything that is not a figure."

"So it stays out of the report."

"It does not pertain to my House," said Vantic. "I do not send my House answers to questions it has not asked." She stepped past him onto the stair. "I shall come. I shall sit where I am put."

He watched her go up. It was the answer he had asked for, and it had come with its reasons attached, in order, the way a sound figure comes with its working under it. He believed every one of them. He did not think they were all of it.

She came down in the steamboat in the morning with the rest of them, and sat in the stern beside Imani, and the two of them said nothing to each other for the whole of the river, which Emeric thought was probably the most comfortable silence either had had in a year. Armand stayed on the island, with the sealed letter in his coat and the relay under his hand, and stood on the quay to watch them go.

She was in the water when they came round the last bend.

Fletcher had put her back in on the spring tide three days before, off the stocks and down the greased ways into the lagoon, as he had said he would, and she lay at her mooring behind the bar, black and long and very low in the water, with steam up already and a thin line of smoke going straight up from her stack into a still grey morning. The screw was down on its arm in the water behind her. She had no mast and no sail, and the lower water had stopped remarking on it. The steamboat came alongside her and looked, beside her, like a child beside its father.

Bram went aboard the steamboat's engine and stayed there. Imani went with him, with her canvas roll, and Vantic after her, to the place in the stern she had had on the river. Fletcher's six were on the beach below the cottages in a row with their arms folded, as they had been the first time, and behind them the lower water went on with its morning. A steamer getting steam up was not a thing anybody stopped work for any more. Nobody on the beach had been told that anything would happen, because nothing was going to happen that anybody on the beach would see.

Emeric went aboard her with Ward and Ferra, and Fletcher took the helm.

Bram's lad was already below at her fire, as he had been off the bar. Nobody at the table had counted him when they counted four. Nobody ever had, which was most of why he was the one Bram sent.

They steamed her out through the gap in the bar and on, with the steamboat labouring along in her wake and pouring smoke. Past the last of the crab pots. Across the lane where the salt boats went east and west along the shore, with nobody in it. On again, until the cottages had gone down, and the long pale line of the beach, and the dunes, and the salt-house chimney stood alone out of the water like a stick in a pond, and went. It was as calm as Fletcher had said it would have to be. The light air off the land had died a mile out and left the sea lying under them like oil, grey under a grey sky, with nothing on it anywhere.

Fletcher rang down, and she lost her way and lay still. Ward went round her rail one way and Fletcher the other, as they had the first time, and they met at the bow.

"Nothing," said Ward.

Fletcher looked astern at the rim of the sea where the land had been, and at the steamboat lying a cable off with Bram's smoke going up from her as straight as a plumb line, and nodded.

"The screw," Emeric said.

Off the bar, the first time, she had gone up running, with the screw driving her and the sea half gone from under her before it came up and raced, the way Fletcher had said a swan goes off a pond. That had proved the stones. It had not proved the thing he needed next. The lake at Kaldo had no run in it he would trust her to, and no harbour he had ever seen had room to run a ship at the sea and let go of her. A ship that could only leave the water at a gallop could only leave it where there was a gallop's worth of water. Today she would stop, dead, and go up from nothing.

Bram's lad, below, swung the arm, and the screw came up out of the water dripping and locked against the transom, clear.

She lay dead in the water, rocking very slightly.

Ward put his left hand on the lift lever.

The cells in her hold began to thin. Emeric had them in the medium, a hundred and eighty small steady places in their rails in the dark, and the sixteen stations low in her frames taking the feed, and the reserve under her deck beams, cold, waiting, and the reading stone over its dish watching for her to lean. He watched her come up.

She came up out of the water the way a swimmer's back comes up out of a still pool. The sea ran down her sides. It ran off her plating in sheets and then in streams and then in threads, and then it was dripping off her keel, and then there was a gap between her keel and the sea with light in it, and the sea closed over the place where she had been and was flat again, and she was hanging over it.

Ferra's hand closed on the rail beside his.

She did not make a sound. She held the rail as she held her book, square and hard, and looked down over it at the water going away underneath them, a foot, a fathom, the height of a man, two, and her face did nothing at all, and her hand did not let go of the rail, and after a while he felt her other hand come to rest on the rail beside it, near his, not touching.

"She's lying," said Fletcher, from the helm.

He said it the way a man says the wind has gone round. His hands were on the wheel, which did nothing, and he did not take them off it. He was feeling her through the deck, as he had on the stocks, and she was lying on nothing, level, a little down by the stern where the engine was, and the deciding stone was catching it.

At the height of a mast Ward held her.

"Push," he said, and moved the right lever off its stop.

The housings at her quarters breathed, and she began to go forward.

She went slowly. She went at about the pace of a man walking fast, and then of a horse trotting, and no faster, because Emeric had said no faster and Ward had heard him. Below them the steamboat got under way and came after her, smoke pouring, Bram at the engine and Imani in the bow with her canvas roll and Vantic in the stern, and fell in under her, twenty yards below and a little behind, and kept station there. The whole way, if anything went, there would be a boat in the water under them and a sea under the boat.

Ward leaned her, the way he had learned to lean himself, and she came round, and ran out over the empty sea.

There was nobody to see her.

He had thought about this for a long time. He had thought about the lake in the thaw and the man in the skiff with his lines in his hands, and the four versions Saban had laid out on the long table by the end of that week, and the barge, which had been the best of them because it was the one the capital already knew how to think about, and which had gone on down the river to the sea on its own, the way a good story does. He had thought about Fletcher's six men walking her deck at her mooring, rail to rail, reading her lean in inches over stones that were asleep. He had thought about the letter with the chancery's mark on it, and the answer that had gone back down the road to Milford under Kaldo's seal, every word of it true.

And there was this. A still sea going away on every side to a rim with nothing on it. A gull that had followed the steamboat's wake out from the bar, a long way from anywhere, and had gone round them once, low, and was sitting on the water now looking up at them with one eye as though it had been cheated. That was the whole of the audience. He had built her for it.

"One crabber," said Fletcher, at the wheel, without looking round. "One man at his lines, and that would be in every harbour on this coast by the new moon."

"How far?"

Fletcher thought about it the way he thought about a price.

"The coasting boats go along as far as Emberfell," he said. "Where the mountain smokes. They take salt down and bring sulphur and pumice back, when they can get it. Those men would have it in the fish market there inside the month." He turned the wheel a spoke, which did nothing, out of habit. "News goes faster by boat than by road on this coast. It always has. So there'll be no crabbers."

Two miles out, by Fletcher's eye, Ward brought her round.

He did it wide and slow, leaning her, and she came round in the air above the sea with the steamboat below turning with her, tight, trailing smoke, and then they were running back the way they had come, over the gull, which had not moved.

Emeric was watching the cells.

They had thinned evenly the whole way, the way a lake goes down in a dry month, and the splinters in the rack in her hold, which Ferra had looked at through the hatch twice without being asked, had gone from bright to something less than bright. It was what the arithmetic had said. Ferra had halved the arithmetic, and it was very nearly what Ferra had said, too. There was enough to come down on, and some over. There was not enough for anything else.

"Down," he said. "On the water."

Ward brought her down.

He had taken the push off her a quarter of a mile back and let the air have her way, so that she came to the sea with none. Off the bar she had met it running, and skipped on it like a flat stone, and sat down into it hard enough that Fletcher had her up the ways the next morning.

He brought her down the way he had brought himself down in the foundry, a notch at a time, and the sea came up toward them grey and flat, and at the height of a man Fletcher said, "Now," quite quietly, and Ward held her there. Fletcher looked over the rail at the water. He looked at it for some time. Then he nodded, and Ward took her down the last of it, and her keel touched the sea.

It was the gentlest thing Emeric had ever felt a ship do. She did not come down onto the water. She settled into it, the way she had risen out of it, the sea coming up her sides as it had run down them, until she was lying in it and the water had forgotten she had ever been anywhere else.

The first time, Fletcher had spent the best part of a fortnight under her on his back with a lamp, going along her plates one at a time, and had found nothing, and had not believed it.

Fletcher put his hand flat on the wheel for a moment and closed his eyes.

"Right," he said. "She's right."

The steamboat came alongside while Bram's lad was swinging the arm down, with Bram's head up out of her engine hatch and Imani in her bow with the canvas roll unopened across her knees. Vantic sat in the stern, where she had sat all morning.

Ferra saw her first. She had come to the rail as the boat came in and had looked for her, Emeric realised, before she had looked for anyone else, and she was looking at her now across a few yards of flat water. Vantic had her book on her knee, shut. She had her pen in her other hand, uncapped, as though she had taken it out to write with, and had not.

As they watched, she capped it, and put it away, and sat with her hands folded over the book.

Ferra did not say anything. She looked at Vantic for as long as it took the steamboat to make fast, and then she turned away from the rail, and he saw her face as she turned, and it was the face she wore over a column that had come out exactly as she had expected and not at all as she would have liked.

"She told you it wouldn't go in her report," Ferra said, very quietly.

"Yes."

"She didn't tell you it wouldn't go in her book. Everything goes in her book. The price of a candle goes in her book." Ferra went to the ladder. "She took it out to write, and she didn't. That's the first time. In six years, that's the first time I've watched her decide not to."

Fletcher rang for steam, and the engine came up under their feet, the old familiar knock of it, and she steamed home across the empty sea toward a land that came back up out of it a piece at a time, the salt-house chimney first, as if she had never done anything else in her life.

The six were on the beach when she came in through the gap, in their row, as if they had not moved all day. Behind them the lower water was going on with its afternoon. Nobody else looked up. A steamer had gone out and come back.

The oldest of the six came down to the edge of the water when they came ashore in her boat. He did not look at the ship, and he did not look at Emeric. He looked at Fletcher, with no expression at all, the way he had looked down from her rail on the day Emeric came.

"Two hours," he said. "Your smoke. Out there."

"She went light," said Fletcher.

The man thought about that for a while, in the way he had that did not show on him anywhere.

"Light again," he said. Then he went up the beach to the cottages and did not look back.

Emeric watched Fletcher watch him go, and understood that the thing Fletcher had said he was only holding had got heavier since the night in the cottage, and would get heavier again every time she went out past the chimney, and that Fletcher knew it, and would carry it anyway, and would send the bill.

They went back up the river that afternoon, and the story was ahead of them by the time they reached the island. It had come up on a salt boat, and it was the only story the lower water had, which was that Fletcher's iron ship had gone out to sea again with the prince aboard, and come back again, with no sail and no wind and nothing wrong with her.

"It's the dullest thing anybody has said about this barony in a year," Saban said at the table that night, with deep satisfaction. "I've been down to the quay twice. A coaster that doesn't wait for the wind. The prince's stones in her somewhere, because the yard knows that much and the yard drinks. Ugly. Very large." He turned his cup round on the table. "Nobody's arguing about it. Nobody argues about a steamer."

"The man on the beach," said Ward.

They looked at him.

"The old one. With the burn on his hand." Ward said it in the tone he used for the number of men on a road. "He's counted her smoke twice. He'll count it the third time."

"He'll wait for Fletcher to tell him," Emeric said. "Fletcher says he always has."

"And when Fletcher doesn't," said Ward, and did not finish it, because he did not need to. Ferra wrote one line in her book and did not read it out.

It was Imani, of all of them, who brought it round to the next thing, and she did it without looking up from the sheet in front of her, which was Ferra's log of the flight.

"You have a room," she said, "above this town. In the other place. We were all in it. You told us it was wrong, because you'd never seen the town from above, and you'd built it out of maps and streets you'd walked, and the parts you hadn't walked were vague." She put the sheet down. "Now you have a thing that goes above the town."

"It went to the height of a mast."

"It went where you told it to."

Saban had begun to smile. He had been wrong about a great deal in that room, confidently and in detail, on the first day, and he had been wrong about most of it ever since, because nobody could prove otherwise.

"I should very much like," he said, "to be told I'm wrong from a great height. It'd make a change from being told it from across a table."

Ferra did not look up from her book.

"High," she said. "Over the island, over the lake, up. High enough to see the whole of it." She wrote something. "That's what the next one's for."

"Over the island," said Imani, "in front of four thousand people."

"At night," Emeric said.

Saban looked at him over his cup. "You'll see a great deal of dark."

"There's a moon."

Ferra wrote that down as well.

Nobody at the table argued, and Emeric, looking down it, understood that the argument he would have to have now was not whether, but how high, and with whom, and that he would have it with Imani, and would lose most of it, and that this was right.
