Chapter 110 - Near Miss

Imani said it at the table, as she had said she would, on the first Monday that the Standing Order sat after the room, in front of all of them. She said she did not agree to the file, and gave her reasons, three of them, in order, and Ferra wrote them down in the book in her small upright hand under the date, word for word, and read them back, and Imani said yes, that was what she had said, and the table went on to the price of copper rivets.

That should have been the end of it. Emeric had thought it would be.

It was not, because on the Wednesday Ferra came up to his room with a list.

It was not the file. The file lived in her room, in the left-hand box, in her cipher, and he had not seen a page of it since the room and did not expect to. This was a list of what the file would need, and where she meant to get it from, and she put it on his table and stood beside it with her hands folded while he read it, because that was how she had brought him things for nearly four years and being Chancellor had not changed it.

The census. The school roll, which was Sela's. The pay sheets. The burial book, which was Doran's. The salt boat's passengers, from the boatman, who could be paid. The garrison's own roll, from Ward.

And at the bottom, the ward's register.

"The ward keeps a book," Ferra said. "Everyone who comes through the door. Name, house, what was wrong with them, what she did, whether they lived. Every one since the plague. It's the best book in Kaldo." She did not say it with any particular feeling. "Better than mine. Mine only has who lives where. Hers has who's ill, and who's been ill, and who drinks, and who comes in every winter with a bruise on the same side of the face."

He looked at the line.

"She won't give it to you," he said.

"No," said Ferra. "She won't give it to me." She waited. "She might give it to you."



He went down to the ward on the Friday, late, after the lamps on the wall had come on, because she would not talk about anything that mattered while there was a patient awake who could hear her, and in the day there always was.

The night was clear and very cold. The ice on the canal ring had gone grey and soft at noon and frozen again hard at dusk, and his boots rang on it at the bridges. At the top of the lower ward the ward's windows showed yellow at the end of the lane, the long low building that had been a tithe barn and was now the only place in Kaldo where anyone with a cut or a cough or a child coming went to have it seen to. There was a lamp stone over the door, half charged, giving the doorway a thin white light, and in it, on the step, a cat that belonged to nobody and lived on the ward's scraps, which looked at him and did not move.

He went round to the side door, because he always did.

Imani was at the desk under the lamp, writing. She did not look up when he came in. She finished the line, and blotted it, and then looked up, at his hands first.

"It's late," she said.

"I know."

"Then it's the book." She put the pen down. "Ferra's been at the door twice this week and gone away again without knocking. She thinks I didn't see her." She leaned back in the chair. "Sit down. Not there. That's wet."

He sat on the stool by the desk, the one he had sat on in December with his wrists in her hands, and found that he had noticed it was the same stool.

Down the long room behind her, in the half dark, three beds were full. The cooper, propped up on two pillows, breathing in the slow wet way of a man who would be in his own bed in a week if he did as he was told, and asleep. A boy from the dock quarter with his arm bound to his chest. And at the far end, near the stove, an old woman Emeric did not know, lying very still under two blankets with one of Imani's women sitting beside her on a stool, knitting by the light of the stove door.

"Will she live?" he said, before he had meant to.

Imani did not look round.

"No," she said. "Not this week. She's ninety. She came in to die where it's warm. Her grandson's on the salt boat and her house is a lean-to and she doesn't want him to come home and find her in it." She was quiet. "That's in the book too."

"That's why I'm here."

"I know why you're here."

He told her anyway. He told it the way he had told it to himself on the walk down: that the file was not for hurting anyone, it was for knowing where the doors were before Garrin found them, that a man who could be leaned on was a man who could be leaned on through his sick child or his wife's bad chest or his father's drinking as easily as through his son's walk to school, and that the one book in Kaldo that knew where all of those were was hers. That it would be in Ferra's cipher, in Ferra's box, read by nobody but the two of them, burned when he died. That he would not ask for anything but what bore on who could be leaned on. That he would not ask for anyone's illness for its own sake.

She let him say all of it. She sat with her hands folded on the desk and listened, the way she listened to a man describe a pain, all the way to the end, without nodding.

"No," she said.

"Imani."

"No." She did not raise her voice; the cooper was asleep. "You want to know what's in my book. I'll tell you what's in it. There's a woman in the lower ward who comes in every winter with her ribs cracked on the left side, and every winter she tells me she fell on the ice, and every winter I write fell on the ice, and I bind her ribs, and she goes home. Because if I wrote what happened she'd never come back. She'd lie in that house with her ribs broken and her lung filling up rather than come to a door where somebody would write it down." She leaned forward. "There's a man who drinks. There's a girl who came to me last spring in a way she couldn't tell her mother. There are two men in the garrison with the pox that's got nothing to do with the plague. And every one of them came in because they knew that what they said in here stayed in here, and the only person who'd ever read it was me."

"Ferra would never-"

"I'm not talking about Ferra." Imani's eyes were very steady. "I'm talking about the door. You'll have Ferra copy my book into her cipher. Line by line. Every woman who fell on the ice. And in the copying, the girl's name goes past her eyes, and the pox, and the drinker. And she'll never say a word, I know she won't, she's the most closed mouth in this barony. But she'll know. And one day the woman with the ribs will come up to the keep for her husband's pay, and Ferra will be at the window, and Ferra will look at her." She sat back. "That's all. She'll look at her a little longer than she looks at anybody else in the line. And the woman will see it. And she'll know. And next winter she won't come."

He did not say anything.

"It isn't the reading," Imani said. "It's that it's been read. That's what a door is. You told the table that. You said you weren't going to have another man made into a door." She put her hand flat on the register on the desk, the big cracked book with its spine mended in two places. "This is a door. It's my door. It opens one way. People come in, and they tell me what's wrong, and it goes no further than this desk, and that's why they come in, and that's why the cooper's going to live and the plague didn't take the lower ward a second time. You put it in a box in the keep, with a key, and a second key, and it stops being a door that opens one way."

The old woman at the end of the room said something in her sleep. The woman knitting by the stove leaned over her and said something back, and she was quiet.

Emeric looked at the register under Imani's hand.

He had thought he had come down the hill to persuade her. He saw now that he had come down the hill to find out whether he could, and that he had known on the walk that he could not, and had come anyway because it was the last place in Kaldo where somebody would tell him he was wrong to his face and then show him exactly how.

"Garrin didn't need your book," he said at last. "He watched a boy walk to school."

"Then watch the boys walk to school," said Imani. "Have Saban stand across the lane. Have Ward put a man on every school door. Have Ferra write down every child in Sela's room and who brings them and who fetches them, and where their fathers work, and how far that is from the board." She took her hand off the register. "But you don't need to know who's got the pox to stop a man taking a boy. You want to, because you want to know everything, because the last time you didn't know something you ended up at the bottom of a boat. That's not the same as needing to."

It went in a long way, further than he liked.

"You're right," he said.

Imani looked at him.

"You're right," he said again, because she was still looking at him as if she had not heard it. "The ward's book stays at the ward. Nothing from it goes to the keep. Not a name. If Ferra needs to know that a man in the garrison is ill enough that it matters, you'll tell her he's ill, and not with what." He thought. "And I'll tell the table. On Monday. That the Secretary of Health refused the Chancellor and the holder both, and won."

"You don't have to tell the table."

"I'm going to. It's the kind of thing Ferra writes down." He looked at her. "You said you'd say it at the table, and you did. I'm saying this at the table."

Imani looked at him under the lamp for long enough that the cooper turned over in his sleep.

"Well," she said. "That was quick."

"You were right quickly."



She made tea, because it was the ward and there was always water on the stove, and they drank it at the desk. She put her feet up on the second stool. He did not remark on it. She had not slept, she said, since Tuesday, because the cooper had been bad on Wednesday night and the old woman had come in on Thursday and on Friday morning the boy had come in with his arm, and every time she lay down somebody knocked.

"You could have one of your women sit up," he said.

"I do. That one." She nodded down the room at the woman knitting by the stove. "She knocks."

The lamp was low. She turned it up, and it was still low, because the stone in it was nearly spent, and she looked at it with dislike and did not get up to change it.

"Linnet was supposed to fill it Wednesday," she said.

"Give it here."

She looked at him. Then she reached over and took the stone out of the lamp, a plain lamp stone the size of a hen's egg, dull, with the faintest grey light in its middle, and held it out across the desk.

He took it. Their fingers did not touch. He closed his hand on it and let a little of himself go into it, not much, the easy draw that a lamp stone takes, like water finding its level, and felt it fill, and felt it stop when it was full, and opened his hand, and it was white.

He held it out to her.

She did not take it. She looked at it, white in his palm, lighting both their faces from underneath, and then at him.

"You do that," she said, "the way I bind a wrist."

"It's only a lamp stone."

"I know what it is. I've watched Linnet do it. She shuts her eyes and frowns and it takes her as long as it takes me to say a prayer." She still did not take it. "You didn't even look at it."

He put it on the desk between them. It lay there, very bright, and threw their shadows up the wall behind her over the shelf of jars.

She got up to put it back in the lamp. He got up at the same time, to do the same thing, because it was his stone now in a way and he did not like to make her do it after a week without sleep, and the desk was narrow and the lamp was on the corner of it, and they reached for it at the same moment and both stopped.

She was very close.

He had not meant it. He did not think she had. They were standing on the same side of the corner of a desk in a ward at night with a lamp stone shining up between them, and her hair had come loose at one side, out of the cloth she tied it up in, a long dark curl of it fallen against her neck, and she had not noticed, and he noticed it. He noticed the smell of the spirit she washed her hands in, and under it something else that was only her, and the tiredness in her face, the skin under her eyes gone dark with it, and her mouth.

She did not move away.

Neither did he. He could not have said afterward who had moved closer, or whether either of them had, or whether it was only that neither had moved away when the time came to and so the distance had closed on its own, the way water closes. He saw her look at his mouth. He saw her see him see it.

And then, with her face a hand's breadth from his, so close he could feel the warmth of her breath, she stopped.

She did not step back. But something in her stopped, and he felt it stop, the way he had felt a man's breath stop in the dark of a hold, and he stopped too.

"What are you?" Imani said.

She said it quietly. It was not the voice she had used on the bench in the laundry yard, in the spring before the war, grey and sick from the ether, listing the dog that was always sick and the knot and the cloth he had held like a man who had held it a hundred times. It was not angry. It was very nearly gentle. She asked it the way she asked a patient where it hurt, already knowing roughly where, wanting to hear him say it.

"I said I'd ask again," she said. "I'm asking."

He did not answer.

He wanted to. He wanted to more than he had wanted anything since the hold, and he stood with her face a hand's breadth from his and felt all of it come up into his mouth at once, Earth and the train and the trial and his mother's stone and the coin lifting off a table and a child flying over a river on his own weight made light. And he looked at her, and he thought of the ward's register, and the woman with the ribs, and a door that opens one way.

And he could not. Not because he did not trust her. He trusted her more than almost anyone in Kaldo. Because if he said it, it would be said, and she would know, and every time she looked at him afterward across the table or the ward or the desk she would be looking at a man she knew, and he did not know who that man would be to her, and he was afraid of it.

He said nothing. He stood there and said nothing for so long that the nothing became the answer.

He watched her hear it.

She did not look angry, or hurt, or anything he could name. She looked at him with the whole of the question in her face, and then something came into her eyes. Not the dog, or the cloth, or the knot.

A bed. A coat over her. A wall she had fallen asleep against with her head on her knees, and how far it was from the wall to the bed, and him at a lamplit table, Ferra beside him, saying quite easily, You walked.

She stepped back.

It was one step. She took it carefully, the way she stepped back from a bed when she had done all she could, and she reached past him and picked up the lamp stone off the desk and put it in the lamp, and the room went back to being a room, with the light in the right place.

"Goodnight, Emeric," she said.

She did not say it unkindly. She said it the way she had said it on her own doorstep in the first summer, the first time she ever dropped his title, with her hand flat on the frame. Go home.

He went.

At the side door he looked back. She had sat down at the desk again with the register open in front of her and the pen in her hand. She was not writing. She was looking at the page, and the lamp stone he had filled was shining very white on her face and her loose hair and her hands, and she did not look up, and he went out into the cold.



The twin year closed on the Monday after.

It closed without anyone in Kaldo noticing but four people, at Ferra's trestle in the back room of the exchange, which she still used on pay days and for anything that had to be done where the window was, because the window was where the post came in.

The last pair had gone out on the Friday the week before: the crown's copy of the barony's weekly report into the bag at the post house on the north road, with the gate clerk's slip to say so; and the twin, the same words, the same fold and the same nicked seal, down the river to the House's coast house in Fletcher's punt, which Fletcher had sailed himself because it was the last, he said, and he liked to finish a thing. The crown's copy would arrive nowhere at all, as none of them had arrived anywhere, and Kaldo would know that by the silence, which was the only kind of answer the crown had ever sent it.

Ferra had the index open on the trestle. The long narrow book, the twin year's book, in her hand from the first line to the last. She ran her finger down the left-hand column, the crown's copies, and then down the right, the House's, and then back up the middle, where she had written each week's lodgment, and the clerk's slip, and the coast house's receipt, and the House's dates as they came back down.

"Fifty-two pairs," she said.

Ward was by the door. Saban was on the stool with his hat. Emeric stood at her shoulder.

"Fifty-two into the crown's bag," Ferra said. "Fifty-two slips. Fifty-two twins down the river." She put her finger on one line, near the bottom, and kept it there. "Fifty-one both ways. The crown's copy lodged and receipted, the twin received at the coast and at the capital, and dated at both." She did not move the finger. "And one."

The fifteenth of November. The report with the two pages about Milford in it. The crown's copy into the bag, a little before ten in the morning, with the slip. And the twin in the middle of a bale of Paxton's fleece in Dunnel's cart, on the coast road, a dozen miles short of the coast house, on the twenty-fifth.

The right-hand column on that line was empty. Ferra had not drawn a line through it, or written anything in it. She had left it empty, as she had left it empty since the day the drover came.

"Keller wanted a year," she said. "Dated, paired, attested. It's a year." She took her finger off the line. "It's fifty-one, and one that burned, and a man to say why."

"It's enough," said Saban.

"It's what we have." Ferra closed the index. She did it carefully, the way she closed every book, squaring the edges, and then she put it in the left-hand box with the gate clerk's slips and Paxton's four lines and the slip with the name on it that Saban would not say aloud, and shut the lid, and put both her hands flat on top of it.

Nobody said anything for a while.

It had been a year. A year of Hale walking the crown's copy to the post house every Friday morning in the rain and the snow and the July heat, and standing at the window while the gate clerk wrote out the slip, and walking back. A year of the twin going down the river in a bale, a cask, a sack of salt, Fletcher's punt. A year of Ferra's finger going down the columns every Monday. Dunnel standing up on a moving cart to wave his scarf. Fifty-two Fridays. He had not let himself count them until now.

"May," said Ward. "When the roads are dry."

"May," said Emeric.

Ferra did not take her hands off the box.

That night he lay awake under his window and did not think about the box. He thought about a lamp stone, white on a desk, and a curl of dark hair against a neck, and a voice asking him where it hurt. He thought about it until the lamps on the wall had burned to the grey before dawn and the first cart came up the high street, and then he got up and went down to the dawn drill and let Ward put him on the ground seven times, and did not mind any of them.
