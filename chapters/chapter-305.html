Chapter 305 - Two Strings

"That's him," said Ward, very quietly. "The channel."

He said it in the voice he used for the depth of a river, wool against wool under the hoods, and Emeric heard it with half of himself. The other half was already listening to what Ward meant.

It had come through the mesh. Nothing came through the mesh; he had lain under it for an hour with the world in a blanket and only his bare right hand in the mud for a window, and this had come through as if the wool were not there. And it had come up from underneath. Not across the bank, the way a man's note came, from where the man was: up through the floor of him, through the dark under his own room where Armand had stood on the night of his stone and found every room in the world standing on one black water, like the trees of a wood on their roots. Something on that floor had opened a door upward, a cellar door in a house he had lived in all his life without knowing it had a cellar, and the cold came up the stair.

And it was not one thing. Even through the blanket he could tell that much. It was a chord, and he could not pick the notes out of it with his head in a bag.

He put his hood back.

He knew what it cost while he did it. Ward's hand came across in the dark for his wrist, too late, and stayed there; and then Ward's own hood went back beside him without a word, because Ward was not going to lie deaf in the reeds while his prince listened. The night came in at both of them at once: the screaming horses and thirty men on the hard, the far bank's six roaring across the water, the small notes of the six on the rise, and Ward's room beside him, open, a wall with a lamp behind it. And under all of it, holding all of it, the cold. Larger than a room had any business being, older than anything he had put his sense on in thirteen years, and cold the way the bottom of the sea is cold, which is not a temperature but a fact about how far down you are. It did not push. It opened, and the whole bank lay under it, the way a field lies under weather.

Now he could pick out the notes. There was the great cold one that was the man; and inside it, held the way a coin is held in a closed fist, another. Fine. Clear. Very much smaller. Warm.

The grey bead in his band answered it.

It sat at the clasp on his left wrist, under the suit's sleeve and Ward's fingers, where it had lain against his skin every day since he strung it and never once done anything he had not told it to. Now it rang. Not loudly; it was the size of the end of his little finger. But he felt it go against the bone, the way a string goes that nobody has touched.

His grandmother had kept an upright piano in her front room, in a house Marcus had not thought of in years, a world away, and when he was small she had shown him a trick with it. You held the pedal down so that all the dampers came off the strings, and you leaned in under the lid and sang one note into the dark of it, loud, and stopped; and one string in all that wire went on singing your note back at you, alone, because it was the one cut to that length. Nobody had touched it. It had heard itself.

He had cut that bead from his mother's stone. He had felt, in the cutting, the shape of a room in it that somebody had walked out of, and he had watched the shape go down with the stone as it shrank, and stay, exactly the same, the way a voice stays the same when the person speaking goes away down a corridor. He had never known whose voice. There was nothing before his seventh birthday. There was a wall where his mother should have been, and he had stood at the wall a thousand times and put his hands on it, and there had never been a door.

The bead knew. It was ringing against his wrist with the small warm note inside the cold one, string to string, the same length; and he did not believe in anything, he had never believed in anything, he had stood through every prayer of his childhood at his father's elbow and heard words; and she was there.

She was there. In the man's right hand.

The man sat his grey on the road fifteen yards above the pinned wagon, with the space round him that nobody had ordered, the reins in his left hand. His right lay closed on his thigh, as it had been closed at his side in a receiving line in the capital thirteen years ago while the left went up to bless a prince on his way out of the door. Coming down the cutting, through the mesh, his note had read smaller than a temple guard's. Emeric had looked at him once and put his eyes on the carriage. He knew now what he had been looking at: a man who could make himself small.

Emeric did not look at the hand.

He looked where the face was instead, because a man who has just found the thing he wants most in the world does not look at it if he means to keep it. There was no light to see it by; a pale shape, turned toward the reeds. He did not need the light. He had the face from the receiving line and every feast day of his childhood: a man of fifty, perhaps, creased at the eyes, with a grey that had not finished coming in, and not a line added in all the years since. Marcus had known what eighty looked like and had left this on the bench, with no second instrument to check it against.

He had a second instrument now. He had been carrying it on his wrist since the winter he cut it.

Nineteen, he counted, because Ward had stopped. Twenty. It was a thing to hold on to.



Inside the gravity stone's ten yards nothing had moved. The wagon lay on its cracked hoops with its pair flat on their bellies in the traces and the driver spread on the gravel; and round it in a ring, pressed down beside their horses like leaves in a book, lay the three Church men. The two temple guard in their long pale coats. And a narrow man in plain black, beside his bay, with his face, even now, turned toward the river.

Emeric had not looked at him in the records hall in October. He looked at him now.

The narrow man was not trying to get up. And from him, finer than Imani's hollow view, there went out the thread Emeric had felt between his shoulder blades in a gallery over the capital's kitchens, and at the edge of a fire on the road south, and at the end of a table in the records hall: laid along the outside of his note, the way a man lays his palm along a wall to learn how thick it is. Measuring.

And now, with the cold come up under the bank, he could feel where the other end of it went.

It went back along the road to the man on the grey horse, as plain as a line from a float to the rod; and whatever the thread laid its palm on, Emeric felt arrive at the other end. He felt himself arrive there. A boy with nothing open in him, read in a gallery; a drunk read at a fire; a duke read through a door, and the answer filed. It had never been the narrow man's patience. It had been a hand at the end of a line.

Twenty-four, he counted. Twenty-five.

Then the cold turned.

It turned the way a lamp turns when somebody picks it up in a dark room and the light goes along the walls; over the van, over the sentry posts where the three lay in their mesh and gave it nothing, over the rise where Saban sat with the handset in his lap among six thin ordinary notes like a thousand others; and it came to Ward, and stopped.

Emeric felt it find a knight's note, the long steady burn of a man who had carried his mana in his arms since the rite, with the old cracks in it that a delver could feel like the grain in a board; and in the middle of the knight, open, a room.

The pale shape on the grey horse turned toward Ward.

It stayed there a long breath. Emeric could not see the face and did not need to. He had spent twenty-two years reading faces at his father's court, and he could feel this one doing nothing, carefully, as it had always done nothing: whatever was behind it had been told a sum was wrong and had gone back up the column to check.

Then the lamp came on to Emeric, and stayed.

The thread had told the man everything a thread could: that the line had not died unopened; that the note was loud. It could not tell him that the prince stood on a riverbank at night with his own door open, listening. Now he knew; and Emeric felt him know it, the way you feel a stranger's eyes meet yours in a crowd and not go away.

The left hand lifted a little, reins and all; a pale movement in the dark, no higher than a blessing given in a hall to a young man on his way out of it.

The right stayed closed.

And then something came along the cold for him: not the watcher's palm laid along the outside of a wall, but a hand on the latch of his door.

He shut it. He brought his sense in so hard that the bank went dim round him, Ward and the river and the horses all at once, until he was holding his door at the width of a hair with his whole weight on it; and he felt the hand on the other side lean too, without hurry, much heavier than he was. It tried the latch. It found that the latch held, and something of why, and let go, the way a man lets go of a door he has satisfied himself is locked and goes away to think about the lock.

He was breathing as if he had run up the keep stair. He had not moved his feet.

And while it leaned on him he had done the other thing, without deciding to: he had emptied his arms. Out of his legs and his back and his hands, until there was nothing inward in him that a hand on a latch could find, nothing a knight did; a mage's sense and a mage's door and no more. He could not hide the note with the hood back. He could hide what it was for. It felt like standing on a bank in a borrowed coat, and it made him slow, and he left it so. The man on the grey horse could have the mage. He was not having the rest.

"Fifty-eight," said Ward beside him, under his breath, who had found the count again somewhere.

Emeric had lost the count.



At sixty the weight came off the ring all at once, and the horses scrambled up out of the gravel, and the two temple guard came up off the road.

They did not come up like men pressed flat for a count of sixty. They came up like men who had drilled getting up; and each put his right hand into his left sleeve as he came, and brought it out with something in the palm.

Doran had told him once, the winter Saban filed the priory, at the church table with the death book open between them. Doran had been two years at a mother house as a young man, copying, and the lay brothers drilled in the yard before light every morning, in ranks, in their habits, with stones in their sleeves; they drew them out and put them back on a count, all together, like men saying a prayer with their hands. He had thought it was a devotion. He had said so, and turned a page, and added that he had not thought about it since.

The nearer man's thumb went down on his stone, and fire came off it.

Not a mage's fire. The man's note was a farmer's, thin and ordinary, and the fire came out of the stone, a bolt the size of a man's head, flat and fast across the hard at the reeds where the two of them lay. Emeric felt him pay for it, the small note going down like water in a cup somebody had drunk from.

He had the river. A mage beside a river, Armand said, does not need to be clever. He put his bare hand in the river. It would not close; it did not need to. He stood the shallows up between the bolt and the bank in one pull, a sheet of brown water the height of a man, and the fire went into it and came out as steam with a sound like a smith quenching, and the sheet fell back in a long slap that wet the van's horses to the girths.

The second man had turned. He had his thumb on his stone and his arm out toward the reeds, where something had moved.

Arthur came out of the reeds behind him.

Emeric did not feel him come; the mesh gave nothing. There was a shape behind the man in black with its hood up and no face, and the short black tube against the side of his neck, and the man going down on the gravel without a sound, as if he had remembered something he had to do on the ground. Mira had the first one before his second stone was out of his sleeve; she came out of the dark on the river side, low, and touched him once, and caught him, and had a coat over his head before Emeric had finished looking.

The narrow man got up off the road.

He did not run, and he did not reach into his sleeve. He brushed the dust off the knees of his coat, first one and then the other, and looked at nobody. His thread lay along Emeric's note as lightly as it had in the records hall, and at the far end of it the man on the grey horse was reading.

Back up the cutting the captain was roaring for lanterns, and every time somebody got one lit on the hard a book shut on the rise, and it went out again.



They were up on their knees in the reeds by then. He had held the hair-width door open since the piles, by habit, the door that made a drill yard look like a drawing and showed him where a man was going before he went. It showed him now: pale as breath at the edge of his sight, a rider of the van turning his horse a stride ahead of the real one; a man on the hard getting up off the gravel a breath before he got up. Everything that had weight, and feet to put it on, went a little ahead of itself into the dark.

The man on the grey horse had no ghost. Nothing he did began in his weight. A sword begins in the feet; a spell does not. Emeric had never fought a man who could do everything he meant to do sitting still.

And then there was a ghost after all; only it was the ground's. So faint he almost missed it: the lip of the bank under the two of them sliding toward the water, the near piles leaning out with it, the way a pan of wet sand goes when you tip it.

The real ground went. The trodden mud at the hard's edge went soft under his knees, as a road goes soft in a thaw, with the water coming up into it from below. No ring anybody could see. There was earth here already, and water in the earth, and the man had only told the one to stop holding the other, which cost almost nothing, which was the whole of the trick. Under the landing stage the piles groaned and leaned, and one of his own knobs of ice came away off the nearest with a crack and went bobbing off downriver like a turnip.

Emeric had his bare hand coming up.

Ward's was there first. He had torn his right glove off with his teeth.

Ward let his arms go. Emeric felt the rope open beside him, the steady burn going out of the forearms all at once, so that for a breath the man at his shoulder was only a big man on a sliding bank with nothing in him a sword could not go through. Then the ring came up off his right palm, low, toward the ground. Dull amber, the width of a plate. It shook as it drew itself, as it had shaken in the yard on a frosty morning in front of fifty-nine people; it went on shaking for the half of a breath that was all the time in the world on a bank going into a river; and closed.

The bank stopped, as a floor stops. The mud under Emeric's knees went hard again, harder than before, packed like a threshing floor for twenty yards along the bank; the piles stood leaning where they had got to and went no farther; and the river ran on round them as if nothing had been asked of it.

Ward put the warmth back into his arms.

"Hm," said Ward.

Out on the road the pale shape had turned to Ward a second time.

Whatever the face did, the cold had stopped moving and was resting on Ward, the way a scholar's finger rests on a line he has read a great many times and has just seen say something new. A knight had opened his hands and cast. Whatever the man had in his books, and Emeric was suddenly sure he had books, older than any in Armand's tower, there had not been a page in them for that.

Emeric did the only thing he could think of that would tell nobody anything. He froze the road.

It was wet from the horses and the shallows he had thrown, and the heat was easy to take; he pulled it from twenty yards through the bare hand and let it go off his palm into the river beside him, and laid a skin of black ice under the grey horse's four feet to bring the horse down and the man with it. A small thing. A ring the size of his palm, turning sunwise, closing.

It met its inverse in the air between them and went to nothing.

Not a clash. No steam, no flash, nothing left over. His spell was simply not there, and the horse stood on wet road, and a ring had come up over the man's left hand for exactly as long as it took to close, turning exactly the other way and keeping its hand. The right turn. Not the flip. The thing every tower mage got wrong under pressure and Emeric had always got right, because in the slow place he had the time to look.

The man had had the time too.

Nobody had ever reversed him. He had not known until that moment that he had been carrying the fact about with him like a coin in a pocket.



The man turned his horse's head away from them, toward the river.

The ferry was a hundred yards out and going away; he had let the slack patch go when the rope was cut, and the river had her. She turned slowly as she went: the king's black carriage in the middle with its panels painted out, the greys in the traces with their ears flat, the coachman and the ferryman sitting against the downstream rail with sacks over their heads and their wrists tied, as quiet as men in church, and two more in the river hanging off her side ropes; and at the upstream corner, with the long ash pole in both hands, Rylan.

Two of the van had come out of the flash's blindness enough to see her go, and been brave about it. They had put their horses off the end of the stage into the shallows after her, up to the horses' chests, swearing, with the water white round them.

The ring that came up over the man's left hand was so faint Emeric nearly missed it.

It was drawn in breath on cold glass. He went into the slow place after it and read what he could. The turn: sunwise, a hand he knew. The core: a discharge. The road: across the water to the man on the pole. The amount he could not read at all. It was laid in under the ring where the ring did not show it, and whatever his eye told him the ring held was a lie the ring was telling on purpose.

He cast the inverse of what he could see.

Half of it met half of it, out over the shallows, and went to nothing. The rest went on.

It went white across the shallows with a crack like a beam breaking in a fire, and the two riders were in the road of it. He felt their notes go, both at once, like two lamps put out with one hand; and then he was seeing it, the white going through horses and men and water and on, thinner, much less, to the ferry's corner, where it found the wet ash pole and the man holding it.

Rylan went backward off his feet across the deck and into the near wheel of the king's carriage, and lay there.

The pole went into the river, smoking.

One of the riderless horses came up out of the shallows onto the hard below them, close enough to touch, with its stirrups flying and its eyes white, and went away up the bank into the dark. The other horse did not come up. Neither did either of the men.

Emeric had his sense on the ferry before the thunder finished. Rylan's note came at him out of the dark where the river suit should have hidden it, through the place where the sleeve and the mesh in it had burned away. It had jumped, as a man's does when he is hit, and come down into the shape he knew, cheerful and bothered and steady; and wrong all along the left ribs, and wrong in the arm from hand to elbow, hot. And beside it on the deck, kneeling by the wheel already, a note that was very hard to read, because it had spent a great many years making itself hard to read.

"Still here!" Rylan shouted, across the water. It came out higher than he meant. "Lost the pole!"

The man on the grey horse had turned again. Not toward the bank, nor the river: up the road, away from the landing, toward the rise where it came down out of a wood, at a walk. The narrow man caught his bay and rode after him, in no more hurry.

And the bead on Emeric's wrist went with them.

That was what it felt like. A pull, slight and steady, at the bone, like a child's hand on a sleeve in a crowd. The small warm string was going away up the road in a closed right hand, and the bead was ringing after it, fainter at every step of the grey horse, and wanted him to come.

He could go. He had legs that would have him at the top of that road before the grey horse reached the trees, if he let the rest of himself back into them, in the open, in front of the hand on the latch. Or he could go as a mage, at a mage's pace, and arrive in a dark wood with a man who had reversed him without trying. He did not know what he would do there. He knew what he wanted to do there. It was not a thing he had ever wanted before, and it was very simple, and he could feel it in his hands.

Ward stood at his shoulder, half a pace back, where he had stood since Emeric was seven, and did not say a word.

The ferry was turning in the current, farther every breath. Below the crossing the reeds ran out and the river went into a bend over gravel, quick and loud, and beyond the bend the road came down to the water again, where the captain had a lantern relit and was getting his men in order.

Emeric turned his back on the road and went down the bank into the river.



He was in it to his waist when the white hull came up out of the wood.

He felt it before he saw it. Back up the rise among the trees where the grey horse had gone there were stones, a great many, and working. That was the wrong part, and he knew it was wrong before he knew why: they were working steadily, the way the K Discovery's worked under her matrix, and nobody was touching them. He went to the place where the man should be. Every hull in the kingdom had that place, the casting seat, where a mage sat with his hands on the lift and poured himself into it a breath at a time, because the stones did not hold, they were held. He had thought that line on the morning he left the capital, watching the little sky-ships settle on their tiers, and had built a ship to make it untrue.

There was nobody at the seat.

Two notes went aboard, the great cold one and the fine thin one behind it, among a handful of others already there, ordinary, waiting; and the stones took all of them without anybody asking, and the hull lifted.

Then he saw it over the treetops against the stars. Small, no longer than a river barge. Pale; not lit, but pale of itself, as a gull is pale on a dark sea, so that the eye kept sliding off it. It rose without a sound, level and slow, the way a thing rises that is in no hurry because nothing in the world can follow it; and climbed; and turned west. Not north over the hills, where the K Discovery sat in her cloud with her lamps dark waiting for three words. West, and a little north of west, toward nothing he had a map of; and far out, at the very edge of what he could feel, it began to bend north.

He let his breath go.

The bead was quiet. He had not noticed it stop. The pull had gone thin as the hull went up, and at some point between one push of the current against his chest and the next there had been nothing to pull.

In the reeds across the hard there was a boy. He had been there all along, Emeric realised: a child's note, crouched in the mud since the flash, gone small and still the way a hare goes still in a furrow. The ferryman's boy. He was standing now, up to his shins among the stems with his head back and his mouth open, looking at the sky over the wood where a pale thing had gone up and away; and Emeric could feel him trying to keep it, as a child tries to keep a dream at the moment of waking, and losing it already.

However he told it in the morning, nobody would believe him. Emeric found he was sorry about that.



Declan had the line before anybody asked him.

He had gone off the ferry's side when Rylan went down, with the mouthpiece still in his teeth, and got the mooring rope off its post at her end; and now he was coming across the current toward the reeds with it, swimming on his side, the rope paying out behind him, the river pulling him and the ferry both toward the bend.

Emeric put his bare hand flat on the water.

He had held a patch of this river slack for an hour and a half already tonight, and he could feel it in his shoulders and behind his eyes. Water was not his; it cost him three times what it cost Hedda, who had taught him. He did it anyway. He told the river to stop moving between the ferry and the bank, only there, a lane of slack water twenty yards wide; and the current went round it like traffic round a cart broken down in a street, and Declan came across the lane as if he were swimming in a millpond.

Arthur and Bren were waist deep beside him by then, hoods back, their faces wet and very young. Declan put the rope's end into Bren's hands without a word and went back for the ferry.

They brought her in on the rope, Arthur and Bren and then Ward behind them, with Emeric holding the lane until the pain behind his eyes was a lamp somebody kept turning up; and she came across the slack nose first and went into the reeds above the bend with a long hissing of stems along her planks, and grounded. Mira had gone up to the road's edge, and lay in the grass watching the captain's lantern.

Emeric let the river go. It came round his chest cold and quick, and he stood in it a little while with his good hand on the rail before he climbed up.



His father was on his knees on the deck by the carriage wheel, with his coat off.

He had got out of the carriage on his own: the off-side door hung on one hinge, its black paint cracked in a star where a heel had gone through from inside, and under the flaked paint showed the carved gold of the arms the Church had painted out. He had his coat folded under Rylan's head and his sleeves pushed up, and his hand flat on Rylan's chest on the right side, holding him down.

He did not look up.

"His ribs are gone on the left," said the king. "Two. Perhaps three. He keeps trying to sit up."

"I'm sitting up," said Rylan, from under the hand, "because the arm's on fire, Majesty."

"Then put it in the river."

"Yes, Majesty."

Rylan put his right arm over the side into the water to the elbow, said a word Emeric had not heard him use in thirteen years, and lay back on the king's coat with his eyes shut. His sleeve was gone from wrist to elbow, and what was under it was the colour of a plum.

"There," said Harrold. "Now lie on the other side."

"It's the side with the ribs."

"I know which side it is."

Then he looked up.

He was thinner. Three months in three rooms over a cloister garden, eating what he was given and watching it, had taken something off his face that would come back and something that would not, and he was greyer at the temples than Emeric remembered. The eyes were the same: gold, in the dark. Emeric had hidden the same eyes under a hat at every inn on the road south.

His father did not get up. He kept his hand on Rylan's chest and looked at his son streaming river from the chest down; and when he spoke it was at the volume of the whisper, the one that died at arm's length, though there was nobody on that ferry to hear but a knight with his eyes shut and men with sacks over their heads. He did not seem to know he was doing it.

"You were told not to come."

"Ward was told."

"Ward is your man."

"Ward," said Emeric, "has been your man all my life, and he's standing on that bank."

His father looked at the bank. Ward was standing on it, up to his knees in the reeds, with the rope still in one hand, looking at the road.

Something happened at the corner of the king's mouth that was not a smile. It went away again.

"It is done," said Harrold.

He had said it once at the full voice, in the capital, with a listener at the door, to tell his son nothing. Emeric heard the difference, and said nothing back. Anything he said on that ferry would have been too much, and they would both have had to stand in it.

"The greys want cutting out," said his father, "before the near horse puts its foot through the deck. Have you a knife?"

Emeric gave him his knife, left-handed. His father looked at the right hand, once, and did not ask. The king got up, stiffly, in a way that told Emeric something about the three rooms, and spoke to the near horse in a horseman's low nonsense until its ears came up off its skull; and cut the traces in four strokes, and led it off the bow into the reeds. Then the off horse. They stood in the shallows with the harness hanging off them, and drank.

"Waylon would have had them out in half the time," said the king.

He came back, and stood over Rylan, and looked down at the arm in the river.

"Augustine," he said. Not a question, quite.

"Gone."

"Gone where?"

Emeric looked at the wood on the rise, and the stars over it, and the empty place among them.

"Later," he said.

His father looked at him a long while, with the river going by. Then he nodded once, the way a man nods at a figure he will check another day, and pulled the painted-out door off its last hinge and laid it flat on the deck beside Rylan.

"Then we'll carry him on this," he said. "It's no use to anybody as a door."



They went up the river in the reeds in the order Ward gave them, which was not the order they had come down in.

Mira first, because the mesh hid her from anything that could feel and the night from anything that could see. Then Rylan on the carriage door, Declan and Arthur at its foot and Bren and one of the six at its head, and the king beside it with one hand on its edge as if he were helping to carry it, which he was not. The rest of the six with their rifles slung. Saban behind them with the radio on his back and its cane bowed over his head like a fishing rod, swearing whenever it caught in the reeds. Ward last.

They left the four of them tied on the ferry, the two from the river too, whom Declan had hauled out on his way back, sat in a row with sacks over their heads where the captain's lanterns would find them before morning. Nobody had seen a face. Nobody had been killed by Kaldo. Two men were dead in the shallows below the landing, and the river was taking them toward the bend; and Emeric did not know their names, and found that he wanted to, and knew Saban would find them in a week if he asked, and that he would ask.

A quarter of a mile up, where the reeds stood higher than the king's head, Ward said stop, and Saban knelt in the mud and took the handset off its hook. He listened to it a while with his cap pushed back, as he had listened at a treeline in May; then pressed the switch and said Ferra's three words into it, clearly, in the order she had written them on the card in his cap: the one that was the ship's, the one that was the king's, and the one that meant come now.

"Fletcher's coming down," he said.

"How long?"

"He didn't say." Saban put the handset back on its hook. "He said yes. In her cipher. I'd say she sat up very straight when she wrote it."

Behind them a horn went on the road, and another, farther. Emeric let his sense out thin over the meadow and felt thirty-odd men going the wrong way down the road toward the bend, where a ferry might have gone that had lost its pole, with the captain at their head and his note going like a drum; and far up the road by a sat-down wagon, two temple guard working their coats off their heads and finding nobody there.

The king walked the whole two miles and did not ask to rest. At the edge of the wood, where the reeds gave out into the pasture, he stopped with his hand still on the door's edge and looked up at the cloud over the hills, breathing like a man who had been in three rooms since April.

"I am told," he said, to nobody, "that she doesn't make a sound."

"She makes a sound," said Rylan, from the door, with his eyes shut. "Close to. You'll see."



She came down out of the cloud at a little before one with her stones dimmed almost to nothing and her lamps out, a long shape blacker than the sky, and stopped at a man's height over the pasture with the trim keys clicking and the grass lying down flat and silver under her keel.

Emeric did not look at his father looking at her. He could feel the hard careful note beside him go still for the length of a breath, the way a man's note goes still when a thing he has known for years as a line in a cipher letter is in front of him, the size of a church, hanging over a pasture laying the grass down.

She came down the last yard onto her legs. Her hatch came down on its chains, which on her was the ramp, and laid its lip in the grass, and the light inside came down it, dim, the colour of a lamp turned low in a sickroom.

Ferra was standing at the top of it.

She was in her grey with her coat over it and every pin in, and the ship's log under her arm; and she stood with one hand on the chain and did not come down. He watched her count them out of the wood. She found Rylan on the door, and stopped on him, and went on; found the king, and went on; and came to Emeric, and stayed.

They took Rylan up first, then Saban and the six and the three, then the king with Declan's hand under his elbow on the slope, which the king allowed. For the king she dropped the full court curtsey, the one she had learned for the investiture and not used since, all the way down to the deck, in a wet hold; and came up out of it and said, "Majesty. Mind the step. It's wet," and the king looked at her for some time and said, "Thank you," and minded it.

Emeric came up the ramp last but one, with Ward behind him.

He had put two fingers under his own jaw at the edge of the wood while nobody was looking and counted, the way she counted, and it had not been under a hundred, and it was not under a hundred now.

Ferra put the log down at her feet. Then she drew the cord out from under the top button of her dress, where it had lain against her skin for a night and a day and a night, and lifted it over her head, the pins catching; and reached up and put it over his. The key came down warm against his breastbone through the wet wool. She tucked it in at the suit's neck with two fingers, and turned the wool down on the left side where it had not been turned under, and smoothed it flat.

"Once," she said.

"Once."

"I read it as it came." She looked at the log at her feet. "She went up, and she sat in the cloud three hours and more, and she came down. Nobody flew." She did not look at him. "I'd have known."

"I know you would."

She looked at the wet line across his chest where the river had come to. "You were in the river."

"To the waist. After."

"That's not flying," said Ferra, and he could see her deciding which column it went in.

Behind her, in the hold, his father had turned round and was looking back at the two of them: at the key going in at his son's neck, and the woman's hand on his collar. He said nothing. He looked at it as he had looked at the ship, as a thing he had had in cipher for a long time; then let Declan take him in.

Ferra picked up her book.

"Imani's in the line room," she said. "She's been there since four in the afternoon. She sent Bettany down to the dock with a stretcher at midnight because I said one man hurt and didn't say which, and she hasn't forgiven me." She looked at him then, at last, full, grey-green and steady in the sickroom light. "And she'll know. Whatever it is that's wrong with your face. Before you've said a word."

"Nothing's wrong with my face."

"Emeric," said Ferra, "I've been looking at your face for thirteen years," and went up into the ship.



Fletcher took her up on her stones, slow and level, through the cloud and over it, and turned her south and east for home and climbed her on, sealed, to where the wind ran their way; and did not touch the brass guard over the lever under the bow helm's panel, or look at it.

The saloon was dim and smelled of river. Rylan lay on the carriage door with his arm in a bucket of the ship's water, and Ferra knelt by him with the ship's box open and her pen in her teeth, doing what Imani had written for a burn on the card inside the lid, line by line, reading each line before she did it. Declan sat by him with a hand on his good shoulder. The three and the six were asleep against the bulkhead the way soldiers sleep, all at once, where they sat. Linus was at his chart table, writing the time.

The king stood at the forward iris with his hands behind his back, looking down at whatever there was to see through the holes in the cloud, and did not sit, and nobody asked him to. Emeric did not go and look at what he was looking at. He sat on the bench by the after iris, where Ward was.

Ward had his hands on his knees. His note was steady, because it was always steady; and under it, in the room that had been open on the bank and was shut now, there was something Emeric had felt in it only once before, in a yard on a frosty morning with a stone hanging a hand's width off the cobbles. It was very like fear, and Ward would have knocked him down for calling it that.

"That was him," said Ward.

"Yes."

"I have felt that once in my life before tonight." Ward did not turn his head. "I had no name for it. I had a boy on my arm and the crystals in my plate going to nothing and I ran. For hours, with that at my back. And I have felt it every night since in my sleep, and told myself it was the running." He was quiet. "It was not the running."

Emeric said nothing.

"Not on the ship," said Ward. "Not with him aboard. Not until I've thought."

"Not on the ship."

Ward nodded, and shut his eyes, which Emeric had seen him do at sea perhaps three times in nine years, and leaned his head back against the bulkhead and did not open them again until Kaldo.

Emeric sat with his left wrist on his knee and his right hand lying open over it, because it would not do anything else.

He did not believe in it. He wanted that understood, even by himself. He knew what a string did when it was the right length, and that a bead cut from a stone with the shape of a room in it would answer that shape wherever it came from, as a piano wire answered a small boy singing into it in his grandmother's front room. That was all it needed to have done. A copy of a shape; a pattern kept in a stone. He could have drawn it on the board for Ottoline.

And she had been there.

He knew the difference between a string that rings because it is the right length and a voice that knows its name has been called. He did not know which he had heard on the bank, and could not stop knowing that he had heard one of them.

Ferra came and sat by him on the other side when Rylan's arm was done, with linen threads on her sleeves and a smudge of the burn salve on her jaw. She did not ask him anything. She looked at his hand over his sleeve, and put hers over it, so that there were two hands over it, and wrote nothing.

"He'll live," she said, after a while. "Rylan. He says he's owed a ferryman's wage for the night and he wants it in writing."

"Did you write it?"

"Of course I wrote it," said Ferra. "One night, ferryman, at the river rate. And the pole."



They came down over the lake at twenty to five, with the sky over the eastern hills gone from black to slate, into the reeds at the secret dock; and Bettany was on the quay with a lantern and two orderlies and a stretcher, and Rylan would not get on it.

"I walked onto that ship," he said, which was not true. "I'll walk off it."

"You'll walk to the ward," said Bettany.

"I'll walk to the line room. She's counting throats at the door." He got himself up onto his feet with Declan under his good arm, white as the linen, and stood swaying in the lantern light with his strapped arm held against his chest like a parcel. "I want mine counted."

Bettany looked at Declan. Declan looked at the sky.

"Then I'll walk behind you," said Bettany, "with this," and the orderlies picked up the stretcher, and they all went up the hill.

The king did not go with them. Where the path forked Ward said that the king would come up the water stair to the postern, the long way, with himself and Saban, so that nobody at the gate saw a man at five in the morning they would tell their wives about. The king heard him out. He looked up at the keep, black against the slate with one window lit high in it, and Emeric saw him find the lit window and look at it for some time.

"You're expected," said his father. It was not quite a question.

"In the line room."

"Then go."

He went.



The line room was at the top of the keep, with its shutters open on the lake and the hills. One lamp was lit low on the table under the window. The night clerk sat at the sounder with his sleeves rolled up, and the wireless set on its shelf hissed faintly to itself, with nothing in the hiss now that anybody needed.

Imani was standing in the middle of the floor, facing the door, in her scrubs with her ward coat over them and her hair come loose at the temples from its cloth, her hands quite still at her sides, which was not like her. The chair she had not been sitting in stood pushed back from the table with her satchel on it.

She did not look at his face. She looked at his throat.

He stood in the door and let her, with the key against his breastbone under the wet wool, knowing exactly what she would find. She crossed the room and put two fingers under his jaw on the left side, where she had put them in the dark the night before last, and shut her eyes, and counted.

"A hundred and one," said Imani.

"I came up the stair."

She opened her eyes and looked at the whole of his face, the way Ferra had said she would; and he saw her see whatever was in it, and decide, as plainly as he had ever seen her decide a dose, that it would keep until she had time to take it off him properly, and not at five in the morning.

"That's the stair," she said, and took her fingers away. "I'll let you have it."

"Count mine," said Rylan, from the door behind him.

He stood there with Declan holding him up on one side and Bettany on the other and the stretcher on the landing behind, his face the colour of the wall; and he was grinning.

Imani looked past Emeric at the strapped arm, and the way he stood with all of himself held off the left side, and the colour of him; and Emeric watched her stop being a woman who had stood in the middle of a floor since the afternoon and become the other thing, all at once, like a light switched on.

"The ward," she said. "Now."

"I walked up."

"I can see you walked up. That's what's wrong with you." She had her satchel off the chair. "Lie on that. Not on the left. Don't argue with me. Bettany, his feet. Declan, take that thing off your chest before you fall down the stair in it." She was past Emeric and in the door. "And nobody tell Nessa it was lightning."

"Tell her I fell off a boat," said Rylan, lying down on the stretcher on his right side with great care. "It's true. I did. Twice."

"Shut up," said Imani, and went down the stair beside him with her hand flat on his chest, counting.

He heard them go down: Rylan telling Bettany about the pole, Imani telling him to shut up again, boots on the turn of the stair, the door at its foot; and then only the sounder ticking now and then, idly, the way it did when the line to Wayford had nothing to say.

The clerk wrote in the line book. Emeric read it upside down. 4.52. Ship in. All home.

He went to the window. Down the slope, on the water stair below the postern, three men were coming up slowly in the half dark, one a head taller than the others and one with his cap in his hand; and the one in the middle stopped halfway and looked out at the lake for a while before he went on.

His right hand still would not close. He put its thumb on the grey bead under his sleeve.

It was cold. It was a stone. He kept his thumb on it anyway, at the window, until the three men had gone in under the postern arch and the light had come up enough over the hills to show the colour of the water.
