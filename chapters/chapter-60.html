Chapter 54 - The Sick-Day Column

The etching floor had been running for two years by the autumn of the sixth year, and Emeric had stopped going down to it, which was how he knew it worked.

It occupied the long building that had been the cooperage, with the north wall opened for light and the floor taken up and relaid because Vesk had complained about the draught for a month and had turned out, on measurement, to be right about the draught. Four of Vesk's apprentices now, up from two. Nevin at the far bench under the best of the window, still unable to read a word of anything, still cutting the cleanest line in the barony, with every tracing he made now going across to Armand's bench at the end of the week and coming back with a number written on it that Nevin could not read either and that Vesk read to him.

And around the four of them, doing everything that was not cutting, twelve labourers.

They prepared substrate, which meant grinding, wetting, facing and stacking blanks until the surface would take a line. They handled the charged stock, which came down from the store in trays and went back up in trays and was carried by hand because a barrow jarred it. They fed and turned the ink stones, which had to be kept wet and which stained the hands of anybody who touched them a dull grey that did not come off for three days. They swept. They carried. They stood at the benches holding things steady for men who needed both hands.

Nobody in Kaldo, including Emeric, had ever thought about them as doing anything except carrying.

Imani had kept a register since she was nineteen years old.

She had started it in a market town two provinces away, in a hand much worse than the one she had now, because a physician she had worked under had told her that a healer who cannot remember what happened last winter is a healer who learns nothing in thirty years. She had kept it through everything since. She had kept it through the plague, in the weeks when she was writing four and five names a day and not sleeping, and the pages from that spring were the worst handwriting in the book and the most complete.

What she kept now was better, because the barony had made it worth keeping better.

Kaldo paid a man his wage while he was hurt. Emeric had put that in place in the second year, over Ferra's objection, and had then been proved right about it in a way that still annoyed her slightly, and the consequence of it was that the barony had to count the days. A man who is paid for not working is a line in a ledger. So Ferra's work rolls held every day every man on the barony's payroll had not worked, and why, in one word, taken from the overseer.

And Imani's register held the same days from the other end, with the actual cause in it, because the overseer wrote fever and Imani wrote what it had been.

Two sets of paper, kept by two people, for two entirely unrelated reasons, covering the same eleven hundred working souls. Neither of them had put the pair together on purpose. Neither of them had put the pair together at all.

She came up to the keep on a wet afternoon in the ninth week of autumn with the register under her coat, and she did not make an appointment, because she never had.

"I have something that doesn't behave," she said. "I don't know what it is and I want to say that first."

"Sit down."

"I'd rather stand. I've been sitting with this for five weeks and I've talked myself out of it twice."

She put the register on the table and opened it where she had marked it, and what she showed him was a column of small numbers that meant nothing to him at all.

"Days lost," she said. "By crew. Per hundred men, per season, so the crews compare. The farm sits about here. The garrison sits a little under, which is what you'd expect from young men who are fed and not doing anything very hard most of the year. The foundry sits above everybody, which is also what you'd expect, because the foundry burns people."

"And?"

Her finger moved down two lines and stopped.

"The etching floor sits a third under the farm. It has sat a third under the farm every season since the floor opened. Eight seasons."

Emeric looked at the number and did not know what to do with it, which she had expected.

"That could be twenty things," he said.

"It could be four things and I have tested all four." She had not raised her voice and she was not going to. "Age. They are not younger. Two of them are the oldest men on any crew in this barony. Food. The barony feeds every crew out of the same kitchen and I checked the issue against Ferra's book rather than asking anybody. Hours. They work the same hours as the foundry and fewer than the farm in August. Housing. Nine of the twelve live in the lower rows with foundry men on both sides of them, and the lower rows are the worst housing we have, and the men on both sides of them go down at the ordinary rate."

"Then it's chance."

"It is not chance across eight seasons in the same direction," Imani said. "I would like it to be. I have wanted it to be for five weeks."

She turned the page.

"There is a second thing and it is the one that made me come up here rather than wait for the winter. I dress the cuts on that floor. I dress everyone's cuts. They close faster." She said it flatly. "Not miraculously. Nobody's hand knits overnight. A bad gash on a farm hand runs nine or ten days before I stop worrying about it, and on that floor it runs six or seven, and I did not notice it for a year and a half because six and nine are not different enough to notice one at a time. They are different enough to notice ninety at a time."

"You're telling me something is making them healthier."

"I am telling you a column is wrong," Imani said. "I am not telling you a cause and I would ask you not to put one in my mouth. I have one group of twelve people who are different from everybody else in this barony, and they have exactly one thing in common that nobody else has, and it is not age or food or hours or housing."

"What is it."

"They spend eleven hours a day in a room full of charged stone, handling it with their bare hands."

He asked Ferra for the work rolls that evening and she brought them up herself, all three years of them, in the box, and set the box on the table next to the register.

Nobody said anything about the two sets of paper sitting side by side. Ferra went back down to the counting room. Emeric sat in the long room with a lamp and worked through both of them until well past the middle of the night, matching name to name, and came out the other end with exactly what Imani had come in with, which was a difference he could not explain and could not make go away.

At some point in the small hours he stopped writing for a while and sat still, and what he was thinking about was a different set of books entirely, from a different autumn, when he had been a stranger here and had put a priest's burial ledger beside a healer's count of the dead and had found a gap that nobody had put there on purpose and that had been visible the whole time to anybody who had thought to lay the two sheets down together.

He did not write that down either.

Armand heard it the next morning standing up, with his coat still on, and did not say one interesting thing about it.

That was the value of him. Emeric had walked down expecting, without quite admitting it, to be met with something, and what he got was a man of seventy-odd who listened to the whole account with his head slightly to one side and then asked four questions, none of which were about mana.

Who wrote the register. Had she known, when she wrote each entry, which crew the man was from. Had the overseer's word ever been changed afterward. And had anybody, at any point, told Imani what result was wanted.

"No to the last one. I've only known since yesterday."

"Then it is worth an hour of the bench," Armand said, "and it is not worth a sentence more of either of us talking about it until it has had one."

He would not be drawn further. Emeric tried twice on the way across the yard, once about the rate at which a body might take in charge and once about whether the tower had ever measured a man for anything at all, and Armand answered the second question and ignored the first.

The answer to the second was no. The tower measured stone, and instruments, and the output of a working, and in four hundred years it had never systematically measured a person, because a person was not an experiment. A candidate was assessed once, by a senior, by eye and by feel, and graded, and that grading followed him for the rest of his life and was never revisited.

"Never," Emeric said.

"Why would you," Armand said. "A man's capacity is what he was born with. You do not measure a man's height twice."

He said it without any awareness at all that he was saying something, and went to fetch the reference stones.

He took the twelve over four days, two and three at a time, in the room off the foundry office with the bench in it, and he did it badly on purpose.

Badly meaning slowly. He measured each man three times on three separate days at three different hours, because a man measured after eleven hours of carrying is not the same instrument as a man measured at dawn. He measured four of the foundry crew in among them and did not tell anybody why he was doing that. He wrote every reading in his own notation, which nobody in Kaldo could read, and he would not let Emeric stand in the room.

"You will look at a number and have an opinion about it," he said, "and then I will be measuring in a room with your opinion in it."

The labourers found the whole business enormously funny for about a day and then found it tedious.

It was on the second of those days, waiting, that Emeric went and found the page in the notebook and read what he had written on it.

It was from his second winter, four years back. He had come up out of the trove with a shape in his head that he had not been able to put down since, which was that if a stone could be brought to hold more than it was born holding, then the same might be true of the thing a stone was a poor imitation of. He had written three pages about it. And at the bottom of the third page, in a harder hand than the rest, he had written that he would never test it on a person. Not once. Not on a prisoner, not on a volunteer, not on himself under any circumstance he could presently imagine, and that if he ever found himself constructing the argument for why this particular case was different, he was to stop and read this line.

He had meant it. He had kept it. In four years he had not once been tempted, and he had been mildly proud of that in the way a man is proud of a virtue that has never been expensive.

And he had had twelve people in a room full of charged stone for two years.

On his payroll. In a building he had ordered built, at work he had assigned them, under a roof he had specified, handling material he had told them to handle with their hands because a barrow jarred it. Nobody had designed it. Nobody had proposed it. Nobody had asked any of the twelve whether they wished to take part in anything, because there had been nothing to take part in, because it had not been an experiment.

It had been producing data for two years. It had been producing data because Kaldo counted sick days, because Kaldo paid injured men, because Kaldo wrote everything down. Every instrument he had built to make the barony honest had been quietly assembling the exact thing he had forbidden himself, in the open, in two ledgers, in two different hands, while he congratulated himself on a line in a notebook.

He sat with that for most of an afternoon and did not find a way to make it into something other than what it was.

He told them on the fifth day, all twelve, in the etching floor with the benches stopped and Vesk standing at the back with his arms folded.

He did not make a speech. He had thought about making one on the way down and had worked out that a speech was for him and not for them.

"There's a difference in the records," he said. "Men who work this floor lose fewer days than anyone else in the barony and have done since it opened, and cuts close faster on this floor than they do anywhere else. That's all we have. Nobody knows why. It might be the charged stock. It might be something none of us has thought of. I don't know whether it's good for you or bad for you, and I want to be clear that I mean that, because a thing that helps a cut close is not automatically a thing you want in you for twenty years."

Somebody at the back asked whether the master meant they were being poisoned.

"I mean I don't know," Emeric said. "If I knew, I'd tell you that instead. Here's what I'm offering. Anyone who wants off this floor goes off it. Today, or at the end of the week, or whenever you like. Same wage, on the roll, no loss of standing, and the place stays open for you if you change your mind. You don't have to give a reason and nobody is going to ask you for one and nobody is going to remember it."

He did not tell them they were free to stay. That would have been putting a thumb on it.

There was a long quiet. Vesk, at the back, did not move a muscle.

Two of them took it. A man named Leff, who had three children and who said so out loud, which he had not needed to do, and who was on the drainage crew inside a fortnight and reasonably happy there. And a woman named Ada, who said nothing at all, and took her tray back to the store, and put it down, and went out, and did not look at anybody on the way.

Ten stayed.

Emeric spoke to each of them afterward, one at a time, because he wanted the reason and he wanted it out of earshot of the others. Nine of them gave him a version of the same answer, which was money. The floor paid better than the farm because the work wanted hands that did not shake, and none of the nine was in a position to take less, and two of them were fairly blunt about the fact that a lord asking whether they would like to be poorer was not a question with two ends to it.

He wrote that down too.

The tenth was a man called Tobin, who had been on the floor since the day it opened, who was somewhere in his thirties, who had come to Kaldo after the plague from one of the lower water hamlets and had done four different jobs before this one.

"Money," Emeric said. "Or something else?"

"Money's most of it." Tobin considered it. He had the grey stain on both hands to the second knuckle. "Not all of it."

"Go on."

"You're going to find out what it is," Tobin said. "That's what this place does. Somebody notices a thing, and then everybody's counting it, and then in a year you know." He shrugged. "I've been in that room two years. If there's an answer, it's about me. I'd rather be standing where it happens than hear it secondhand at the Mere."

It was, Emeric thought, the only answer anybody had given him all day that he actually recognised.

Armand came up on the sixth evening, late, after the lamps had been lit, with a sheet of paper folded once in his hand.

He did not sit down and he did not put the sheet on the table.

"You have them," Emeric said.

"I have thirty-six readings and four controls."

"And?"

"And I am going to run the whole set again on Monday, against a fresh standard, with a different order of men, and if it comes out the same I will tell you what it says." He tucked the sheet inside his coat. "Do not ask me tonight. If I say it out loud now I will have said it, and then I will spend Monday looking for it instead of measuring."

"Is it interesting?"

Armand stopped in the doorway with his hand on the frame, and for a moment he looked every one of his years.

"I taught a boy the fundamentals of this subject for six years," he said, "and in that time I told him about four hundred things I was certain of. I have been in this barony fourteen months and I have had to take back nine of them."

He went out.

"Goodnight," he said, from the corridor. "It is interesting."
