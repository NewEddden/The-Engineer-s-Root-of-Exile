Chapter 60 - The Watch Post

The needle moved two hours after sunrise on the twenty-sixth of May, and the man on it was asleep.

He woke because it clicked. The galvanometer needle in the line room was hung so that it ticked against its stop at the end of a swing, a small dry sound like a fingernail on a table, and the man on the needle that morning had heard it at sunrise, one slow tick to the left and back, all well, as he had heard it every morning and evening for a month, and had gone back to sleep on his stool. This was three ticks to the left, fast, and he was on his feet before he was awake, looking at Abloh's board on the wall, where the flame was.

By the time Emeric got up the stair the needle had done it twice more. Ward was already there, in his shirt, with his boots in his hand. He had not put them on. He was watching the needle.

It swung again: the flame. Then it swung differently, and the man on the needle looked at the board and said, "Boat," and then, as it came again, "Boat," and then again, and stopped saying it.

"Say again," said Ward.

Emeric put his hand on the telegraph key and sent the spread fingers up the river, two miles, to the hut on the bend, and waited. The answer came back the same. The flame, three fast. Then the boat, over and over, so many times that the man on the needle stopped counting and looked at Ward.

"No bell," Ward said.

Emeric looked at him.

"If they were on the river, he would send the bell. Come at once. He has not sent it." Ward sat down on the stool and began to put his boots on. "Fires. Boats. Not moving." He pulled the second boot on and stood up. "His man will be on the road. Wait for him."



The second man from the hut came down the river road at a run in the middle of the morning, with the crossbow on his back, and had to be given water before he could make any sense.

The wall-crew man had seen smoke at first light, he said, a long way up, over the trees on the far bank, past where the hut could see. More smoke than a farm made. So he had left the key with the second man, and gone up along the near bank through the woods on his own, an hour and more, and lain in the reeds, and looked across. There was an old landing on the far side up there that nobody had used since before the plague. It was being used now. Barges, the big flat ore barges off the Grayrock run, a dozen or more, drawn up on the mud. Men round fires. Horses. He had not been able to count the men, so he had counted the fires, the way Ward had told him to in the spring, and there were forty, about. Then he had come back and sent what he had, the only way he had to send it.

Emeric thought of a small fire on the far bank in March, far up past the bend, going out while he looked at it.

"He went up on his own," Ward said.

"Yes, sir."

"Leaving the key."

"With me, sir."

Ward was quiet. "Tell him that was right," he said. "And tell him if he does it again I will have him back on the wall." He turned to Emeric. "Forty fires. Five or six to a fire. Two hundred, and a little over, and the knights among them."

"When?"

"Not by day. We would see them for a mile from the bend and another from the wall. In the dark, because he thinks the dark is his." Ward looked out of the window at the river, high and brown and empty under a clear sky. "Tonight, if he is in a hurry. Tomorrow, if he wants his men rested. Either way, the dock quarter comes in now."



Doran rang the bell at noon. Everybody in Kaldo knew what it meant by then; it had been said at every well and every pay trestle for a fortnight.

The dock quarter came over the east bridge all afternoon. Emeric stood at the bridge foot inside the water gate and watched it come, slowly, a family at a time, and more particular about it than he would have guessed. People brought what they could carry and then went back for what they could not. A man came across with a door on his back, his own front door, off its pins, because, he told the gate crew, it had taken him a winter to hang it straight and nobody was going to burn it on him. A woman pushed a handcart with a cradle in it and no baby, and the baby on her hip. Two boys drove four goats across in a body, and the goats stopped in the middle of the bridge and would not go on until one of the gate crew picked up the leader bodily and carried it. Old men came with their nets rolled on their shoulders and nothing else.

Ferra sat at a trestle just inside the gate with her book and the list of empty houses, and every family that came through stopped in front of her and was given a number, and the number was a house, and the house had a door. She did not look up much. When a woman cried at the trestle, Ferra gave her the number anyway, and a second one, a little lower down the street, for her sister.

The children had their slates. Sela had said they would, and they did, clutched against their chests or hung round their necks on string, and Emeric watched them come through the gate and turn off up the lanes without being told, each to a door they had seen once. Sela stood at the top of the first lane with Arthur and ticked them off on her own slate as they passed.

The boy from the net lofts did not go to his house. He stood at the top of the lane by Sela with his slate and watched the gate, and every time a woman came through who was not his mother his face did something. Sela did not send him on. She stood beside him and went on ticking.

His mother came over in the last of the afternoon, with a bundle of netting and a stewpot, and he went down the lane to her at a walk, holding his slate out so that she could read the number, and did not run, though anyone could see that he wanted to.

Not everybody came. Ferra had the count by dark: seven hundred and ninety-one over the bridge, and nine who had stayed. A few old men in the net lofts who had lived through the plague in them and did not see why they should leave them for anything smaller. A woman with a cow that would not set foot on the bridge, and who would not leave the cow. Ward said what he had said at Ferra's table, that the ones who would not come could not be made, and he would not send men to drag them, and he did not.

Bram did not come either.

He sent the two boys home across the bridge at four, with a message for Ferra that was mostly about the boys' supper, and stayed in bay three with his hammer and his wheel and the stack of plate that was going to be the last of the breastplates. Emeric went over to tell him the bridge would be held from the town side once it was dark, and Bram listened, and said he knew where the bridge was, and went on working.



Tess was at the powerhouse when he got back across.

She had her tool roll, and a box of spare brushes she had cut from the copper strip that week, and her bench book, and she had set them all down inside the door beside Harl's stool, and she was not leaving. She said so before he could ask.

"No," Emeric said.

"A brush goes in the night and the lamps go out." She said it to the machine, not to him. "Who changes it? Harl doesn't know how. The six don't know which end is which. You'll be over the bridge." She waited. "You said it at Ferra's table. The lamps are the defense. Bram told me."

The six men Ward had chosen were in the yard behind the powerhouse with their bows, pretending not to listen. Harl sat on his stool between his buckets with his hands on his knees and looked at the machine.

"She's useful," Harl said.

It was the most he had said to Emeric in a week. Emeric looked at the dynamo humming on its bed, and the river door barred behind it, and the six men, and Tess with her tool roll under her arm, and could not find the hole in her argument, because it was his argument.

"Stay behind the machine," he said. "Whatever happens. Keep it between you and the door."

She nodded, once, and went to put her tools where she could reach them in the dark.



That evening he wrote the page he had been putting off since April.

He wrote it at the table in his own room, by the lamp, and threw the first sheet away because it said too much. The second was one side of one sheet. The wage held: the households' pay, for as long as there was anything to pay it from. The school opened again as soon as there was a roof to open it under. Ferra's book was the barony's book, and no one else's hand went in it. The dynamo was Tess's, and the bench book with it. Hale was to write to the crown, plainly, and say what had happened, and send it by the road, and then by the river, and then by the road again. There was nothing on the page about himself.

He folded it, and sealed it, and locked it in the drawer with Paxton's letter, and put the key back on its cord round his neck.

Ward was in the gate yard when he came down. Emeric started to tell him about the drawer.

"I know where the key is," Ward said, and that was the end of that.



The church was lit.

He saw it from the top of the lower ward on his way down to the wall, every window yellow on the dark shoulder of the inner ward, and he went along the lane toward it without meaning to, and stopped short of the door.

Doran had put the box out at the start of May, on a trestle by the font, without saying anything about it in church or anywhere else: a plain deal box with a lid, the coffin-maker's plainest, cut down to the size of a loaf, with a slot in the lid. A man who could write his letters now, from the evening class, could write one to whoever he liked, and seal it, and put it in the slot, and Doran would keep it. If the man came back, he could have it again. If he did not, Doran would carry it to whoever it was for, himself, and read it to them if they could not read, and then forget it.

Tonight the box was open on the trestle, and there was a line of men in front of it. Doran was taking the letters out, one at a time, and giving each man back his own, and a stick of wax, and the candle. The men took their letters to the benches and broke the seals and bent over them. They were adding to them. Now that there was something real to add.

Emeric stood back in the dark of the lane where the light from the door did not reach.

Declan was on the front bench, writing with his whole arm, as if the pen weighed more than the hammer Bram had hit him with. Behind him one of the gate crew had a slate and was working a word out on it first, in chalk, his lips moving, before he would let it near the paper. And at the end of the second bench, by himself, with his back half turned to the room and his left arm curled round his page so that nobody could see it, sat Osric, who had complained about everything Emeric had ever asked of him, writing very slowly, with the tip of his tongue between his teeth.

Doran saw him when the third man had gone up to the box, and came out onto the step, and stood beside him in the dark, with his hands folded.

"They're adding things," Emeric said.

"So I'm told." Doran looked back in through the door at the benches. "I don't read them. That's what the box is for."

He went back in. Emeric went on down to the wall.



It was past midnight when he came into the exchange. Nothing had come down the river. The needle had sent all well at dark, one slow tick, and nothing since, and Ward had stood half the garrison down to sleep on the wall in their iron.

Ferra was still at her trestle, under one lamp, with the book. The rest of the long room was dark, and full of the sacks of grain along the back wall, and smelled of them.

"Seven hundred and ninety-one," she said, without looking up. "I know where every one of them is sleeping."

"And the nine?"

"Their names are here." She turned the book a little toward him, and he did not look at it. "And Bram."

"And Bram."

She went back to the page. She had written the whole dock quarter into the town that day, house by house, and she was going over it again, family by family, in the same small hand, and he could not see that she was correcting anything. Her pen went down the column and stopped, and went on.

"Where will you be?" she said.

"Wherever it starts."

She wrote something. He thought it was a number, but he could not see what.

"Ward says once the bridge is held, it's held," she said.

"Ward will hold it."

"Yes."

That was all she said about it. She did not tell him to be careful. He waited for it, and it did not come, and he understood that it was not going to. He could feel the cord of the key against his collarbone, under his shirt. He had a sentence ready about the drawer, and what was in it, and where the key was. He did not say it either.

The lamp hissed. Somewhere out in the town a dog barked, and stopped, and another answered it from across the river, from the empty dock quarter, where there should not have been any dogs left.

"Ferra," he said.

She looked up.

He did not know what came next. He had said her name, and she was looking at him across the trestle in the lamplight with her pen still in her hand and a smudge of ink on the side of her finger, and the loose strand of red that always came down by evening was down, and he did not know what he had meant to say after it, or he did, and there was no way to get from where he was standing to the end of it.

She waited.

"Get some sleep," he said.

"After the count." She looked down at the page, and then up again. "Emeric."

He waited, as she had.

She put the pen down. Then she stood up and came round the end of the trestle, and reached up, and turned his collar down where it had folded under on one side, and smoothed it flat with two fingers. Her fingers touched the cord of the key, under the cloth. She did not ask what it was.

"It was turned under," she said.

"Thank you."

She went back round the trestle and sat down and picked up the pen.

He went back up to the wall. Behind him the lamp burned in the exchange until it was light.



Nothing came down the river that night.

At dawn the needle in the line room swung once, slowly, to the left, and back. Ward was on the wall above the river tower, where he had been all night, looking at the bend.

"Tonight," he said.
