Chapter 300 - Tally

Keller's letter came south on the twenty-second of April, eight days after the vote, and it was the shortest thing he had ever sent them.

It came the way the copy of the charge had come in December, in a satchel on the knees of the same young clerk, who had grown out of his chilblains and into a better coat and who said, at the study door, before anybody could ask, that Master Keller had told him he was to wait for a receipt and to eat whatever Maren gave him. Ferra wrote the receipt. Maren gave him eggs.

Inside the oiled cloth there was one sheet in Keller's hand, and under it a second sheet ruled in two columns by a clerk, and nothing else.

The six read it at seven that evening at the real table in the wine store, because Ferra said it was not a thing for the study, where anybody could knock. Bram came straight from the works with his apron on, because he had not been home since the morning Tess went under and did not mean to go until he was made to. Saban came in a dry coat from the night train. Armand came down the stair three steps at a time, as he did everything now, and sat at the fire end with his long legs out under the table and his robe's hem a hand above his ankles, and said that he did not need the rug and would they please stop looking at him as if he did.

Ferra read it out.

Keller said that the council had found against the king by two parts in three, exactly, forty-three to twenty-one, and that it would not have done so by forty-two. That he had not been able to see, on the morning of the fourteenth, any reason for Lord Grier to have voted as he did, since Lord Grier had voted against every motion of the Lieutenant's for six years, generally from the back of the benches and generally drunk. That Lord Grier had been sober on the fourteenth. And that Keller, who was a magistrate and not a man for guessing, had since gone to the trouble of finding out what Lord Grier owed, which was a matter of record in four courts, and to whom, and when it had been paid; and he enclosed the result for the Chancellor's interest, since he could make no use of it himself.

She laid the second sheet on the table and turned it so that they could all read it.

On the left, in the clerk's hand, the vote: forty-three, and under it the names, and halfway down, underlined once, Grier. On the right, his debts. Three lending houses, named. The vintners' company. Two old earls, for horses. His own steward, four years of wages. A saddler in the upper market, eleven Gold Rand, owed since the winter of year nine. Beside every line a date, and the date was the same week: paid in full, and the quittances sealed and delivered to Lord Grier's door by a rider nobody in the street could name, on the seventh of April. Seven days before the vote.

At the foot Keller had written, in his own hand, one line more.

Somebody paid the saddler. Nobody pays a saddler. K.

"He means he can't prove it in a court." Armand had his hands folded on his stomach, and had shut his eyes, which he did now when he was thinking hard, as if his new face got in the way. "He can see it as well as we can. A man's debts all paid in one week by nobody, and the man changes his vote the week after. Any child could see it. A court needs to see who paid, on paper, with a name on it, and he hasn't got it." He opened his eyes. "He's sent it to us because he knows we have people who can find paper."

Ward had not touched the sheet. He sat with his hands flat on the oak, and Emeric felt his note, the wall with something behind it, go very still and stay there.

"Forty-two," said Ward. "Without him it is forty-two."

"And forty-two isn't two in three," said Ferra. "Forty-two is a king who kept his oath, and goes home to his own rooms, and Keller writes it down." She put her finger on the underlined name. "That's what eleven Gold Rand to a saddler buys."

"Rather more than eleven," said Saban.



He went to the tithe house on the twenty-fourth, at closing, alone.

Vantic had the sheet already. He saw it on her desk under her gloved hand as soon as the clerk let him in: not Keller's, a copy of it in Ferra's large plain hand, and he understood that Ferra had walked across the square that morning before the bank opened, and that the two of them had sat at this desk for some time, and that neither of them had thought it necessary to mention this to him.

"Your Grace."

"You know who bought the paper."

"Sit down." She waited until he had. "Yes."

"Three houses held Lord Grier's paper," she said. "Hendry's, Cowle's, and Pellam Brothers. They are small houses and they had been trying to sell it for two years and nobody would buy it, because nobody buys a drunk's paper at any price you could say aloud. In the first week of April all three of them sold it, at full face, in one morning, to one buyer. The buyer wrote it off the same afternoon. In full. Every copper." She folded her hands. "The buyer was the crown book."

He had known. He had known since the wine store, since Saban's voice saying it in June of the year before, three doors down from the House's capital office. He found that knowing it and hearing it in her flat voice in her own bank were not the same thing.

"Tullio's."

"My brother's book. Which was the House's book until last June, and which took a great deal of money on deposit in March from a household that has never before deposited a copper with anybody, and held it against purchase, and purchased." She did not look away. "The household's under-purser paid it in himself. His name is in the book. The mark that bought the paper is my brother's private mark. I have known it since I was eleven years old. He taught me to make it, as a game, so that I could leave notes on his desk that the clerks couldn't read."

"Keller could use that," he said.

"Keller could. Once." She let him hear it. "And then every borrower in the kingdom would know that the House of Ducatis opens its books to a magistrate when its head has a quarrel with her brother. Every lord who banks with us. Every carter with a Silver in the long room. Every one of the three hundred parishes. The House is its books, Your Grace. A house that hands one man's account to a court to win an argument is a house nobody sane will ever lend through again; and the ones who lend through it anyway will be the ones who mean to use it the same way." She laid her gloved hand on Ferra's copy. "I'll give you the route. Who paid, and how, and through which hands. On one condition."

"That it never goes to any court."

"That it never goes to any court. Nor to the council. Nor into a charge, nor an answer, nor a letter to Keller, nor the Almanac, nor anything the duchy prints. Not a copy. Not a line of it." She did not raise her voice. "You may know it. Your table may know it. You may act on it however you like, because a man may act on anything he knows. But what's in my brother's book stays in my brother's book. If it ever comes out of it, by you, I will say so in public, and close the House's door in Kaldo, and you will have to find somebody else to hold your town's money, and there isn't anybody."

"Agreed," he said.

She looked at him a moment longer. Then she opened the drawer of her desk, took out a single sheet folded in three, and held it across the rail.

"The under-purser's name is Rudd," she said. "That's what the book can tell you. It can't tell you who took the money to Grier's door, or what was said when it got there, or who told Grier which way to vote. Paper doesn't know that." She let go of the sheet. "Grier does."



Maeve's letter came on the twenty-sixth, in the evening, by a man in grey who had walked up the castle hill from the night train and would not give his name at the gate, only hers.

Emeric felt him before the porter knocked: a quiet even note, a little tired, not frightened of anything. He was perhaps forty, with a plain face and a grey coat with no badge on it and boots that had walked a great deal, and he stood in the study door with his hat in his hands and held out a folded paper sealed in plain grey wax with no device at all.

"From the lady," he said. "Into your hand, Your Grace. I'm to take back anything you want to send. I'll not read it."

It was four lines, in Maeve's hand, which he had not seen since a letter about a pony when he was ten and knew at once, large and quick and leaning forward as if it were late for something.

The council found against Father by one voice, and it was Grier's, and you know what Grier is.

They've put him in the close, in the Church's keeping, two rooms and a guard on the stair, and they read what goes in and out.

I've been to see him. He told me to tell you nothing. I'm telling you this.

I am asking you to bring him out. M.

He read it twice, and then gave it to Ferra, who read it once and put it face down on the table.

"Tell her," Emeric said to the man in grey, and stopped.

The man waited.

"Tell her yes," he said.

The man nodded, once, as if that was the answer he had been sent for, and put his hat on, and went down the stair; and Emeric felt him go out of the gate and down the hill at a walking pace, steady, toward the station and the five o'clock, and did not take his sense off him until he was past the brickfield.



"No," said Ward.

It was the next morning, at the real table, and Ward said it before Emeric had finished the sentence, as he had said it in October.

"I haven't said what."

"You are going to say Grier. You are going to say you will go north and read him." Ward's hands were flat on the oak. "His Majesty's letter said do not come north. Not you. Not the captain. Not the old man. Until he sends for you. He has not sent."

"He can't send. They read his letters."

"Then he has not sent." Ward did not move. "It is his order. I have carried his orders for thirty years."

"And they've moved him." Emeric put Maeve's four lines on the table, face up, next to Keller's sheet and Vantic's. "He took himself into the council's keeping so nobody would ride south. He did that. It was his and he was right to. He didn't put himself in the close. He didn't give himself to the Church. They did that, after a vote that was bought, with a lord whose saddler got paid." He heard his own voice and did not lower it. "His order was for a hearing he could win. He lost it to a bribe. I'm not going to sit in Kaldo keeping the books honestly while he sits in two rooms in the close with a guard on the stair because of eleven Gold Rand."

Ward looked at the wall.

Emeric knew that look; he had known it for twenty-seven years. It was the look Ward gave the wall when he meant to be disagreed with, and meant to be found right later, and meant to file it where Emeric would find it if he was wrong.

"Vantic's route stops at the money. She said so. Rudd paid it in, the book bought the paper. It doesn't say who carried it to Grier, or what he was told." Emeric turned Vantic's sheet over. "Grier knows. And we can't use the book. Not in court, not ever, not a line of it. I gave my word." He looked round the table. "But we can use a man. If Grier tells us who came to his door, that's a man, not a book. Saban can find a man. Keller can put a man in a witness box."

"And if he doesn't," said Armand, "you'll read him anyway."

"No." He said it at once, and meant it, and saw Armand hear that he meant it. "Never forced. Not since Marrow. If he says no, I'll come home with nothing and we'll find another way. If he says yes, I'll read him, given, the way I read the deserter. That's all."

"You," said Ward. "Not Saban."

"Saban can't read. Nobody else at this table can read a man who isn't a delver. I have to be in the room with him." Emeric looked at him. "So it can't be loud. It can't be a ship on his lawn and a horn and a lit yard. It can't be what the Isle was." He heard Imani breathe in, and did not look at her. "Nobody can know we were there. Not his watchmen, not his household, not the capital. Nobody hears a thing. Nobody sees a face."

Ward was still looking at the wall.

"Then you are not going in first," he said.

It was not a yes. It was, Emeric understood after a moment, the first condition of one; and round the table everybody else understood it too, and nobody said so, because if anybody had said so Ward would have taken it back.



Bram ran the dust mill for the first time on the twenty-eighth, on South Sentinel, and put out every light on the works shore.

He had built it over the winter in the Dust House, the bunker shed at the far end of the shore, half sunk in the dunes with sandbags to its eaves, as far from Fletcher's slips as the shore went; and he had built it, Emeric thought, the way he had once built the first furnace, which was to say entirely, and alone, and refusing help until the day it was ready and then refusing to be thanked. Since the morning his younger daughter swallowed her stone by the tree he had not been home. He had gone down to the secret dock on the K Endeavor's evening run with a bag and his big hammer, and taken a cot in the Dust House's lean-to, and written to Ferra that he would come back when there was something to come back for.

The vacuum drain stood in the middle of the bunker on its own footings: an iron chamber the size of a pantry, its walls as thick as a man's hand, studded with bolts, with a round door on hinges like a strongroom's. Through its walls, in tubes, the copper drain plates came out to a bench of liquid-mana flasks in a rack, Bram's coupling, so that whatever came off the stones inside would be caught and not wasted. On its roof a small iron cup was sealed in with a screw ram above it, and a motor beside the ram. On its far side a fat iron pipe went out to a fan in a housing, and from the fan into a tall iron cone like an upside-down bell, and from the bottom of the cone into a hanging row of long cloth sleeves, tied off at their feet into tins.

"The cyclone separator," Emeric said, putting his hand on the cone, because he had drawn it for Bram in January off a cement works in a book on the other side of everything, and Bram had built it without once asking what the word meant.

Inside the chamber, on four iron trays, lay forty spent fire stones from the charging room's reject drawer and the Forge's offcuts, all one kind, sorted by Ferra's tins.

The knock stone went into the cup on the roof: a lamp stone, cheap, one of the edge stones Saban bought by the hundred from the east that would burst if you pushed them. Bram ran the motor himself. The screw ram came down on the cup with a long whine and a sound inside the iron like a cork pulled out of a bottle a long way off, and Emeric, standing behind the sandbags with his sense open, felt the chamber go dead.

Not quiet. Dead. A hole the shape of a pantry in the middle of the bunker, with nothing in it at all.

And then the hole was not the shape of a pantry. It went out through the iron walls as if they were paper. It went out through the bunker's walls and the sandbags and the dunes, and across the works shore in a breath; and down the shore, three hundred yards off, the light stones over Fletcher's slips went out one after another in a row like a hand run along a line of candles, while the battery lamps in the lean-to burned on, small and yellow and suddenly the only lights on the shore, and the lamp stones in the bunker went black in their brackets, and the whole shore was dark under the noon sun except for the sun.

Emeric felt his own mana go thin in him, like the floor dropping an inch under a man on a stair; and then the island's air came back in round him slowly from the sea side, and the slips' lamps came up one by one, and somewhere down the shore a rigger was swearing very fluently about a crane.

Inside the chamber, when Bram opened the door with a bar, the forty stones lay on their trays exactly as they had gone in, grey and dull and whole, and not one of them was dust.

Bram looked at them for some time.

"Walls," he said.

"The walls are thick."

"Walls is thin." He put his hand flat on the iron. "Dense walls fill a dead patch quick, you said. These is a hand. It went straight through a hand. It went through the dune." He took his hand off. "It wants three. And a jacket of sand between, packed. And the cup wants to be inside the iron, not on top of it, so it's dead from the middle out and not the roof down." He was already walking to the lean-to for his book. "The stones didn't go because the dead patch didn't stay long enough to drain them. It ran out the walls before it could do the work. Thin bucket. You can't boil water in a sieve."



On Thursday it held.

He had built the second skin round the first in two days, with four of Fletcher's welders working in shifts and himself never off the floor; a second iron box round the first, a hand off it, and a third round that, and every gap between rammed full of wet sand by apprentices with poles. The cup was in the middle now, down a sleeved hole through all three walls, with the ram's rod coming in through a gland.

The ram came down. The cork popped, very faint, very far off. Emeric felt the dead patch open in the middle of the chamber like a held breath; and push at the inner wall; and push; and stop.

The slips' lamps stayed lit. The battery lamps stayed lit. The lamp stones in their brackets on the bunker wall did not so much as flicker.

Inside the iron, where he could not see, he felt the forty stones let go. Not at once; one, and then three, and then all of them together, like a field of wheat going over in a wind, every faint stubborn thread of what was left in them coming away through the copper and out along the tubes to the flask rack, which began, softly, to glow blue. And then where each one had been there was, for the length of a breath, a smaller hole inside the big one; forty little dead pockets the size of a fist, one on each tray, deepening the dark; and then nothing, and the fan's note changed.

Bram had his hand on the fan's switch. He looked at Emeric.

"They've gone," Emeric said.

The fan ran four minutes. When it stopped, and the vents had let the liquid mana's breath into the chamber with a blue fog like a cold morning, and the door came open, the trays were bare iron. In the tins under the cloth sleeves there was a grey powder finer than flour, a little warm, that slid in the tin when Bram tipped it like water; and in the cone's tin, more.

Bram took a pinch of it between his thumb and finger, and rubbed it, and looked at the grey on his skin.

"Forty," he said. "In four minutes." He wiped his thumb on his apron, and there was a long grey streak down the leather. "The girl did it with a mortar. Forty stones, by hand, at her bench, with a pestle. Took her a month." He went to the bench where his book was, and wrote in it, slowly, with his tongue in the corner of his mouth; and then he stood with his hand flat on the open page and did not write anything else for a while, and Emeric went outside so that he would not have to be seen.

The book was Tess's. It was the Dust House log, with the warm cube in it and her small quick hand on every page; and under her last entry, which said, If it matters while I'm out, run it, Bram had written in his own big hand the date and the run and the count and the time, and then, because there was room, Done.



Imani shut it on the third of May.

She came out on the K Endeavor's Monday run with Bettany and a case, because Ferra had read her the mill's figures at supper and she had put her fork down at the word flour. She stood in the Dust House in a mask of her own, the ward's cloth and wire, and looked at the cloth sleeves hanging from the cone, and at the grey on every flat surface in the bunker, the beams and the bench and the lid of the flask rack, fine as frost; and at the six men of the mill crew, who had grey in their eyebrows and the creases of their necks and round their nostrils, and two of whom were coughing.

"Two of them are coughing," said Imani to Bram. "Since Friday. Shut it."

"It works."

"I know it works. I've read the sheet. Forty in four minutes and three runs a day." She did not raise her voice; she used the voice she used on the ward to a man who wanted to walk on a broken leg, which was worse. "I've seen what this does. Not this dust. Quarry dust, and the glass-cutters' in the old town before the plague, and the men who dressed millstones. You breathe a powder this fine and it doesn't come out. It goes to the bottom of the lung and sits. It doesn't hurt for ten years. Then a man can't climb his own stair, and then he can't breathe lying down, and nothing I have in the ward takes it out again." She looked round at the six grey faces. "I'm not having it. Not in my lifetime, and not on men I've got the names of."

The crew looked at Bram. Bram looked at the cone.

"What do you want," he said.

"Masks. Proper ones, on every man in this shed, every minute the fan's running and an hour after. Abloh's, with the same cloth as your bags, since it catches the dust in the bags it'll catch it at a man's mouth. Two of them cough, so two of them are off this shed and on the slips and in my book once a month for a year." She was counting on her fingers through her glove. "And it runs wet. You said the lightning dust has to be milled wet anyway. All of it wet, then. A spray in the cone, and the tins sealed before they come out of the shed. I'll want a sheet on the door. I'll write it."

Bram stood with his big hands hanging at his sides and his apron streaked grey down the front where he had wiped his thumb on it every hour for a week. Emeric, by the door, saw him look at the grey on his own forearms as if he had not seen it before.

"Right," said Bram.

He pulled the fan's switch himself, and chalked SHUT on the chamber's door in letters a foot high, and under it, smaller, I. SAYS.

"Ten days," he said. "For the spray and the bags. Abloh can have the masks done in three if he stops sulking about the tents."

"I'll ask him nicely," said Imani, and took her own mask down for the first time since she had come in, and Emeric saw that she had been holding her breath behind it more than she was letting anybody see.



Emeric built the grip boots in the drawing office in the week the mill stood shut, because he had to, and because, he admitted to himself on the second night, it was a better week to be building a thing than to be thinking about two rooms in the close with a guard on the stair.

The stealth suits were already built. They had been built in the skip with the MK3, three of them, off Abloh's patterns and Mira's measurements and his own drawings, after the Isle: close black wool over a fine iron mesh, knees and shins plated and nothing else, the held stones at the collarbones and the hips in shuttered ports, a hood over a narrow-visored helm, a slim chute between the shoulders, the shock batons and the throwing knives in their racks. They had hung on their frames in bay three for a year and been put on twice, for fitting, and never once flown. He had made them for the next time, after a horn on the third platform at the Isle and a lit yard and a farm boy with a spear in his side; so that the next time nobody would hear a horn.

What they could not do was walk on a wall.

He could. He had found the planted step at his own window the night his stone week ended, and taught Armand it in February on the keep tower for the castle's entertainment; and it was the thing he wanted for Grier's house more than any other, because Grier's house had a wall and a gate passage and a watch on both and eaves you could cross under. But the planted step was two things at once, an outward cast on your own body and an inward thread driven down through your soles into the stone, and only he held both. The knights could cast, a little, since Ward had shown them they could; but none of them could cast like that, through their feet, with their weight in it. And the stealth suit's mesh, which made a man's note as faint to any sense as a cat's, would not let a cast through it anyway.

So the stone had to do it, and the stone had to be outside the mesh.

He drew it at the long table in the drawing office with the K Amara's steady drawings pinned on the board behind him, because the answer was in them and had been since December; and it came out so plain that he sat for a while with the pencil in his hand, a little ashamed of how long it had taken.

A gravity stone the size of a thumbnail, flat, set into the sole of each boot under a thin leather tread, outside the mesh. Another in the palm of each glove. Written by him, with Ottoline's die for the setting, to do one thing: push toward whatever it was pressed against. And that was all. No steady. No gimbal. Nothing to read where still was. When a sole was flat on a wall, the wall was still, and the stone was against it, and it pushed toward it the way a bolted stone pushed toward the wall it was bolted to. When the sole came off, there was nothing still under it, and the stone went quiet, as a held stone went quiet when its steady stopped; and it would not push the man toward the open air, because there was nothing there to push toward.

The held stones in the suit's ports would make him light. The boot stones would make the wall his floor. And the knight would feed them both the way a knight fed every stone he wore, by touch, without a glyph, through his band, which was why it would work for them and not need him at all.

He gave the drawings to Bram's works on the fifth with a note. The boots came back on the eighth: soft black leather to the calf, the stones under the tread showing only as a faint smoothness on the sole like a worn place, the palms of the gloves the same. He tried a pair on at the drawing office's end wall with nobody there but the draughtsmen's cat, and stood on the wall for a count of twenty, and came down, and went to find the three.



He showed them on the ninth, at dusk, in the inner ward, against the north face of the keep where nothing faced it but the dome's dark colonnade.

They came in the stealth suits, which they had put on in bay three without being told twice, and stood in a row on the flags with their hoods down and their helms under their arms: Arthur, tall and quiet, already looking at the wall; Mira, a head shorter, looking at Emeric; and Bren, looking at the boots.

"Under your heel and the ball of your foot. And in your palms." Emeric held up his own gloved hand. "You know how the held stones work. Ask Mira; she rated the plate."

"They read where still is from the steady," said Mira. "So they can push you sideways. A stone needs something still to push from. If it's not the ground it's the steady."

"Or a wall," said Emeric. "These have no steady. They don't need one. When your foot's on the wall, the wall's what's still. Lift your foot and that stone stops. That's all there is to it."

"That's all," said Bren.

"That's all of how it works. It isn't all of how you do it." He turned and set one foot against the keep's north face, at knee height, and let the held stones in his own borrowed suit take his weight off, and put the boot stone into the wall, and stepped up onto it.

The ward turned. It always did, the first time, even for him; the wall came round underneath him and became the floor, and the flags became a wall at his back, and the dome's colonnade hung off to his left like a cliff. His hood fell sideways. A coin he had not known was in his pocket slid out and dropped past his boots, straight down, to the real ground, and rang on the flags.

He walked up the keep's face three paces and stood there, level, looking down at them along his own body, with the sky behind his head and the three of them standing on a wall that went up to the dark.

"It's the balance," Emeric said. "Not enough in the stone and you peel off. Too much and you crack the wall. Feed it the way you feed your lift on the deck, a little at a time, and feel where it sits in your gut, and don't think about it. If you think about it you'll fall." He walked down again and stepped off onto the flags, and picked up his coin. "Arthur."



It took a week of nights.

They did it after dark, because the boots glowed faintly at the soles when they were working, gravity's violet, and because Ward said a thing you would use at night you would learn at night. The castle's inner walls first, where the ward was paved and a fall was a short one onto flags with Imani's straw sacks laid along the foot; then the underside of the keep's great arch over the gate passage, where the old stone vaulted twenty feet up over the cobbles and the lamps were out; then the rafters of the old mill shed by the river, Harl's shed, where the beams went across forty feet in the dark under a slate roof and the floor below was water.

Bren fell off the first wall eleven times on the first night, which he counted aloud.

He fell off it the first way, by not giving the stone enough: he would get both feet on, and stand, and grin, and then think about it, and his feet would slide down the stone with a sound like a man sliding down a door, and he would sit down very suddenly in Imani's straw. He said it was like standing on ice. He said it was like standing on ice that wanted you to fall over. On the second night he fixed that, by giving it too much.

Emeric was across the ward with Arthur when he heard it: a crack like a slate dropped on a flag, and a patter like gravel, and Bren's voice saying, "Oh, no."

He had gone up the inside of the old gate passage and out onto its vault, upside down, his hands and feet flat on the arch, hanging over the cobbles like a fly on a ceiling; and he had pressed. Round his left boot and both palms the plaster of the vault, which had been on it since Wexler's grandfather, had gone into a star of cracks a yard across; and as Emeric watched, the star let go, all at once, a piece the size of a door, and came down past Bren onto the cobbles in a shower of white, and Bren came down with it, on the chute's harness and Arthur's arm, because Arthur had been under him.

They stood in the white dust and looked up. Where the plaster had been there was bare stone, and in the bare stone, very faint, the print of a sole and two palms pressed into it, a hair deep.

"You pressed it into the rock," said Mira, from the wall behind them, where she had been standing sideways for a quarter of an hour.

"I didn't mean to."

"Nobody means to press a hand into a rock, Bren."

"I wanted to be sure I'd stay on."

"You stayed on," said Arthur. "The ceiling didn't."



Mira was the natural, and nobody had expected it, Mira least.

She had gone up the keep wall on the first night as carefully as a woman going up a ladder in the dark, foot and foot and hand, and stood there, and come down; and gone up again, and stood longer; and the third time she went up she did not stop. She walked up the north face of the keep to the string course under the first windows, and turned, along the wall, level, with the windows going past her shoulder; and came to the corner and went round it, onto the east face, without a pause, as if a corner were a doorway; and on the second night she found the underside of the keep's gate arch and walked the length of it upside down, from one side of the passage to the other, with her hands loose at her sides and her hood hanging down toward the cobbles, the way a woman walks down a corridor in her own house to see who is at the door.

Emeric watched her from the passage's mouth with Arthur.

"She's not feeding it," said Arthur. "Look at her feet."

He looked. Her feet went down on the vault and came up off it the same way every time: flat, quiet, without any of the little shove the others gave, the shove of somebody telling the stone what to do. She gave it nothing she could have named. She simply knew where the vault was the way she knew where the ground was, and her stones knew it with her.

Arthur was slow.

He was slow the way he had always been slow at anything new, which was the way that made Ward hold his tongue. He went up the wall a step and stood and came down, every time, for the whole first night, and never once fell, because he never once went past what he knew; and on the second night he went up two steps; and on the third he walked the north face to the string course and stood there for an hour in the dark, not moving, until Bren asked whether he had fallen asleep. He had not. He was feeling where it sat, he said, afterward. In his gut. Like the duke said.

It was the fifth night before it came to him, and they were at the mill shed by then.

Harl's old shed had no lamps, by Ward's order. The river ran black under its floor through the old race, loud enough to cover a man's breathing; the beams went across in the dark over it, forty feet of old oak, tarred, with the slate roof above them a man's height off; and there was no moon. Arthur went out along the underside of the third beam and did not stop.

Emeric felt him go. His sense was the only light in the shed; he had it open, quiet, along the beams, and he felt Arthur's note go out under the third beam upside down at a slow even walk, and come to the wall, and up the wall onto the underside of the roof's ridge, and along the ridge between the slates and the beams where there was no room to be anything but flat, and down the far wall, and back along the underside of the first, past Bren, who was still sitting on it.

"Was that you?" said Bren, in the dark.

"Where are you?"

"Under you."

Bren said a word Rylan would not have let him say in front of Wren, and nearly fell off.



Ward would not put the boots on.

He said so on the first night, standing at the passage's mouth in his coat with his arms folded; and on the second; and on the third, when Bren offered him his own pair, which were too small. He said a knight belonged on the ground. He said he was the captain, and the captain stood where he could see the whole of the yard. He said, on the fourth night, when Mira came down off the arch and stood in front of him and asked him directly why, that he was too old to stand on a ceiling, and that she could put that in her book.

On the sixth night, at a little after two, when the three had gone to bed in the garrison block and the ward was dark, Emeric was at his own window with the lamp out, doing nothing in particular except not sleeping and thinking about a guard on a stair in the close; and his sense, which was out on the town as it always was at that hour, felt Ward's note come out of the garrison block's door below and cross the ward.

He did not think anything of it. Ward walked the ward at night; he had walked it every night for twelve years. The note went across the flags, slowly, to the north face of the keep, where nothing faced it but the dark colonnade and nobody was awake; and stopped there.

And then it went up.

A step. The note lifted a foot off the flags and stayed there, against the wall, very still, the way a big man stands on a chair he does not trust. A second step. A third. And then a long pause, the length of ten breaths, with Ward's note hanging three feet up the north face of the keep in the dark, level, with every thread of it held so hard and steady that Emeric could feel it from forty yards and two floors up like a tight rope humming; and then, all at once, it came down, not falling but stepping off, onto the flags.

It stood there a while.

Then it went back across the ward to the garrison block, at a walk, and the door shut.

Emeric sat at his window in the dark. After a while he found that he was laughing, silently, with his forehead on the cold glass, and could not have explained to anybody why it made him want to.

In the morning Ward came across the ward at six as he always did, in his coat, and stood at the passage's mouth with his arms folded to watch the three come down off the inside of the arch; and Emeric, beside him, said nothing at all for as long as he could bear it, which was about a minute.

"You were on the north wall," he said. "At two."

Ward did not turn his head.

"I wanted to know what I am asking them to do," he said.

"And?"

"It is unpleasant." Ward watched Mira come down the last of the arch's curve onto the passage wall and down the wall onto the cobbles like a woman coming down a stair. "I stood on it. I did not like it. I will not do it again." He paused. "You were not to be awake."

"Yes," said Ward. "I had forgotten. That is the trouble with you." And then, after another moment, not looking at him: "Three steps. Do not tell the girl."

Across the ward, at the end of the east range, on the third floor, a window stood open in the morning; and in it, with his elbows on the sill and a cup of something in his hands, with his new hair coming in dark brown, almost black, at the roots of the white and his robe too short at the wrist, sat Armand, who had walked up the keep tower and out onto the underside of the parapet in February in front of Ferra and Imani, without any boots at all, and had been called unseemly by nobody but himself. He lifted his cup to Emeric, very slightly, and to Ward, and drank. He had been watching every night. He had a chair by the window for it.



Bren's steady tumbled on the seventh night, over the keep, and it was the best thing that happened all week.

They had gone up the keep to its top that night, all three, for the last of it, in the full suits with the hoods up and the held stones lit in their shuttered ports: up the north face, and round the tower's corner, and out along the underside of the parapet that ran round the leads, sixty feet up over the inner ward, where the stone stood out a yard from the wall on its corbels and there was nothing under it but the dark and, a long way down, the dome's iron ribs going up against the town's glow. That was the job. Grier's house had eaves like it. You went out under them upside down while the watch went by below with a lamp.

Mira went first and walked the north side of the parapet's underside end to end and back, and stood at the corner, and waited.

Arthur went second, slowly, and came to the corner, and stood beside her.

Bren went third, and came out under the parapet on his hands and feet, upside down, at a fair pace; and at the corner, where the corbels turned, he went to swing his body round the angle from the north side onto the east the way he had seen Mira do it, in one long pivot; and did it, he said afterward, a bit quick. His right boot came off the stone first. His left palm came off with it. He hung for half a breath by his left boot alone, upside down, swinging out over sixty feet of dark like a bell on a rope; and to catch himself he threw his weight the other way, hard, the whole of him round the left foot, in a spin that went from his boot through his hips to his chest, where the small steady was turning quietly in its disc over his breast; and the steady, which had been made to ride a man falling off a deck and not a man turning on one heel faster than a weathercock, went over in its gimbal and tumbled.

Emeric felt it go. He had his sense on all three of them; he felt the little rotor in Bren's breast, that had been turning as smoothly as a top, wobble, and lurch, and fall over on its side in its rings and start to spin the wrong way round. And the held stones at Bren's collarbones and hips, which had been reading where still was from it, lost it, all four, at once.

They did what they had been written to do. He had written it himself, first, before anything else, as he had written it in the K Amara's trim stones: if you lose your still, you push up and down and nothing else. They stopped pushing Bren toward the parapet. They stopped pushing him anywhere sideways at all. They went straight back to the one thing a gravity stone does when nobody is telling it anything, which is to hold a man up against the world's own down; and they were still lit, and still fed, and Bren was still feeding them, hard, the way he had been feeding them a breath before to hang on.

His left boot came off the stone.

The boot stone, with nothing under it, went quiet, as it was meant to. And Bren, upside down, lightened past his own weight and with all four held stones pushing straight up as hard as he could make them, rose.

He rose like a cork let go at the bottom of a bucket. Not fast; steadily. Upside down, with his arms and legs out like a starfish, past the parapet's lip, past the leads, past the top of the keep, turning very slowly on his own long axis the way a dropped coat turns in water, into the dark over the inner ward.

"Bren," said Mira.

"I'm all right!" said Bren, from above them. "I'm all right. I'm going up."

"Stop going up."

"I'm trying!" His voice was coming from higher now, and from a little to one side, because he had begun, as he turned, to drift on the night's small wind toward the dome. "It won't. It won't go sideways. Mira, it won't go any way but up. I'm pushing it sideways and it's going up."

"Stop feeding it," said Emeric, at a pitch he hoped would carry and not wake Maren.

"If I stop feeding it I'll fall!"

"You'll come down. You won't fall. It's a lift. It goes up and down. Ease it. A little."

A pause. Bren, forty feet over the leads now and turning, gradually right way up and then gradually past it, seemed to consider this. Emeric felt him ease it; he eased it, being Bren, by about half, and then panicked and fed it back, so that he dropped ten feet in a lurch with a noise like a man who has missed a stair, and then shot up twenty.

Below them, from the ward, a voice said quite clearly, "Is that Bren?" It was one of the garrison's night men, at the gate, with his lamp up; and from the east range, from the third-floor window that was always open now, came a long low sound that Emeric did not at once recognise and then did. Armand was laughing. He was laughing the way he had not been able to laugh for twenty years, with his whole new chest, helplessly, out of an open window into the dark over the ward.

Bren was over the dome now, or very nearly, sixty feet above its crown ring and drifting, turning slowly over and over against the stars with his cape streaming out below him like a pennant on a windless day, calling down that he could see the whole town, that he could see the river, that he could see Maren's kitchen lamp and would somebody please tell her it was not his fault.

Emeric went up after him.

He did not use the suit. He used himself, the old way, lightened, and the hole in the air ahead of him that the night's wind went to fill, and gravity holding him where he wanted to be. It took him about four breaths to come up beside Bren in the dark over the dome, near enough to see, through the narrow visor, a pair of very wide eyes.

"Hello, Highness," said Bren.

"Stop feeding the stones."

"Will you hold on to me?"

"I've got you." He took a fistful of the harness at Bren's back, under the chute, and put his other hand flat on the disc on Bren's breast, where through the glass the little rotor was still wobbling round on its side like a top at the end of its spin, and sent a thread of his sense into the rings, very gently, the way you would put a finger on a spinning plate, and stood it up. It fought him for a moment and then caught, and steadied, and began to turn the right way, smoothly, with a faint hum he could feel through his glove.

And four held stones, at Bren's collar and hips, found where still was, all at once, and woke up again; and Bren, who had been pushing them sideways as hard as he could for a minute and a half to no effect, was suddenly pushing them sideways with every effect in the world, and went off across the sky over the dome like a cat off a hot stove, with Emeric hanging on to his harness.

They came down, eventually, together, by the long way round, over the colonnade and the dark ward and the garrison block's roof, in a series of lurches and sideways slides that Mira, watching from the parapet with Arthur, said afterward looked like two men having an argument in the air about which way the ground was; and landed on the flags of the inner ward in front of the gate, on their feet, more or less, in front of the garrison's night man and his lamp.

The night man looked at them. Then he looked up at the sky over the dome, where there was nothing now but stars, and then at them again.

"Evening, Your Grace," he said. "Evening, Bren."

"Evening, Quill," said Bren, and sat down on the flags rather suddenly, and took his helm off, and lay back flat on the cold stone with his arms out and his face to the sky, and began to laugh.



Ward was in the garrison block's door. Emeric had felt him come out at the first shout, and he had been standing there in the dark ever since.

He came across the flags, slowly, and stood over Bren where he lay, and looked down at him.

"What did the stones do," he said.

Bren stopped laughing, mostly. "They went up, Captain."

"Only up."

"Only up. And down. Not sideways. I pushed and pushed." He lifted his head off the flags. "The steady fell over. I spun too quick at the corner and it fell over in its rings, and then it was like a lift. Like the deck stones. Up and down, and nothing else at all."

"Good," said Ward.

Bren looked at him.

"If you had been over Grier's lawn," said Ward, "with his watch below you, you would have gone up. You would not have gone sideways into his wall, or into his window, or into the ground. You would have gone up, like a fool, in front of the stars, and anybody who looked would have seen a fool going up, and not known what he was." He looked at Emeric. "That is what he wrote it to do. Is it not."

"It's the first thing I wrote," said Emeric. "Before anything. If it loses its still, it can only go up and down. It never runs away with you."

"Then he is right to have written it first." Ward looked down at Bren again. "And now you know what it does when it happens. You found out on a keep, at night, with your friends under you and the duke behind you and Imani's straw on the flags. Not over a lawn." He paused. "You will not spin like that again at a corner."

"No, Captain."

"You will spin like that again at a corner," said Ward, "tomorrow night, eleven times, until you can feel the steady going before it goes, and stop. And then you will not do it again." He turned. "Get up. You are lying on my yard."

Bren got up.

From the window at the end of the east range there was still, faintly, the sound of an old man laughing in a young man's chest, and then a cough, and then the window shut.



"You're not done," Emeric said in the morning.

They were sitting on the steps of the garrison block in the sun with their breakfasts on their knees, the three of them, still in the under-suits with the hoods pushed back, because they had not been to bed long enough to see the point of changing. Bren had a bruise along his jaw from the vault that had gone a good deep plum overnight and that he kept touching to see if it still hurt. It did.

"We can walk on the arch," said Bren. "All of us. Even Arthur's fast now."

"I'm not fast," said Arthur.

"You're faster than you were."

"You can walk on an arch," Emeric said. "Grier's house isn't an arch. It's a wall with stones on it that feel who's near, and a gate with a lamp, and a watch that walks the lawn every quarter of an hour, and inside all of that a room, and inside the room the thing we came for. Nobody said anything for a moment. Mira put her bread down.

"Your house," she said.

"You're always awake," said Bren.

"Yes."

Bren looked at his boots. He had been looking at his boots for a week, one way and another, but this was a different look, and Emeric waited for it.

"That's not what I mean."

"I know what you mean." Emeric looked at the garrison block's door, where Ward had come out with his cup and was standing with his back against the frame and his eyes on the yard, listening the way Ward listened, which was without seeming to. "At the Isle we went in at the gate. Bren shut his mouth.

"Bram's turning you blanks. Wood, with chalk on the point. A chalk mark on a man and he's down."

He went in. The garrison took it as a holiday.

By noon the whole castle knew, which was what Emeric had meant to happen, and by three the whole of the lower town knew, which was not; Maren had told the baker, and the baker had a sister. Ward put twenty men on the night in four reliefs and gave every one of them a shaded lamp and the order, and the men who were not on it came anyway, off duty, with their own lamps, to sit on the wall walk with their feet hanging over and a jar going round and bets being laid on which of the three would be caught first and where. Quill, who had had the gate the night Bren went up over the dome, was holding the book. The odds on Bren were very short.

Ward took the room over the gate passage, the old porter's room with its two slit windows on the ward and one on the town, and put a table in it, and a lamp, and a speaking tube that ran down to the passage where a runner sat. He had told himself, setting it up, that this was the test he wanted for the suits and not for the three. The mesh made a man's note as faint as a cat's. He had built it to do that, and he had stood in the bay at Bram's works and felt the three of them in it from across the floor, and it had worked, at twenty feet, in a quiet room, on people he knew were there. What he did not know was what it did at night, in a whole sleeping town, against the one sense in Kaldo that had been open at that hour every night for years. He would have liked to say that the answer did not matter to him either way. It mattered a good deal.

Ferra was in the study. He could feel her from here, across the ward and up through the east range, the clear bright note he would have known in a crowd of a thousand: sitting, the lamp low, a pen moving. The sun went down behind the west wall at a quarter to nine.



Nothing came for a long time.

He had expected that. He had his sense spread thin and wide over the whole of the town, the way he kept it every night, only listening harder: the garrison in the ward, twenty notes he knew, the knights among them denser and banked like coals; the off-duty men on the wall walk and their jar; Maren in her kitchen, banking the range; Imani on the ward's far side, in the doorway of the infirmary with a basket of straw sacks she had brought out on principle; Armand's window, open, with Armand in it, and a second chair beside him that Emeric could not account for until he felt who was in it, which was Saban. Beyond the castle, the town. Thousands of people going to bed. Emeric was already on the roofs with Mira.



She was very good.

He had known she would be, after the arch; but he had felt her only on walls he had chosen, in a yard he knew, going where he had told her to go. This was hers. She went across the lower town on the roofs from the chandler's to the cooperage to the long roof of the old tithe barn without once coming down to the street; and once not on any roof at all. She went north along the line of the river, away from the castle, as though she were going somewhere else entirely, and twice she stopped dead on a ridge for so long that he lost her among the pigeons, and had to find her again by waiting for the one note under the eaves that moved when the others did not. "Garden corner," he said. "She's in."

"Where now?"

"The east range. Up the outside of it. North face, toward the stair tower." He leaned forward a little, without meaning to, as though the table were the ward. "She's on the stair tower. She's on the roof of the stair tower. She's gone in at the top. The door to the leads."

"It was barred," said Emeric.

The east range's stair went down from the leads through three floors, round and round inside its tower, and at the second floor a passage went off it to the study. Ward had a man on each landing. Emeric felt Mira come down past the first landing without the man there knowing she had passed him; she went down the inside of the tower's outer wall above the turn of the stair, he thought, on her boots and palms, while the man stood under her with his lamp shaded, looking down the steps. At the second landing there were two men. One of them was looking up.

The note of the one looking up went down, suddenly, the way a man's note goes down when he sits, hard; and stayed down; and said nothing. The second man had his back to it. He did not turn round until Mira was past him and into the passage, and then he turned, perhaps because he had heard the first man's breathing change, and put up his lamp, and saw nothing in the passage at all, because Mira was on its ceiling.

"She's in the study passage," said Emeric. "On the ceiling. She's put down the man on the second landing. The other one's in the passage under her, looking the wrong way." He was on his feet. "Twelve feet from the door."

Ward was already at the door of the porter's room, and gone, and Emeric heard him go down the passage stair three at a time, which he had not heard Ward do in years, and out across the ward at a run.

He followed it all from the table, because the rule he had made was that he would not leave the table, and because he did not trust himself not to put his own hand on the latch if he went. Mira's note on the passage ceiling, going toward the study door, slow, slow, stopped, waiting for the man under her to turn his back again. Ferra's note behind the door, writing. Ward's note coming up the east range stair like weather. The man in the passage, turning at the sound of it. Mira, on the ceiling, a yard from the door. Mira dropping, light as a cat off a shelf, onto the flags in front of the door, with her hand going out for the latch.

The study door was opened from the inside before her hand touched it, and Ward stood in it with his lamp up. He had come in at the far door. He had come the other way round, through the old library, the way Ward had walked the castle every night for twelve years.

"Mira," said Ward.

Across the ward, from the open window at the end of the east range, Emeric heard Saban begin to clap, slowly, and Armand hush him.

Ward came back across the ward a while later at a walk. He came up the passage stair at a walk, and into the porter's room, and put his lamp on the table, and sat down.

"She put down the man on the second landing," he said. "He says she came down off the wall onto his shoulders. He says he did not know until she was sitting on him." He moved his lamp an inch to the left. "She asked me how I knew the library door. I told her I have walked this castle every night for twelve years. She said, then that is not a thing I could have known, and it is not fair, and then she said that she would know it next time." He looked at Emeric. "Two."

"Two," said Emeric.

And then, because it had been sitting at the back of him for an hour, unattended, the way a sum sits at the back of a man's head while he does another: "Where's Arthur?"



He did not know.

That was the plain fact, and he made himself look at it. Two had come over the west wall at five to eleven. He had found both. Bren had been on the ground and Mira on the roofs and there had been no third, and he had been so busy with Mira that he had stopped asking where the third was; he had assumed, in so far as he had thought about it at all, that Arthur was still outside the wall on the west meadow, taking his time, being slow, the way Arthur was always slow. Arthur would come at two. At three. Arthur would come when the garrison was tired.

He went out over the town again, wide and thin, looking now for one thing.

The meadow beyond the west wall was empty but for sheep. The wall itself: nothing. The streets, the river front, the lanes: Bren, sitting in the gate lane with a garrison man on either side of him, all three now sharing the jar that somebody had sent down from the wall walk; the night soil cart; two drunks; a woman with a child that would not sleep. The roofs: pigeons. The castle wall, the ward, the garrison, the east range, the stair tower with its men on each landing and the man on the second one sitting where he had been put down, feeling the back of his neck. The keep. The keep's parapet, and in it, under it, the jackdaws.

He knew those jackdaws. They had been there when he came. There were eleven, give or take, roosting in the holes along the parapet's underside where the corbels stood out from the wall; small sleeping notes in a row, every night, all along the north and the east side.

Tonight there were twelve.

The twelfth was not in a hole. It was on the underside of the parapet itself, between two corbels at the north-east corner, flat against the stone, upside down, sixty feet over the ward. It was as faint as the others. It was as still as the others. And he had no idea how long it had been there, he realised, with a slow cold sinking somewhere under his ribs; because he had felt the jackdaws at dusk, when he first opened his sense on the castle, and counted them without counting, the way a man counts the stairs he has gone up every night of his life, and had not given them another thought since.

Arthur had not come over the west wall at five to eleven. Arthur had come over at twenty past ten. Nose down, stop and start, a quick little run sideways along the stones and up the water stair and along the tannery drain. Arthur had spent the whole of a fortnight's afternoons, Emeric remembered now, lying on his back in the works yard watching the draughtsmen's cat, and Bren had asked him why and he had said because it was interesting.

"Fox," said Emeric aloud.

Ward looked at him.

"The fox at twenty past ten. It wasn't a fox. He came in first, alone, an hour ahead of the others, moving the way the cat moves, and I told the board fox." He was on his feet again. "He's on the keep. Under the parapet, at the north-east corner, with the jackdaws. He's been there since before the other two came over the wall. He was waiting for them to be caught."

"Then he is still outside the study," said Ward. "Ferra's door is held. The library door is held. The landings are held. The windows of the study are on the ward and the ward is full of my men with lamps." He did not move from his chair. "He cannot come down from the keep without coming across the ward, and if he crosses the ward he is seen. He has made himself a very cold hour, that is all. Let him have it. At dawn we will put a lamp up and call his name and he will come down with the jackdaws."

It was sound. It was exactly what Emeric would have said. He sat down.

And then Ferra's note, across the ward, did a thing he had never once felt it do in all the years he had been feeling it: it stopped writing in the middle of a stroke, and stayed stopped, the way a person stops when they have been told very firmly not to look up and have just heard something; and then, a moment later, went on writing.

He reached for the keep. Eleven jackdaws, asleep in their holes. The twelfth was not under the parapet any more, and he could not find it anywhere at all.

He was across the ward and up the east range stair and in at the study door before Ward was out of his chair.



Ferra was at the table, with the lamp low and her pen moving and the household book open in front of her at the wheat. She did not look up.

Three brass weights held three corners of the page. The fourth corner was curling up, slowly, the way a page does when it has been held flat a long while and is let go.

Where the fourth weight had been, there was a wooden knife the length of a finger, with a smear of chalk on its point.

The study's window was open. It was always open in May. It looked out over the ward, two floors up, onto the flags where Ward's men stood with their lamps, and up, across the ward's width, at the north-east corner of the keep's parapet; and between the window's sill and the keep's corner, he saw now, there was nothing at all but forty yards of dark air over the men's heads, with every one of their lamps shaded downward onto the flags, and not one of them pointed up.

He had built the shades. He had told Ward to shade the lamps, so the three would not be warned.

"When," he said.

"Just now." Ferra wrote a figure, and checked it against the line above, and wrote another. "While you were all shouting about the library, and after."

"You didn't look up."

"You told me not to." She blotted the line. "I felt the page go. And then there was a sort of draught. And then the knife." She looked at the knife, finally, and then at him, and he saw that she was trying very hard not to laugh and was not going to manage it for much longer. "He said excuse me."

"He what?"

"Very quietly. In my ear. He said excuse me, my lady, and took it." She put her pen down. "Arthur's very polite. I've always liked that about him."

Behind him, in the doorway, Ward had arrived, and stood, and looked at the table and the curling page and the wooden knife, and said nothing at all for a very long time.



Arthur came down off the keep at six, when Ward put a lamp up from the middle of the ward and said his name.

He came down the way he did everything, slowly: out from under the parapet at the north-east corner, round onto the keep's face, down it on his boots and palms at a walk with the jackdaws going up off the parapet around him in a cloud and complaining, and onto the flags. He had been on the underside of the stone since a little after half past ten the night before, apart from two short crossings through the dark over the ward on his held stones, to the study window and back, a man's height above the shaded lamps, and he was stiff, and he did not try to hide it. He came across the ward to where Emeric stood with Ward and put his hand into the front of his suit and took out the brass weight and held it out.

Emeric took it. It was warm from being carried against a man's chest all night.

"Mira's plan," said Arthur.

"I'd guessed."

"Bren loud on the ground, because they'd believe it of Bren." Arthur said it without any smile at all. "Mira close, because you'd have to watch her. Me first and slow, before you were really listening. And then nothing, for as long as it took." He looked down at the weight in Emeric's hand. "She said you'd count the birds at dusk and then stop counting them, because you'd counted them every night for years. She said everybody stops counting the things they always count. She said that's what a habit is."

"She's right."

"She said you'd say that too."

Across the ward Mira and Bren were coming out of the garrison block, Mira with her hood back and her hair in a knot and her face perfectly composed, Bren behind her eating something. The wall walk, which had not gone to bed, saw Arthur standing with the duke and the captain and worked out what it meant, and the roar it set up then was louder than the one for Bren in the gate lane, and went on for longer, and ended with Quill leaning out over the parapet above the gate and shouting down to ask Ward whether the odds were to be paid out on the whole night or only on the catches, because if it was the whole night he was ruined.

Ward did not answer him. Ward was looking at Arthur.

"You were on the stone," he said. "Eight hours."

"Seven and a half, Captain. I came down for the weight."

"You found that window in the dark. Across the ward. With no lamp."

"Through the pane, Captain." Arthur touched the narrow violet slot in his helm. "It's brighter than the moon through it. I could see the lady's figures on the page." Then he looked as though he wished he had not said that, because Ferra's figures were Ferra's.

"In the cold."

"It wasn't so bad. The birds were warm." Arthur hesitated. "One of them went to sleep on my boot."

Ward looked at him a moment longer. Then he turned to Emeric, and said, in the voice he used for things he had decided, "He goes in first at Grier's. Not you. Not the girl. Him." And then, after a pause, to Arthur, in exactly the same voice: "Knights do not creep."

"No, Captain."

"Last night," said Ward, "they did," and went off across the ward to his breakfast.

Emeric stood in the ward with the brass weight in his hand and the jackdaws settling back into the parapet over his head, one by one, complaining. He made himself count them. Eleven. He would count them, he thought, every night from now on, and in a year he would have stopped again, because that was what a habit was, and Mira was right; and the man on Grier's lawn had habits too, and his would be older than Emeric's.

From the window at the end of the east range, Saban called down, quite cheerfully, that he would like to borrow the girl for a month, and Armand, beside him, said that he could not, because Armand had asked first.



Ferra wrote the week down on the night after, at the study table, with the lamp low, while he sat across from her with his feet on the warm floor and did nothing at all.

The mill: shut, ten days, the spray and the bags, Abloh's masks in three, two men off the shed and in Imani's book. The boots: three pairs and three of gloves, and his own, written off against the works. The arch over the gate passage: one piece of plaster the size of a door, not to be replaced, the print to stand. Bren: one steady, tumbled at the north-east corner of the parapet at a quarter to one, righted by hand at a height of about a hundred feet over the dome at about two minutes past, no harm. She asked him the height and he guessed and she wrote guessed after it. The weights: four, one taken from under her hand at about a quarter past one by Arthur, who said excuse me, and returned at six, warm. She did not ask him anything about that one.

And under that, on a line of its own, the date, and the word Grier, and nothing after it, because there was nothing yet to write.

"When," she said.

"When the moon's down. The end of the month. Linus has the maps."

She looked at the word for a while.

"Ward hasn't said yes," she said.

"He said I'm not going in first."

"That isn't yes."

"It's Ward's yes."

Ferra put her pen down, and blotted the page, and closed the book on the word.

"Then you'd better go in last," she said.
