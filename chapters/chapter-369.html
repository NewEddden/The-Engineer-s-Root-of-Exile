Chapter 369 - Light in Glass

The first good thread came off the Forge's draw tower at twenty to four in the afternoon on the fourteenth of October, and Orrin, who had been making glass most of his life and had never once in all that time been surprised by it, stood with the end of it between his finger and thumb and said a word that Tess wrote down.

It didn't look like anything. That was what got him, he said afterward. It looked like a hair. It came down out of the bottom of the furnace in a line so fine he could only see it where the light caught it, pulled from the soft tip of a glass rod as thick as a man's wrist, and it went down through the coating cup and the little curing lamp and onto the drum at the bottom, and the drum turned, and it kept coming. A hair, wound on a reel. A hair a mile long.

"It's two glasses," said Tess, from her stool by the gauge. "Remember. Not one. A core, and a coat of a different glass round it that bends the light less. That's what keeps it in." She had the micrometer on the end of the thread that Orrin was holding. "Five thousandths of an inch, the whole glass. The coat over it, eight. Like he said."

"Like he said," said Orrin. He looked down the tower at Emeric. "You told me that number in June. I thought you were making it up."

"I wasn't."

"I know you weren't. That's what's annoying."

The glass itself was the hard part, and it hadn't been Orrin's. Kaldo's glassworks made good glass, window glass and bottle glass and the lenses Ivo ground, and the vein glass for Amara's loop, which was the best of all of it; and all of it, held up to the light in a block a foot long, was faintly green. It didn't matter in a window. It mattered very much in a thread that light had to go down for a mile, because every bit of green in it was something in the glass that wasn't glass, and every one of those somethings drank a little of the light. Over a yard you'd never know. Over a mile there'd be nothing left at the far end.

So the rod Orrin had drawn from had come out of the Forge, the real Forge, in the cellar under the castle behind the second door, made from sand and the other things that went into glass, declared and weighed, the way the Forge made everything; and the Forge's trade was purity. It didn't put anything in that it hadn't been told to. Orrin had held the rod up to the window when it came up from the cellar in its padded box, and looked down the length of it, and couldn't see it was there.

"Now we find out," said Emeric.



They tried it on the Works floor first, because it was the Works floor, with the whole reel of it laid out in a long loose figure on clean paper down the length of the shed and back, a mile of glass hair in coils, with Wren walking along it in her socks making sure nothing was crossed.

At one end Tess had a lamp: an ordinary electric bulb in a black box, with a shutter in front of it worked by a key, and the end of the thread in a brass collar at the shutter's mouth. At the other end, a mile of glass away and about forty feet across the shed, Ambrose had a cell.

The cell was the new thing. Ambrose had made it in September out of a black mud nobody had wanted, the stuff that settled at the bottom of the copper works' refining tanks and got shovelled out twice a year. There was a grey stuff in that mud that Emeric had known about since the copper works opened and had never had a use for. Selenium, he'd called it, when he'd shown Ambrose; Ambrose had written it on the jar. Put a thin layer of it on an iron plate, and light on it, and a small current came out the other side. More light, more current. No light, none. It was the slowest, weakest, oldest way of turning light into electricity that Emeric knew, and it was the only one Kaldo could make this year, and it was enough. Ambrose's cell sat in a box of its own with the far end of the thread in a collar against it, and its wires ran to a relay, and the relay ran to a bell.

"Ready," said Tess.

"Ready," said Ambrose.

Tess pressed the key.

The shutter opened. There was nothing to see; the light went into the end of the thread and was gone, down a mile of glass laid in coils on paper, with Wren standing in the middle of it, and nobody watching could see a glimmer anywhere along it, because there wasn't one. It all stayed in.

Across the shed, the bell rang.

Wren yelped. Then she put her hand over her mouth, because nobody else had made a sound, and stood among the coils looking at the bell as if it had spoken to her.

Tess let the key up. The bell stopped. She pressed it again, three short and one long, and the bell went three short and one long, and then she sent the whole of the Motion Shop's test line, the one every telegraph boy in Kaldo learned first, and Ambrose read it off the bell aloud as it came, letter by letter, to the end, and it was right.

"That's it," said Tess. She sounded flat, the way she always sounded when something mattered too much to sound like anything. "That's it, then."

"How much made it?" said Emeric.

Ambrose had a meter on the cell. He looked at it. "Half. Bit more. Of what went in." He looked up. "Over a mile. Through that." He nodded at the coils on the floor, at Wren still standing in them. "Half."

"The Grayrock wire loses more than that in a mile on a wet day," said Tess.

"It does."

Ambrose was looking at the thread where it came into his collar. He touched it, very lightly, with one finger. "Is it faster?" he said. "Than the wire. Is that the thing?"

"No," said Emeric.

They looked at him.

"Not much. Light in glass goes about as fast as a signal in copper, give or take. Both of them a good deal slower than light in the open." He came down the shed and stood by the bell. He wanted to say this once, plainly, and then not again, because it was the kind of thing people got wrong for a hundred years if you let them. "It's not faster. It's everything else. You can put more down one thread than down a hundred wires, once we know how. It loses nothing worth talking about, once the glass is clean enough, so it'll go further between stations than copper ever will. It doesn't spark and it doesn't heat. Nobody can listen to it from outside without cutting it, and if they cut it, you know." He touched the black box over the cell. "And there's no current in the glass. None. So lightning can't touch it. A storm that'd burn every telegraph set on the Grayrock line goes straight past this."

"And a dead zone," said Tess.

"That's what we're going to find out."



The first real line went down on the harbour floor on the twentieth, from the old customs tower at the root of the sea wall, where the Harbour Board's brass housings were still waiting for their switches, out along the foot of the wall to the light at its end, and then across the floor of the mouth to the first of the current gate's footings. Two miles of it, in four lengths, spliced. Each length ran inside a sleeve of lead pipe, and the lead inside a sleeve of tarred rope and steel wire wound on by Abloh's rope walk, so that a dragged anchor would slide off it rather than break it. Finn and two of the yard's divers laid it on the bottom off the launch, a length a day, in the thin cold water, with Zale in the launch on his stick telling them where the bottom was softer.

When it was in, Tess sat at the Board's end in the customs tower, at a deal table under the empty brass housings, with her lamp box and her key, and sent down it.

Two miles away, at the foot of the light, in a steel box bolted to the sea wall's stones with its own small battery and its own selenium cell and its own relay, a bell rang. Ambrose's boy, sitting on the wall beside it with his collar up, rang it back down a second thread in the same sleeve.

Tess heard it come back. She didn't say anything at all. She wrote the time on the edge of the Board's chart, in very small figures, where Ferra would see it and be annoyed.

It wasn't much. A bell in a box at the end of a wall. In a month the mines would be on their chains, and the current gate's eight housings would be bolted to their grown footings across the mouth, and every one of them would be fired from this table down a thread of glass that a storm couldn't touch. But that was a month. This was a bell.

"Again," said Emeric, behind her.

She sent it again.



They made the dead zone on the twenty-third, on the old proving field north of the Works, a mile from anything that mattered, because you didn't make a dead zone anywhere near something that mattered.

It was a crude thing to do and they did it crudely. Tess had laid a second test line out across the field on stakes, a half-mile of the glass in its lead sleeve, with her lamp box at one end and Ambrose's cell box at the other. Beside it, on the same stakes, for comparison, ran an ordinary telegraph wire, and beside that, a third line, the kind Kaldo had built in its first years before anybody knew better: a pair of keyed light stones, one sending and one answering, with no wire between them at all, only mana and a stone's instruction.

And in the middle of the field, on a flat stone, Armand set down a spent fire stone that had been charged up far past what it was meant to hold, by Emeric, that morning, slowly, until it was humming.

"Back," said Armand.

They went back. He went back with them, all the way to the field's edge, with the slowness that he still used out of habit, though his body didn't need it any more. Then he turned round and looked at the stone across two hundred yards of grass, and reached out toward it, and pushed.

The stone overloaded. There wasn't any noise. The air went out of the middle of the field, or that was what it felt like; a wind came past them from the stone outward, quite hard, flattening the grass in a ring that ran away from the flat stone across the whole field and past them and on, and Emeric felt his own sense go out with it as if a lamp had been blown out in the room behind his eyes. The mana was gone. Not low; gone, the way it went in a dead zone, blown out of the ground and the air and every stone in the field like smoke out of a chimney when the door's opened. Every stone he had on him went quiet at once. Tess, on her stool at the field's edge, put her hand to the pin at her collar, which was a stone, and said, "Oh," because it had gone cold.

"Now," said Emeric.

Tess pressed the key. The lamp in her box was a plain bulb on a plain battery; it lit. The light went into the glass and down the half-mile through the middle of the dead ground. At the far end, in Ambrose's box, the selenium cell took it. Ambrose's relay closed and his bell rang, and rang, and rang, as clear as it had in the shed.

The telegraph wire worked too, which was the point of the telegraph wire: it was the same as it had always been.

The light stones didn't. The sending stone sat on its stake in the dead ground with its glyph gone out like a coal dropped in water. At the far end the answering stone sat on its stake and did nothing whatever, and went on doing it, and would go on doing it until the mana came back into that field some time tomorrow.

"That's every stone signal there is," said Emeric. "The tower's. The Church's. Anybody who ever trusted a stone to carry a word."

"I know," said Tess. She was looking at the dead stone on its stake, and then at the glass beside it, ticking away under her own hand. "So the light goes through it."

"The light doesn't care. It's not magic. It's light, in glass, from a bulb, to a plate of grey stuff out of the copper works' mud. There's nothing in any of it for a dead zone to take." He watched her press the key again. "Put a light stone behind the shutter instead of the bulb, and it'll flicker faster than any shutter can move, and you'll get ten letters down it for the bulb's one. And in a dead zone the stone goes out, and you put the bulb back, and you get your one letter. It never stops. It only slows down."

"The mundane floor," said Tess. "Under the magic." She'd heard him say it ten thousand times. She said it now as if she'd thought of it herself, which, he supposed, she had, more than once, in her own words, on her own bench.

"Every bit of the city's defences that has to talk," he said, "talks down this. The Board to the mines. The towers to the line room. The Sand Room. If the Church ever comes over that wall with a dead zone on purpose, they'll find out that the one thing in Kaldo it doesn't touch is the thing that tells us they've done it."

Armand had come back from the field's edge and was standing beside them with his hands in his cardigan pockets, looking at the flattened grass.

"I made those in the tower for sixty years," he said. "For prisoners. On purpose." He looked down at Tess's lamp box. "And a bulb and a bit of glass walks straight through it like a cat through a hedge." He didn't seem to know whether he was pleased. "I'm going to sit down."



Tess didn't say anything more about it that day. She took the lamp box home on the tram on her knees, and Osric carried the cell, and that night at a quarter to eleven the line room logged a call from her pad to his at the castle that said only: Page 40. Come and look. Not tonight. T.

He went in the morning.

Page forty was in the notebook she'd kept since the stone press first ran, the one with the seams in it and the warm cube, and it had been a blank page last week. It wasn't now. At the top, in her small square hand, was a heading, A stone that thinks in light, underlined twice. Under it, a drawing. It was a crystal, cut flat on two faces, a control stone's matte grey with its branching lines inside drawn in fine pencil, and on one face she'd drawn rays going in, and on the other rays coming out. Between them, inside the stone, the lines branched and crossed in a grid, like the grid on Amara's seeing stone that she'd etched for the eyes.

"A control stone decides," said Tess. "That's what it's for. It takes something in and does something about it." She put her finger on the incoming rays. "So far we've only ever told it things with mana. The touch. The note. A delver's runes. What if we tell it with light?" She moved the finger across the stone to the other face. "And it answers in light. Down a thread. To the next stone, and that one decides, and answers. And none of it's copper, and none of it's a spark." She looked up. "You'd have something that thinks. Out of glass and light. That a storm can't touch."

He looked at the page for a long time.

He had known this one was coming since the night in September when he'd written light in glass on the build list, and Tess had looked at it over his shoulder and said nothing. He'd known, because on the other side of everything, at the very end of the road he'd only read about, there had been men trying to build exactly this, with light instead of current, and not quite managing it. And she'd come at it from the other end. From a stone that already decided, which they'd never had.

"It's not this year," he said.

"I know it's not this year."

"It's not next year either. Not while I'm gone. I don't know how to do it. I don't think anybody does."

"Then I'll find out." She turned the page over so it lay face down on the bench, which was a thing she did with ideas she wasn't ready to be argued out of. "You'll be back."

He made a note of it, later, in his own book, at the end of the night's long division: that there would have to be a book in the pile for this. Something on light and glass and purity that went further than he'd told her. He wrote Tess, pg 40 beside it so he wouldn't forget, which he wouldn't, and put the pen down.



Imani asked for the plate on the twenty-fourth, a Friday, and she asked for it the way she asked for most things lately, which was by having already arranged it.

It had been sitting on Tess's back bench since the summer. It wasn't much. A sheet of glass a foot square in a brass frame on a stand with three levelling screws, because glass held mana and nothing else would do. A sieve of fine spent-stone dust, all of one kind, water dust, from the Dust House's sweepings, because mixed dust muddled. A slow count, a bare palm on the plate's rim, and you watched.

The dust moved. That was the whole of it. A person put a hand on the edge of the glass, and the dust on it, which had been lying there in a fine even grey, began to creep: very slowly, too slowly to watch, the way the hour hand of a clock moves. Over the count it drew itself into a figure. Lines and rings and branching shapes, like frost on a pane, or like the patterns sand makes on a drumhead when you bow the rim. And then you took your hand off, and the figure stayed, and you could spray it with fixative or press a sheet of Kaldo paper on it, and keep it.

Everybody's was different. That was the thing. Emeric had known it for years without ever having seen it; he'd felt it every day with his sense open, the note each person rang in him like a bell with its own sound. He'd called it a fingerprint once, privately, a long time ago. Here it was. Not felt. Drawn, in grey dust, on glass, where anybody could look at it whether they could feel a stone or not.

Tess had called it a note plate. Emeric had called it something else, a long word from the other side of everything about sound and patterns in sand, and Tess had said that was too long, and Ambrose's boys had started calling the whole business notework, and that was what had stuck.

The one doing the reading was Paskin.

He had been one of Armand's three readers, years ago now, the year the tower's old way of reading capacity once at birth had been found to be wrong. He'd been a quarry man for twenty years before that, with hands like a pair of shovels, and he'd ruled the first sheet of that study himself, a sheet the size of a tabletop, because he liked to see a thing all at once. He was greyer now. He had a magnifying glass on a cord round his neck, and a pencil behind his ear, and the plate's sheets laid out in front of him on Tess's bench in rows.

"Hand," he said.

Emeric put his hand on the rim.

The dust crept. It went slowly, and then not slowly; it bunched toward the middle of the glass in a hard dense knot and then threw out from the knot a figure that went right to the edges of the plate and over them, branching, thick, so many lines in it that the grey went nearly black.

Paskin looked at it through his glass for a long time.

"That'll do," he said, which from Paskin was a lot. He pressed the sheet onto it and lifted it and laid it with the others. "Next."

Tess put her hand on the rim. Hers came up quite differently: lighter, fewer lines, but very fine and very regular, a figure like a snowflake with all its arms the same, laid out in a grid as if somebody had ruled it.

"That's you all over," said Emeric.

"Shut up," said Tess.

And then the two Imani had brought.

They were sisters from the dye house, seventeen, alike enough that half the lower ward still called them by one name and let them sort out which of them had answered. Imani had their twin entry in her own register, from their enrolment at the records hall, bracketed together, each with the other's number. She'd asked them, and their mother, and they'd come because Imani had asked. They sat side by side on the bench with their hands in their laps and their sleeves blue to the elbow, and looked at the plate as if it might bite.

"Both on it," said Paskin. "Same plate. Same time. One each side." He looked at them over his glass. "You'll not feel anything. It's dust. It's not going in you."

They put their hands on.

The dust came up from both sides at once, two figures growing toward the middle of the glass from opposite rims, and for most of the count Emeric couldn't have told them apart if his life had depended on it. They were the same figure. The same branchings, the same rings, as if one had been drawn and the other traced off it. He looked at them with his own sense open too, and felt them, and they were the same note, almost; the same bell, with a crack in one of them so small that he had to hold still to hear it.

Paskin didn't look up from his glass.

"There," he said, and put the end of his pencil down on the plate, very gently, not touching the dust, at a place a little to the left of the middle where one branch on one side went up and the matching branch on the other side went up and a hair to the right. "And there." Another, near the rim. "And there. That's where they part." He drew three tiny circles in pencil on the plate's brass frame, opposite each. "That's who's who."

The two girls leaned over to look. One of them laughed. The other one said, "I've always said I was the one on the right," and the first one hit her.

Imani took the sheet when it was pressed. She didn't put it with Paskin's rows. She put it in a card folder with the twin entry's number on the front and the words Health register. Not for the gate. I. under it, and the folder went into her bag, and the bag went under her arm.

"That's mine," she said, to nobody in particular, and nobody argued.



He went up to the Well that night on the supply run, because he wanted to look at BR-01 from above, and because he'd been in rooms all week.

From the station's deck at Band height, with the cold coming through his coat (a new one, Ferra had made a remark), he could just see it, if he knew where to look: a speck to the south and a little east, hanging over the bay where he'd left it, catching the last of the sun when everything below it had gone dark. Every quarter of an hour, at a time that wandered, it talked to the station he was standing on. He couldn't hear it. He could feel it, quite clearly, from three miles off, the way Armand had said anyone could: the six lift stones round its rim and the pin in the middle, holding their charge in the Band's thick air like six coals and an ember.

He stood at the rail and looked at it for a while.

The glass carried light because the glass held the light in and gave it nowhere else to go. The Band held mana the way glass held light. Dense, and still, and kept up there by its own weight in a layer five miles thick round the whole of the world. Every relay they put in it would sit in that layer like a bead on a thread.

He wondered, standing there, idly, the way you wonder in the last ten minutes before sleep, whether the Band might carry more than a radio's voice between them. Whether something laid in mana the way light lay in glass could be sent along it, from one stone to the next.

He didn't try anything. He put it on the list in his head beside the eye and the rod and the lit-up kingdom, and went in out of the cold, and found that the station's cook had kept him a plate.
