Chapter 95 - Cylinder

The coal came down from Grayrock on the last day of September, and with it, sitting on top of the load with his knees up and his boots black to the ankle, came Haddon.

He was about twenty, long and thin and the colour of the hill he came from, with a bundle on a stick, and when the barge tied up he jumped onto the wharf before the second rope was round the post and stood looking up at the wall lamps on their iron arms with his mouth open, as though he had come to a fair.

The bargeman said he had asked to come. He was a collier's son from the pits on Grayrock's east hill, and all summer, loading coal for Kaldo, he had asked every bargeman what Kaldo did with it, because Kaldo had always burned wood and charcoal, and nobody could tell him. His mother had told him to go and look. So he had.

"What for, though?" he said to Emeric, when Bram had brought him up to bay three and stood him in front of the drum. He did not know who Emeric was, and when Bram told him he looked politely interested and asked again. "All that coal. What's it for?"

Emeric showed him the drum, and the fire tube through it, and the drawing on the wall of the engine it would drive.

Haddon looked at the drawing the way the hill men looked at a seam, as if it might be lying to him.

"You'll want somebody to burn it, then," he said. "Somebody as knows coal. Your lot here burn wood." He said it without contempt, as a fact, the way he would have said they spoke differently. "Wood's a toy fire. Coal's a proper fire. You can't treat it the same or it'll sulk on you."

Bram looked at Emeric over the boy's head.

"Can you start tomorrow?" Emeric said.



The cylinder was cast on the third of October.

It was the biggest single thing Bram had ever poured: a tube of iron as wide inside as a big man's shoulders and as long as a man was tall, with a wall as thick as a thumb, a little over half a ton, all in one pour, because a cylinder poured in two goes had a seam in it, and a seam was where the steam would get out.

The mould was loam, sand and clay and horse dung and chopped straw worked into a stiff pudding, plastered over a core of brick in the casting pit and swept round with a board on a spindle until it was as true as a board could make it. The outside of the mould went round it the same way, a hand's width out. The iron would go into the gap.

Orrin held the heat.

He had done it for the lathe bed in March, standing at the cupola's side with his palms out, and Emeric had made him explain to the bellows boys afterward what he was doing, and the explanation had been mostly about the rate. This time Emeric put Haddon beside him.

"Show him," he said.

"Show him what?"

"What you do. All of it. Without the magic."

Orrin looked at Haddon, and Haddon looked at Orrin, and it was clear within a minute that they were going to dislike each other, and clear within an hour that they were enjoying it.

The trouble was that they did not know heat the same way. Orrin felt it, with his hands out and his eyes half shut, the way a man feels a stove in the dark, and pushed a little of himself into wherever it was cooling. Haddon looked at it. He pulled the plug out of the spy hole in the cupola's side with his tongs and put his eye to it and said what he saw, out loud, all the time, as though he were reading a clock.

"Cherry," he said. "Bright cherry. Coming on orange at the back. Orange. Yellow at the tuyere." He put the plug back. "She's drawing well."

"She's going cold on the left," said Orrin.

"She's cherry on the left."

"She's cherry on the left now. She'll be dull in ten minutes."

Haddon took the plug out again and looked. Then he looked at Orrin, who was standing with his hands out and his eyes half shut and no expression at all.

"How do you know?"

"I can feel it going."

"Before it goes?"

"That's the point of me," said Orrin, with dignity.

Ten minutes later the left of the bed went dull. Haddon saw it go. He swore at it in hill speech, which Emeric did not follow, and took the long bar and broke up the bed on the left and called for charcoal, and the colour came back. Then he went and stood beside Orrin, and looked into the spy hole, and looked at Orrin, and looked into the spy hole, for the rest of the morning.

By noon he had it. He could not feel what Orrin felt. But a bed that was going to go dull went somehow flat before it went dark, the shine went off it, the coals stopped looking wet, and if a man knew to look for the shine he could see it a few minutes before the colour went, which was nearly as good as feeling it.

"Shine goes off before the colour does," he said, as if he had found it himself.

"Tell the bellows boys," said Emeric.

"Tell them yourself," said Orrin. "I'm by the job."

They poured at a little after one. Four men carried the ladle on its bars to the pit without a word between them, and tipped it, and the iron went down into the mould with a noise like a kettle the size of a house, and the yard filled with steam. Then there was nothing to do but wait three days for it to cool.



When they broke the mould off, the outside of the cylinder was beautiful, and the inside was scrap.

Bram saw it first, crawling into the pit with a lamp. The outside wall was smooth and grey and true. The inside, where the iron had lain against the core, was full of holes, hundreds of them, from a pinhead to a pea, set close like the holes in a loaf, and when Bram cut into the wall with a cold chisel they went in a finger's depth, a honeycomb from end to end.

A cylinder was a hole that a piston slid up and down in, with the steam on one side of it and not on the other. A cylinder full of holes was a sieve.

Emeric stood at the edge of the pit and looked at it, and knew what the holes were, and did not say.

Bram was a long time in the pit. He cut out a chip of the inside wall and looked at it in the light. Then he went down again and put his hand flat on the broken core, on the loam where it had lain against the iron, and kept it there. Then he smelled his hand.

"Wet," he said.

"Outside of the mould dried. It's had the sun on it a fortnight. Core's brick, and brick holds water, and there's no wind in the middle of a core." He tapped the chip. "We poured iron onto wet loam. Water goes to steam, steam goes into the iron, iron sets round it." He looked at half a ton of it lying in the pit. "That's mine. I knew it. I wanted it poured."

The second mould went quicker than the first, because the core bricks and the sweeping board could be used again: eight days, and then three more, because when it was built Bram had Haddon build a fire in the core.

A small one, charcoal, in an iron basket let down into the core on a chain, kept in three days and three nights, just hot enough to drive the last of the water out. Haddon sat up with it, asleep on a sack in the pit, waking every few hours to look down at the fire and say its colour to nobody. On the third morning Bram put his hand on the loam, and smelled it, and nodded.

The second cylinder came out of the pit sound. Bram cut a chip from each end of it, inside, where nobody would ever see, and the iron was close and grey and solid all through.



Boring it took nine days.

The cylinder lay across the bed of the big lathe in the lean-to, chocked and clamped, with an iron bar through it carried in a bearing at each end. On the bar was a cutter head with four hardened cutters, and the bar turned, and a screw fed the head along so slowly that nobody could see it move, and the cutters took a curl of iron as thin as paper off the whole inside. Then back, the cutters set out a hair, and through again.

The water wheel turned it all day through the shafting, with a long thin grinding song, and every evening Tess came down in her chair and measured.

She had a gauge for it, an iron rod with a screw in one end and a pointer, and she set it across the bore at the top and the bottom and each side, at both ends, and wrote the numbers in a column with the day. On the first evening they were all different. On the ninth they were the same, everywhere, to the finest mark the pointer had.

"Vesk could have trued that in an afternoon," Orrin said, on the fourth day. He had come by to look, as he came by to look at everything. "Hands on it. One afternoon. Two, it's a big one."

"I know."

"Then why the nine days?"

"Because there'll be a next one. And one after that. And one day Vesk won't be here, and Bram will want to bore a cylinder, and he'll have a lathe that can." Emeric looked at the bar turning in its bearings. "The first lathe needed Vesk. This one doesn't need anybody but Bram."

"You're doing yourself out of a lot of good mage work," he said.

"I'm doing you out of it."

"That's what I said."



Rylan had the board on the second night of the boring, for the third night that week.

He had asked for it. Emeric came up a little before ten, as he did most nights now, and found him on the stool with his feet on the bench and a lamp beside him, looking at the board as if it were a man he did not yet trust.

"What does it do," Rylan said, without taking his eyes off it, "when it goes wrong?"

Emeric sat down on the bench.

"Which wrong?"

"Any of them. All of them." Rylan took his feet off the bench. "I sit here. Something happens. A shutter comes down, I know what that is. The needle swings over, I know what that is. A man. Two men. Ward." He nodded at the board. "What does it look like when it's the board's fault and not a man's?"

It was a good question. Nobody had asked it. Emeric had thought about it himself, and written it in his own book, and not on the wall.

"Two ways," he said. "If the wire's cut, the shutter for that stretch drops and the fault bell goes. The same as the line. You've heard the line's fault bell."

"Everybody's heard the line's fault bell."

"The other way is the stone. A stone that's going wrong doesn't lean on the stack the way a man does, steady, and then let go. It leans and lets go and leans again. The shutter drops and lifts and drops. The needle can't make up its mind. It chatters."

"What makes a stone go wrong?"

"A crack. A dead place, if one opened near it. Or another charged stone, in the water." Emeric thought about how to say it. "A charged stone holds its mana tight inside it. That's what makes it a stone and not a man. Carry one past a sensor on the wall and the sensor hardly knows. But the water stones sit in the wet, and water carries what's in it. Put a charged stone in the water near one of them and keep it moving, and the stone feels it come and go and come and go, and can't settle. It chatters like a cold man's teeth."

Rylan looked at the board.

"Show me," he said.

So they went down. Declan was in the guardroom and came up grumbling to sit the board, and Arthur, who should have been in bed and was not, attached himself to them on the keep stair and could not be got rid of, and they went down through the sleeping town to the water gate with a lantern, and out onto the river stair.

The river was up a little with the first October rain, and black, and quick, and talking to itself at the foot of the stair. Emeric took his hand lamp out of his coat. It was a charged fire stone in a little iron cage on a cord, the same as half the garrison carried now for a night lamp, and he had charged it himself that morning. He gave it to Rylan.

"Out over the water," he said. "Let it dip. Swing it."

Rylan stood on the bottom step with the cord in his fist and swung the little caged stone out over the river in a slow arc, so that it dipped into the black water at the bottom of each swing and came up streaming, and went out again. Arthur stood on the step above with his arms wrapped round himself, watching. After the fourth swing Emeric sent him up.

He was back in less time than seemed possible, red in the face, having been up to the line room and down again at a dead run, while Rylan went on swinging.

"It's going," he said, and had to stop and breathe. "The river stair. It's going like teeth. Declan said to say. Like teeth."

Rylan swung the stone once more, out and down into the water and up, and then stopped, and held it still, and looked at it hanging on its cord with the river running off it. Then he handed it back.

"And who do I wake?" he said.



Ward was in the line room when they came back up, standing in front of the board with his hands behind his back, with Declan on the stool trying to look as though he had not been told to stay there.

"It chattered," Ward said.

"I made it," said Emeric. "Rylan wanted to know what a fault looks like."

Ward looked at Rylan, and nodded, as if that were a reasonable thing for a man on the board to want to know, which it was.

"Then he will want to know what to do about it," Ward said.

So they wrote the card.

It took most of an hour, because Ward would not have a word on it he could not defend to the stupidest man in the garrison at three in the morning, and named the man. Emeric wrote it with a burnt stick on a board of planed pine, big enough to read by a bad lamp, and Ward struck out half of it and made him write it shorter. When it was done it said four things.

A shutter down and the needle steady: a body on that stretch. Send the walking pair to it.

A shutter down and the fault bell: a cut wire. Send the gate crew with a lantern to find the cut.

A shutter that drops and lifts and will not settle: a stone at fault. Wake the prince.

And at the bottom, in smaller letters because Ward said it would come up rarely and when it did the man would have time to read slowly: anything not on this card, wake the captain.

Ward read it down twice. Then he put one finger on the third line.

"That is one man," he said.

"It's a stone. Only one man can mend a stone."

"I know who can mend it. I am saying that when it chatters, the whole wall is one man. In his bed. Asleep." Ward did not raise his voice. He never did. "Everything else on this board wakes somebody who can be replaced."

"If a stone goes, there's nothing anybody else can do about it. Nobody but me can even tell what's wrong with it. You'd be waking a man to stand at the river stair and look at a wall."

"Yes," said Ward. "That is what I would be doing."

They looked at each other across the line room with Declan and Rylan very still between them.

"It's my stone," Emeric said.

Ward looked at the card again for some time.

"As you say," he said, and went down the stair.

Emeric nailed the card to the wall beside the board himself, with four nails, at the height of a sitting man's eyes. Rylan watched him do it. Rylan watched every letter of it as Emeric had written it, and Emeric did not think he had been able to read more than a few of them.



Two nights after, at dusk, with Rylan on the board again, the water gate's shutter dropped.

Emeric was there, with Tess's figures for the bore, and he looked up at the bell and saw the needle beside the water gate's window swing over and stand. Not far. Less far than for the small man on the gate crew. But over the line, and steady.

Rylan was already at the window. He whistled down to the walking pair at the foot of the keep, two short and one long, which was the water gate, and they went off along the walk at a trot, and before they were halfway the needle came slowly back and lay still.

They were back in less than a quarter of an hour. They came up the keep stair slowly, because one of them had a hand on the ear of a boy.

The boy was nine or so, all knees and elbows, in a coat that had been somebody else's, with a bundle in a cloth held against his chest. He had his father's jaw, and he came up the last of the stair on his toes, because of the ear, with his face held very straight.

"On the walk," said the man with the ear. "At the water gate. Coming along from the lower ward."

"I brought your supper," said Finn.

Rylan looked at his son.

"You brought the walking pair," he said. "Down the walk. At a trot. With crossbows."

"Mam said to bring it hot. The walk's quicker. You go round by the street, it's cold by the time you're up." Finn held the bundle out. "It's still hot."

The walking man let go of his ear. Rylan took the bundle. He did not say anything else for a while, and Emeric saw him decide not to, which was a thing that took some doing.

Finn rubbed his ear and looked round the line room with frank interest, at the board and the line's key and at Emeric, whom he knew by sight, as every child in Kaldo did, and was not impressed by. Then he saw the card by the board and went close to it.

"A shutter down and the needle steady," he read aloud. He read it fast, easily, the way Sela's best readers read, with the stops in the right places. "A body on that stretch. Send the walking pair to it. A shutter down and the fault bell. A cut wire. Send the gate crew with a lantern to find the cut." He went down it without a pause. "A shutter that drops and lifts and will not settle. A stone at fault. Wake the prince." He looked round at Emeric. "Is that you?"

"That's me."

"Anything not on this card, wake the captain." He turned round, pleased. "That's Ward."

Rylan was standing very still with the bundle in his hands. He was looking at the card, not at his son.

"That's Ward," he said.

On the slate under the board, where the man on the board wrote every swing he saw with the hour, as Tess had asked, Emeric chalked what the needle had done for Finn. It was the smallest mark the board had ever written down. A good way below the small man on the gate crew. A good way above the dog.

"He's the lowest it's ever read," he said.

Rylan came and looked at the slate.

"How low did you set it?"

"As low as a person goes." He put the chalk down. "Everything alive carries some. A man more than a boy, a big man a little more than a small one. A dog carries less than a boy, though it weighs more. I set the line under the least a person carries. Under him." He nodded at Finn, who was investigating the line's key with one finger and being watched by nobody. "A baby, I don't know. A boy does. A dog doesn't."

"And a big man?"

"Further over. You and Ward put it in the same place, a long way over."

"And a mage?"

Emeric thought about it. He had not walked a mage past one of them yet. He had not thought to.

"Further than you," he said. "A good deal further. I'd guess against the pin. A strong one would bang it against the pin and the man on the board would know he wasn't looking at a man." He picked up the burnt stick from the bench, and under the four lines on the card, small, he wrote a fifth. Needle hard against the pin: a mage, or a crowd. Wake the captain.

Rylan watched him write it.

"Could a man get past it?" he said. "Without it reading him at all?"

"Only by carrying less than your boy does."

"How does a man carry less?"

"He'd have to pour it out of himself." Emeric looked at the board. "You can't. Yours only works inside you, that's what the rite did. A man like Hale can't move his at all, he doesn't know it's there. A mage could pour his out, a little at a time, the way he would for a spell. But a mage has the most of anybody to pour. He'd have to empty himself below a boy of nine." He shook his head. "I don't know what that does to a man. I'll ask Armand."

Rylan nodded slowly. Then he sat down on the stool with the bundle on his knees and unwrapped it. It was bread, and a fold of something hot in a cabbage leaf, and two apples, and Finn came and sat on the floor beside the stool without being asked and ate one of the apples and a third of the bread, and Rylan let him.

The walking pair took Finn home by the street with a lantern, and at the head of the stair he turned round and told Emeric that the board was the best thing in Kaldo, better than the lamps.

Rylan went back to the board. Emeric sat on the bench with Tess's figures, and turned the pages, and did not read them.

After a while Rylan got up off the stool and went to the card on the wall. He stood in front of it with the lamp behind him. And he read it, from the top, one line at a time, slowly, in a voice so low it was hardly a voice, his lips moving on every word, his finger going along under the letters, stopping, going back, going on. He read the whole of it. It took a long time. He read the last line, the one Emeric had just written, twice.

Emeric turned another page of the bore figures, and kept his eyes on the board.



The bore was finished on the ninth day. Tess's numbers came out the same at every place she put the gauge, and Bram had the bar out of it and the cylinder off the lathe and standing on its end in the lean-to by evening, and they all stood round it.

Vesk came forward, past Bram and Tess and Orrin and Haddon, and put his arm into the cylinder to the shoulder, and drew his palm slowly down the whole length of the bore and back, with his eyes shut, listening to it with his hand.

Then he took his arm out, and wiped his palm on his coat, and nodded once, to Bram.

Bram treated it afterward as if it had been a certificate with a seal on it.



Ward came at him from above at the dawn drill on the next morning, in the yard behind the guardhouse, with the frost not yet off the sand.

It was the downslash, Ward's oldest cut, the ash blade coming down from over his head with his whole weight behind it, so fast that the art was to have moved before it started. In three years Emeric had learned to get out from under it about half the time. The other half he took it on his guard, or on the shoulder, and was told about his feet.

This time he was not there when it came down.

He did not decide to move. He was simply somewhere else, half a step to the left and inside Ward's arms, with his own blade already going, and the ash blade came down through the place where he had been and hit the sand. And his blade went across Ward's ribs under the raised arms, a clean full cut, hard, harder than he had ever landed anything on Ward in three years, and he felt it go in through the padding and stop on bone.

And he stopped.

He stopped dead, with his blade still against Ward's side. He had hit him. He had hit Ward with the whole of his arm and something more than his arm, and he had felt the rib under it, and for the space of a breath he did not think about the drill at all. He thought that he had broken it.

Ward's blade came up off the sand and lay along the side of his neck.

"You stopped," Ward said.

"I thought I'd hurt you."

"You did not."

Ward took the blade away from his neck. He stood back. He did not touch his ribs, though Emeric was nearly sure he wanted to. He looked at Emeric the way he had looked at him once before, years ago, on a morning in the first autumn, when a guard had come up to meet a feint before Emeric had decided to raise it. A long level look, from under his brows, with nothing in it that could be read.

Neither of them said anything.

Then Ward said "Again," and came at him from the left, and Emeric lost the way he usually lost, twice, and they did the leg hold on the frosted sand after, as they had every morning since Rylan showed it in April, and went in to breakfast.



He could not sleep that night.

He lay in the dark and felt the rib under his blade, again and again, and the half step he had not decided to take. At past midnight he gave it up and got up and dressed and went down through the keep, meaning to walk the wall to the river tower and back, the way he walked when the drawings would not come right.

There was a lamp in the gate passage.

Ward was at the guard table under it. He had a sheet of paper in front of him that was not barony paper, a little smaller and a little greyer, and a pen, and he was writing in his small square hand, the hand he used for nothing else that Emeric had ever seen. The sheet was close to full. There was a second sheet under it, already written, face down.

He had seen this once before, nearly a year ago, the night before the road north.

Ward did not stop when Emeric came round the corner of the passage. He finished the word he was writing. Then he laid his forearm across the page, slowly, the way he had laid it across the other one, as a man lays his arm round his bowl, and looked up.

"Highness."

"I couldn't sleep."

"No," said Ward.

Emeric stood in the passage. He did not ask. He looked at Ward's arm lying across the page, and at the second sheet face down beneath it, which was more than Ward had ever written about anything, to anyone, in his hearing.

"Goodnight, Ward."

"Goodnight."

He went out through the gate meaning to walk the wall, and stopped at the foot of the tower stair, because every stone on it would ring for him, and some man on the board would send the walking pair at a trot after the prince. He went back up to bed. From the turn of the keep stair he could see the gate passage, and the lamp in it was out, and the table was bare.
