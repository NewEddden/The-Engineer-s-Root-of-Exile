Chapter 91 - Low Water

The river went down in the last ten days of August the way it had gone down the year before, all at once and then slowly, and the lamps went with it.

Tess had said it would. She had a column that said it would, three years of it now, the river's height at the race against the hours the lamps had burned, and the line on her page bent down at the end of August as surely as the year bent down toward autumn. She had shown it to Bram in July and to Emeric in the first week of August and to Ward in the second, and Ward had looked at it for some time and asked her which night, and she had told him within three days either way, and been right to the night.

On the twenty-third the wall lamps began to stutter. Not out. Worse than out. A lamp that was out was dark, and a man on the wall knew where he stood with dark. These burned and then went dim and then flared and went dim again, as the slow dynamo down at the race caught and slipped and caught, and every time they dimmed the shadows under the wall jumped toward the houses, and every time they flared the men on the walk were blind for a breath.

Ward went round the wall that night and chose. Two would burn on the low nights, as last year: the one over the South Gate and the one at the river tower. The other six went out of the circuit at Tess's copper cut-out, so that the slow wheel had two arcs to drive and not eight, and the two burned steady.

And the rest of the wall went dark, and Ward's men walked it.



Emeric walked it with them on the fourth night, because he had been watching them do it for three, and wanted to know what it was that he was watching.

It was long. That was the first thing. The wall of Kaldo went right round the town, and from the South Gate tower to the river tower and back the other way was most of an hour at a walk, with a lantern, in the dark, and in the stretches between the two lamps the dark was complete. Ward had the garrison doing it two to a stretch, all night, with a lantern each, in turns of three hours, and the garrison was thirty-two men and the farms had taken eight of them for the harvest, and the men on the walk were the men who had stood the day on the gate, and Emeric could see it in how they walked.

He went round with Rylan and Declan on the stretch above the lower ward. It was the worst stretch. The wall walk narrowed there to the width of a man, between the parapet and a drop on the town side where the houses had been built right up to the wall's foot and their roofs came up to within a few feet of the walk, so that a man who knew what he was doing could have stepped from the roofs onto the wall without a ladder. There were three drains under it. There was the water gate at the far end of it, and below the water gate the stair down to the river, where the boats came in.

Declan counted his steps. He did it under his breath, the whole way, and when Emeric asked him why he said that in the dark a man ought to know how far he had come, and that it was the kind of thing he had always done, and was, as he had said at the yard fire in the spring, not a noble occupation. Rylan said almost nothing. He walked at the back with his lantern held low and his eyes on the roofs, and once, when a cat came over a ridge and dropped down onto the walk in front of them, he had his knife half out before he saw what it was. He put it back without a word. He looked tired in a way Emeric did not have a name for. Emeric thought it was the walking and the three-hour turns, and did not ask.

At the water gate they stopped, and Declan held his lantern out over the parapet and looked down at the river stair going down into the black water, and the river going past very low and very quiet.

"Anybody could come up that," Declan said mildly. "In a boat. In the dark. We'd see him at the top if we were standing here when he came. We'd not see him if we were at the other end of the stretch." He did not seem worried. He was describing a door. "Which we are, half the night."

Emeric stood at the parapet with his lantern and looked at the stair.

Somebody was coming along the walk behind them. He did not hear it. He felt it, the way he had felt Sela's boy across the long room the first autumn Armand was in Kaldo: a faint warmth at the edge of his own sense, a thing that was alive and was not him, getting nearer. He turned. It was the next pair of the garrison, come early to take over the stretch, a lantern swinging, and he had known they were there before the light of it came round the tower.

That's only you, Armand had said.

He thought about Armand in the chair with his eyes shut, breathing out into the whole house, naming who was in the corridor. He thought about the charged fire stone on Tess's bench in the powerhouse, which he had picked up a hundred times, and about the thing he had noticed and never thought about: that when Tess's hand came near it on the bench, before she touched it, the stone's hum changed. Very slightly. As if it had felt her coming. He had put it down to his own sense and thought no more about it.

But it was not his sense. He had felt it in the stone.

A charged stone held its own field round it, a little way, the way a fire held its own heat. Everything living carried some mana in it, a little, less the smaller the creature, and a body that came into a stone's field pushed against it, and the stone felt the push. Not who. Not what. Only that something had come, and roughly how much of it. Armand sent his breath out and listened for it to come back. A stone did not need to send anything. It only had to listen. And a stone did not need a river, or a wheel, or a man walking a wall at three in the morning with his eyes closing.

"Highness," said Declan.

The new pair were waiting to be let past. Emeric stood aside on the narrow walk to let them by, and then went down the tower stair, quickly, and did not go to bed.



He drew it in the line room until it was light.

A stone for each stretch the lamps did not cover. A stone at the water gate, and one at the head of the river stair, and one at each of the drains. Each one set in the wall where a man would have to pass close to it to come in. Each one listening. And when a body came into its field, it would not glow, which would tell the man in the dark where it was. It would close a contact on a wire. And the wire would come up the wall and along the line to this room, where it would drop a shutter on a board and ring a bell, and the man sitting the board would know which stretch, and roughly how much, and send the walking men there, and not to the other end of the wall.

The stones did not need a river. The wire did not need a mage. Either one could fail, and the other would still be there.

He went down to the powerhouse in the morning with the drawings under his arm, to ask Tess for the river column, so he could say to Ward how many nights a year the wall would need it.

Tess had the river column on her board already. Beside it she had a second column he had not asked for and had not known she was keeping: every night since the low water began last year that the wall had gone dark, and how many men Ward had put on it, and for how many hours, in Ward's own figures, which she had got out of the gate crew a night at a time.

She turned the board round so that he could read both.

"You'll want these," she said.

He looked at her.

"You walked it last night," Tess said. "The whole gate crew knows you walked it. You don't walk a wall at night to see if your boots fit." She looked at the drawings under his arm. "What is it?"



Ward heard it in the gate passage that evening, standing, with the drawings held up to the last of the light from the yard and his face giving away nothing at all.

He read every sheet. He read them as he read a wall, from one end to the other and then back, looking for the place where it would give. Emeric had watched him do it to the line, and to the lamps, and to the bridge in May of last year with Grayrock forming at the far end of it, and had learned to wait.

"It does not see who," Ward said at last.

"No. It sees that somebody's there, and where, and roughly how much of him. Your men see who."

"And when it is quiet, my men sit by the board instead of walking."

"Yes."

"Then they will stop walking." Ward lowered the sheets. "Not tomorrow. In a year. When the board has been quiet every night for a year, a man sitting it at three in the morning will stop believing it, and then he will stop watching it, and then he will be asleep." He said it without any particular weight, as a man reports the weather. "That is not your stones. That is men."

"Then the walking goes on. Fewer of them. Less often. And the board tells them where to walk when it matters."

Ward considered that, and did not say yes, which from Ward was very nearly yes.

"What breaks it?" he said.

Emeric had expected it. It was the question Ward asked of everything.

"A dead zone blinds it. Same as any stone. Cut the wire and the board goes quiet for that stretch, but a cut wire shows on the board as a fault, the same as the line does now. And it can be fooled the other way. Anything alive that comes close enough will move it. A dog. A boat at the foot of the river stair with a man asleep in it." He shook his head. "Until I set it right, a dog could bring your men to the wrong end of the wall."

"Then set it right."

"That's the work."

Ward handed the sheets back. Then, at the door into the yard, he stopped.

"The stones," he said. "Where do they come from? It is not the barony's money. Ferra would have told me."

"Mine."

Ward knew what that meant. He had seen the chest come south three years ago and had never once asked what was in it. He looked at Emeric for a while in the last of the light.

"How many?"

"Twelve."

"Out of how many."

Emeric told him. Ward did the sum in his head. He did not say anything about it. He nodded once, the way he nodded when a horse was sound, and went out into the yard.



He went down to Bram's kitchen late on the last Thursday of the month, past midnight, because the sixth lamp's cut-out had stuck and Tess wanted to know whether it would want a new spring, and Bram would know, and he had thought Bram would be up.

Bram was not up. Bram was asleep in the chair by the stove with his mouth open and his boots off and his feet on the fender, snoring with great regularity. Ferra was up.

She was at the table with the lamp pulled close and the year's book open, and three other books open round it, and a column under her pen that went most of the way down the page. She looked up when he came in. She did not seem surprised.

"It's done," she said.

"What is?"

"The year." She looked down at the page. "I wasn't going to have it till Monday. I had it at ten." She put her hand flat over the foot of the column, where the total was, so that he could not read it. "Have you eaten?"

"Yes."

"When?"

He thought. "Noon."

"Sit down."

"Ferra."

"Sit," said Ferra, "down."

He sat down. She got up and went to the shelf over the stove, stepping round her father's feet, and came back with half a loaf and the end of a cheese and a knife, and then went back to the pot on the back of the stove and put what was in it into a bowl, cold, beans and something, and put that in front of him too, and a spoon. Then she sat down again, and drew the year's book back to her side of the table with her hand still flat on the foot of the page, and turned to one of the other books, and went on with something else.

He ate. He had not known he was hungry until the bread was in his mouth. He ate the beans cold out of the bowl and the bread and most of the cheese, and Bram snored, and the stove ticked, and Ferra did a column in one of the other books and did not look up once, and did not need to.

When the bowl was empty she turned the year's book round on the table and took her hand away.

He read the figure at the foot of the column. He read it again. He looked at the lines above it, because he did not believe it, and the lines above it were in her hand and he believed them, and he came back down to the figure and read it a third time, and then he laughed. He had not meant to. It came up out of him in the quiet kitchen too loud, and Bram stopped snoring and said something in his sleep and started again.

Ferra watched him laugh. Her face did not do anything at all, except that it was turned toward him, and the lamp was on it, and she was not looking at the page.

"Eat the rest," she said, and pushed the cheese back across the table.



He went home by the church, because it was the shorter way and he was tired.

There was a light in Doran's vestry. It was very late for that. He looked in without meaning to, because the shutter was open for the heat and the lamp was on the table under the window, and there were two men at the table.

Doran sat on one side of it in his old cassock with his finger on a slate. On the other side, with his elbows on the table and his big hands round a slate pencil as if it were something that might get away from him, sat Rylan. He was making a shape on the slate. He made it slowly, with his tongue in the corner of his mouth, the way Arthur held the soup bowl, and when it was done he looked at it, and then at Doran, and Doran looked at it and said something too quiet to hear, and Rylan rubbed it out with the heel of his hand and began again.

Emeric stood in the dark of the lane.

Then he went back the way he had come, the long way round the churchyard, so that the shutter would not have him in it if Rylan looked up, and went home to bed.
