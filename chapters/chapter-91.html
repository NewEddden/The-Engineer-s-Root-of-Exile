Chapter 91 - The King's Price

Ward had made ready for fifty.

He had not said so to anybody. He had simply done it, over the five weeks the return was on the road north and the answer was on the road back, in the way he did everything, without a table or a sheet. Two more men on the Left Bridge in the daytime. The coach house cleared and swept and the carriage on its bare axles pushed to the back wall, so that there was stabling for thirty horses under a roof. A second key to the strongroom cut and given to nobody, and kept in a place he did not name. Ferra's objection was in the book, dated, in her hand: that the Crown might answer with a royal commission, men sent to hold the working for the realm. A commission was fifty men at the least, and clerks, and a captain who answered to somebody in the capital and not to anybody in Kaldo, and Ward had counted the beds.

The relay brought the number down the Wayford line on a still morning at the end of summer, from the junction post, in the marks: a party under the Crown's seal on the north road, passed the junction at first light, riding south.

"How many?" Emeric said.

Ferra read the rest of the line off the log. "Eleven."

Ward, at the window, did not turn round. After a moment he said, "Eleven," to the glass, in the voice of a man who has carried a heavy thing up a long stair and found the door at the top standing open.

They were an assessor, two clerks, a weigher with a locked chest, an assayer who was not introduced as one and was plainly one, four men of the Crown's guard in plain coats, a groom and a boy. The assessor was a narrow grey man called Oswin Fenner, who had been in the chancery's service for thirty years and wore it on him like the dust of the road. He presented himself at the Western Gate at the hour he had sent ahead that he would, and gave his name, and his writ, and asked for nothing but water for the horses and the loan of a table.

He did not ask to see the working. He asked to see the barony's copy of the return.

Emeric gave it to him in the long room, and Fenner read it through once standing, with his own copy open beside it, and then sat down and read it again with a lens, as though the letters might have changed since he read them in the capital. When he came to the date he stopped.

When first known to the holder: the spring of his ninth year at Kaldo, by the discovery of a survey among the late baron's papers.

Fenner took out his own book, a thick one bound in black, and wrote in it. He wrote the date and nothing else, as far as Emeric could see across the table, and then he closed the book and put it away and went on reading from the line after.

He did not ask about the two years. He did not ask about the covenant, or the survey, or whose papers, or why a baron with a dead man's survey in his hand had taken two springs to tell his king what was in it. He read to the end of the return and set it down square to the edge of the table, and said that he would be obliged if he could see the working in the morning.

Nobody at the table said anything about it afterward. Ferra looked at the place in the return where the date was for some while, and then at Emeric, and he knew she had seen exactly what he had seen, which was a careful man declining to ask the only question that anybody in the capital would have wanted answered. A man does not decline a question like that on his own account. Somebody had told him not to ask. Or somebody had told him to write it down, and that it would be asked later, by somebody else, when it was more useful.

He did not know which. He found that he did not like either.

They went up to Grayrock by the road.

The boat lay in its shed below the outside foundry with the doors shut and a padlock on them, and nobody suggested using it. Fenner looked at the river once, from the bank, on the way out of the town, at the place where it came down from the lake broad and quick and empty of barges, and asked whether it was navigable above the lake. Emeric said that it had been, before the plague. That was true. Fenner wrote nothing down.

The road took two days, and on the second evening they came in under the hill with the light going, and Bryda was at the door of the hall, and Ilsa was beside her with a ledger under her arm.

Hask drew it for Fenner in the morning.

He did it at the head of the sealed shaft, crouched on his bad knee in the dust by the chimney, with a stick, for a narrow grey man in a chancery coat who had asked, courteously, how the working was kept safe. Emeric had not asked him to. Hask had simply held his hand out for the stick before anybody else could answer, and drawn it: the shaft going down, the crosscut from the Low Drift, the chimney and the fire and the second opening above the flame, and then the gallery, going down past the fork, with the long wall of tarred canvas down its middle and the short wall across the fork and the two doors, the one at the mouth and the one he had added himself, and the arrows, down one side and round and up the other.

It was the third time. The first time it had been Emeric's drawing and Hask had drawn it back to prove he could. The second time Hask had drawn it back and made it better. This time there was nobody's drawing to draw back. It was his.

"Air goes where it's easiest," Hask told the assessor, and put the stick down. "You make where you want it the easiest place."

Fenner looked at the lines for some time. Then he looked at Hask, and said that it was admirably clear, and wrote something in the black book, and Hask looked at the ground and went pink to the ears and got up off his knee.

They went down after that. Emeric and Ward and Hask, and Fenner, who went down the carpenters' ladders at the pace of a man who had never been down a ladder in his life and was not going to let anybody see it, and the weigher, who did not want to and came anyway. At the gallery mouth the canvas door swung shut behind them on its own weight, and the draft pushed at their backs, steady and cool, and Hask held up the lamp.

The flame stood.

It stood at the mouth, and it stood at twenty paces, and at the fork where the second door hung it leaned toward the gap under the canvas and stood there too. It stood at forty. It stood at forty-six, where Emeric had closed his hand on a stone in the dark three months before with his chest on fire, and he walked past the place now breathing, with a lamp, behind a stranger in a chancery coat, and found that his hands were not quite steady and put them in his pockets.

The face was at the end. Hask's men had cut it back a yard in the last month under his eye, a little at a time, and the stone in it did not look like much by lamplight: black, blocky, flecked through with points that caught the flame and held it. It did not look like the most valuable ground in the south. It looked like a wall at the bottom of a hole.

Fenner put his hand flat on it, and kept it there for some time.

"It is lighter than it should be," he said at last. "The whole face. You can feel it lean."

"Yes."

"I had been told that. I had not believed it." He took his hand away and looked at the palm, as though something might have come off on it. "Very well. Take it down."

They took down what the day needed, and no more.

That had been settled at the table in Kaldo before anybody rode anywhere, and settled by Ferra, who had brought one figure to it: the things already on the year's column that the ore and the tolls could not carry. The rest of Fletcher's hull, and the spare section. The roof on the hospital. One hundred and sixty Gold Rand, and nothing else. Saban had asked why not more, since the stone was there and the Crown was there and the buyers were there. Emeric had said that every pound that came out of that face was a pound that would never be in it again, and that a stock was not a flow however much it felt like one on the day you opened it. Saban had said that it felt very much like one. Emeric had said yes, that was the danger.

Hask's men cut it in the afternoon, in pieces the size of a man's fist, with the assessor sitting on an upturned basket by the fork writing down every stroke, and it went up the shaft in a canvas sling to the pithead. Twelve pieces. None of them heavy for their size, and every man who carried one said the same thing, which was that it felt as though it wanted to be carried.

The weighing was in the tally house, and that was where the two sets of weights disagreed.

Fenner's weigher unlocked his chest and set out the Crown's weights: brass, old, each stamped with the chancery's mark and a number, nested in a velvet tray, the pound and its halves and quarters and its ounces. Beside them, on the same table, Ilsa set out Kaldo's: iron, a year old, cast by Bram off the master pound, which was the water in a brass cup cut from the master foot, and which the rag clerk at the keep stair had been paying by since the end of the summer before.

They put the Crown's pound on one pan of the scale and Kaldo's on the other, as a courtesy, before they began, because nobody had ever thought to do it.

Kaldo's pound went down.

Not far. The weigher, who was very good, found the difference with his smallest brass and then with a pinch of shot, and it came to a little over a twentieth. Kaldo's pound was heavier than the king's by a little over a twentieth of itself.

The weigher looked at the iron for some time without any expression at all.

"Yours is wrong," he said.

"It comes off our foot," said Emeric, "and our foot comes off an iron bar in a case in the foundry yard, and nobody's ever held it against yours till now." He looked at the brass in its velvet. "Neither of them's wrong. They're two pounds."

"There is one pound," said the weigher. "The king's."

"Then we'll use the king's," Emeric said. "The price is in the king's pound. The fifth is a fifth whatever you weigh it in. Use yours for all of it. Ilsa will enter ours beside it."

Ilsa had already ruled the page. She did it in two columns, the Crown's weight on the left and Kaldo's on the right, and as each piece came off the scale she entered it twice: once in the weigher's brass, and then, after he had lifted it off, once more on Kaldo's iron, which Emeric weighed himself on the same pans. Twelve pieces. Twenty-four lines. She did not look up once.

At the end she ran down both columns and did a sum at the foot of each, and then a third sum beside them, and sat looking at it.

"It's the same," she said.

The weigher looked at her.

"The difference," Ilsa said. "Piece by piece. Every line on the right is short of the line on the left by the same part. Not near it. The same, every time, to the ounce." She turned the ledger round so that he could see it. "If either of the sets were bad, it would wander. It doesn't wander. They're both good. They're just not each other."

The weigher read down the page for a long time. He was not a man who liked what he was reading, and he was also a man who weighed things for a living, and in the end the second thing won.

"Matched," he said, grudgingly, and Ilsa wrote the word at the foot of the page in her small upright hand, and underlined it, and the assayer who had not been introduced as one looked at the iron pound for a moment as though he might like to take it home.

Twelve pounds and a half by the Crown's weight. The fifth came off the top: two pounds and a half, in three pieces, chosen by the assessor and not by anybody from Kaldo, wrapped by the Crown's own clerk in the Crown's own linen and sealed with the Crown's seal on the tally-house table. Fenner wrote a receipt for it in his own hand, and read it aloud, and signed it, and sealed it, and gave it to Ferra, who read it twice and put it inside her document case without a word.

Then the writ.

Fenner read it out at the tally-house table with the stone on it in its linen, to Emeric and Ferra and Saban, and Bryda at the end of the table, and Vantic, who had come up the road with them because a sale of this size would be a line in her report and she would rather see it than read about it. The Crown accepted the declaration. The Crown accepted the fifth, on the terms proposed, taken on what was raised as it was raised, and would send an agent to be present at every raising or would appoint one in the barony, at its choosing. And the Crown reserved to itself, for the term of the holder's charter, the right of first purchase of any gravity stone sold out of the barony, at a price to be named by the Crown's assessor, lot by lot.

Fenner put the writ down.

"The first lot," he said. "The Crown's price, today, for clean stone by the Crown's weight, is two Platinum Rand the pound."

Nobody at the table said anything for a moment.

Saban was the first, and he said it to the table and not to the assessor, quietly, the way a merchant thinks aloud.

"It means there's one buyer," he said. "Every other buyer in the kingdom stands behind a man who never has to bid. He names the price and we take it or we don't sell. The Circle pays five, I've heard. It'll never pay five again, not to us." He turned his hand over. "It's a ceiling. A good one, today. Every day after, it's a ceiling."

Ferra did not look up from the copy of the writ that Fenner's clerk had made for her.

"It says offered first," she said. "It doesn't say sold. If the Crown declines a lot, the lot's ours to sell where we like. And it says the Crown's assessor names the price." She put a finger on a line. "It doesn't say how long the Crown has to answer. There's no term on the offer. A lot could sit in our strongroom waiting on a yes for as long as somebody in the capital wanted it to." She made a small mark in the margin. "That's the hole."

Emeric had been looking at the three pieces in the Crown's linen.

"It means nobody in this kingdom gets gravity stone from Kaldo except out of the king's hand," he said. "Every duke who wants a hull, every house that wants a carriage, the Circle, anybody. They go to the chancery, not to me. The Crown decides who has it." He looked at Fenner. "Nobody can ever say I armed anybody. That's worth a great deal to me. And nobody can ever buy from me without the Crown knowing who, and when, and how much. That's worth a great deal to somebody else."

Fenner inclined his head very slightly, and did not say which part of that he agreed with, and wrote something in the black book.

"The Crown pays first purchase by bill," said Vantic, from the end of the table. "Not coin. At ninety days."

Everybody looked at her.

She had not been asked. She did not look as though she wished she had not spoken, but she looked, for a moment, as though she had heard herself do it. Fenner glanced up from his book, and at her, and then at the writ, and after a moment turned the last sheet over and found the line, near the foot, in small hand: payment by bill of the Crown upon its bankers, at ninety days.

"That's so," Fenner said, and looked at her again, a little longer, and wrote nothing.

Vantic opened her own book and wrote in it and did not look up.

Ferra wrote something too, small, in the back of hers.

They sold eight pounds by the Crown's weight. Sixteen Platinum Rand, one hundred and sixty Gold, the year's need to the coin, by a bill of the Crown at ninety days, which Fenner wrote out and signed and which Ferra read four times. The rest of the lot, two pounds of clean stone, went back into its linen and into the Kaldo chest, on which the Crown's fifth had now been paid and receipted, and which was therefore, from that hour, the barony's own.

Fenner left in the morning, with his eleven, and the Crown's fifth in the locked chest on a led horse, and eight pounds of gravity stone that belonged to the king.

At the landing, when the horses were already brought round, he took a letter out of the inside of his coat and handed it to Emeric.

"I was asked to carry this," he said. "By His Highness. He was particular that it should come by my hand." He looked at the letter and not at Emeric. "His Highness was kind enough to recommend me for this commission."

He mounted, and rode, and did not look back.

Emeric read it on the road down. It was not long. It was in Corbin's own hand, which was large and easy and very good, and it was warm. It said that the whole court had heard, and was delighted. It said that their father had smiled when the return was read at council, which their father did not often do, and that Corbin had told him, in front of everybody, that he had always said his little brother would find his feet in the end, and that everybody had laughed. It said that Corbin had asked the chancery to send its most careful man, so that nothing could ever be said afterward about the valuation, since there were always people who would say things. It said that he hoped to visit the south one day, and see this wonderful stone for himself. It said that he was, as ever, Emeric's loving brother.

Every line of it was a kindness, and every kindness had been paid out in public, in council, where people would remember it, the way Corbin had always paid them. There was nothing in the letter that could be answered, and nothing that could be quoted against the man who wrote it, and Emeric read it twice and understood it completely.

Corbin knew what was under the hill. Corbin had chosen the man who wrote down the date. And Corbin wanted him to know both.

He folded it and gave it to Ward to keep, and they rode on.

The strongroom at Kaldo was a vault under the keep that had held the barony's coin since before there was any, and for most of ten years it had held very little. It held, now, the reserve in coin that the loan table had put there, two quarters' service that was never to be spent. It held the sealed receipt for the Crown's fifth, and the Crown's bill for sixteen Platinum at ninety days, in a flat case of their own. And it held a chest.

Ferra entered it all by lamplight, standing, with the ledger on the shelf by the door, and Emeric stood in the doorway and watched her do it.

Raised, twelve pounds and a half, by the Crown's weight; eleven pounds and fourteen ounces, by Kaldo's. The Crown's fifth, two pounds and a half, delivered, receipted. Sold at first purchase, eight pounds, sixteen Platinum, by bill. Retained, two pounds, clean, the Crown's share paid.

She drew her line under it, and wrote the net at the foot of the column, and looked at it.

Two pounds. It was not very much. It sat in the chest in the lamplight in two black pieces, flecked through, each the size of a man's fist, and it was his, lawfully, free and clear, with a king's receipt to prove it. It was his to use. It was his to build with.

He had not the least idea how.

Four stones under a carriage that held and did not lift. One old man on a bank who had lifted a boat for four minutes and been useless for the rest of the day. Eight years of drills in water and air and stone, every night, and every ounce he had ever held up carried on himself, never once through a stone, because it had never left him short. He could explain to anybody how a propeller bit water and why a boiler failed, and he did not know how to make this lift a pebble.

Ferra had finished the column. She was waiting, with the pen above the next line, where the stone's keeper would be entered, the one who would answer for what was taken out of that chest and why.

"Who has the key to it?" she said.

He looked at the two black pieces for a long moment. Then he told her, and she wrote it in the column: the keeper of the stone, and the man he was going to have to ask.

Armand.
