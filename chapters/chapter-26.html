Chapter 26 - Nine Divisions

He did not announce anything, and the not announcing was the whole of what he had learned that year.

A year ago he would have had the west farms in the market square inside a fortnight, with a drawing. He had the fix in his hand by the end of the first hour he had spent looking at those fields, and the fix was correct, and a correct fix delivered from a set of steps to four hundred people had already cost this town a season and rather more than a season.

So he found out who the farmers were that other farmers watched, and he went and argued with those three men, in their own barns, across most of a winter.

It took eleven weeks. It would have taken an afternoon to say it.

The barns were cold in a way the castle was not cold. A castle is cold like stone. A barn in February is cold like standing outside with a roof over you, and every one of those conversations was had by two men with their hands inside their coats, breathing visibly, in a smell of old straw and cattle that had not been there for eleven years and had not come out of the walls.

Maddock was the first and the hardest. Thirty-one years on the same ground west of the bridge, a face like a boot, and an entirely justified suspicion of anyone arriving with a plan.

Oren was the second and was worse to argue with because he was polite about it. He agreed with everything, at the time, and then did not do any of it, and Emeric had to come back three times before he worked out that this was how the man said no.

The third was Geddes, who held the sourest ground on the west shore and had been waiting most of his adult life for somebody to tell him it was not his fault.

There was a hand in Maddock's barn on the second visit feeding a flail by hand, working steadily through a heap of last year's thin barley, who did not look up when a prince came in and did not stop when a prince left. Emeric registered him as a man who was good at a boring job and thought nothing else about him at all.

His name was Farris.

The argument itself was not complicated and Emeric spent eleven weeks failing to make that be the point.

Kaldo farmed two fields. One in grain, one lying open, and the open one doing nothing all season except being rained on and then going back into grain the following spring. The same grain, in the same ground, year after year, since before anybody currently alive had held a plow.

What he wanted was nine divisions. Nine at the least, and the number mattered less than the shape of it. Grain. Roots. Clover and beans, which was the part nobody believed. Pasture. Fallow. In a sequence, cut into the layout of the field itself rather than bolted onto the side of it, so that a man walking his own ground could see where he was in the cycle without having to remember.

The clover and beans took four weeks on their own.

He could not tell them why it worked. He knew that it worked. He knew what he had been taught about it in a life with laboratories in it, and none of that vocabulary existed here, and he was not going to stand in a barn and say the word nitrogen to a man who had thirty years of evidence that beans were something you ate.

So he did the thing he had been doing since the wells, which was to stop explaining and start offering. Two acres. Geddes's worst two acres, which Geddes was prepared to lose because he had been losing them annually for a decade. Beans in the spring, grain the year after, and Emeric would pay him the difference if it came in worse.

Geddes said that he would take the coin now if it was all the same. Emeric said that it was not.

The least popular instruction of the entire winter was the manure, and it was also the one that did the most work.

It was piled where it was convenient, which meant near the byre, which meant near the house, which meant that the richest thing in the barony sat in a heap four feet from a door and rotted into the same forty square feet of ground for a generation.

He wanted it carted. Out, across, and spread, onto the divisions that needed it, on a rota, in winter, by men who were already cold and already tired and who would rather do almost anything else.

There is no clever version of that argument. He made the boring one eleven times and lost it eleven times and then Maddock did it on his own ground without saying anything about it, in the last week of February, and the thing went around the west farms in four days.

He lost two of the arguments outright and he was happier about those than about any of the ones he won.

He lost on drainage. He had the trench lines running along the low edge of Maddock's north corner, which was correct by the fall of the land and by every principle he had, and Maddock said no, and said it flatly, and said that the water sat in that corner in March and had sat in it in March for thirty-one years and would sit in it this March, and that the fall of the land did not come into it because there was something under that corner.

Emeric asked what was under it.

Maddock said that he did not know what was under it. He knew where the water sat. He had known since he was nine.

Emeric moved the lines.

He lost on timing. He had the clover going in on the third division at a date that had come out of somewhere in his own head, from information that had been collected on another continent in another world by people who had never seen this lake, and Oren said, politely, agreeing with everything, that it wanted another fortnight here.

Emeric asked him why, three different ways, and got three versions of the same answer, which was that it did.

Emeric gave it another fortnight.

He was visibly pleased about both of these for the rest of the month, and Rylan, who had been on every one of those rides, found it insufferable and said so on the road back from Geddes's place.

"You have lost two arguments to men who cannot tell you why they are right and you have been unbearable about it since Tuesday."

"They can tell me why. They cannot tell me in words I brought with me."

"That is the same sentence with more in it."

"It is not." Emeric considered how to put it and failed, which he was beginning to think might be the honest form. "If those two men had agreed with me about everything, I would be building a farm out of things I remembered. I would rather build one out of things that are true here."

Rylan said that this was exactly the sort of remark he meant, and they rode the rest of it in a reasonably companionable silence.

The third and fifth divisions wanted livestock and Kaldo did not have any.

Everything with four legs in this barony had gone into a pot in the plague years along with everything else, and what had survived was old, and what had bred since then had bred badly, and the whole standing herd of the west farms was about what one decent holding in Milford kept for its own table.

Milford had cattle one day west and had been selling Kaldo beef for eleven years without much interruption, on the entirely reasonable grounds that a town which has stopped producing anything still eats.

So the first diplomatic act of Emeric's tenure was not a treaty, a marriage, a threat or a scheme.

It was buying cows.

It went fine.

Paxton's factor was a broad, cheerful man named Dunmore who met them at a yard outside a market town at the appointed hour, had the stock ready, walked them through it without hurrying, charged a price that Ferra checked twice against three years of Milford's own sale records and found to be exactly correct, and took the barony's money.

He asked one question about Kaldo, which was whether the road past the west farms had been mended, and when Emeric said that it had he said that was good, because his carters had complained about it for a decade.

Nobody tried to poison anyone. Nobody arrived with an escort. Nobody made a speech about the future of the region.

Emeric rode back behind a string of cattle in the late afternoon and said, out loud, that it had been the most restful afternoon he had had since he arrived in this kingdom.

Ward did not disagree with him, which after eight months Emeric had learned to read as enthusiastic assent.

Paxton himself did not appear and did not send anybody who mattered. What came back, ten days later, was a short letter, correct in every particular, acknowledging the transaction, wishing the barony of Kaldo well, and containing nothing whatsoever.

Emeric read it twice looking for the thing under it and concluded, eventually, that there was no thing under it. Paxton was waiting to see what he was. He had a barony with no debt and a neighbor who had been dying for eleven years and had suddenly started buying cattle, and the correct move was to sell him the cattle and wait.

It was exactly what Emeric would have done and he respected it and found it mildly annoying.

The survey ran through the last week of February and the first two of March, in mud, and it was the best time he had had in Randillia.

Men walking a field with pegs and a rope. The rope was a hundred and twenty feet and every foot of it had come off the iron rule bolted to the bracket in the castle yard, because copies of copies were forbidden and had been since November, and two of the crew had walked up the hill to do the measuring themselves rather than take somebody's word for a length.

The divisions got cut and marked. The fall of the land got measured from the split at the head of the island all the way down the western branch to the joining below the town, which nobody in the history of this barony had ever had a reason to do. The trench lines got pegged along the grade.

It was the first time anything in Kaldo had been laid out to a common measure. Half the crew had come out of the parish hall class, and it went faster than it had any right to go, because a man who can read a figure does not have to be told the same figure four times.

Emeric stood at the top of the west slope on the second morning and watched two men have a long and increasingly personal argument about whether a peg was in the right place, using a word he had taught them in November, in a hall, with a piece of chalk.

Neither of them looked up. Neither of them knew he was there.

He stood in the mud for about four minutes being purely and uncomplicatedly happy, which had not happened to him since before the road.

Ferra came out on the third day because somebody had to record the divisions against the tithe map, and the two of them walked the grade together for most of an afternoon and spent nearly all of it disagreeing about the ninth division.

Her case was arithmetic and her case was correct. The acreage did not support nine. By her figures the ninth division came out at a size that cost more in edge, in turning, in cartage and in walking than it returned, and she had the numbers with her, and she had done them twice.

Emeric conceded it. He said so plainly and did not hedge.

Then he argued for cutting it anyway.

"If the rotation has a gap in it the first year, it has a gap in it forever. Not because the ground cares. Because the men do. The ninth is the one everybody can see is optional, and the moment one part of it is optional the whole thing is a suggestion, and in four years there will be two fields again and nobody will be able to say when it happened."

She thought about that for about a hundred yards of muddy track.

"That is not an agricultural argument."

"No."

"It is better than the agricultural argument." She made a mark. "I am recording that you conceded the acreage."

"Record it."

Neither of them enjoyed losing and both of them enjoyed the afternoon, and they came down off the west slope at dusk still arguing about something else entirely.

Petram was on the pegging crews under guard.

The work was hard and the weather was worse and nobody spoke to him. Not out of cruelty. Out of the ordinary human arrangement in which a man who has been put in front of forty people and named is simply not spoken to, and that is the end of the matter, and it does not need organizing.

Rylan would not work the same stretch of ground. He never said anything about it and never made any sort of point of it. He was simply always at the other end of the field, and the crew bosses worked it out inside a week and stopped putting them together, and nobody ever discussed why.

Corliss came on rest days.

He walked out from the lower ward, which is a good distance in March, and sat with his cousin for an hour or two in whatever shelter there was, and by every account the two of them did not say a great deal.

The coming was the point. Emeric had understood that much when he arranged it and it was the only part of the arrangement he had been right about.

He went out once a month himself and asked the same three questions.

He never got very much in one visit. That was the design. A man gives you nothing when you empty him in an afternoon and he gives you a little every month for a year if there is nothing else in his month.

How they had found him. What the approach had looked like from the inside. Who had spoken first, and what had been offered, and in what order, and what the second conversation had been like compared to the first.

By the end of the year Emeric had a working picture of how the organization recruits, which is the single most valuable thing anybody in this kingdom possesses about it, and which was worth precisely nothing, because his only source was a confessed assassin who had already testified once and been sentenced for it, and no crown court in Randillia would hear a second word out of that mouth.

So he kept it in his own book, in his own hand, and added to it every month, and told nobody.

By the middle of March there was a field on the west shore laid out in nine divisions to a foot that had not existed a year ago, with the trench lines pegged along a grade nobody had ever measured, and cattle in a byre for the first time since the plague.

And standing in the middle of it with a peg in one hand and a guard forty feet away was the only man in the barony who knew how Emeric's enemies found the people they used.
