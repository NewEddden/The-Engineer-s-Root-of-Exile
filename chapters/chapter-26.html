Chapter 26 - Grain for Stone

Grayrock sat higher than Kaldo and poorer than it had any right to be, and Emeric read the poverty off the town before he read a single face in it, because the town he could read.

He came up the river road with Ward at his side and Saban a length behind, and no colours flying, because a prince riding into the town whose baron had died attacking him did not announce himself with banners if he wanted to leave again.

The hills closed in as the road climbed, bare grey stone shouldering up through thin grass, the kind of country that grew ore and not much else. He could see the mine works from a mile off, the head-frames and the spoil heaps and the water running grey out of the washing sheds.

He could see, too, in the way a systems man sees, that the works were running at half of what they were built to run. Half the head-frames idle. Spoil heaps gone to weed. A washing shed with its wheel stopped and nobody troubling to start it.

This was a town that had been bleeding hands for a generation, to the old war and the plague and a baron who skimmed the veins until no wage was worth going down the shaft for, and the spring's muster had taken the last of what the mine still had. A mine is only ever as deep as the number of men willing to go down it, and Grayrock had run short of willing men long before Renner spent the rest of them on Emeric's wall.

That much he could see clean. The mine told him the truth about itself the way a ledger did, without wanting anything from him, and he trusted it.

It was the hall he did not trust, and the woman in it, because a woman was not a mine and would not tell him the truth about herself just by being looked at. He had learned that about himself the hard way often enough to ride the last mile with his certainty turned all the way down.

Her name was Bryda Renner, and she received him in a hall built to look richer than the town could afford and left unkept since the season her husband died.

She did not rise when he came in. That was the first thing, and Emeric filed it and did not know what to make of it, which was the trouble in a single gesture. A lord's widow keeping her seat before a prince could be an insult, or a grief too heavy to stand under, or a woman too tired to perform a courtesy she did not feel. He could not tell which. A year ago he would have picked one and been certain of it and been wrong.

"You killed my husband," she said, before he had finished sitting, in a voice with nothing in it he could get a grip on. Not heat. Not quite cold either. A fact set on the table between them because leaving it unsaid would have been the lie that poisoned everything after.

"He came over a wall I was standing on," Emeric said. "I defended it. I won't tell you I'm sorry he's dead, because you'd know it for a courtesy and think less of me for spending it. He rode up my road to take my barony and my life and he lost. That's the whole of it, and I think you know it's the whole of it, or you'd have had me killed on the way in instead of letting me sit down."

Something moved in her face and he could not name it.

That was the maddening thing, sitting across a scarred table from the woman he had widowed. He could feel her running a calculation as surely as he could feel a rune resolving, and could not read a single term of it. Ward, beside him, would have a read. Ward read people the way Emeric read stone. But Ward would not say it here, and Emeric had to make the deal with his own bad eyes, and knew it.

"No," she agreed. "I'd not have let you sit." She let that stand a moment. "Say what you came to say. You didn't ride up here through my dead men's country to talk about my husband. You want something out of these hills, same as everyone who's ridden up this road since Renner went and got himself killed. The difference is you're the only one who's come to my door in person, and a man who comes in person wants a thing he can't take by letter. So. What is it."

He laid it out plainly, because plain was the only register that worked on someone who could smell a courtesy and hold it against him.

"Ore. The high-grade veins, the crystal and the good iron, at a fair standing price, exclusive to Kaldo. In return, grain."

"You've a town that mines and doesn't farm, sitting in stone country that won't feed it, and you came out of a war short of hands and short of the season's planting both. I've a barony that farmed a surplus this year and needs stone it can't dig. You need to eat. I need to build. Each of us has the thing the other can't make. That's not a favour I'm doing you and it's not a favour you're doing me. It's the plainest trade there is. Grain for stone."

"And exclusive," she said. "Meaning I sell to you and not to the men who've been offering above your price all season to keep it from you."

"Meaning that. Yes."

She looked at him for a long moment, and he made himself sit in it and not fill it.

The old Emeric would have filled it. Would have walked her through every reason the deal was sound, laid out the arithmetic until she had no room left to say no and no reason left to say yes freely. He had learned that a deal argued into a corner was a deal that came apart the first hard season. He wanted her to choose it rather than be cornered into it, so he shut his mouth and let her do the sum herself.

"You know what you're asking me to do," she said at last. "The others, Sethe, the coast money, the ones leaning on you, they're a known thing. I know exactly what they are. They'll squeeze this town for its ore and leave it the poorer, same as the crown's leeches did before them, same as my own husband did when he skimmed the veins and called it a baron's due. Bad, but I know the shape of the bad. I could live inside it, the way this town's lived inside it for forty years."

She turned a ring on her finger, the only tell he could catch and could not read.

"You're the unknown. You might be a real partner. You might be nothing. You might be worse than all of them and better dressed. I don't know which, because nobody knows which. That's what you're asking. Trade a known bad for an unknown that might be good."

"Yes," Emeric said, because it was exactly right and dressing it up would have insulted her. "That's what I'm asking."

"Then why would I."

"Because you already have the one thing that makes it not a coin flip."

He leaned in. This part he could do, because this part was not reading her. It was telling her a true thing about the world.

"You fought me. Your men came up my road on a picture that said Kaldo was a broke plague town with a mad prince, ripe for the taking. They found a wall two dozen men held against a couple hundred, and a farm where a ruin should have been, and a town that beat you."

"You didn't hear about my turnaround secondhand from a merchant with a reason to lie. You saw it. It cost you your husband to see it. Everything the coast money doesn't know about me, everything the dispatches keep the capital from knowing, you already know, because you paid to learn it in the field. You're not gambling on a stranger. You're gambling on the man who did the thing you watched with your own eyes. That's not blind. That's the best-informed bet anyone in this kingdom could make about me, and you're the only one holding it."

She took the deal.

She took it the way she had said the thing about her husband, flatly, a fact placed on the table, no warmth in it and no pretence of warmth. A widow choosing the better bet for a broken town because the sum came out that way and not because she had decided to like the man across from her.

Emeric had not asked her to like him. He had asked her to count, and she had counted, and the count had landed on him, and that was worth more than liking, because liking soured and a good sum held.

They set the terms, Saban and the widow's mine-master doing the real work of it, standing prices and delivery weights and who carted what down which road, while Emeric watched and Bryda watched and the two of them said little. It was done inside an afternoon, because both sides needed it and neither side had the luxury of pretending otherwise.

They rode down out of the hills in the last of the light with the ore problem solved that had haunted Kaldo since the first pour. The supply that had been a money problem wearing a supply problem's coat, the veins under a hill that had not belonged to him, all of it turned in a single afternoon into a standing trade that fed a hungry town and armed a growing one.

The coalition had a hole in it now, its weakest member peeled off and standing on Kaldo's side of the line, and the men still leaning on him had lost the stone they had been counting on to keep him weak.

Saban was in a talkative mood on the road down, the way he got when a deal had closed clean and there was nothing left to worry at. He ran through the cartage, and the two bridges that would want work before winter, and a factor in the capital who had married badly, and then, without any change in tone, something his road men had brought back that had no bearing on anything.

"Odd thing about the lending houses," he said. "Ducatis send factors all over the kingdom, dozens of them, and when they come into Randillia City they queue at the gate like anyone. Except there's one or two that get met. Senior partners, out at the gate, in the rain. My man's watched it twice now and can't tell me why. Same house, same road, and some of them get met."

"Family, maybe," Ward said.

"Family don't work under a factor's name," Saban said, and shrugged, and moved on to the bridges.

Emeric was somewhere else and let it go past him, and it should have felt like a clean day's work. It did not, and he made himself sit with why, because the wrongness of a win was information and he had learned to read it.

The deal rested on a handshake with a woman he could not read. That was the whole of the discomfort, and it was the courier road again in a different country.

He had built a seal no forger could copy and a fold no interceptor could fake, and then he had handed the sealed letter to a man he could not be sure of, because the paper was the part he could make perfect and the person was not.

Here it was again. The trade was sound, the arithmetic clean, the sum held. And under all of it sat Bryda Renner, who had every reason in the world to hate him and had shaken his hand anyway, and he could not tell, riding down out of her hills, whether he had made a partner or bought a season's grace from someone counting a different sum than the one she had shown him.

She had not mentioned the deeper veins. She had not mentioned much of anything about the hill beyond the standing stock, and he had taken the omission for a widow's economy of words, a woman saying only what the deal required.

He filed it as nothing. It was the kind of nothing he was worst at reading, and he did not yet know it was one.

He had solved the stone. He had not solved the woman. And the difference between those two, he was starting to understand, was the difference that was going to decide everything the arithmetic could not reach.
