Chapter 26 - The Four Days

Rylan came in off the Wayford road at noon on the eighth day, and he did not get down off the horse.

"Say it from up there if it's quicker," Emeric said.

"Five hundred and twenty. Give me forty either way and I'll not argue." He was grey around the mouth and he had ridden the last stretch in daylight, which he had not done once all summer. "Two hundred and fifty off Grayrock and I've counted them twice because I didn't believe it either. A hundred and twenty out of Wayford, household men and about thirty hired. The hundred and fifty on the plain out of Milford haven't moved and haven't struck camp and haven't sent a man forward."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Two hundred and fifty," Ward said. "Off a hill with four thousand people on it."

"They're not farmers." Rylan finally swung down. "It's the workings. He's emptied the workings. Every man off the face and every man off the sorting floor and the carters with them, and there's not a stalk of grain touched on that side because miners don't cut grain."

Emeric heard the arithmetic land and then heard the second half of it land underneath.

"That's why he can do it in August," he said.

"That's why he can do it in August."

He went up onto the wall on his own for a quarter of an hour afterward, which he had promised himself he would stop doing and had not.

Ward had said nine days if Renner was in a hurry and fourteen if he wanted everybody in one place, and Ward had been right about both, and Ward had been right about the grain, and it had not once occurred to Emeric on the rise or in the long room or at any point in the six days of cutting to ask Ward how many. He had asked when. He had built every hour of the last eight days out of when, and when was the only part of it that Ward had been guessing about, and the number Ward had never been asked was the number that was standing on the Wayford road.

Two hundred was a thing he had costed. He had costed it in March in the margin of a drawing, badly, in a hand he could barely read now, against a wall that had not existed yet.

"You'd have built it the same," Ward said, from four paces behind him, having arrived the way he arrived.

"I'd have built it the same and I'd have known what I was building it against, which is a different man standing on it."

"Aye. Well." Ward looked out at the road. "Now you know. You've got four days of knowing, which is more than anybody gave me at nineteen."

They dressed forty men out of a pile.

That was the part Emeric had not expected to be the thing he remembered. He had spent five weeks watching harnesses come off the benches and had thought of them as suits, and they were not suits, they were plates, and every plate in the building had been cut to a bar of iron on a wall on the charging floor, and the consequence of that arrived on the eighth day in a way no drawing of his had predicted.

They did not fit men into suits. They laid the whole of it out on the floor of the lower ward in rows by kind, greaves with greaves and pauldrons with pauldrons, and put a man in the middle of it, and built him.

Tarrow's eldest, who had spent a summer standing in a warm shed with his fist in a pile of grain, was thin through the shoulder and long in the arm and would not have fitted into any single one of the twenty-four harnesses that came off the bench in July. He fitted into a harness assembled out of four of them in about eleven minutes.

"That's the stick," Bram said. He had come down from the foundry with the last six sets in a cart and had not slept, and he said it the way he said everything, which was as though he were finishing a sentence he had started some months ago.

"That's the stick."

"I called it madness for a morning."

"You called it madness for a morning and then you filed the bar for a day and a half, which is the only part that ended up mattering." Emeric watched them build the next man. "I thought I was buying spares. I was buying sizes and I didn't know it."

The last six sets were not finished and he made a point of saying so out loud, twice, in the ward, so that nobody could tell themselves otherwise later. Two had no gorget. One had a pauldron that had come out of the quench wrong and had gone back in and come out grey, and Bram had put it on the cart anyway with a chalk mark on it, and had told Emeric to his face that he would not wear it himself and that it was better than cloth.

Forty men in Kaldo steel. Thirty-six plain and stamped and interchangeable, and four with a crystal in a sealed housing and a breaker in the coupling that Bram had fought him over for four days in June.

Which left the crossbows, and the crossbows left the arithmetic he had been walking around since the seventh day.

He did it in the yard, in daylight, with Ward and Imani and Ferra standing where people could see them standing.

"Sixty crossbows," he said. "There's no armour behind them. There was never going to be armour behind them, and I want that said before I read any names, because I'm not going to stand here in four days and have anybody hear it for the first time."

He read the names.

He had written them the night before with the muster roll and Ferra's household book and Imani's list of the twenty-six families side by side, and he had struck out every man who was the only working adult in a house, and every man with a child under two, and every one of the eleven Imani had written to in the spring, and he had got to fifty-one and then had to go back and put four of them in again because fifty-one was not sixty.

Nessa's brother was one of the four.

"You'll want to know why him and not somebody else," Emeric said, to the yard, "and the answer is that he has a wife who isn't well and no child under two, and the man I took off the list to make room for him has a child of eight months. That's the whole of the reason. It isn't a good reason and there isn't a better one, and if anybody wants it changed, come to me tonight and I'll change it, and then somebody else stands here instead."

Nobody came that night. Two came the next morning, both of them to volunteer, and one of them was Maren's elder son, who had stood in a field in June and told Emeric the machine took it off them.

Corliss came up to the castle on the ninth day with his arm out of the strapping and his coat over his shoulder.

"Six weeks," he said.

"Eight."

"Six is enough to hold a wall."

"Eight was your number." Emeric had the page in front of him and did not need to look at it. "You gave it to me in the long room with your arm open and Imani standing behind you, and I wrote it down because you said it, and I'm not going to be the man who kept the number when it suited him and lost it when it didn't."

"That's a clerk's answer."

"It's the only kind I've got that survives being written down." He put the pen down. "You told me where they'd come. Ward's got the four best men in this barony standing at the joint because you said put your best four there and don't let anybody talk you out of it, and I haven't, and I won't, and that's yours whatever else happens. You'll be in your house, and it'll be the worst four hours of your life, and I'd rather you hated me for it than have you on that wall two weeks short with a hundred and twenty Wayford men coming up a graded slope."

Corliss stood there for a while.

"Sixteen years I bought boxes for people out of a column you didn't know about," he said. "You put a line in the book for it and you took me off a roster in front of fifty-seven men and now you're keeping me off it for my own good. I'd like it noted that I can't tell any more whether you're the best thing that's happened to this town."

"Neither can I."

"Aye. Well." He picked the coat up off his shoulder and put it on properly. "Then we're the same amount of use to each other."

Imani asked for the church on the tenth day and he said no.

"It's the biggest roof in the barony," she said. "It's stone, it's got a floor I can wash, it's forty paces from the north well and it holds two hundred people standing. I've got beds for nine. Nine, and four of those are the ones I keep for the birthing."

"I know what it holds."

"Then say the objection, because I've been up since three and I've not got the patience to guess at it."

"The objection is that if I walk up that path and ask Doran for his floor four days before Renner comes over the rise, then by suppertime every one of eighteen hundred people in this town knows that the prince has asked for somewhere to put the bodies." Emeric said it flat. "They've watched me build a wall and a foundry and a machine that threshes and they've watched me put forty men in steel, and they've mostly decided to believe it, and they've decided it in a town that lost four hundred men to a levy and doesn't hand that out twice. I can spend that. I can spend it on one thing. I'm not spending it on a floor I can get on the fifth day."

Imani looked at him for long enough that he understood she was deciding something rather than considering it.

"You're right about the town," she said. "You're wrong about the floor."

"Say the second part."

"A ward isn't a room. It's boiled water and it's clean cloth and it's somewhere to put the dirty cloth that isn't where you put the clean, and it's four people who know which is which before anybody's bleeding on them. That takes two days and I've got four." She was already going. "So I'll do it in nine beds and a barn and it will be worse, and you'll get your floor on the fifth day, and the difference between those two things will be a number neither of us will ever know."

He sat with that for some while after she had gone, and then he wrote it down, and then he went and did the thing he had decided four days earlier and told nobody about, which was to walk up the path and ask.

Doran said yes before he had finished the sentence.

"You'll want to know what it costs you," the old man said, at the door, in exactly the voice he had used two years earlier about a fence and a field below a mill.

"I'd assumed you'd tell me."

"I will write to the diocese. I always write to the diocese." Doran's hands were folded. "You have not asked me for anything I did not expect. I have had the pews stacked against the south wall since the day the levy was called, and I did not tell you, because a priest who prepares for the crown's man to lose is a priest who has said something out loud."

The stones went in on the tenth day and the eleventh.

Three of them, sited off the distances Ferra had written down on empty ground at the far end of the eastern arm in July, in a line across the graded approach eighty paces out from the joint. Emeric walked the line four times with the plate and the readings, and moved one of them nine paces, and then moved it back.

Then he stood the four Enhanced men in a rank at the joint and had the stones lit and watched Declan's breaker throw open with a sound like a dropped latch.

"Eighty's too close," Bram said.

"Eighty's too close for the fourth man in the rank and right for the other three, which means it isn't a distance, it's a falloff, and I've measured it on empty ground with nobody standing in it." Emeric had the plate in both hands and was not happy. "Ferra. Write down that the numbers came off an empty field and that I knew that when I sited them."

"I have written it," Ferra said. "I wrote it in July."

He moved the stones out to a hundred and ten and lost most of the effect on the approach for it, and got Declan's housing to hold, and stood looking at a killing ground he had cleared to a hundred and twenty paces, which fell away from the joint down the long slope, past the fence line, above the north well.

He noticed that. He noticed it the way he noticed things that year, which was fully, in one piece, with nothing to do about it, and he put it in the column and went to see about the western wall.

The western stretch was held with three men and two of the unfinished harnesses. There was no version of the four days in which it was held with more.

Two men stayed on the granary, off that same stretch, as they had since the rise.

He practised in the empty room on the last night for half an hour, the same three things, the same two hundred times.

Water pulled thin across a flame until it was a sheet a hand wide and no thicker than a nail. A blade of moving air, held flat, held still. A slab of stone the size of a door raised a hand's breadth off the floor and dropped.

None of it would have impressed anyone. He was entirely clear about that and had been since he was nine, and it was, on the last night, the single most reassuring fact available to him, because a thing that costs exactly what it costs is a thing you can count, and he had four hundred and something men coming up a slope in the morning and a very small number of spells.

Then he got the list out and left the space empty at the top and wrote underneath it:

Five hundred and twenty. I asked Ward when and I never once asked Ward how many, and he was guessing about the first and would have known the second, and the number I did not ask for is the number on the road. Two hundred was in a margin in March in my own hand. I have been building against a number I wrote down before I had a wall and I never went back to it.

Renner emptied the workings, which is why he can do this in August, which is a thing about Grayrock I have known since Saban first said the seam was thin and never once turned into an arithmetic about men.

Forty men in steel because Bram filed a bar of iron for a day and a half in June while calling it madness. I bought spares. I did not know I was buying sizes. It is the fourth time this year that the useful part of a thing I built was not the part I built it for, and Ferra's page for that is now longer than the page for anything else.

Sixty crossbows and no armour behind them, read out in the yard in daylight, and I put Nessa's brother back on the list because the man I had put in his place has a child of eight months. That is the whole reason. I have written it down so that I cannot improve it later.

Imani asked for the church and I refused her and then went and asked for it myself an hour later, having decided four days ago that I would. I told her the truth about the town and I did not tell her the truth about me. Doran has had the pews stacked against the south wall since the levy was called. Two of us prepared for the same morning and neither of us said so, and only one of us was protecting anything.

The stones are at a hundred and ten because eighty opened Declan's coupling, and the numbers I sited them off came off an empty field, and Ferra wrote that down in July when I could still have done something about it.

The approach falls away from the joint above the north well. I have cleared a hundred and twenty paces of open ground uphill of the water I spent my first season fixing, and I have nowhere else to put it, and I am writing it here so that whatever happens next, nobody including me gets to be surprised by it.
