Chapter 26 - Head of Water

The older of the two men who had laid Imani's flags was a mason, and had been one for thirty years before the war, and had not laid a stone since the plague, except to mark a grave, until Imani's floor. He did not talk much. He did not correct anybody, as far as Emeric could tell, unless something was going to fall down.

He walked out to the fallen span with Emeric and Bram in the second week of October, a mile and a half beyond the wall, along the line of the arches where they strode across the west farms with the water running in their channel overhead and the farmers carrying buckets underneath.

At the break the channel simply stopped. Two piers stood with nothing between them, and the water came to the lip of the last whole arch, hung there in a long clear sheet, and fell thirty feet into the sand. It had cut itself a pool, and the pool had cut itself a stream, and the stream wandered off across a field toward the river as though it had somewhere to be. Nearly everything the Mere had sent toward Kaldo for years had ended here, in a farmer's ditch.

Ferra had found this span in the ledgers in the summer. It had been mended twice, on paper, in two different years, and paid for both times.

The mason looked up at the two broken ends without saying anything. He walked round the nearer pier. He put his hand flat on it, high as he could reach, and then low, near the ground, and looked at his palm.

"Stone span?" Emeric said.

"Spring." The mason wiped his hand on his apron. "With twenty men and a frame."

It was the longest thing Emeric had heard him say.

So it would be timber for the winter. A flume, a trough on trestles across the gap, lined with lime plaster so it would hold water, and the stone made good when there were twenty men and a frame. Bram drew the trestles in the dirt with a stick. The carpenter came out from the exchange and looked at Bram's drawing and redrew it with his own stick, without comment, a little to the left, and Bram looked at the carpenter's version, and at his own, and nodded.

The flume took eleven days. The trestles went up first, walked out across the sand one bent at a time and braced against the wind off the farms. The trough went up on them in lengths, lifted by a dozen men off Ferra's count and a rope over the last arch. The mason lined it himself, three coats of lime plaster on the inside, and would not let anybody else touch the joints. On the ninth day a length slipped off its trestle and broke in half, and they spent the tenth building it again. On the last day the mason knocked out the clay dam at the lip of the arch, and the water came over it, and went into the trough, and along the trough, and out the far end into the channel on the other side, and kept going toward Kaldo.

The flume sagged in the middle. It leaked at three joints and wept at a fourth. It held.

The farmers under the arches stood in their field with their buckets and watched the water go over their heads in a box, a mile and a half from the wall, where it had been falling into their ditch for years. One of them said something to another. Emeric did not hear what it was. He did not need to.



At the wall the aqueduct had always ended in the keep's cistern, and the keep had taken what little reached it and let nobody else near it.

Now they tapped it before it got there. A new cistern went in against the inside of the wall, with the old channel feeding it, and only what spilled over it went on to the keep.

"The keep's cistern will run lower," Hale said, the morning they cut the channel.

"Yes."

Hale made a note of it, and of the date, and did not raise it again.

The sand filter went into the new cistern: a bed of gravel at the bottom, coarse sand over it, fine sand over that, and the water let down through the whole depth so that whatever the Mere carried, and whatever the arches and the flume had added, settled out on the way. Civil works were not his trade. He had the method out of a slim grey municipal handbook he had once read cover to cover on a train in the other life, for no reason at all, and he could see the diagram on its page as clearly as he could see the cistern in front of him, and he knew exactly how little a page knew about Kaldo.

The mason watched the first barrow of sand come up from the river and stopped it with his hand.

"Not that."

"It's sand."

"River sand. It's round." He rubbed a pinch of it between his fingers and let it fall. "Round packs. It'll choke your bed inside a month. You want it sharp." He jerked his head toward the west. "Old quarry on the Milford road. That's sharp."

Emeric thought about grain shape and packing and how the book had said sand and not which sand, and the mason was right, and the book had assumed a world where you bought filter sand graded in sacks.

They used the quarry sand.

From the wall cistern the carpenter ran pipe down the slope: elm, the trunks bored end to end with a long auger and tapered at one end to drive into the next, the joints packed and bound. He had done it before, he said, for a brewer, a long time ago. It went down through the town to a second cistern at the top of the lower ward, with a tap on it at the height of a woman's bucket, and on the way it gave off one branch, two streets above Cooper's Row, that went through the wall of the old market hall and ended in a lead spout over a stone trough in the corner beside the side door.

Imani came to look at the line of the pipe before a single length went into the ground. She walked it from the wall to the hall with the mason, and at the hall she stood by the plugged drain under the back wall with her arms folded.

"Where does the overflow go?"

He had that one ready. "Into the canal ring. And out of the ring through the old east sluice into the river, below the bridge."

She nodded once. "And this?" She put her foot on the plugged drain. "Everything that comes out of a ward. Where does it go?"

The mason had an answer for that too. A soakaway behind the hall: a pit dug deep and filled with rubble, and whatever went down it would go into the ground and be the ground's business.

Imani looked down the slope.

From the back of the hall the ground fell away toward Cooper's Row the way all the ground on that side of the town fell, and Emeric had drawn that fall in ink on Doran's table in the spring, with the dots of the dead marching down it.

"The ground's business is Cooper's Row's business," Imani said. "No."

Nobody argued. The mason looked at the slope, and then at Emeric, and Emeric said a covered drain, under the east wall, into the river below the bridge, below every intake in the barony. It meant digging the length of two streets and going under the wall. It meant nine more days.

"Nine days," Imani said, as though she were writing it on her list, and went off down the hill to whoever was waiting for her.

Ferra priced all of it against the shop: the trough, the pipe, the cisterns, the drain, the plaster, the men. The shop could carry it, and she said so, and the shop's own bars were going into the ward by the crate, which she also said, so that he would know it was paying twice.

When the water reached the wall cistern, the overflow went into the canal ring for the first time in years, and the green skin that had lain on the ring all summer began, very slowly, to move.

The drain under the east wall took its nine days. Imani walked the length of it on the tenth before she would let a bucket go down it.



Doran was on the parish step one evening near the end of the month as Emeric went by, and spoke to him for the first time since July.

"The last family went down to the hollow on Thursday, and I consecrated the ground on Sunday. You kept away, as I asked, and I wanted you to know it had been noticed."

That was all. He went back inside, and the door closed.

Emeric stood on the step a little longer than he needed to. There had been a night in the spring when Bram had stayed behind in that house after everybody else had gone, and whatever had been said in it, Emeric had never asked, and was not going to ask now.



The ward opened on the last Tuesday of October, with water in the corner.

Eleven beds stood in two lines down the long room on the chalk marks Imani had moved three times. The register lay open on a desk just inside the side door, and the first name went into it before the morning was half over: a carter's boy from Cooper's Row with a gash in his calf from a wheel rim, carried in by his father, who stood just inside the door turning his cap round and round in his hands and kept asking what it would cost, until Imani told him that it would cost him going home and getting some sleep.

There was a box on the desk beside the register, with a slot in the lid. Those who could spare something put it in, and nobody asked who had. The carter put in two coppers on his way out, and Imani did not look up.

Emeric stood at the side door and did not go in. Ward stood on the step behind him. Ward had never once come through Imani's door on his own account, and he did not come through this one on Emeric's.

The laundry hearth was in the walled yard beside the hall, built by the mason on the second day after the flume, with Bram's three kettles on it and a line strung across the yard from the hall wall to the next house. Imani's two women had it going before dawn. At midmorning a third arrived, a widow with forearms like a baker's, and said that Sela had sent her, and said nothing else, and took a paddle and started on the sheets.



Imani's yes came on the Wednesday, by a boy from the lower ward, who recited it at the keep's gate exactly as he had been given it. Emeric could come in for her morning on Thursday, at the hour, and he was to bring a sand-glass.

Hale found him one in the steward's room, small and old, that ran for about as long as a slow count to sixty, and asked no questions about what it was for.

Twenty children came up from the exchange. Imani had them in the front of the hall, on the clean flags by the door, well away from the beds, with the boy from Cooper's Row asleep at the far end of the room under a grey blanket. She told them about hands first, because it was her ward and hands came first. She made every one of them wash in the stone trough under the new spout, with Abloh's soap, and look at the water afterward, and some of them did not like what they saw in it.

Then she looked at Emeric, which meant it was his turn.

He showed them where the pulse was. Two fingers on the inside of the wrist, below the thumb, pressed lightly, not hard, because pressing hard stops the thing you are looking for. Then the neck, under the angle of the jaw, for when the wrist will not tell you.

A boy at the end could not find his own.

"Then you're dead," Emeric told him, "and you ought to lie down."

The children laughed. Imani did not.

The boy found it, on his neck, and looked so astonished that the children laughed again, and Emeric showed them the glass. Turn it over, count every beat until the sand runs out. That number is the person. Count it again in an hour, and if it has changed, something has happened, and you find out what. He let them count each other in pairs while the sand ran, and the numbers that came back were all over the place, because half of them were counting their own pulse in their own fingers. Imani went down the pairs and fixed their hands, one at a time, without a word.

He could have stopped there. It had been a good morning, and the hour was most of the way gone.

"Come outside," he said. "All of you."

Imani looked at him. Then she came too.

The laundry yard was open on one side to the cart street, and the hearth was hot under the kettles, and two of the north-well women stopped work and stood with their paddles to see what the prince wanted with their fire.

He put his hand out over the hearth.

The ring came up over his palm, small and bright in the grey morning, with the mark for fire at the center of it, and closed, and a ribbon of flame lifted off the coals under the kettle and came up into the air above his hand. The children made the sound children make.

"I didn't make that," he said. "It was already there. I'm only moving it." He turned his hand and the ribbon turned with it. "Making fire out of nothing costs about ten times what moving it does. So anybody who tells you he's a great mage because he made a fire out of nothing is telling you he's a great waster. Remember that."

He drew the ribbon together into a ball the size of a fist, and held it. A few strokes changed at the edge of the ring and the ball stretched out into a bar, as long as his forearm, and then flattened into a disc that turned on its edge like a coin. Then he sent it away from his hand and round the yard, low, at the height of a child's head, along the line of the wall, and turned it at the corner, and turned it again at the post of the drying line, and brought it back. Out on the cart street a carter had pulled his horse up to watch.

It was a small fire. Any Circle mage could have made a bigger one without looking. Fewer could have made one go round a corner.

At the end, because the children were silent and because Imani was standing with her arms folded and had not yet looked impressed, he pulled the flame up into a shape, and the shape was a small dragon, wings and neck and a long tail, no bigger than a cat, and it hung in the air over the yard and turned its head.

It held for a count of three. Then the tail began to sag, and the wings went soft at their edges, and he let it go before it could fall apart in front of them. It went back into the hearth as a long gold thread, and the kettles hissed.

The tail had brushed the drying line on its way down. There was a brown scorch the length of a hand on the corner of a sheet.

Imani looked at the sheet. Then at him.

"That's a sheet," she said. "I'll have it by Friday."

He was breathing hard. He noticed it at the same time the children did, because he put his hand on the wall of the hall to steady himself and a girl at the front saw him do it, and before he could say anything she had his wrist and her two fingers on the inside of it, the way he had shown her, and was counting.

"Fast," she said. "It's ever so fast."

Three more of them tried it and agreed. Then Imani came through them and took his wrist herself, and turned the glass over with her other hand, and counted it properly while the sand ran.

The sand ran out. Her fingers stayed on his wrist while she said the number, which was a good deal higher than the one he had shown them for a boy sitting still, and a little after that. Then she let go.

"That's what it costs," Emeric told them, when he had the breath for it. "Every bit of that came out of me. Nothing's free. Not even moving a fire round a yard."

"Can we be mages?" a boy asked.

"If you study hard," Emeric said.

It was half a lie, and he heard it as he said it. One in five hundred was born with the faculty open, and nothing he knew of opened it in the other four hundred and ninety-nine. Except that Armand's notebook said made mages existed: old knights who had lived among stones for fifteen or twenty years until the sense came awake in them. Fifteen years. A handful of men. Nobody, so far as he knew, had ever tried it on a town.

He kept that to himself. It was not a thing to say to children on a cold morning.

Another boy, a dock-quarter boy with his hands still red from the trough, asked whether men could be nurses.

"Yes," Emeric said.

"Yes," said Imani, at the same moment, and then, because she did not let anybody have a thing without the reason for it: "Some of them fight. When it hurts, they fight you, and somebody has to hold a grown man down without hurting him worse. You can't always do that with a woman's arms. You'll want men who don't mind blood and don't mind being told."

The boy took that in as though he had been offered a trade.

A girl asked what the ward was for, after. When it had more beds.

He told her what he could see, plainly, because she had asked plainly. A maternity ward, a room only for mothers, and for babies being born, with nobody coughing in it, and women whose whole work was that. Surgeons, people whose only work was cutting and closing, a wound or a belly, and who did nothing else all day and got very good at it. Anesthetists, people whose only work was to keep a patient asleep while that was done, asleep and breathing, and to bring them back up after.

"How?" the girl said.

"I don't know yet. Nobody does." He looked at the hall, at the lime and the new door and the beds he could see through it. "But it starts with a room, and somebody whose ward it is."

Nobody said anything after that. The kettles were the only sound in the yard.

Imani was looking at him.

"Miss Imani?" said a girl at the front.

Imani did not answer.

"Miss Imani?"

She turned to the girl as if she had been called from another room, and answered whatever it was the girl had wanted to know, and then she clapped her hands and had all twenty of them back inside to wash again, because they had been out in a yard. For the rest of the hour she was brisk with every one of them, and with the women at the kettles, and with Emeric, whom she sent back to the keep a little before the hour with the sand-glass in his hand and a reminder about the sheet.



Ward said nothing about it that day.

He had stood on the step through the whole of it, and had watched the ring come up and the fire go round the yard, and had watched the dragon, and had watched the children count, and on the walk back up to the keep he said nothing at all, about any of it, at the same pace as usual.

The next morning he drilled Emeric for two hours instead of one and put him on the ground twice as often, and did not explain, and Emeric did not ask.

Rylan had it from the market by the end of the week.

"They're saying you make dragons for children."

"What else are they saying?"

"That it's a good trick." Rylan considered. "One of the carters reckons he could get a crowd for you at Wayford. Two coppers a head."

Emeric thought about the pulse the children had counted, and the brown scorch on the sheet, and the sand running out. A good trick. It was the very shape of the man the capital had sent south, a prince who did tricks for children, and the market had found it for him without his lifting a finger.

"Tell him no," he said.



The tap at the top of the lower ward ran for the first time on the first of November.

There was no ceremony. The mason opened the cock at mid-morning, and water came out of it, clear and very cold, and splashed on the new flags under it, and he closed it again, nodded at it, and went away to look at something else.

By noon there was a line.

Emeric stood at the end of the lane and watched it, because there was nothing else he needed to be doing just then, and because he wanted to.

Near the end of the line was an old woman with a yoke and two buckets. He had seen her in April, at the lowest well by the south-east wall, drawing from the bottom of the slope the whole town drained into, with the only clean water in Kaldo four hundred feet up the hill at her back, behind a wall.

She came up to the tap in her turn, and set the first bucket under it, and turned the cock the way she had seen the woman in front of her do it. The water came out. She filled the bucket, and the second, and hooked them onto the yoke, and settled it on her shoulders, and went off down the hill at the pace of somebody who has carried water every day of her life and expects to carry it every day that is left.

She did not look round. She had no reason to know there was anyone at the end of the lane.

He did not go over.
