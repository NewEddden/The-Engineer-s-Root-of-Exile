Chapter 308 - Sauce

The two sauces went out to South Sentinel under Ward's seal.

They rode in the Hod's hold in two brown crocks lashed to the same ring, each lid tied down with waxed string and a blob of red wax on the knot with Ward's mark in it: the mark he put on anything in the armoury he did not want touched, and had never before, so far as anybody knew, put on food. Mira had chalked a 1 on one lid and a 2 on the other in the castle's cold store at six that morning, with Ward standing against the door, and since the Hod came off the slip she had been asked which was which nine times, by four people, one of them a duke.

"There," Mira said, the ninth time, and went back to her bench.

Emeric sat down on the long locker across from the crocks and looked at them, and could not have told them apart for a thousand Gold.

"Stop looking at them," said Imani, from the bench, with her eyes shut.

"I'm not."

"You're trying to feel which one's yours through the wax. It's tomatoes. It hasn't got a note."

It had not. He had tried. He went and sat beside her, and she put her feet in his lap without opening her eyes, which was where they had always been going.

Saban was holding the money. It was Rylan's pool, because it was always Rylan's pool; Rylan had run a pool on every question in Kaldo with two answers since the year of the suction pads, and Saban had held the stakes in a tobacco tin with a broken hinge. This one had gone up on the long room's board on Tuesday in Rylan's left hand, because his right was strapped to his chest with the burned forearm wrapped to the elbow in Imani's gauze: WHOSE SAUCE. By Thursday night every name in the kitchen, the laundry and the garrison mess was under Maren's column, and four were under His Grace's.

"Four to one against you," said Saban, pleasantly, under a straw hat nobody had seen before. "Ward, Ned, Osric, and Tess."

"I bet against Maren," said Tess, from the corner, where Osric had put her with a cushion at her back and her chair lashed in the rack above. "Maren said a woman who'd been asleep since April didn't get opinions on sauce yet."

"And Ward?"

Ward sat on the bench by the hatch in his black, with his helm left behind in Kaldo for the first time in the Hod's history and his hands on his knees where it ought to have been. He did not look round.

"Somebody had to," he said.

His father was at the port. He had stood there since the Hod lifted, looking down at the sea going by grey-green and empty to the edge of the world, and had not sat down when Ned told him he might, or when Armand offered him the bench with a little bow Armand had made to nobody since he came south. Nothing in Harrold's face had moved in thirteen years and nothing in it moved now; but Emeric, who could feel his father's note from the locker without trying, had felt it go, over that hour, from something held very still to something only still.

Harrold had been out a month. Ferra had told him about the island the night before, at her table, with Linus's chart, beside the place where her table had a hole in its top the size of a finger; and he had asked only who paid for it, and when she said she did, had looked at her, and then at the hole, and said nothing at all. At five in the morning he had been standing in the dark by the Hod's ramp, before the stable boys were up.

"That's not cloud," said Ned, from the helm, without turning his head. "That's the hills."

Harrold found it: the long grey smudge on the edge of the sea that was not cloud.

"How big?"

"Bigger than Kaldo and Milford and the four baronies," said Ferra, from the far bench. "We think. Linus is still walking it." Her hands were folded in her lap. Her book was in Imani's bag, where Imani had put it at the foot of the ramp with a look that was not open to discussion.

"And nobody knows it's there," said Harrold.

"Nobody you'd know."

His father considered that. Then, for the first time since the slip, he sat down, on the end of the bench beside her, with his hands on his knees; and the two of them watched the island come up out of the sea and did not say anything else.



The yard came up under them on the bay's south shore in a sun an hour and a half high: the long sheds, the slipway, the deep berth, the works shore running away east with the Dust House at its end behind its bank like a dune with a door in it. It was the eighth of August, and a rest day on the island too, by Ferra's order. As the Hod came down on the hard, Emeric found he had let his sense out over the yard from habit and was counting it. The gate watch, two. The cook at the long hut. And Fletcher, at the far end of the slip, alone, on a rest day, working at a plate seam with a file that Emeric could feel through the iron like a man scratching at a door.

"No," said Imani.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to say Fletcher." She put her hand flat in the middle of his back and pushed him toward the ramp. "Fletcher can file a seam on his own. Go and carry something."

He carried Maren's things: the big pot, two baskets of onions and peppers under damp cloths, a side of pork from the island's own farm, a sack of Milford maize in the husk, a crate that clinked; and Maren herself came last, in her second-best apron with her sleeves rolled off the old burn scars, and stood at the top of the ramp looking at the island as though it had been laid out for her inspection and was not quite clean.

"Where's this fire," she said.

He pointed west along the shore, a mile and a half, to where the bay curved round under the hills to a pale beach and a long dark grove behind it. "Bram's taken it round on the cart."

"Then I'd better go and see what he's made," said Maren, "before he lights it."



The white was laid out on the cot in the yard foreman's spare room, three folded piles with a card on each in Ferra's hand: E., F., I. He did not touch it, because he did not know what it was for.

They came in without knocking when he had got as far as the trousers, and looked at him, and shut the door, and dressed him, because he was doing it wrong. A jacket of white cloth that crossed at the front and tied, cut loose; Imani turned back the sleeves. A white sash, and a leather belt with a chain at the hip that did nothing, which Abloh had wanted and Ferra had priced. White trousers gathered at the ankle on a band of Isle rubber. Sandals strapped up the shin, which Imani knelt to lace while Ferra held his elbow, and he stood between the two of them and let them, and the back of his neck went hot.

The cloth was rayon, off Kaldo's own looms, and it moved like water. He had not seen cloth move like that since another life.

"Abloh says if anybody spills anything on it he'll know whose fault it is," said Ferra, standing back with her head on one side, as she stood back from a column. "He said you'd look like a sail somebody forgot to put up. I told him that was the idea."

"What's the idea?"

"That you aren't up." Imani got to her feet with a hand on his knee and looked at his eyes, one and then the other, as she looked at a patient's. "Nobody's up today. Not you, not her, not Ward, not your father. Turn round."

He turned. She lifted his locs off his neck in both hands, long and heavy, and gathered some and let the rest fall; and something small and cold went onto the end of one loc and slid up it and was pinched closed. Then another.

He knew them by feel. The little box of plain gold cuffs Abloh had sent up with the investiture clothes four years before, so that a duke's hair should catch the light in a hall full of lords. He had worn them twice since, both times for rooms full of people he did not like. They lived in a drawer under a pair of gloves he never wore, and Imani had evidently known which drawer for some time.

"They're for ceremony," he said.

"They're gold." Her fingers went past his ear and stayed there a breath longer than they needed to. "They aren't for anything. That's the point of gold. Ferra says."

"I said that was the point of gold," said Ferra. "I didn't say it was a good point."

He looked at the two of them, then, properly. Imani in a long white wrap without sleeves over cropped trousers, her hair high under a grey band with the curls already loose at her temples, a leather bracer on her left wrist and below it the old kettle scar, pale and shiny, where it had always been. Ferra in a white halter, bare-shouldered, with a long sheer panel falling from her belt over grey trousers tied below the knee, and her copper bun stuck through with two dark pens that had no book to go with them. And on each of their belts, at the left hip, a short knife in a plain sheath: Bram's work, one pattern, three of them.

That was Ward's rule, since the cave under the Isle and since the first night on the Sentinel's marsh: nobody walked inland on the island without a blade. Nobody had ever had to draw one. That was not the point, Ward said.

"You've got a knife," he said to Ferra.

"I've sharpened two pens with it." She put her hand on it flat, as she put it on her book. "I'm not going to stab anybody. I'd have to do the paperwork."

He had a sentence about how they looked. It was not a good sentence, and he could see the end of it from the beginning, and so could they; and Imani put her hand over his mouth before it got there.

"Later," she said. "Walk first."



The path to the falls went up beside the carved face, and to reach it they had to go through the palms.

Bram's cart stood under the grove with its shafts down, and Bram beside it with Maren, both looking at the thing on it with the same face. It was half a boiler off the works' scrap row, cut along its length, the bottom half on four iron legs and the top half hinged over it so it opened like a mouth; a grate in its belly, a damper at each end, a stovepipe chimney sticking up like a thumb, and a handle of turned ash on the lid, because a man burned as often as Bram did not put an iron handle on anything he meant to open hot.

"It's a fire with a lid," said Maren.

"It's a barrel smoker," said Emeric.

"It's a fire with a lid that he drew on the back of a feed slip," said Bram, to the drum, "and I made, and it's better than he drew, because his chimney was in the wrong place. Smoke goes where it's let. It doesn't go where it's drawn."

"When's it lit?" said Maren.

"Noon," said Emeric. "Then the pork's in by one, and it wants six hours slow and the sauce in the last hour, and if the coals are banked at the far end it'll hold at..." Imani's hand closed on the back of his belt.

Maren looked at the hand, and at him.

"Go up your hill," she said. "I lit fires before you were born."

When he looked back from the foot of the stair Maren had her head in the drum and Bram was explaining the dampers to her back. Ferra took his hand, which she did not do in front of anybody in Kaldo, and led him up.

At the cliff's top, fifty feet above the palms, the old makers had cut a ledge along the edge, a hand deep, and whoever came up the stair stopped there. The grove lay below them like a row of green fountains, and the pale beach, and out in the shallows, near the top of the tide, the long dark shape of the slab under the clear water, with the yard's cutter moored over its near end. In it stood Arthur, Mira and Bren, and Armand in a pair of the garrison's bathing drawers and nothing else, talking.

"He's learning to swim," said Imani. "He told Mira that in nearly ninety years nobody had thought it worth his while and he'd come to suspect they were wrong."

As they watched, Armand stepped off the cutter's side very upright, as a man steps off a stair, and went straight down and did not come up; and Mira, without any change of expression, knelt on the thwart and put her arm in to the shoulder and brought him up by the hair.

He could feel the nearest rim stones from here, under the water, fuller than in June; Fletcher's men had been at them with flasks since spring. He had turned on the ledge to go down and see how much fuller before he knew it, and both of them were watching him do it.

"It's a stone," said Ferra. "It'll be a stone tomorrow. The fourteenth on the north rim had a flask on Monday. It's two-thirds."

He looked at her.

"I read the yard's slips on Thursday," said Ferra, with dignity, "before Imani took the book. I can't help what's in my head."

Imani laughed at both of them out loud, on the old makers' ledge, with her head back; and took his other hand, and turned him round toward the hill.



The stream went up the hill in steps, small falls none higher than a man, each with a round brown pool under it; and the path went beside it through ash and alder and then oak, until the sea's noise was behind them and there was only water, and ferns, and moss on everything that held still.

A bird ran out onto the path ahead, brown, hen-sized, with a red flash at the eye, and stopped and looked at them and did not run any further. Imani crouched and held out her hand. It came and pecked her knuckle once, hard, and went off into the ferns unhurried, as if it had settled something.

"They don't know what we are," she said, looking at her knuckle. "I'd like to be something nobody knew what I was, for a day." She stood up. "Oh. I am."

Going up, Ferra stopped now and then to look at something, a fern unrolling, a beetle the colour of a blued spring, and said nothing about it and went on; and he understood after the third time that she was not counting them. He did not think he had ever seen her look at anything for nothing.

He felt the falls before he heard them, a great soft steady pressure through the trees like breathing. Then the oaks opened on a deep round fold in the hill, walled with grey rock as high as the keep, green to the top with fern and moss and small trees growing out of the cracks; and down its back wall from a notch against the sky the stream came in one long white fall into a round pool, with a noise that filled the fold and went on under everything. The spray came off it in a fine cold smoke, and the sun came straight down into the fold and lit it.

Ferra stood at the pool's edge with the spray in her hair and her mouth a little open. Imani sat on a flat stone, undid her sandals, put her feet in the water, and said something short and heartfelt about how cold it was.

And Emeric, at the edge of the oaks, was looking at the notch, and the height, which he made a hundred and ten feet; and something at the back of his head had already begun, unasked, to look for somewhere to put a pipe. When he looked down, Imani was watching him with her chin in her hands.

"Say it," she said. "You've got the face. Say the number, and then it's out of you and you can come and put your feet in."

"It'd run the island," he said. "The yard and the works and the houses. With some over." He let his breath out. "That's the number."

"You've been off duty since seven o'clock," said Imani, "and you've been back on it four times. Fletcher. The fire. The stones. That." She held up four wet fingers. "It isn't a criticism. I only want you to know. You're very bad at this."

"I know."

"I'm doing very well," said Ferra, "considering. And I haven't got my book."

He sat on the stone beside Imani and undid the sandals, which took a long time because Imani had laced them, and put his feet in. It went up his legs like a blade and into his teeth, and he made a noise; and Imani laughed and put her cold wet hand on the back of his neck under the locs until he made it again.

Ferra came and stood in front of them barefoot in the shallows, her sandals in her hand and the sheer panel floating out behind her on the water. She looked at the fall for long enough that he thought she had forgotten them.

"I'm not going to write this down," she said, to the fall. "Not the cuffs. Not the bird. Not him saying the number." Her colour was up in the cold. "I've written down everything that's happened to me since the trestle. I'm going to have one day I haven't got. I've decided."

"Then come here," said Imani, "and have it."

Ferra dropped her sandals, and Imani reached up and took her by the belt and pulled, and she came down between them on the stone in a heap of white, wet to the knee and laughing, with one pen coming out of her hair. Emeric caught the pen, and then caught her face with the pen still in his hand, and kissed her. She tasted of spray, and kissed him back as though she had decided to do it in front of people and was only sorry there were none; and when she let go, Imani turned his face round with two cold fingers on his jaw and had the other half, slowly, with her hand flat on his chest through the white, and he felt her fingers settle over his heart and begin, from pure habit, to count, and then felt her lose it.

"Damn," said Imani, against his mouth. "I'd got to forty."

She started again. So did he. Ferra's hand came round into his locs and found a cuff and turned it idly round and round, the way she turned a pen; and the fall went on filling the fold, so that nobody in the world could have heard anything they said, if they had said anything, which for a while they did not. He had his arms round both of them on a wet stone on an island no chart had, with the white getting wetter, and did not care in the least whose fault it was. He lifted Imani a little against him, without thinking, as he had a hundred times when nobody could see, and she said sharply into his mouth, "Not here. You'll put us in the pool. You dropped us on the saloon floor."

"That was the first time," said Ferra into his neck, muffled, "and the bruise was the shape of a lamp hook." She lifted her head. Her face was pink all over and her bun half down, and the second pen was gone into the ferns behind the stone, where it would very likely stay a hundred years. "Later," she said; and heard herself, and stopped.

It was what they always said. Work first, fun later: it had been their joke since the night he first tried to pull her off Dray's rents, and it had always meant there was a ledger or a ward round or a furnace between now and later. She looked at the fall, and the fold, and the two of them, and her own empty hands; and he watched her look for the work that came first, and not find any.

"There isn't any," said Imani. "Is there."

"No."

"Then it's later. Now." And Imani pulled Ferra's face down by the half-fallen bun and kissed her on the cheek, hard, the way she did when she had won something; and Ferra laughed, high and helpless, as she laughed about four times a year, and lay back across both their laps with her hair coming down into the pool, and looked up at the spray going over.

They stayed until the sun had moved off the pool. Nobody counted how long.



When they came down the cliff stair a little after one the drum was lit, and he could smell it from the top: oak and Milford apple wood, and fat. Maren had lit it herself, without a fire stone, and lit it right, and she had never seen his four lines on the feed slip. She had looked into the drum for a quarter of an hour, by Bram's account, and asked two questions.

"Where the smoke went," said Bram, from his crate, with his eyes shut, "and where the fat went. Then she said it's a fish house lying down."

"It is a fish house lying down," said Emeric.

"I know it is." Maren did not look round. "I smoked herring on the dock for twelve years. You put the fish where the smoke is and the fire where it isn't. I didn't need you for that."

The beach had filled while they were up the hill: five of Abloh's tents under the palms with their doors tied back, a kettle fire, a long trestle in the shade; and all along the sand, people who worked for him doing nothing at all. That was what he saw first.

Rylan sat in the shallows to his waist with his strapped arm held straight up out of the water like a man about to ask a question.

"If that bandage goes in the sea," said Imani, in a voice of iron, "I'll take it off you."

"Nessa said the same," called Rylan, without opening his eyes. "Between the two of you I'll have to learn to steer with my feet."

Further along, in the wash where the sand ran flat and the small waves came in over it, Tess sat with her back against Osric's knees and her legs out in front of her in the water to the calf, and her bench book on Osric's lap above her head where the sea could not reach it. When a bigger wave came up Osric lifted her back six inches by the waist without a word and set her down again when it had gone.

Declan was on a driftwood log by the kettle, whittling. Ned was asleep under a palm with his hat over his face. Saban stood knee-deep with his trousers rolled, looking at the horizon with his hands behind his back as though reading it for errors. Out on the slab, coming up now on the ebb, Mira was walking Armand along its edge by one hand, and Armand was observing in his tower voice that the sea was very much heavier than he had been led to expect.

And at the top of the beach in the first palm's shade sat Ward, in his black, with his boots on and his back very straight; and beside him, a little apart, his father.

Harrold had taken his boots off. That was all he had done. They stood side by side beside him, and he was looking at his own two bare feet on the sand as though somebody had given them to him that morning in a parcel and he had not yet decided what they were for.

After a while he moved his toes in the sand.



The beach things were in the third tent, three piles and three cards.

He went in first, because Imani said so, and came out in wide trousers to the knee with a drawstring, in a dark cloth printed all over with long leaves, and nothing else but the cuffs in his hair and sandals with a strap between the toes, which Abloh considered a mistake. The print was a pearwood block cut by one of the school's older girls from a palm leaf Arthur had brought home. He looked at the leaves on his own thighs, cut by a girl who had never seen a palm, and did not know what to do with his face.

Then they came out.

He had told them about beaches once, years ago, on the warm floor in Ferra's room: other beaches, on the other side of everything, crowded and hot, with people lying in the sun all day in almost nothing. He had drawn some of the almost nothing, badly, because they asked, and Ferra had folded the drawing in four with her ears going red and put it in her book. He had forgotten it entirely. Ferra did not forget things that were in her book.

She came out first. A knitted top and bottom in pale cream, with a thin brass chain at the waist that did nothing at all; over them a long open robe of the same knit to her shins; a bracer on her left wrist; her bun put back up with two new pens out of Imani's bag. And freckles, a whole sky of them, further than he had known; and she stood in the sun with every one showing and her chin up and her colour climbing into her hair, the way she stood in the long room to read a figure nobody wanted to hear.

Imani came out behind her in a short shirt of the leaf cloth, pale, tied in a knot under her breasts; under it, high on the waist, a knitted bottom in an open stitch that showed her brown skin through it in small round windows; her hair in one long braid over her shoulder, and her knife on her ankle over the sandal's laces, because there was nowhere else to put it. She came out smiling, as if she knew exactly what she looked like and was pleased with it, which she did, and was.

He had been holding Maren's basket of onions. He did not know he was holding it until there were onions all over the sand, rolling away down the slope toward the water, and he was holding an empty basket.

Nobody on the beach said anything for a long breath. Then Rylan, in the shallows, with his arm in the air, began to laugh and could not stop, and had to hold his ribs with his free hand and say ow between every breath, and went on laughing.

"Those are my onions," said Maren, from the drum, in a terrible voice. "You'll pick up every one and wash it in the stream, not the sea. And then you'll put your eyes back in your head, Your Grace, because I need you for the pork."

Imani crouched beside him on the sand and put an onion in the basket; and then put two fingers on the inside of his wrist on the basket's handle, without asking, as she always did.

"Ninety-six," she said. "Sitting still. Holding onions. You've seen us in a great deal less than this."

"Not on a beach."

"Not in daylight, you mean. It was Ferra's idea. She took your drawing to Abloh in March and didn't let him ask a single question." She put another onion in. "He told Maren afterward that the duke had drawn him two small sails and a flag and he couldn't find the boat. Maren told him to make the sails."

Up the beach Ferra had sat down at the tent door with her robe round her knees, looking very hard at the sea, and her ears were the colour of the coals in Bram's drum.

"She's going to burn," he said.

"She's going to burn. I've got Maren's ointment. I'll do her shoulders; you do the onions." Imani stood. "And Emeric. Shut your mouth. It's very sweet, but there's sand coming."



He was back on duty within the hour, and it was his father who caught him.

At half past three the tide was at the bottom of the ebb and the whole slab was out of the water a hundred yards off, its sixteen rim stones dark along its edge like the bolts on a hatch. He had been lying on the sand with Imani's head on his stomach, thinking about nothing, quite successfully, for nearly a quarter of an hour; and then he felt the fourteenth stone on the north rim from where he lay, and the thirteenth beside it, which was less. He was halfway down the sand before he had decided to get up.

His father was standing in the wash at the water's edge with his trousers rolled to the knee, as if he had walked down some time before and forgotten to go back. Harrold looked at him, and at the slab, and at him again.

"Where are you going?"

"To look at a stone."

"Why?"

There was a perfectly good why. He looked at his father, who was waiting, perfectly patient, with the sea going in and out over his feet.

"It isn't urgent," he said.

"No. I didn't think it was." Harrold looked back at the slab. "You walk like a man going to a report."

"I've been told. By everybody, since seven o'clock. Imani's counting. That's five."

His father was quiet a while.

"I counted the gate watch at the yard this morning," he said. "Two at the gate and one asleep on the shed roof. I looked to see where I'd put a third if it were mine. I didn't mean to." He moved his feet in the wash. "I've been off duty a month. I've walked every street in your town and sat in Ferra's counting room every evening, and every night I've counted the guard on your gate from my window, at ten and at two, and written it on the back of a letter and burned the letter in the morning. Ward found the ash in the grate the second week. He didn't say anything." A pause. "I don't know what to do with my hands."

Emeric looked at them. They were at his father's sides, not behind his back, where they had been every time he had seen Harrold standing for as long as he could remember. They hung there open, as if they had been put down and not picked up again.

"Neither do I," he said.

"I noticed."

Up the beach Imani had got to her feet with her hands on her hips; and beside her Ward got up from under his palm and folded his arms.

"Is she coming down here?" said Harrold, not turning round.

"Yes."

"Will it be worse than the council?"

"Much worse."

She came down the sand unhurried and looked at a duke and a king in their bare feet one after the other, the way she looked along a row of beds to see who was going to be trouble.

"Sit," she said. "Both of you. Up there, in the shade, for an hour. You can count anything you like as long as it's the waves." She looked at Harrold. "That's a prescription, Majesty. I write them for kings the same as anybody."

His father looked at her a long breath, and then, very briefly, at her left hand with the old pale scar across its back; and something went across his face that Emeric did not know, and was gone.

"Yes," said Harrold.

They went up and sat, with Ward on Harrold's other side, the three of them in a row in the first palm's shade. After a while a seal came up out of the wash, a big grey cow with a face like a wet dog's, and hauled herself up the sand toward them and stopped, and looked at Harrold.

Harrold looked back.

It went on for some time. It was the look Emeric had seen his father give the ambassador of Duvrenne across a table for most of a morning in the year he was twelve. The seal held it, and blinked slowly, and held it; and then rolled over on her side with her flippers in the air and went to sleep.

"Well," said Harrold.

Ward made a sound. It was very small. Emeric had heard it perhaps three times in nearly thirty years, and did not look round, in case it stopped.



The pork went in at one and came out at six, the sauce went on at five, and there were two pots of it.

The crocks had stood all afternoon in the stream with Declan sitting above them on the bank, whittling. Ward cut the seals on the trestle in front of everybody. Then two tin bowls and two brushes; Maren took one and Emeric the other, neither knowing which, and they painted the two halves of the pork at the drum's cool end with their backs to each other, and shut the lid.

He had made his on Thursday night at the end burner in Maren's kitchen after she went up. He had made it a hundred times, in a small kitchen with a window over a car park on the other side of everything, for nobody but himself. Tomatoes off Milford's glasshouse ranges cooked down the colour of old brick; dark honey from the beekeepers Ferra's census had turned up on the Milford road; the distillery's vinegar; mustard; garlic off Zale's string; salt smoked at the dock's fish house until it was the colour of tea; and for heat, the small red peppers from Milford's tropical house, grown from a twist of seed in the bottom of one of Zale's sacks, which nobody at Milford would eat. He had known what it ought to taste like before he began, and tasted it off a spoon until one in the morning until it did; and then stood alone in Maren's dark kitchen smiling at a crock, a long way from anywhere.

Maren had made hers on Friday. She had not let him in the kitchen.

"Who judges?" said Rylan. "Not me. I've two pennies on it."

"Not Ferra," said Tess. "She'll price it."

"Not I," said Armand, at the trestle's end in a blanket, looking extremely pleased with himself. "I have spent the afternoon being taught to float by a young woman a great deal my junior. How much my junior depends on which of me you ask. I am not fit to be trusted with a verdict."

They looked, all of them, at the one man on the beach who had no pennies in Saban's tin, had never tasted anything Emeric had cooked, and had given judgment on more things than all of them together. Harrold was holding a tin plate somebody had given him, as a man holds a thing handed him at a ceremony.

"No," he said.

"Majesty," said Saban, "with the greatest respect, you are the only man on this island who can't be bought. I've checked."

His father looked at Saban, and at the two bowls, and at Maren with her arms folded, and at Emeric.

"Who kept the crocks?"

"Ward. Under seal."

"Mira chalked them in the cold store," said Ward. "I held the door. Nobody touched them after. Declan sat on them."

"Near them," said Declan, from the log.

Harrold put his plate down.

"Then I'll give it," he said.



The pork came out of the drum at six black on the outside and pink under the black in a ring as deep as a fingernail, and fell off its bones under the fork. Maren had never seen the inside of a smoker before noon, and she had cooked it right, and she knew it, and did not look at him.

There was the maize, roasted black in its husks in the coals and eaten with butter and the smoked salt, which Bram admitted was better than in porridge; a dozen flat mottled fish Declan had taken in the cutter that nobody could name and Maren filleted in the time it took to say they would do; beans; bread; ale out of the stream, and ginger beer for Rylan, who was not allowed anything else.

And two plates in front of his father with a rib on each.

Harrold sat at the trestle's head with his bare feet in the sand, and said mildly that if they were all going to stand there and watch him it would take a great deal longer. They did not sit down.

He took the first rib in his fingers. Emeric had never seen his father eat with his hands in front of people. Harrold did it the way he did everything in public, entirely, without hurry, as though anyone who thought otherwise was mistaken. He ate it to the bone, wiped each finger, ate a piece of bread looking at the sea, and then ate the second; and sat back and looked at the two plates with no expression at all, as he had sat at the council table a thousand times while a lord said what he came to say and the king made up his mind about something else.

"Maren," he said. "Where did you learn to cook?"

"On the dock, Majesty. For the boats." Her arms were still folded. "Hennick's company, out of Kaldo, before the plague. Thirty men on six boats. Twelve years, off a fire in a shed on the quay with no door, and they came in at all hours wet and ate standing up." She stopped. "Why?"

Harrold did not answer. He looked at the plates.

"The first is very good," he said. Rylan had his hand over his mouth. "It's the best thing of its kind I've eaten, and I've eaten at four courts. It tastes as though whoever made it knew exactly what it was meant to taste like before they began, and made it taste like that. Every part of it is where it was meant to be." He touched the first plate's edge with one finger. "It tastes like something made very carefully for somebody who isn't here."

Emeric, at the end of the trestle with his own plate in his hands, did not move.

"The second isn't as careful. There's something in it I can't name. Salt, but not salt." He touched the other plate. "It tastes like it was made for thirty men coming in off the sea in the dark, wet, to be eaten standing up." He looked down the trestle at Maren. "It's the second."

Mira said, "Two," very quietly, and the beach went up.

Rylan was laughing with his hand on his ribs saying ow, ow; Saban had the tin open; Bram went to the drum and opened it for no reason and looked into the empty firebox. Maren had not moved. Her face was going a dark plum colour from the neck up, as it had once in a kitchen full of people who would not get out, and she looked at the king.

"Nobody's said that," she said. "About the boats. Not since the boats."

Harrold inclined his head to her, very slightly, the way Emeric had seen him do it to an envoy at the end of a treaty: a thing concluded.

Emeric put his plate down and came round to the trestle's foot and stood in front of her.

"Salt but not salt," he said. "What is it?"

"It's the brine," said Maren, after a while. "Off the sprat cask. You put sprats down in salt for the winter and they go to nothing at the bottom, and what's on top's a brown brine that stinks like the dock at low water, and a spoon of it in anything and it tastes of more. The men called it the boats' sauce. Hennick's wife started it." She paused. "I've kept a cask going since the boats. I never put it in anything of yours. You never asked."

He had known it from the first rib: a thing he had bought in a bottle with a paper label in another life and never once wondered about. Fish and salt and time. Maren had had it in a cask in her scullery for thirteen years, and he had walked past it every morning of them.

"Will you give me the recipe?" he said.

The beach, which had been making a great deal of noise, stopped.

Maren's face did several things in a short time. Then her arms came unfolded and she put her hands on her hips.

"You'll write it down?"

"With measures."

"I haven't got measures. So you'll have to stand there and watch me, won't you. On a Friday. With your hands in your pockets, and not tell me where the chimney goes." She looked at him a while longer. "You burned your hand on the pan, that Midwinter in the snow. You didn't notice."

"I remember."

"Write it down anyway," said Maren; and because the whole beach was looking at her and she had never been able to bear it, she went to the drum and stood beside Bram looking into the empty firebox, the two of them, with their backs to everybody.

"Somebody write it down," said Emeric. "Before she changes her mind."

They all looked at Ferra, who had her hands in her lap and her mouth pressed together very hard.

"I haven't got my book," she said.

Imani reached into the bag at her feet without looking, took out the book, and put it on the trestle in front of Ferra with a small flat sound and a pencil on top.

Ferra looked at it a long breath. Then she opened it to a clean page at the back, where she kept the things that were bought and not yet used, and wrote: 8 Aug. Maren's. The boats' sauce. Brine off the sprat cask, Hennick's wife's. And looked up.

"How much brine?"

"Till it's right," said Maren, from the drum, without turning round.

Ferra wrote: Till it's right. M. And underlined it.

"That's not a measure," said Emeric.

"It's a column," said Ferra, and shut the book and put both hands flat on it, and did not let go of it again all evening.



Fletcher came along the shore from the yard at seven with his hands black to the wrist, ate two ribs, wiped his mouth, and said, "The third seam on the cutter's port side. I've had a file on it since..." and Imani said, "No," and he ate another rib.

As the light went he noticed he had begun, without thinking, to tap one finger on the trestle beside his plate. Ferra reached over without looking and took his hand off the board and put it in her lap under hers, on the book.

"Not on the table," she said.

"It's a trestle. It's deal."

"I know what it is. I've only got the one good one." She did not look up. "I'm not having you go through a trestle as well. Bram would never let me hear the end of it."

Across the trestle his father lifted his head and looked at her, and at Emeric's hand in her lap; and for the second time since the crossing, Harrold laughed. It was a short low sound, mostly breath, and he put his hand over his eyes while he did it and took it away and was the king again. Bram, at the drum, turned round. Ward, at the trestle's foot, did not look up from his plate, and his shoulders were not quite straight.



His father swam out to the slab at dusk.

Emeric felt him go, from under the palms, with Imani asleep against his side and Ferra's head in his lap: his father's note going down the beach, and stopping at the water a long while, and going in.

The bay had gone the colour of a pigeon's breast, flat, with the tide coming in over the slab and the rim stones going under one by one; and a hundred yards out a dark head was going steadily across the still water. Harrold's shirt lay folded at the edge of the wash with his boots set on top of it so the wind should not take it. Ward stood at the first palm with his arms folded, watching.

Emeric did not get up. He watched his father reach the slab and pull himself up onto it, easily, a man strong all his life without anybody being allowed to see it, and stand streaming in the last of the light. Then Harrold sat down on the slab's edge with his legs in the bay and looked out to sea, east, past the headlands, where there was nothing at all and the first stars were coming out.

His hands were on his knees. Emeric could see that from the beach: both of them, palm up, open, as a man holds his hands who has carried something a long way and has only just been allowed to put it down, and does not yet know what they are like without it.

He sat until the tide was to his waist. Then he swam back, and came up out of the wash in the near dark, and put his shirt on; and found Emeric there, who had got up at last and come down the sand.

"It's cold," said Harrold.

"It's deep."

"Yes." His father stood with his boots in his hand, dripping. "I haven't swum since I was a boy. I'd forgotten I could."

They went up the beach together. Halfway, Harrold stopped and turned his head toward the drum, where Maren was raking the last coals into the sand with Bram holding the lantern for her.

"You were humming," he said. "At the fire, this afternoon, when you painted the meat."

Emeric had not known he was. He tried to think what it had been, and then knew: a thing off a car's radio in another life, a summer he had not thought of in a long time, a song he had never known the words to and had hummed at stoves ever since.

"Was I?"

"You do it when you work." His father looked at the drum. "She did."

"Badly."

"Badly." Harrold was quiet. "You're in tune. You were in tune at five, at your slate. I never knew where you got it." He began to walk again. "Not from me."

That was all he said. Ward had come down from the palm and fell in at his father's other side, half a step behind, the way he had walked behind Emeric for nearly thirty years; and Harrold, without looking round, put out a hand, wet, and laid it a breath on Ward's shoulder, and took it away.



At nine, by the kettle fire's light, Bren got up without a word and fetched the Hod's sixth tent bag off the cart; and the whole fire stopped talking and turned round to watch, and Mira stood up at the edge of the firelight with her arms folded.

He did it in silence. Ned, who had his watch out, said afterward it was two minutes and eleven seconds against the quarter of an hour Ward allowed a man in the castle yard, and Ward did not dispute it. Bren stood back breathing hard and looked at Mira.

Mira walked once round the tent, slowly, and looked at every peg.

"There," she said.

The fire went up. Armand applauded in his blanket with both hands over his head, as at a recital, and Bren sat down very fast next to Declan with his face in his knees; and Declan, without looking up, patted him twice on the back of the head and went on whittling. It was a seal, Emeric saw, going past the log: a hand long, out of pale driftwood, on its side with its flippers in the air, asleep.

"It's for your father," said Declan. "In the morning. Not now. He's busy."

His father sat on the far side of the fire with a cup of ale he had not drunk, and Ward beside him, a foot of sand between them, saying nothing. Tess was asleep in her tent door with her head on Osric's knee. Before she slept she had sat in the wash in the dusk with her boots off and the sea coming in cold over her feet, and moved her toes in it, all ten, slowly, one foot and then the other, and watched them do it; and said, to the beach in general, that she could feel every grain of sand going out from under her heels. Osric had gone very still behind her, and then put his face in her hair. Nobody at the fire had said anything, or looked; and Tess had written it down.



Their tent was the last in the row, under the oldest palm by the cliff, a little apart from the others. He suspected Ward.

They lay on Abloh's cots pushed together under the felt liner with a battery lamp turned low, the sea going in and out a hundred feet off and the palms rattling over the canvas, a sound he would think of for the rest of his life as the Sentinel's. Ferra lay along his left side with her cheek on his shoulder and, he was nearly sure, her book under her pillow. Imani lay on his right with her braid undone and her hair everywhere, and two fingers under his jaw.

"Sixty-four," she said. "That's the lowest I've ever had you. In thirteen years. Lying down, sitting up, asleep." She was quiet, counting. "Sixty-two."

"You'll make it go up, talking about it."

"Then I'll stop talking."

She stopped. Ferra's fingers had found the cord at his collarbone, where the key hung, and were turning it very slowly, as she had turned the cuff at the falls. He kissed the top of Imani's head, and Ferra's hair; and felt the two notes closest to his own settle, and slow, and begin very gently to go.

He let his sense out once more from habit, to see where everybody was. Bren asleep in the middle of his tent with his arms out. Armand asleep upright against the log, with a note even in sleep like a man who means to do something difficult again in the morning. Bram asleep at the foot of his drum. Tess asleep, and Osric not. And on the far side of the big fire's coals, two notes he knew, neither asleep: his father's and Ward's, a foot of sand apart; and as he felt them, his father's moved a little toward the other, and stopped there, and stayed.

He did not count the gate watch. He did not feel for the rim stones in the bay, or Fletcher's seam, or the Dust House. He drew his sense in, off the beach and the yard and the island, until there was nothing in it but the canvas and two notes on either side of him and his own between them, going slow.

"Fifty-eight," said Imani, very low, out of what he had taken for sleep.

He was asleep before she could tell him the next one.



He woke at seven to a hot tent and sun on the canvas and Maren swearing at the kettle fire, and knew he had slept the whole night through without once waking to go and see to something; and lay there a while in the heat, not knowing what to do with that either.

Imani was gone. Ferra was sitting on the edge of the cot in her robe with the book open on her knee. She had been up since twenty past five, with no gauge here to go to, and in an hour and a half, by the look of the page, she had written three lines.

She turned the book round on her knee so he could read it.

9 Aug. E. slept from ten to seven. Off duty: one day, nearly.

And under it, smaller, pressed so hard the pencil had gone through the paper at the full stop:

I didn't write the rest. F.
