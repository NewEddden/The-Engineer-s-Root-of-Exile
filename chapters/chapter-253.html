Chapter 253 - Nineteen

The Hod lay in the secret dock with her bottom off.

Fletcher's men had floated her up the lake on the Saturday night behind two rowing boats, with her stones dead and her deck awash to the hatch coaming, and walked her into the dock at the far end on ropes, and pumped the dock dry round her on the Sunday; and on the Monday they had begun cutting the stove planking away from her ribs a strake at a time, so that by the Tuesday afternoon she stood on her keel blocks in the stone basin like a fish laid open for the pan.

Emeric walked in under her and looked up.

The lift rail ran the length of her above his head along the frames, and Fletcher's men had unbolted its iron sleeve and lifted it off in lengths, and laid the lengths on trestles down the side of the basin, so that the stone of the rail lay bare in its cradle. The sleeve had come off whole, every length of it, without a mark. Two yards forward of the helm post's foot, where the crack had opened, Fletcher had chalked a ring on the stone and written a date in the ring. The crack itself was hardly anything. A dark line across the grey stone the length of a hand, no wider than a hair; the sort of line a man would have scratched with his thumbnail to see if it was dirt.

Every source in the ship had gone through that hand's length of stone. The bank, the reserve, the head tank, all four flasks. All of it had come down the one rail and gone out through that line into the dark over the sea.

"Two paths," said Fletcher, behind him. He had come in under the hull with a lamp, although it was full day outside; under the Hod it was not. "That's what you'll want. You'll have drawn it already."

"I've drawn it."

"Then don't show me yet. I've a bottom to put on her first, and if you show me a drawing I'll start thinking about the drawing and put the bottom on wrong." Fletcher held the lamp up to the crack, and looked at it the way he looked at a split in a good oak, with a kind of personal offence. "Ten months. I put my hand on that sleeve a hundred times."

"So did I."

"And it was whole every time." Fletcher lowered the lamp. "It passed every test there was, last August. Then it lay in her ten months under a sound sleeve, through every cold night and every full bank, waiting for the one that was colder." He turned away toward the dock steps. "Autumn. If nobody asks me for anything else."

Tobe was at the far end of the basin, by the stern, on a trestle under the Hod's quarter with a bar in his hands, levering the last of a sprung strake away from her frames. He had his gloves off. He was not supposed to have anything heavier than a cup in his hands, not for another week; Imani had said so, to him and to Cade, on the slip on the Thursday afternoon with the fishermen's tar still on their clothes: cool water morning and night, nothing heavier than a cup until she said, nothing with a stone in it for a fortnight, and gloves for the dirt if they went near the yard at all. He had his gloves off and a crowbar in his hands.

Emeric went down the basin to him.

Tobe saw him coming, and did not put the gloves on, and did not stop working either. He got the bar's end under the plank and leaned on it until the last trenail let go with a sound like a green stick breaking, and the strake swung loose on its other end, and he caught it and walked it down to the floor of the basin and laid it on the pile with the others. Then he straightened up and looked at his palms.

The lines were still there. Fainter than on the Thursday. On the Thursday, on the slip, with the lamp close, they had stood up from his skin red and fine and raised, fernlike, branching out from the heel of each hand toward the fingers like the pattern frost makes on a window on a hard night; and they had been hot to the touch, so that Imani, holding his wrist, had taken her own fingers away once and looked at them. Now they were pink. A pattern on his hands that a man might have got from lying on a rope mat.

"Fading," said Tobe.

"She said they would."

"She said weeks." He turned his right hand over, and back. "Cade's are going quicker than mine. He says it's because he's younger. I say it's because he did less." He did not smile, but something in his face was near it. "We had a word about that."

"Which of you won?"

"Ned." Tobe picked his gloves up off the trestle and looked at them and did not put them on. "Ned says we both held her the whole way down and he's not having it argued over in his dock." He was quiet a moment, turning the gloves. "Tomorrow. The long room. Ned says all of us."

"All of you."

"Nineteen, Ned says. I counted on my fingers in the boat coming up the lake and I made it nineteen too. Everybody that's been up more than a season." He looked at Emeric then, straight, the way he had looked at a weeping firebox corner once, in the second year, before he had spent four days cutting out its rivets. "Is it about my hands?"

"It's about all of you," said Emeric. "Your hands are how I found out I had to say it."

Tobe thought about that. Then he put his gloves on, both of them, and went back up onto the trestle for the next strake; and Emeric, going back up the basin under the opened hull, heard the bar go in behind him and the long creak of the trenail before it let go, and did not look round.



He wrote it that night at his own table.

Not a speech. He did not make speeches; he had told himself that at the top of the sheet, in pencil, underlined, and then sat looking at the underline for a while. What he wrote was what he would say, in order, so that he would say all of it and not stop partway when it got hard. He wrote it out in full. Then he crossed things out. Then he wrote it out again.

Ferra came up at eleven with her book under her arm, as she did on the nights she came up, and found him on the third sheet.

She did not ask what it was. She came round the table and stood at his shoulder and read it, because she read everything that was put on a table in front of her and had never in nine years pretended otherwise. He let her. He went on writing.

She read to the bottom of the sheet, and put her finger on the top of it, and went down it again slowly with the finger. Her lips moved very slightly. He knew what that was. He had seen her do it down a column of grain prices.

"What are you counting?"

"Eleven," said Ferra.

"Eleven what?"

"Eleven times you say I." She took her finger off the page. "In the first half. I stopped at the half. And two times you say you, and one of those is 'you'll want to know', which is you again, really, telling them what they want." She put her book down on the end of the table. "It's very good. It's honest. Every word of it's true. It's the truest thing I've seen you put on paper." She pulled the other chair round and sat in it. "It's about you."

"It has to be about me. I'm the one who did it."

"Yes."

"Then that's what they need to hear. That I did it, and that I knew, and that I didn't tell them. If I stand up there and talk round it, it's worse."

"Nobody's asking you to talk round it."

"You're asking me to say it less."

"I'm asking you to say it in the right place." She looked at the sheet, not at him. "You've put it first. Your part. What you chose and when and why, and that you're sorry, and how sorry, three ways. Then Armand's numbers, at the end, after you've finished being sorry. So they'll sit through your part waiting to find out what happened to them." She turned the sheet a quarter round on the table toward herself, the way she turned a page to read it level. "Tobe's hands went red and hot on Thursday. He doesn't know why. Cade doesn't. Ned flew a ship down near three miles on a slant on the strength of two men nobody had ever told could do that. What they want to know tomorrow is what's in them. And you want to tell them what's in you."

"That's not fair."

"No," said Ferra. "It's accurate. Fair's a different column."

He put the pencil down.

"If I put the numbers first," he said, "it looks as if I'm hiding behind Armand. Standing up in front of nineteen people with a sheet of figures, letting an old man explain what I did to them so I don't have to."

"You're not hiding behind Armand. You're hiding behind yourself." She said it quite flatly, as she said a sum. "If you stand up and say I did this to you for the space of a quarter of an hour, then nobody in that room can say anything for a quarter of an hour, because you're the duke and you're apologising, and they'll sit there and wait for you to finish and then say it's all right. Because what else do they say to you? Tobe's not going to tell you it isn't all right. Maren isn't. They'll make it all right for you, and they'll go home, and they still won't know what's in their hands."

He did not answer that. He picked the pencil up again and turned it end over end.

"They should know it was me."

"They'll know. Say it. One sentence. Two. After." She took the sheet and turned it over, blank side up, and put it back in front of him, and laid her hand flat on it. "First, what's in them. On paper, each one his own, so he can take it home and look at it when you're not there. Then what Armand thinks it means. Then what they can do about it, which is the part you've not got on there at all." She tapped the blank sheet. "Then your part. Then they ask you anything they like. And then you stop."

"What they can do about it." He heard his own voice go flat. "Ferra. They can't do anything about it. It's done. It doesn't come out."

"They can come off the ships."

"It won't undo it."

"It's not for undoing. It's for choosing." She looked at him then. "They didn't choose any of it. Not the stones in the still, not the Band, not the mark. You chose for them, every time, because you'd done the sum and they hadn't. So give them one thing to choose. Off the ships, with their place and their pay kept, or on the ships, knowing. And if a single one of them gets up and walks out, that's what it costs you, and you pay it, and you don't go after him."

He argued. He knew, afterwards, that he had gone on arguing for a good deal longer than the argument had been worth having; that he had said the same thing in four different ways, each a little more reasonable-sounding than the last, and that each time she had waited until he finished and then said the same short thing back. He said that a man who had done wrong ought to say so before anything else. She said that he could say so before anything else, to her, now, as long as he liked, and that she would listen to every word, and that tomorrow wasn't for him. He said that it would look cold. She said that a sheet with a man's own figures on it, in Imani's hand, with his name at the top, was the least cold thing he could put in front of anybody, and that she should know, because she had been putting sheets in front of people for nine years and watching their faces. In the end she said that he was running out of reasons, and that he had begun running out of them about a quarter of an hour ago.

He stopped.

He looked at the blank side of the sheet with her hand on it. Then at the other two sheets, the first and second drafts, on the floor where he had put them, with all their crossings-out.

"Eleven," he said.

"In the first half."

"How many in the second?"

"I didn't count the second." She took her hand off the sheet. "I'd have had to start a new page."

He laughed. It came out of him before he had decided anything, short, more breath than sound; and she watched him do it with her chin on her hand and did not laugh herself, quite, though the corner of her mouth went. He picked up the pencil and began again on the blank side, and at the top of it he did not write anything about speeches.

He wrote: 1. Their own numbers. And then sat looking at it.

"Imani has a card," said Ferra. "For the clinics. She's had it drawn since March, for the holdings, so that a Grayrock nurse and a Kaldo nurse read the same thing off the same form. Name. Year of birth. Census number. Blood, from the register. The smallpox mark. Every illness and every cut." She put her book on her knee and opened it. "There's a space at the bottom she hasn't decided what to put in."

He looked at her.

"I'll send a note down tonight," said Ferra. "She'll be up. She's always up the night before a thing she doesn't like." She wrote a line in her book, and tore nothing out of it, as she never did, and closed it. "And I'm staying."

"Ferra."

"Not for that." She got up, and took the lamp off the table, and looked down at him and the half-written sheet. "So that you don't get up at three and turn it back over and start again with I." She went toward the inner door. "You'd do it. I know you. You'd lie there for two hours thinking it looked cold, and get up, and do it."

He would have. He did not say so.

She sent the note down by the night porter, and came back, and got into his bed with her book and read in it by the lamp on her side while he finished the sheet at the table; and when he came to bed at past one with it finished and folded and put under the inkstand where he would see it in the morning, she was still reading, or appeared to be. He lay down. She put the book face down on his chest, open, as a thing to be gone back to, and put her hand flat on it, and put her head down on his shoulder.

"Four," she said, after a while, into the dark.

"Four what?"

"On the new sheet. I read it over your shoulder when you got up for the ink." She did not lift her head. "All four in the last part, where they go. That's where they go."

He lay there with the book on his chest and her hand on the book and her hair against his jaw; and he thought about the sheet under the inkstand, and about Tobe's palms going from red to pink, and about the dark line in the rail the length of a hand. He did not think he would sleep. He was asleep inside a quarter of an hour, and woke at five with the book on the floor and her gone down to the counting room, and the sheet where he had left it, unturned.



Imani came up the keep stair at seven with a box under her arm and dark rings under her eyes, and put the box down on the breakfast table between the bread and the butter, and sat down, and took a piece of bread, and said, to Ferra, "You stayed."

"He was writing a speech."

"How bad?"

"Eleven," said Ferra.

Imani looked at him across the table, chewing, with the expression of a woman being told the temperature of a patient she had expected to be worse.

"I'd have stayed too," she said, and opened the box.

There were nineteen cards in it. Stiff, cream, the size of a man's hand, printed on the small press: a ruled line for the name, and the census number in a box at the top right, and the year of birth, and the barony, and a row of small boxes down the left side with their headings in her capitals, BLOOD, SMALLPOX, and below those, ruled lines for every illness and every injury and every operation with a date against each. On most of the nineteen, the lines were filled already, in Imani's hand, small and level, from her register: Ned's knee, every winter for nine years; Cade's back, which he had never once told anybody about and which Imani had found in the second year and written down without telling him she had; Maren's burns, old and long, up the insides of both forearms.

At the bottom of each card, where there had been an empty space on the form, she had ruled a new box overnight, and headed it in ink, in capitals, as she headed everything she meant.

CAPACITY.

And under it, on each card, a column of small figures in Armand's hand, which was cramped now and getting worse, copied off his sheets: a figure for every year since the study began, one under the other, each a little larger than the one above.

"He did them last night," said Imani. "He was awake anyway; he's never asleep before two. I took him the cards at midnight and he copied every line off his sheets himself, and complained the whole time that his hand was going, and wouldn't let me do one." She turned the top card round on the table so that Emeric could read it. It was Tobe's. "He wanted to know why the figures had to be on the man's own card and not on the sheet in his own drawer, where they've always been. I told him to ask Ferra."

"Did he?"

"He said he didn't need to ask Ferra. He said it was a woman's idea of a thing and a good one, and that he'd thought of it himself sixty years ago and been told by the tower that a man's numbers belonged to the man who took them." Imani put the card back on the pile. "He's coming down at ten in the chair. He says he'll read the figures himself, and if anybody tries to stop him he'll read them louder."

Emeric picked up Tobe's card and looked at the column at the bottom.

A figure for every spring since the study began. He knew them. He had read them every spring off Armand's sheet with the old man's finger beside them. He had never seen them on a card with Tobe's name on it and a line above them where Imani had written, in her small level hand, the tenth of June, and beside it, BURNS, BOTH PALMS, OVERDRAW. NOT TRACERY. FADING.

"Only he reads it," said Imani. "And me. And whoever has his card in a clinic. Nobody else." She took a second piece of bread. "Not Saban. Not Armand, after today. Not you, unless he hands it to you." She buttered the bread. "That's the rule for every card from now on, for every soul in four baronies, and this is the first nineteen. So if you were going to ask whether the duke gets a copy, don't."

"I wasn't going to ask."

"You were," said Ferra, from behind her cup.

He had been. He put Tobe's card back on the pile.



They came up to the long room at ten in their working clothes, because Ned had told them it was a working day and they were to come as they were, and most of them had come straight off a job: Fletcher with sawdust in the hair over his ears, and Garrick and Wes from the rope walk with tar on their hands, and Maren from the kitchen with her sleeves still pushed up, who had said on the stair to anybody listening that she had bread in and it would not wait for a duke. Tobe and Cade came up together with their gloves on. Ned came last, with his knee, and took the stair one step at a time and pretended he was not.

Nineteen. He had counted them in his head on the Tuesday, from the books, and Ferra had counted them in hers, and the two counts had agreed. Everybody in Kaldo who had flown more than a season. The Hod's three and hull two's crew and the K Discovery's people; the riggers who had stood her watches on the long voyage to Emberfell; the two men from the still crew who had carried fire stones bare-handed for years before they ever went up; Linus, with more hours a mile up than any man alive; Maren, who had cooked in the K Discovery's galley since her first lift and said that a kitchen four miles up was a kitchen and she did not see what the fuss was; and Arthur, who had flown the Hod's Saturday nights since the autumn, and sat at the far end of the last bench as if he expected at any moment to be told he had come to the wrong room. They sat along both sides of the long table where the six sat on other days, on the benches and the six's chairs, and looked at the chairs they were in, and at each other.

Through the windows at the room's end, beyond the inner ward, the first block of ward 2 was going up along the line of the Gate Avenue: scaffold poles to the second floor, a crane's arm swinging a pallet of brick against the sky, men small as pegs along the top course. Nobody at the table looked at it.

Ward stood inside the door, which he had shut. Armand sat at the head of the table in his wheeled chair with a rug over his knees and his sheets in a pile in front of him, as he had sat at that table for nine years, and had his hand flat on the top sheet, high, as if to keep it from blowing away in a room with no wind.

Emeric sat at the side, halfway down, between Cade and the end of Fletcher's bench, with his folded sheet in his pocket.

Imani went down the table with the box.

She did not say anything. She found each card in the box by its name, and put it face down on the table in front of the person it belonged to, and went on to the next; and when she had been all the way down one side and up the other, she put the empty box on the end of the table and went and stood by the window with her arms folded.

"Turn them over," said Ferra. "They're yours."

They turned them over.

He watched them read. That was the first thing, and he had not expected it to be as hard as it was. Fletcher held his at arm's length and moved his lips. Garrick read his, and turned it over to see if there was anything on the back, and turned it back. Wes read his three times, and then looked sideways at Garrick's, and Garrick covered it with his hand without looking, as a man covers his cards. Ned read his slowly, with his finger, all the way down, past the knee, past the knee again, every winter, to the box at the bottom, and stopped there, and kept his finger there. Maren held hers out at the full stretch of her arm and squinted, and said, "Whose is that writing at the bottom? It's like a spider's walked in ink," and Armand, from the head of the table, said, "Mine," and she said, "Well, it's a disgrace," and read it.

Tobe did not read his. He sat with it on the table in front of him, face up, and looked at it, at all of it at once, the way he looked at a casting before he put a file to it; and then he looked at the line above the box, the one in Imani's hand with the date of Thursday in it, BURNS, BOTH PALMS, OVERDRAW. NOT TRACERY. FADING; and then at the column under CAPACITY; and then at his gloved hands on the table on either side of the card.

"Armand," said Ferra.

Armand took his hand off the sheets.

"I'll tell you what the figures are," he said, "and then I'll tell you what I think they mean, and then I'll stop, which Imani has made me promise to do and which I have never in my life done willingly." His voice was thin in the long room, and steady, and he did not raise it. "The figures are what a stone does when you put it in your hand. Every one of you has held one of my reading stones, three days running every spring, at a table in the school hall, because I asked you to and you were too polite to ask me why. I told you it was for the tower's records. That was a lie, and I'll apologise for my part of it, and then that's the last I'll say about my part, because it's not mine to apologise for most of." He looked down the table. "The stone measures what you hold. Everybody holds a little. A commoner holds a little more by the time he's grown, if he's lived near stones, and Kaldo has had stones in every kitchen for nine years. And you nineteen have lived nearer stones than anybody in the four baronies, for longer, and gone up into the Band besides."

"Bigger," said somebody at the far end. One of the still crew.

"Bigger." Armand nodded. "Look at your columns. Every figure larger than the one above it. Not one of you has gone down a year. Some of you have gone up slowly and some fast, and the fast ones are the ones who carried stones in their hands for a living before they ever flew, and then flew most. Tobe. Cade. The two of you from the still." He looked at Tobe. "On Wednesday night two men who were never put through the rite, and who were never in their lives told they could do it, put their hands on a cracked rail and poured into it all the way down from near three miles, which a farmhand can do for a count of forty before he falls over. That was what's in those columns, used once, with nobody watching but Ned."

He let the room have that, and then went on, a little more quietly.

"And there's the thing I most wanted to tell you, and the reason I was glad to be the one telling it. There's no tracery on any of you. Not one line. I've had my hands on every one of you every spring since the study began, and I'd have felt it. Imani's looked at every one of you. You're not knights. You're not mages. You're nothing that has a name in the tower's books. You're bigger, and you're whole."

He folded his hands on the rug.

"A year ago I sat at this table and told the duke what I'd found in fifty years of sheets," he said. "The total decides where you land. The rate decides whether you live. Every soul in the rite's room gets it all at once, in a night, and nine in ten of them die of the rate. You've had it a little at a time for years, so slowly that your bodies built for it as a smith's arm builds for the hammer. That's all it is. That's what's in your hands." He looked at Imani. "That's me stopped."

"It is," said Imani, from the window.

Ferra opened her book.

"Anybody at this table who wants to come off the ships," she said, "comes off. Today, or any day after, by saying so to Ned or to me. He keeps his place in Kaldo and he keeps his pay, the same pay, for as long as he'd have flown. Not the duke's leave. Not anybody's. It's written here." She turned the book round on the table so that the nearest of them could see the page, and there it was, in her square hand, dated, signed with her initials and then, below them, with Emeric's. "And anybody who stays on the ships stays at Imani's mark, which doesn't move for any of you unless she moves it and you know why. That's written as well."

She turned the book back round, and closed it, and looked at Emeric.

He stood up. He had not meant to stand up; Ferra had not said to. He did it because it seemed wrong to say it sitting, and then he was standing, with nineteen people looking up at him, and his folded sheet in his pocket, and he did not take it out.

"When I chose who'd fly," he said, "I chose people who'd carried stones. That was part of why. Not all of it; you were good at it, and you could keep your mouths shut, and I'd have chosen most of you anyway. But part of it. I wanted to know what the Band would do to a body that had lived near stones for years, and the only way to find out was to send one up and watch. And I didn't tell you. Last autumn, when we caulked the Hod, I asked Imani for the top of what was safe and I called it comfort." He looked at Ned. "She set it for you. Not for me, and not for the strongest man aboard. For you, because you hold least and you bleed first. That's the only reason the mark's where it is. The rest is mine."

He sat down.

Nobody said anything. Maren put her card face down on the table and put her hand on it.

Tobe looked up from his.

"Can it be stopped?" said Tobe.

Every head at the table turned to him and then to Emeric.

"No," said Emeric. "Not what's done. It doesn't go back. If you came off the ships today and never went near a stone again, you'd keep what's in that column for the rest of your life."

Tobe nodded slowly. He looked at the column again.

"I wasn't asking for me," he said. "I was asking so I'd know what to tell my sister. She's at Milford. She'll want to know if it's catching." He turned the card over, face down, as Maren had. "It's not catching."

"No," said Imani, from the window. "Not unless she moves into your foundry and sleeps on the stones."

Somebody at the far end laughed, once, and stopped.

Cade said, without looking up: "Would you have told us? If she hadn't gone down."

Emeric had known somebody would ask it. He had written an answer to it on the third sheet, on the Tuesday night, and Ferra had read it over his shoulder, and said nothing, which was worse than if she had said something; and he had crossed it out.

"Not yet," he said. "I was waiting for Armand to tell me it was safe to say. That's not a good reason. It's the one I had."

Cade thought about it, and nodded, as if it were a figure he had been given for a load and found came out about where he had guessed.

Garrick asked whether it would be in their children. Armand said that he did not know, and that nobody in the history of the world knew, because nobody had ever done it this way before; and that if Garrick wanted an answer he should have some children and give Imani their cards, and in twenty years she would tell him. Fletcher asked whether a ship cared, whether a man who held more made a better hand at the racks, and Armand said yes, and Imani said that if anybody tried to use that as a reason to sit with the bypass open she would know by the probe and he would be off the ships by her word and not his own. One of the riggers, a long quiet man who had rigged the K Discovery since her first lift, asked whether it meant he would live longer, and Armand looked at him over the rug for a while and said, "Probably. A little. Don't spend it on anything stupid."

And Maren, at last, turned her card face up again and pushed it a quarter turn toward Imani with one finger.

"Does it mean I can eat the bread?"

That got the whole table. It went round both benches, and up to the head, where Armand put his hand over his eyes; and Imani, at the window, unfolded her arms.

"It means you get one corner a day," said Imani, "like every other soul in Kaldo, and I'll tell you when that changes, and it hasn't."

"I bake it," said Maren.

"Then you'll know where to find your corner."

When it was quiet again, Ned took his finger off the bottom of his card.

He had not said anything. He had read his card and kept his finger on the column and listened to all of it, Armand and Ferra and Emeric and the questions, with his face doing nothing, which was what a lighterman's face did on a river when something was coming down it that he had not seen yet. Now he folded the card along its middle, carefully, as if it were a letter, and put it in the inside pocket of his coat.

"I knew," he said.

They looked at him.

"Not what." Ned put his hand flat over the pocket. "That something. My knee stopped up there. That's comfort, he's right about that. But it didn't come back as bad as it ought, after. Every winter for twenty years on the river, that knee, and these last two it's come back like a younger man's." He looked down the table. "A man knows his own knee."

Nobody said anything to that.

"Who's coming off, then?" said Ned.

Nobody moved. Fletcher looked at his hands. Wes looked at Garrick, and Garrick looked at the window where the crane's arm had stopped against the sky with its pallet swinging under it. The two from the still looked at each other, and one of them shrugged, very slightly, and the other did not.

Ferra waited, with her pen over her book, a long time. Longer than he would have. Long enough for anybody at that table who had been waiting for the room to go easier to see that it was not going to, and that nobody was going to look at him if he stood.

Nobody stood.

"Then that's that," said Ned, and got up, with his knee, and nodded to Armand, and to Ferra, and to the duke, in that order. "I've a book to write in." He went to the door, where Ward stood aside for him; and the rest of them got up after him in ones and twos and went out, with their cards in their pockets, and their hands in their pockets over the cards, as a man walks home with his wages.

Emeric sat where he was.

Ferra did not look up from her book. She was writing the time.

"Four," he said.

"Seven." She did not stop writing. "And 'mine' at the end, which makes eight. You put three more in when you stood up." She blotted the line. "It was the right place for them."



Imani caught him on the stair going down.

She did not stop him. She fell in beside him on the stair, a step above, so that her head was level with his, and went down with him, and said nothing until the turn at the first landing, where the window looked out over the inner ward and the dome and the black circle in its ring of white stones under the glass, with the sprout in the middle of it, too small to see from there, though he looked for it anyway.

"You gave the cabins a mark," said Imani. "Give my blood one."

He stopped on the landing.

"Tobe," she said. "Cade. The two from the still. Ned, even, now, a little. Nineteen people I've got figures for, on cards, who hold more than any commoner in the four baronies, and all of them would give blood if I asked. And every knight in the garrison. They're the healthiest people I've got. They eat best and they heal fastest and they're not afraid of a needle." She leaned on the sill. "And I can't put a drop of it into anybody but each other. Your rule. My rule. Downhill or level. A knight never gives arm to arm to a commoner, or the commoner's got a knight's mana in her veins in a minute with no stomach to say stop. I watched the rats do it." She looked at him. "So the best blood in Kaldo only runs one way. Up. And the people who need it most are at the bottom."

"The rested blood's safe."

"The rested blood has to be drawn the day before somebody needs it, and a childbed bleed doesn't send word the day before." She did not raise her voice. "On the cabins you didn't tell me to rest the air. You put a case of spent stones between the Band and Ned and let some round it by a key on my chain. Do that. To blood."

He looked out of the window at the dome. He saw it already; he had seen it before she finished, which was the thing about Imani, that she asked for things in the order he would have built them.

"Two stones," he said. "The kind from the wall board. The ones that read how much is near them and nothing else. One in a cuff on the patient's arm. One in a collar on the line, where the blood goes past. Each one drives a needle on a dial. Two dials, side by side." He drew them on the sill with his finger. "If the blood's denser than the patient, it goes through a short coil of glass on its way, past spent stones. The scrubber. Small. They drink the excess, the same as they drink it out of the Band, and the blood comes out of the coil down at the patient's level, and the catch goes into your ward's stones. If the patient's denser than the blood, a knight given commoner's blood, there's a charged stone beside the coil instead, and it lifts the blood toward the patient. Toward. Never above."

"And who says when it's level?"

"You do. Or a nurse." He tapped the sill between the two finger-drawn dials. "The stones read. They don't decide. There's a valve, and the nurse turns it until the needle on the line sits where the needle on the patient's arm sits. Then it's level. Then it goes in."

"And in a dead zone?"

"The stones stop, and you're where you are today. Rested blood, and downhill."

Imani looked at the two dials on the dusty sill for a while.

"I'll call it the leveller," she said.

"I'd have called it a giving set. A mana-matched giving set."

"That's four words for a thing a nurse has to shout across a ward at night." She straightened up off the sill. "The leveller. Bettany starts the rats on Monday: dense-fed into plain-fed, levelled and not, the same needles, the same doses, ether for anything that hurts." She went on down the stair, and stopped at the next turn, and looked back up at him on the landing. "You did well in there."

"Ferra wrote it."

"Ferra turned it over," said Imani. "You wrote it. I've read your drafts. I read the one on the floor this morning when I came up." She went on down. "Eleven."



Ned came to the yard on the Friday at six, with the crew book.

It was a yard day, and the class was on the track in the grey, two by two, going round the inner ward at the pace Rylan set; and Ned stood at the rail by the gate with the black book under his arm and waited, the way a lighterman waits at a lock for the water to make up its mind, until Ward came across the sand to him.

He opened the book on the rail. The oath on the first page, and the names under it, and the runs, every one, in Ned's slow hand and Ferra's quick one. On the newest page, under the date of the Thursday before last and the line that said where the Hod had gone into the sea, there was one more line, ruled off above and below, in Ned's hand, pressed so hard into the paper that it showed through on the back.

Never fewer than four aboard. N. Tallis.

"Three of us," said Ned. "One flew and two poured, and there wasn't a hand spare to so much as hold a lamp. If Tobe had gone over, or Cade, I'd have had one man on a cracked rail and a ship coming down on her nose." He closed the book on his finger. "Four. Every run, from now. I'll want hands."

Ward looked at the book, and then at the track, where the class came round the far corner of the ward in their pairs with their breath going up white.

He did not take a paper out of his coat. He did not need to; Emeric, at the foot of the keep stair with his own blade under his arm, saw him not need to. Ward said it in the voice he used to read a roll, flat, every word given the same weight as every other.

"When there is a Band crew, there will be places on it, and they will go first to those who were promised them."

Ned waited.

"Arthur was promised twice," said Ward. "He has his place. Two more were promised in the first year, in the school yard, on the same morning. I said yes to both." He raised his voice, not much; it carried across the sand without seeming to. "Holt."

The pairs came round. Mira Holt dropped out of hers at the gate end of the track and came across the sand at a walk, wiping her face on her sleeve, a carter's daughter from the west side with her shoulders gone broad in a year; and behind her, without being called, because he had been running beside her and had heard his own name not said, Bren Tallis.

Ned looked at Mira. Then at Bren, at her shoulder, who was looking at him.

"And Tallis waits for the count," said Ned.

Bren's face did a thing that Emeric had seen faces do on the morning a list went up on the board in that same yard, the morning Noll Finch read down sixty-one names to find his own at the bottom.

"I was promised the same morning," said Bren. "In the same breath. He said yes to her and then yes to me."

"He did." Ned did not look away from him. "And it's my boat, and you're a Tallis. When your name goes in my book it'll go in off a board in this yard, where every man here can read the figure, and not off my word at a rail. Then nobody says it was the family boat."

"Nobody'd say it."

"They'd say it to you," said Ned. "Every time you got something wrong at the racks. Behind you, where you couldn't answer." He tucked the book back under his arm. "The count's next month. Get a figure."

Bren stood there for a breath longer. Then he turned round without a word and went back to the track, and did not wait for a pair, and ran it alone, hard, faster than Rylan's pace, round the far corner and out of sight behind the well house; and Rylan, at the corner, watched him go past and did not call him back.

Emeric came across the sand.

He did it before Ward could say anything else, and before Ned could open the book for her to sign, because he had stood in the long room on the Wednesday and told nineteen people what he had not told them for years, and he was not going to watch the twentieth sign a page without it. He told her there by the rail, plainly, in the order Ferra had made him use. What the Band did, a little at a time, at Imani's mark. What it had done to the nineteen, in their columns. That it did not go back. That nobody at that table had a line on them, and nobody had bled at the mark, and that if she ever did it would be because somebody had opened the bypass, and Imani would know.

Mira listened to all of it with her hands on her hips and her breath still coming hard from the track.

"Does it hurt?" she said.

"No. Ned's knee stops hurting."

"Does Arthur know?"

"He was in the room on Wednesday."

She thought about that. She looked over at the track, where Arthur was coming round with his pair, and he saw her looking and lifted one hand off his stride without breaking it, which might have meant anything.

"Then I'll sign," said Mira, and held out her hand to Ned for the pen.



Hull two flew on the Saturday, nine days after the Hod went into the sea.

Two rail paths. Emeric had drawn them on the Tuesday and Bram's men had fitted them in four days: the old rail, refitted, down her starboard side, and a second, new, down the port, each from the bank to the lift stones by its own way, so that a crack in one bled one and not both. And at the helm post, set into the brass under Ned's left hand where it would be the first thing he touched every time he reached for a lever, a smooth grey stone the size of a hen's egg, coded by Emeric on the Thursday night to take its warmth from the bank and to go cold when the bank dropped to its reserve. He called it the watchdog, and by the Sunday so did every man who had touched it.

They tested it over the lake at a thousand feet in the afternoon with four aboard, because Ned would not take her up with fewer: Ned at the helm, Garrick and Wes at the racks, and Emeric at the stone-keeper's station with the bank's key. At Ned's nod he drew the bank down, slowly, a key's turn at a time, watching the gauge; and when the needle crossed the red line into reserve, Ned, who had been flying with his eyes on the far shore and his left hand loose on the brass, took his hand away from the stone as if it had bitten him.

"Cold," said Ned. He put his hand back on it. "Like a cellar step." He looked at the gauge, and then at Emeric, and then, without being told, reached down and threw the lever that took the port path off the line, so that the ship flew on the starboard rail alone; and she flew on it, level, with the needle steady, and he threw it back and took the starboard off, and she flew on the port.

"Two," said Ned.

"Two."

"And four of us." He brought her round toward the slip. "I'll have that in my book."

Saban was on the slip when they came down, with his hands in his coat. He had been down the coast for three days with a cart of salt fish. The fishermen at the marsh, he said, told it in the tap-house as a squall: a lighter blown off the estuary, her mast gone, her hands burned raw on the tackle, a bald lighterman who swore like a priest at a wedding. They were proud of the rescue. They had bought the bald lighterman a drink in his absence, Saban said, and drunk it for him.

"A lighter," said Ned, from the gangway. "She's a lighter." He limped down it. "That's not a lie. That's a matter of when you looked."



Bram asked for the second set on the Monday, in the cellar.

They were there for the balls. The big boring mill stood in the works now, built round the masters the Forge had made for it in the spring, and Bram had stood in front of it on the day it bored its first cylinder true and said it was the first thing out of the cellar he could keep; and now he wanted its spindle to run on bearings, and its bearings wanted balls, and a ball made on a lathe and lapped was never quite round.

So Emeric set the lattice to cancel the melt.

Not to hold it. The lattice had held melts in the air of the tube since the first run, at the centre, pinned. This was the other thing: the gravity stones round the tube set against the melt's weight, all of it, every grain of it at once and not only its skin, so that the steel in the tube had no up. Ferra had the reason column open. Imani had come to see whether it was medicine. Bram stood at the glass with his arms folded.

The first drop came out of the crucible's lip and did not fall.

It hung. An orange bead of steel the size of a pea, a hand's breadth below the lip, in the empty middle of the tube, with nothing under it; and it trembled, and drew itself in, and went round. Not nearly round. Round. It hung there in the glow of its own heat going slowly from orange to red, a perfect small sphere with no up and no down, turning very slowly on nothing; and the next drop came out of the lip after it and did the same, and the next, until there were nine of them in a loose ring in the middle of the tube, cooling, every one a ball.

Bram did not say anything for a long time.

"It's not sinking," he said at last.

"There's nothing for it to sink toward."

"Everything sinks toward something." Bram watched the nearest ball go from red to dull. "Water. Steel. Me." He unfolded his arms and folded them the other way. "It doesn't know which way the floor is."

"Could it grow medicine?" said Imani.

Emeric thought about it properly, because she had asked properly. There were things on Earth that men had grown in a place like that tube, high above the world, crystals of things too fine to grow on the ground because the ground stirred them; and Kaldo had not one chemist who could have used such a crystal.

"Not yet," he said. "One day. Not yet."

Ferra wrote in the reason column. He saw it upside down: Nothing yet. The same two words she had written under the jar in March.

When the balls were cold and out and in a felt-lined tray on the bench, nine of them, Bram picked one up between his finger and thumb and turned it against the lamp, and put it back, and said, without looking at anybody:

"What's the cellar's second rail?"

Emeric looked at him.

"The Hod had one rail," said Bram. "Everything went through it. Hull two's got two, now." He put his hand flat on the bench, on the steel of it, beside the tray. "Everything we've got that's true, is down here. Every gauge. Every flat. The taps, the dies, the laps, the hob, the needle die Imani's needles come off. All of it in one cellar under one castle." He looked at the tube. "And that thing kills itself if it's touched wrong. You built it so. So it can't be copied. Good. And then if there's a fire, or a dead zone in this room, or somebody walks through that door you can't stop, the machine's gone and so is everything it ever made true, the same night." He turned to Emeric. "That's one rail."

He was right.

"A second set," he said.

"Of everything." Bram picked up another ball. "Somewhere that isn't here."

They made it that week, three runs, in the evenings, after the works shut: a second of every master the cellar held, every gauge and flat and tap and die and lap, the hob, the needle die; and Bram packed it himself, in oiled cloth, in a crate he built himself out of oak with the lid screwed down and the screws sealed with wax under Ferra's seal, and stencilled on the lid, in the works' black paint, STANDARDS ROOM. SPARES.

"Which is true," said Ferra, reading it.

"It's all true," said Bram. "That's why it's a good label."



Armand took it north on the Saturday, on the people's train, with the books.

Imani's handbook had come off the presses on the Monday, five hundred of it, in grey board covers with A HEALER'S HANDBOOK on the front in plain capitals and nothing else: boiling water, washing hands, wounds and their dressing, childbirth, and the smallpox lesson, with a drawing of a needle and a scratched arm. It was for sale, at a price Ferra had set low enough that a ward could buy a copy for every street. The first two crates of it were going to Grayrock's new clinic, with a box of Imani's blank cards for its drawer; and with them, in the van, under Declan's eye, a sealed oak crate for Bryda's strongroom and Bryda's key.

Armand had asked to take them. He had been grounded since the end of March, since the night he came down from the Band with Ferra's number, grey and shaking and very pleased with himself, and Imani had told him that his Band hours from now on would be taken in a chair in the dome, at Ferra's line, where she could watch the probe and his face at once; and he had borne it for nearly three months as a cat bears a closed door. A train, Imani said, ran on the ground. She gave him three days.

He paid his copper at the window like everybody else, and took his card ticket, and turned it over to see what was on the back, and complained about the draught from the window before the train had left the platform. Declan sat across from him with his arms folded and his eyes shut and did not answer.

"Forty miles of this," said Armand, to the window.

"You used to go up four, in a box," said Emeric, at the coach door.

"Up is better," said Armand. "Up, nobody's knees are in yours." He settled the rug. "Go away. You'll make me sentimental and I'll miss Bryda's dinner."



The gardens below the works began to yellow in the week of midsummer.

Imani found it before anybody brought it to her, because she walked through the gardens on her way to the dock quarter every second day, and on the twenty-third she stopped by a row of beans with their leaves gone pale at the edges, and then by a plum tree whose leaves had brown tips as if they had been singed; and the next day a carter on the town's edge brought his cow up to the ward, lame in two feet, because he did not know where else to take her.

She came up to bay three with a yellowed leaf in her hand and laid it on Tess's bench.

It was the pot room. Emeric had known the pots would breathe something out, and had not once walked the gardens below them. The melt gave off a fume that was nothing to look at and settled on everything downwind. He gave it its name, fluoride, and told Imani what it did to leaves and teeth and the bones of cattle; and Imani, who had already walked the gardens and drawn a line on the map in the counting room where the yellow stopped, said that the pot room would have hoods on every pot and its air would go through water before it went into Kaldo's, and that she would walk the gardens again in a month and see. Tess drew the hoods that afternoon. She wanted to know where the water went after. Imani said, not into the river.



Bettany's rats gave their answer on the Saturday.

Two of them, side by side on the board in the long low room with the cages, under ether, both from the plain-fed shelf; and blood from the dense-fed shelf into both, the same needle, the same measure. One straight. One through the leveller, which was a coil of glass no longer than a finger with three spent works stones packed round it in a brass jacket, and a cuff the size of a ring on the rat's foreleg, and two dials on a board no bigger than a book, and Bettany's broad hand on the valve.

The rat that had the blood straight bled at the gums by the evening. The one that had it levelled did not.

Imani ruled a new column in the rats' book. LEVELLED. She wrote a word in it, and a time, and closed the book on her pencil.

"Now people," she said.

"Not yet."

"No. Not yet." She put the book back on its shelf. "Fifty rats. Then people. Then a knight's blood into a child." She looked along the cages. "And I'll do the first one."

He went back up the hill at dusk, past the counting room. The door was open, and the lamp lit, and Saban was at the long table alone with three sheets of paper in front of him and a pen, writing; the same first line on each, in his trader's hand, and then something different under it on each, a field and an hour. Hull two's run, Emeric thought, the rails for the last stretch of the Wayford line, out over Lisle's wood in July; Saban had asked him for the day that morning.

Saban looked up and saw him in the door, and put his sleeve over the nearest sheet without any hurry, the way Garrick had covered his card in the long room, and smiled.

"Salt fish," said Saban. "Three buyers."

Emeric went on up the stair.
