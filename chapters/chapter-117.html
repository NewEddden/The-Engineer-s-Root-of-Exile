Chapter 117 - Answered

The letter came on the eleventh of June, in the crown's bag, by the north road, in the ordinary way, and that was the strangest thing about it.

Hale brought it up from the post house himself. He did not run. Hale had never run in his life and did not mean to begin on the high street in front of the town. But he walked very fast, with his elbows in, and the letter held flat against his coat in both hands as if it might be taken from him on the way, and he went past the exchange where Ferra had her trestle on post days without stopping, and up the keep stair, and knocked on the door with her name on it, and Emeric, who was in there with her going through the copper for the boat, heard her say come in and then heard Hale not come in.

She looked up. Hale was standing in the doorway with the letter.

"It's from the crown," Hale said.

Ferra put her pen down.

It was a single sheet folded in three, sealed with the plain black wafer of the court's chancery, the seal Kaldo had seen once in four years, on Keller's ruling against Wexler. The address was in a clerk's hand. To the holder of the barony of Kaldo, by his steward. Hale held it out, not to Emeric, but to Ferra, and Emeric saw that he had done it without thinking, and that Ferra had seen it too, and that neither of them was going to say anything about it.

She broke the wafer.

It was three lines. A clerk's hand, quick and clean, the hand of a man who wrote forty of these a day and had stopped seeing them. It said that the chancery of the crown acknowledged receipt of the barony of Kaldo's report of the thirtieth of May, entered under the matter numbered, and gave the number. It said the report had been read. It was signed with a clerk's initials that meant nothing to any of them.

That was all.

Ferra read it twice. Then she laid it flat on the trestle in front of her, the right way up for Emeric, and he read it, and then they both looked at it for some time without saying anything, the three of them, Hale still in the doorway.

"The thirtieth of May," Hale said at last. "I lodged it. At ten. It was a Friday." He cleared his throat. "That's twelve days."

"Twelve days," said Ferra.

"It's the first one." Hale said it as though he were reporting the weather, and his voice went in the middle of it, and he stopped, and stood very straight in the doorway, and looked at the window. "Four years. And it's the first one."

Ferra did not answer him. She got up and went to the shelf where the barony's books stood in their order and took down one that Emeric had not seen her open in a long time: a narrow book, older than the others, with a cracked spine and the corners gone soft. The letter book. The one she had ruled years ago, in the winter Grayrock raised its price, with a column for every letter Kaldo sent the crown. Date. To whom. By what road. Answered.

She opened it on the trestle. The first page was the first autumn. The second, the first winter. Page after page of her small upright hand, the early pages a little larger and less sure, the later ones exactly as she wrote now, and down the right-hand side of every page, in every row, the column headed answered. Empty. Not a mark in it. She had ruled it on every page, every row, and every row of it was blank.

She found the page for the end of May. She found the row for the thirtieth.

She took out the pen. The dark one, with the gold band.

She wrote in the column. Not much. The date the letter came, and the word receipt, and the number of the matter. Then she blotted it and sat back and looked at it.

It was one line of ink in the right-hand column of a book with four years of empty right-hand columns in it, and from where Emeric stood it looked like a single lit window in a street of dark houses.

"There," Ferra said.

She did not say anything else. She turned the book round on the trestle so that it faced him, and pushed it a little toward him, and he looked down at it, at the pen line and the blank lines above it running back up the page into the years, and when he looked up she was looking at him across the trestle, and she did not look away, and neither did he, and Hale in the doorway coughed and said that he would go and tell Bram, and went.



Nobody in Kaldo knew how it had happened. They found out later, from Saban, who found out from a clerk in a tavern in the capital who had had too much to drink and liked to talk about his work, which was how Saban found out most things.

Keller had a clerk. A young man, very thin, with ink on his cuffs and no family worth mentioning, who had sat in the corner of Keller's room in May while Ferra laid out the twin year on the long table, and written down the names of those present and nothing else, and been forgotten by everyone in the room including, very nearly, Keller. In the week after, the young clerk had gone to the chancery's southern desk and taken out the crown's book of the southern road: every waypoint between the capital and the coast where the crown's post was sorted or changed, and who held each one's warrant. Then he had sat down at his table with a stack of the chancery's ordinary paper and written, in his quick clean hand, the same letter to every one of them. Twenty-six letters. A form. The chancery had a hundred forms and this was one of the dullest, the one it sent every few years to every house it paid, asking for the house's returns for the last three years, for the audit, and the name of the warrant's holder, and his oath, in the usual way.

That was all he had done. He had not mentioned Kaldo. He had not mentioned any matter. He had sent a dull form to twenty-six houses, and one of the twenty-six was a square stone house at the head of the slope to the Wayford ferry.

Kaldo did not know any of that in June. Kaldo knew a receipt. Three lines.



Rylan began the program on the Monday after.

It was Ward's order and Rylan's work, and it had been coming since the spring, when Ward had stood in the yard watching the garrison drill and said to Emeric, without turning his head, that there were forty-one men in Kaldo's garrison and forty-one ways of swinging a sword, and every one of them was the way the man who taught that man had swung it, and that was not a garrison, it was a fair. Emeric had said that armies in the books he had read had settled that long ago by writing it down. Ward had looked at him a while and then said, then write it down, and gone back to watching the yard.

Emeric had not written it down. He had given it to Rylan.

So on the Monday the garrison came into the yard at dawn and found a board nailed to the guardroom wall with every man's name down the left side and a row of squares after each name, one square for every week until the winter, and Rylan standing in front of the board with his arms folded and a slate in one hand.

"Same thing," Rylan said. "Every man. Every morning. The same."

He showed them. The warm-up, which was a run round the inner ward twice at a pace Rylan set and nobody was allowed to pass. Then the sets: the sand sack, Bram's new one, filled to a weight Bram had stamped on its side, lifted from the ground to the chest so many times. The practice blades, all weighted the same now, Bram's too, a cut at the post and a cut and a cut, so many. The pole, for the arms. A distance, marked with stakes in the yard, run carrying a man.

"And I write it down," Rylan said. "What you did. Every man. Every week. In that." He put his hand on the board. "Not against the man next to you. Against you. Last week." He looked down the line. "If you did twenty with the sack last week and you do twenty-two this week, that's better, and if Osric does forty and you do twenty-two, I don't care about Osric."

"Nobody cares about Osric," said Declan.

The garrison laughed. Osric did not.

"And you'll eat," Rylan said. "Maren's doing the tables. More meat on drill days. Bread. Water on the yard all day, in the butt by the gate, and you drink it whether you want it or not." He looked at Ward, by the guardroom door, who nodded once. "And after drill, three mornings in seven, you come into the long room and sit at Sela's benches with a slate. Letters. Figures. Every man."

That did not get a laugh.

"I can read," said Declan.

"Then you'll help," said Rylan. "Sit at the end and help."

"I can't," said a levy man near the back, quite quietly, and two or three others shifted their feet.

"Nor could I," said Rylan. "Last August." He looked down the line at them, at each of them, slowly, and Emeric saw the line understand what he meant, because every man in it had stood in the pay line on the last Friday of the year and seen the name go across the sheet downhill with its one letter crossed out. "Three mornings in seven. I'm in Doran's vestry the other four, on my own slate. You can come and laugh at me if you like."

Nobody laughed.

Osric did. Later, at noon, in the guardroom, loudly, to anybody who would listen and several who would not. He said that he had held the gate of Kaldo for years without knowing his letters, and stood on the wall in the war, and taken a ladder off it with his hands, and he did not see what a slate had to do with any of it, and that a knight who wanted to read could become a clerk. He said it the next day too. And on the third morning in seven, when the garrison went into the long room after drill and sat at Sela's benches, Osric went past the door of the long room with his head up and his hands behind his back, whistling the tune he never got the middle of, and went to the wall.

Ward did not say anything about it. He wrote it on Rylan's board, in a square after Osric's name, in his own hand: absent. And left it there, where the whole garrison could see it every morning when they came into the yard.



He had been practising the other thing since the spring.

He had not told anyone. Not Armand, who would have wanted to measure it. Not Ward, who had seen his guard come up too fast in the first year in Kaldo, and said nothing, and had gone on saying nothing about it for four years with a steadiness Emeric had started to find more alarming than if he had asked. It had begun in the hold of the boat in December, though he had not known it then. His own weight made heavy, and a man's breath stopping under his legs; and afterward, by the fire in the guardroom, with his hands shaking, something else he had felt and put away: that in the dark of the hold, when the guard leaned over him, his hands behind his back had closed on the cord that tied them, and the cord had creaked.

A mage cast outward. It was the first thing a mage was taught, and the last thing a mage forgot. Mana went out of the body, told what to be and do, and became fire, or air, or a weight lifted off a coin. A knight did the other thing. A knight, after the rite, had his mana in him in silver lines under the skin, and he drew it inward, into his muscle and bone, and was stronger, and faster, and harder to kill, and could not cast so much as a candle flame. Nobody did both. The Circle said nobody could; that the body would not hold mana both ways, any more than a pipe would carry water both ways at once.

He had no silver lines. He had looked, in the glass, under the lamp, with his sleeves rolled up, the night he came back from Armand's room in January. Nothing. Copper skin and the blue of the veins underneath, the same as any man's.

And still, behind the shutters, at night, when he took a coin off the table and instead of lifting it with a weight let the mana go the other way, down his arm, into his own hand, the hand closed on the coin and the coin bent.

Not much. The first night, the edge of it, a crimp, the way a soft copper bends under a thumbnail. The second night, more. By the middle of May he could fold a Copper Rand in half between his first two fingers and his thumb, and by June he could do it without thinking about it, which was worse, because it meant that one day he would do it without meaning to. It cost. It cost differently from casting. Casting went out of him and left him lighter, hollowed, a little cold. This stayed in him and burned, a deep dull ache in the muscle that lasted into the next morning, the way the day after a long climb aches, and he would wake with his right hand stiff and sore and have to open and close it under the blanket twenty times before he could hold a pen.

It was the hands he worked on. Hands could be hidden. He did it with the left as well as the right, and with each finger alone, which was much harder, and then the other thing he had found almost by accident in the third week, on a cold wet night in May with the fire out, when he had done it with no coin at all and only wanted his hands warm. He had let the mana go down his arms into them, and told it nothing, only to be there, and then, without quite meaning to, told it what a fire was.

His hands had gone hot. Not burned. Hot, from the inside, the way a man's hands are hot after he has been splitting logs in the cold for an hour, and the heat had stayed in them for as long as he held it there, and spread up his arms when he let it, and when he put his palm flat on the cold glass of the window the mist on the glass went clear in the shape of his hand.

He had stood and looked at the shape of his hand on the window until it fogged over again.

Knights did that. He had seen Ward do it. Every winter, on the wall at night, Ward stood his watch in the cold without a coat when the garrison men beside him were wearing two, and steam came off his shoulders in the lamp light, and nobody remarked on it, because everybody knew that knights could keep themselves warm. It was a knight's trick. One of the first a knight learned, after the rite, before the strength and long before the speed.

He wiped the hand off the glass with his sleeve, and let the heat go out of him, and went to bed, and lay there in the dark with his hands under the blanket, still warm.
