Chapter 117 - Stores

The hold had held nothing since the autumn but the rack and the dish.

It was the room behind the engine, between the third iron wall and the fourth, and it had been built empty on purpose: an oak rack across her bored for cells like a rack for bottles, and on her centreline a bracket over a dish of oil, where the reading stone hung watching for her to lean. Now it was going to hold the armour, because the armour had to be somewhere when the men were not in it, and the men could not sleep in it and would not sleep beside it in a cabin the size of a horse's stall.

Fletcher's six built what went in it before they went home. It was plain carpentry and they did it without a question: stands along both sides of her, each the shape of a man's shoulders with a shelf above it, a bench bolted across the forward bulkhead, drawers under the bench, a rack for tools on the wall above it. Four stands. Three had shelves over them. The fourth was bolted to the floor with iron straps and had no shelf, because what went on it was not a suit but a crate.

Emeric and Bram fitted the rest after the six had gone.

The suits came down the river in straw, in crates of their own, and went onto their stands with their capes furled and their toggles thrown to safe and their helms on the shelves above them, Ward's and Osric's and his own. Each stand had a length of hose off the starboard vein that came down behind it and ended at the necks of the hearts, so that when she flew through the band the band's air would run past them and the suits would fill as the ship's cells did. In port, below the band, Orrin would fill them by hand.

The fourth stand took Rowena's crate.

Her suit had come off the forge on the thirty-fourth night, bright and bare, and Bram had crated it in the pattern store the next morning without being asked, in straw, with every piece wrapped. Nobody outside the six had seen it on a frame. Nobody at Emberfell would see it until the lid came off. The crate went down the hatch on a tackle and onto its stand and was strapped there, and Emeric stood looking at it for a while after Bram had gone up.

Rylan's stood in the pattern store in Kaldo, on its frame, finished. Nobody had asked him about it, and he had not come to look.

The door from the engine room had a lock now, and so did the hatch over the hold. Bram had worn the hatch key on his belt since the autumn, and on the last evening he took it off the belt and gave it to Ward, without ceremony, and Emeric gave the key to the new door to Osric. Nobody else was to go in without one of them. Ward put his key on a cord round his neck. Osric looked at his for some time and then put it on the same kind of cord, and said that he felt like a jailer.

The bench was the road workshop, and it was Tobe's.

Bram had fitted it himself: a vise at one end, his own second set of hand tools in the rack, a fire stone set flush in the top of the bench under an iron cap, for heating a rivet without lighting anything in a hull full of oak and oil, and drawers underneath. The drawers were the spares, and Bram had chosen every one of them, and could have said why for each.

Rivets, and leather straps, and padding, because those were what broke. A few spare plates, cut by the forge for the pieces most likely to take a blow: a vambrace, a greave, a shoulder. Spare panes of the dark glass, because a cracked pane would show stone light, and the whole of the glass was that it showed none. Two spare battens from Abloh for the capes, and a spare bellows for each watchdog. A spare chute for each man, packed by Abloh in the loft and wrapped in oilcloth, because a chute that had opened had to be packed again on dry ground, not at sea. A crate of cells.

No spare heart.

"Every suit's got two," Emeric said, when Bram asked. "Either one flies it. That's the spare."

Tobe stood by the bench while Bram showed him the drawers. He was Bram's journeyman, and had built the first engine that ran on the iron with his own hands, and he was going because a ship with an engine in her wanted a man aboard who had built one. He would be the only smith aboard her. Emeric could do the stones. Tobe would do the rest: a strap, a buckle, a rivet, a plate swapped onto a suit that had taken a dent.

"What I can't do," said Tobe, looking at the spare vambrace, "is mend that."

"No."

"There's no seam in it." He turned it over. "I don't know where it was ever joined."

"It wasn't. If one of those cracks, you patch it to get it home, and the forge makes another."

Tobe nodded. He had never had his hands on the suits. None of Bram's men had, because Bram's hands had not been on them either. Bram put the vambrace back in its drawer and shut it and looked at Tobe.

"First time I've trusted anybody with these," Bram said, and went up the ladder.

Tobe stood there for a while after he had gone.

The pans came from Kaldo.

Emeric had asked Bram for pans for the new hob before they went down the river the first time, and had begun to say why, and Bram had worked it out before he got there.

"A stone doesn't lick up the sides like a fire," Bram said. "It only gives you the bottom."

So they were heavy, all of them, with thick flat bottoms that sat full on the plate over the stones, and a lip round the hob they sat in so that they could not walk, and a latch on every lid for a ship that leaned. There were six of them and a kettle and two baking tins, and they weighed more than anybody had expected, and went over Ferra's scale, and she wrote them down without a word.

Maren tried them aboard on her hob, one at a time, the evening they came down. She put water in each and brought it up and watched it, and put an onion in the heavy pan and did not burn it. Then she put them away in the lipped racks the six had built for them, and said nothing at all.

Bram, who had come down to see them go aboard, took that correctly as the highest thing a cook can say about a pan, and went back up the beach looking pleased with himself in the way he had of looking pleased, which was to look at nothing.

The coat was basted and pinned by the third day of the week, and Emeric went to the quay street to stand in it.

It was charcoal, and it was not finished, and the silver on it was only chalk lines, and one sleeve was on and the other was pinned. The tailor walked round him with pins in his mouth. Osric had come along to watch, because he had heard there was going to be a coat, and before the tailor had got the second sleeve on he had three opinions about the collar.

"Keep them," Emeric said, "for when it's finished."

"They'll be worse by then."

"Keep them anyway."

Osric kept them, visibly, for the rest of the fitting. The tailor promised the coat on the eve of midwinter, and not a day before.

They loaded her in the last three days, and everything went over the scale at the top of the beach first.

Maren's stores went first, because there were most of them. Flour and meal, salt meat in casks, fresh meat and lake fish packed in ice for the cold room, butter, cheese, eggs in bran, winter roots, apples, dried fruit, ale. Imani's chest came down, lashed and labelled, and went aft to its place on the saloon bulkhead. The cork jackets from Milford went into the lockers under the benches in the day room, eight of them, one for everybody who could not fly. Bedding. The oars for the boat.

Ferra's strongbox came down on her own knee in the steamboat and went into the stern cabin's locker with the hasp, and Ferra padlocked it herself. It held the writ and the draft of the terms, and the locked box.

Saban's samples went over the scale one at a time, because he wanted each one weighed and written. A crate of Kaldo glass, clear panes and a set of drinking glasses. A ream of the new paper. Ink in stoppered jars. A sheaf of printed pages off the printer. Soap from the soap house, in bars, wrapped. A bar of Kaldo steel as long as his arm, which he carried down the beach himself and would let nobody else touch. He stood beside the scale for every one of them and read the figure for himself before Ferra wrote it.

Neta's quarter-hour glass went aboard in a padded box, because Emeric wanted to time the coast again.

And the swords went aboard on the men who would carry them, Ward's and Osric's and Emeric's own. Nothing else went aboard that could be pointed at anybody. There was nothing on her anywhere to point it from.

Then Ferra put the armour on the scale, and the scale said what Emeric had known it would say since the autumn and had not wanted to say himself.

Three suits of plate, and a fourth in a crate. Pans and tools and a vise and drawers of rivets and spare plates and cells. Stores for eleven for a week and more. And whatever came home with them from Emberfell, which was the point of going. None of it had been in the ton and a third of empty berths. The berths had paid for the house. They had not paid for this.

Ferra did not say it. She put the figure in front of him and waited.

Emeric had the answer, and it was in her bunkers.

In the autumn, on this beach, he had written a rule in the notebook, and it was the rule her whole shape came from. If every stone in her went dark she would still have to be a ship: a steamer that could carry her people along a coast for ten days on coal and water. So her bunkers were sized for ten days, and every station in her had been sized with them full. But since the fit-out she steamed on stones. The coal in her bunkers was never burned. It only went into the fire on the day every stone in her was dead, and on that day the question was not ten days. It was how far to somewhere.

"Enough coal to reach a harbour," he said. "From anywhere on her road. Not ten days."

Fletcher was at the scale, because he was always at the scale when something was going into his ship. He looked at Emeric for a long moment.

"From here to where the mountain smokes," he said, "along the coast, there's nowhere she'd be two days from a harbour. Not in any weather I've seen." He said it because it was true, and it was plain that he wished it were not. "Two days is three ton. And a ton over, for a head sea."

"Four, then."

"She takes fourteen."

"Then ten come out."

Fletcher did not agree. He was a steamship man, whatever else he had been made into this year, and in forty years he had never once sent a hull to sea with her bunkers less than full, and he said so.

"The rule was written for the day nothing works," he said. "Not the stones in her fire. Not the bank. Not your band. That day. That's the day the coal's for."

"On that day," Emeric said, "she reaches a harbour. And burns wood in it, if she has to, badly, the way you've told me every steamer on this coast can. And sends a letter home, and waits. She isn't at sea any more. That's what the rule was for."

"And if the harbour's wrong?"

"Then she's in a wrong harbour with every soul aboard her alive."

Fletcher looked at the bunkers, and at the scale, and at Ferra's book. He was right, and Emeric was right, and they both knew it, and one of them was going to have to lose. It was Fletcher. He did it the way he did everything, flatly.

"Two days short of midwinter's eve," he said, to nobody. "I'll want to remember the day."

Ten tons of coal came up the beach in the barrows and went into a heap under a sail by Fletcher's cottage. It was her cargo, from that afternoon on: ten tons of room for whatever came home.

"And the sails?" said Saban, who had been listening from the scale with his samples.

"Aren't," said Fletcher, before Emeric could answer. "Not for this. They fold flat for the flying, and on the water they'll move her a little in a fair wind if you want to be very patient. You'll not sail a hundred feet of iron anywhere you want to be." He looked at Saban. "Don't count them."

Ferra entered the amendment in the book that held the rule, on the page after it, in her own hand, dated: *Stones dead, she reaches a harbour.* She did not cross the old rule out. She never crossed anything out. She wrote the new one under it and let them both stand.

Emeric tried for one more thing while the barrows were going, because it seemed the moment. A second water tank, aft, so that the showers could run longer than the time it took to say the word.

"New frames under it," said Fletcher. "It's not a weight you've got, it's a shape. That's a refit."

Emeric wrote it down in the notebook, under a heading he had just made, for the next hull.

The letter went out on the forty-third night, a week before they flew, because Ferra would not let them fly without it.

"A queen should be told," she said. "Before she's visited. It's the one thing every court in the world agrees on."

"It won't get there first."

"No," said Saban. He had done the sum before anybody asked him. "A coaster takes three weeks along the coast in winter, if she's lucky with the wind. Sent now, we beat it by a fortnight." He shrugged. "It'll get there. After we've gone home again, most likely, and it'll tell her we're coming."

"Then it goes," said Ferra, and wrote it.

She wrote it fair, from Emeric's draft, in her own hand: that the barony of Kaldo presented its respects to the sovereign of Emberfell, and that the barony's holder, Emeric, son of Harrold and of Amara, would call on her at her harbour at midwinter by sea, with a small party, on a matter of trade. She sealed it with Kaldo's seal and her own beside it. It went down the river the next morning in Saban's coat, and out of the lower water on the first coaster going west along the coast, with salt in her hold, and Saban paid the boatman three Silver for it and saw it into his hands himself.

The name was Fletcher's asking, on the last day, standing at the foot of her ladder with the loading done.

"She wants a name," he said.

"She's never needed one."

"She's never been anywhere." Fletcher looked up at her. "You bring a hull into a harbour, the first man down the quay is the harbourmaster's clerk, and the first thing he wants is her name for his book. A harbour that has to write *unknown* next to a ship in its book is a harbour that's already sent for its guard. I'll not have it said a hull off my beach came into a strange port with no name to give." He folded his arms. "And I'll not have her called *the ship* for the rest of her life, neither. I built her."

Emeric had thought about it. He had thought about it on and off since the autumn, and he had put the thought away every time because naming her was the kind of thing that made her more real than he was ready for her to be.

"The K Discovery," he said.

Fletcher tried it, under his breath, the way a man tries a new tool.

"The K?"

"Kaldo's." Emeric looked along her. Every standard rod in four baronies had a K struck into the end of it, cut by Jorrin into the face of a punch the spring the gauges were made, and the K on the rod meant that the inch on the rod was Kaldo's inch and could be trusted. "Every hull Kaldo builds will carry it."

Fletcher thought about that for a while, and then nodded, once.

"Not painted," Emeric said. "Not on her anywhere. She carries no banner, and a name on her stern is a banner. It goes in the harbour book at Emberfell, and nowhere else."

Ferra wrote it in her own book first, which was the first place it went anywhere. Emeric watched her write it and thought, for no reason he could name, of a clerk in the capital reading a single letter K in front of a name in a harbour book, somewhere along a coast, and knowing exactly what a K in front of a name meant, because that was what a mark in front of a name had always meant. He did not say it. There was nobody on the beach to say it to who would not already have thought it.

The hatch went down over the hold at dusk, and Ward locked it, and put the key back inside his shirt.

She lay at her mooring in the lagoon behind the bar with her tanks full and a quarter of her coal, with her cold room cold and her cork jackets in their lockers, and her name in nobody's book but Ferra's. Eleven people's winter was in her hold, and three suits on their stands, and one in a crate for a queen. The lamps in her day room were lit, and from the beach she looked like any black steamer at anchor on a winter evening, with nothing coming out of her stack.
