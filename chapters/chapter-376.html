Chapter 376 - Fire Company

The bell in the lower ward station went at nine minutes to eleven, and Osric was in his boots before it stopped.

He'd been asleep in the chair in the duty room, which he wasn't supposed to do and always did on the nights he had the station, with the stove ticking and the big doors shut and the motor pumper standing in the bay beyond the glass with its brass cold and its hoses flaked down in the bed the way he liked them. The bell was the long one, three and three, which was the harbour. The board on the wall under it had a lamp lit beside the third hook from the left. East quay.

The lad on the telephone was already shouting it. "East quay. Chandler's. Leask's warehouse. Board says there's flame through the roof and the..." He listened. "Board says the casks are going. Board says it's on the water."

"Who's on the Board?"

"Sergeant Dunmore's the officer. And the sea watch." The lad put his hand over the mouthpiece. "It's her, sir. The lady from the bay house."

"Tell Dunmore I'm coming down the quay and I want the boat out." Osric was getting his coat on, and his helmet, the leather one with the brass comb that the company had voted him in the spring and that he hated. "Tell him I want the boat out now, not when I get there, and the upper ward's pumper down the quay road behind me, and the yard's, and the knights on the wall to clear the quay. And ring the castle." He stopped at the bay door. "And the Works. Tess will want to know her towers aren't on fire."

"Are they?"

"No," said Osric. "But she'll want to be told."



The pumper went down Quay Street with its bell going and its lamps on and six men on the back of it, hanging on, and the whole of the lower ward came to its windows. He could see the glow before they were halfway. It was in the sky over the warehouse roofs at the bottom of the hill, a low dirty orange that came and went with the smoke, and under the glow, when they came round the corner of the fish market onto the quay, the thing itself.

Leask's was a long brick shed on the east quay with a slate roof and big timber doors on the water side, and it held what a chandler held: rope, and tar, and paint, and pitch in barrels, and about three hundred casks of lamp oil from the works, waiting for the lighters. Half the roof was already gone. The flames came up through the hole in a column you could have read a newspaper by, and the slates were going off the rest of the roof like gunshots, one after another, flying out over the quay and smashing on the stones. The heat came across the quay at him like the open door of a furnace. The doors on the water side had burned through, and the casks were going out of them.

That was the worst of it. That was what the Board had meant by on the water. The casks had been stacked at the doors for the morning lighter, and as the doors went the casks went too, the ones nearest the heat bursting their hoops and the ones behind them rolling, and they were going off the quay's edge into the harbour, a dozen of them already, two dozen, and some of them were burst and some of them were going to be, and the oil was on the water, and the oil was alight.

It lay on the black water at the foot of the quay in a long ragged sheet, burning low and blue and yellow, and it was moving. Osric watched it move. It was going out from the quay, slowly, into the inner basin, on the last of the ebb.

Into the inner basin, where the hulls were.

He could see them from where he stood: the long dark shapes moored at the coal quay across the basin, three hundred yards off. The K Endeavor at the head of the line, her stones cold for the winter refit, sitting on the water on her own hull the way the big class could. Hull four astern of her. Escort three on the far side of them, the battleship, with her guns sheeted. Seven hundred feet of steel and timber and stones and Bram's whole winter, and a sheet of burning oil going toward it across the water at the speed a man walks.

"Sir," said somebody.

"I see it."

He got the pumper's monitor onto the warehouse's landward doors, because the landward side had the ropewalk's store behind it, and if that went the whole east quay went with it, and the next street. The lads ran the hoses out to the hydrant on the corner and the monitor on its turntable on the pumper's deck came round under Garvey's hands and the stream went out across the quay into the doors, white and heavy, and the stone in the monitor's pressure housing took it and tightened it, and it went through the doorway into the fire like a fist into a pillow. Steam came out of the door in a great grey wave. The fire in the doorway lay down and got up again.

The upper ward's pumper came round the fish market corner with its bell going, and then the yard's, and he put them on the ropewalk's side, one on the store's roof and one on its doors, to wet them down, and didn't look at the water again for a minute because he couldn't do anything about the water from here.

Then the fireboat came.



She came out of the boat slip under the sea wall's root where she lived, past the Harbour Board in its customs tower with every window lit, and she came fast, her diesel thudding, her bow wave white in the light from the fire. Forty feet of steel with a squat wheelhouse and the two monitors on their raised platforms fore and aft, like a pair of herons on a fence. Tolliver was at the forward monitor. Osric could tell from three hundred yards that it was Tolliver, because Tolliver was standing on the platform's edge without his line clipped on, and Osric would have shouted at him if there had been any point.

She came across the mouth of the inner basin and turned in toward the quay, toward the burning sheet on the water, and the ebb took her.

It was running hard. It always ran hard in the last hour, down out of the basin and through the narrows and out under the light, and a forty-foot boat that turned broadside to it in the narrows got pushed, and the fireboat got pushed: Osric saw her stern swing out with the current, the whole boat going crabwise down toward the narrows while the diesel roared and the rudder fought it, and the monitors pointing at nothing.

And then she stopped.

She didn't stop the way a boat stops on an anchor, with a jerk and a swing. She stopped as if somebody had put a hand on her. The water along her flank, low down, at the turn of her hull, boiled white in three places, and then three more on the other side, and she sat there in the full ebb, broadside, quite still, with her bow toward the burning oil and her monitors coming round.

The ports. Bram's ports, that Dagny had put in her last week and had only ever run in the slip. Six of them, three a side, down at the bilge, each with a propeller in its tunnel and a water stone behind the glass, pushing out. Holding her where she was against a five-knot ebb as if the tide weren't there.

"Huh," said Osric, out loud, to nobody.

Both monitors opened at once.

The forward one went onto the quay's edge and the doors, where the casks were still coming out, and Tolliver laid the stream along the line of the doorway and kept it there, and the casks at the doors stopped rolling and lay where they were in a cloud of steam. The after one went onto the water. Not onto the burning oil; you couldn't put burning oil out with a hose, every child in the fire company's school knew that, you'd only spread it. The man on the after monitor put his stream onto the water in front of the sheet, between the oil and the hulls, flat and hard and low, so that it hit the water and drove it, and the stone in the housing bent the stream into a long fan along the water's skin, and the sheet's leading edge, where it touched that fan, stopped coming.

It stopped. It didn't go back. It stood there on the water, burning, with a wall of flung spray in front of it, and the fireboat sat in the ebb holding the wall up, with her ports boiling and her diesel roaring and the light of the fire on her paint.

It was holding. It was holding and it was going to stop holding, because the ebb was nearly done.



The telephone on the pumper's step rang. Somebody gave it to him. It was the Board.

"Sergeant Dunmore," said a voice, very calm, the way Dunmore always was, which was why Osric had picked him for the Board. "Sir. Sea watch wants you."

"Put her on."

There was a pause and a clatter, and then Ilenne's voice, in his ear, down two miles of wire and the first of Tess's glass, from the customs tower at the root of the sea wall.

"Slack," she said. "Eleven and thirty-two. Then flood."

He looked at his watch in the firelight. Seventeen past.

"Fifteen minutes," he said.

"Fifteen," Ilenne agreed. "Ebb stops. Boat holds the fire, now, on the ebb. Ebb pushes fire out, boat pushes fire back. Good. Even." She said it the way she said the soundings on Ferra's chart, as things that were so. "Then slack. Nothing pushes. Then flood." She paused. "Flood pushes fire in. To the ships. Boat cannot hold the flood. Flood is big, here, in the narrow."

He'd known the flood was coming. He hadn't known it to the minute.

"Can you hold it off?"

"Not me," said Ilenne. "The gate."

The current gate. He'd forgotten the current gate. It wasn't his; it was the Board's. Four housings on their footings across the floor of the narrows, the first four of eleven, put down in November and switched on for the first time in a drill on Monday, when the Board's lines had gone over to Tess's glass. An impeller in each and a water stone in each, pushing sideways. You switched them on together and the narrows had a current across it that hadn't been there before.

"Across," said Ilenne, in his ear. "Not in, not out. Across. Gate pushes the water to the north side. To the shoal. Oil goes with the water." He heard her breathe. "At slack, gate on. At flood, the flood comes in, the gate pushes across, the oil goes to the shoal, not to the ships. On the shoal it burns, and burns out. Nothing there. Sand."

"Will it work?"

"Yes," said Ilenne. "I swim it. Every morning. I know where the water goes."

There was another clatter and Dunmore came back.

"Sir. It's two keys."

"I know it's two keys. Who's got the other?"

"The wall roll's man tonight is Corporal Vane, sir. He's here. He came down off the wall when the bell went, as he's meant to." A pause. "He's asked me to tell you he's never turned it for anything but a drill."

"Nor have you."

"No, sir."

Osric looked at the water. The sheet of oil was burning on it in front of the fireboat's fan of spray, low and blue at the edges and yellow in the middle, and he could see where it had got into the gaps between the boat's stream and the quay, and was feeling its way along the stones. Behind it the warehouse roof went in, all at once, with a sound like a cartload of coal tipped down a chute, and a column of sparks went up into the smoke and came down on the water.

He didn't ring the castle. He'd told the lad to ring the castle, and the lad had, and the castle had said the king was up at the Well with Orla Garran and the drum, and could be rung there. He thought about it for the length of one breath. Then he didn't, because it was fourteen minutes, and the king was five miles up, and two keys was two keys, and the woman on the Board had swum the narrows every morning since August.

"Sea watch says slack is at thirty-two," he said. "Gate on at thirty-two. On her word, not mine. She calls it, you two turn it. Tell Vane that. Tell him it's her call."

"Sir."

"And tell the boat."



At eleven thirty-one the ebb stopped.

Osric felt it more than saw it. The fireboat's ports had been boiling white along her flanks for twenty minutes, and at about half past they stopped boiling so hard, and then the man in the wheelhouse throttled them right back, and then they were only a stir at her bilge, and she sat in water that wasn't going anywhere, and so did the oil. It hung on the black water in its ragged sheet, burning, not moving, at the foot of the quay. Somewhere out in the mouth a gull went past, low, crying.

At eleven thirty-two, on the quay's telephone in his hand, he heard Ilenne say, "Now."

And two keys turned, a yard apart, at a brass board in a customs tower two miles off, and the light went down Tess's glass in a pulse he'd never see, and at the bottom of the narrows, in the dark, under thirty feet of slack black water, four impellers began to turn, and four water stones woke behind their glass.

For a long moment nothing happened at all.

Then the oil began to move. Not toward the hulls. Not out. Sideways, across the basin's mouth, slowly, in a long curve, the whole burning sheet of it bending away from the quay to the north the way smoke bends in a draught, toward the dark side of the narrows where the shoal was, where nothing was moored and nothing ever had been because Ferra's chart said two feet at low water and the pilots all knew it.

The flood came in at eleven forty. He felt that too: the fireboat, holding, swinging her head a little to it, her ports picking up again; and the water in the narrows beginning to run the other way, inward, toward the basin and the coal quay and the hulls. And the oil should have gone with it. The oil should have come straight up the basin on the flood toward the K Endeavor's high steel side.

It went north.

It went north across the flood, carried in the gate's sideways river, the whole sheet of it drawn out long and thin and burning, a long low line of blue and yellow fire going away across the black water toward the shoal, and on the shoal, where the sand came up under it, it lay down, and burned, and burned out, in a long line of small fires that went out one after another, all along the north side of the narrows, like the lamps of a street being put out at dawn.

Nobody on the quay said anything.

The fireboat swung her after monitor off the water and onto the warehouse, alongside Tolliver's, and the two streams went into the burning shell together, and the yard's pumper and the upper ward's came round onto it from the land side, and the warehouse, which had nothing left in it now that would burn very much, began, slowly and with a great deal of steam and hissing and the occasional falling slate, to go out.

Osric put the telephone to his ear again.

"Board."

"Sir," said Dunmore.

"Tell sea watch," said Osric, and stopped, because he didn't know what to tell her. "Tell her thank you. Tell her it went to the shoal."

A pause. Then Ilenne's voice, quite close, as if she'd taken the earpiece off Dunmore without asking, which she probably had.

"I see it," she said. "From the window. Long fire, on the sand." She was quiet a moment. "It is pretty. Is that bad?"

"No," said Osric. "No, it's all right. It is pretty."



Emeric came down the quay at twenty past twelve, in a borrowed coat, with his hair full of soot from somebody else's fire.

He had seen it from the Well. He'd been on the deck at the drum's rail with Orla Garran and a lamp and BR-01's last week of beats in Ambrose's book, five miles over the town, and Orla had said, "That's a fire," in the voice she used for weather, and he'd looked down through the Band's thin clear air at the dark map of Kaldo below with its lamps in their lines, and seen it: a small hot red bead on the east quay, very bright, with a smudge going away from it on the water. He'd rung the line room. Ambrose had rung the station. The station had said the captain was on the quay and the boat was out and the Board had it, and he'd said he was coming down.

It had taken him forty minutes. The Hod's tender off the Well's deck and down in the dark, and a cab from the yard. On the way down he'd watched the red bead through the tender's glass. Halfway down he'd watched the smudge on the water bend away from the bead to the north, in a long curve, and stretch out, and become a line of small lights on the far side of the narrows; and then the lights had gone out, one after another, and by the time the tender touched the yard the bead was a glow and the glow was steam.

So there was nothing left when he got there. There was the warehouse, a black brick shell with no roof, steaming, with two pumpers still playing water on it for the form of the thing and the fireboat lying off the quay with her monitors shut down and her ports ticking. There was the east quay, wet from end to end and covered in broken slate and the black staves of burst casks. There was a smell of tar and wet ash and lamp oil that would be in the lower ward for a week. There were a great many people from the lower ward on the far side of the knights' rope, in their nightclothes and their coats, watching, and some of them clapping the pumper crews when they went past, which the pumper crews pretended not to notice. And there was Osric, on the pumper's step in his leather helmet with the brass comb, with a mug of something the fish market's night watchman had brought him, and his face black to the eyebrows.

He looked up when Emeric came through the rope.

"Majesty."

"Is anybody hurt?"

"Leask's night man. Burns on both hands, getting the cart horses out of the back. They're at the ward. Imani's with him." Osric drank. "Garvey on the upper ward's pumper put his ankle through a hose coupling and says it's nothing, and it's not nothing, and he'll be at the ward too, when I've finished shouting at him." He looked at the water. "Tolliver had his line off on the forward platform for the whole of it. I'll be shouting at him as well."

"The hulls?"

"Not a mark."

Emeric looked out across the basin at the coal quay, where the K Endeavor stood high on her floats with her envelope furled, and hull four astern of her, and escort three beyond, all of them dark and wet and quite untouched, with a few of the yard's men on their decks in the light of lanterns, looking back at the quay.

"What happened?"

Osric told him.

He told him in order, the way a fire company report was meant to be written, from the bell at nine minutes to eleven: the station, the quay, the casks, the oil on the ebb. The pumpers and the ropewalk. The boat across the narrows, and the ebb taking her, and the ports. ("Dagny put them in last week. They'd only been run in the slip. She held in the full ebb broadside like she was tied to a post.") The after monitor's fan on the water in front of the sheet. The telephone, and the Board, and sea watch. Fifteen minutes to slack. The gate. Across, not in or out. Two keys, Dunmore and the wall roll's man, a corporal called Vane who had never turned it for anything but a drill. Her word at thirty-two. The flood at forty. The oil going north across the flood onto the shoal and burning out on the sand.

"I didn't ring you," he said at the end. "The lad rang the castle and the castle said you were at the Well. I thought about it. It was fourteen minutes and you were five miles up, and she knew the water." He turned the mug in his black hands. "It was her call. I told Dunmore to tell Vane that. It's in my report."

Emeric stood on the wet quay in the smell of tar and looked at the shoal, where the last of the burned-out oil lay on the sand in a long dark smear, quite still, in the light from the lower ward's windows.

He had come forty minutes down from the sky as fast as anything in Kaldo could bring him, with his heart going the whole way, rehearsing in his head what he'd do when he got there: where he'd stand, what he'd throw, whether the pull would reach a burning cask on the water from the quay's edge, whether he could make a wall of steam with fire. He'd had all of it ready. He'd had it ready since the Band.

There was nothing to do with any of it. It was out. It had been out for half an hour. A sergeant and a corporal had turned two keys on a mermaid's word, and Dagny's ports had held a fireboat in a five-knot ebb, and Tess's glass had carried the pulse, and a man called Tolliver had stood on a platform without his line and kept a stream on a doorway full of casks, and Osric had made his calls one after another without ringing anybody, and all of it had worked.

He found he didn't know what to say.

"Good," he said, at last.

"Yes," said Osric, and looked at him with an expression Emeric couldn't read through the soot, and then perhaps he could. "That's what it's for, Majesty. The station and the boat and the Board. So that you're up there with a lamp, and it's done." He drank the last of the mug. "You'll want to go and thank her. She's still in the tower. Dunmore says she won't go home till the shoal's dark."

"Osric."

"Sir."

"Tomorrow's the full drill."

"Ten o'clock," said Osric. "Every station, the boat, the Board, the towers, the wall." He stood up off the pumper's step and put his helmet straight. "I'm not cancelling it."

"I wasn't going to ask you to."

"I know you weren't. I'm telling the lads." He nodded over his shoulder at the pumper crews, who were coiling hose on the wet stones and who all, very clearly, had heard. "Last night was a fire. Tomorrow's the drill. They're different things."

He went off down the quay to shout at Tolliver.



Ilenne was at the customs tower's top window when he got there, with the Board's lamps lit behind her in their brass row, and Dunmore at the board with his headset round his neck writing up the log, and Corporal Vane, who was perhaps nineteen, sitting on the bench by the door with his key still on its chain round his neck and his hands between his knees, looking at the floor as though he'd been told something about himself he hadn't known.

She didn't turn round when Emeric came in. She was watching the shoal. The last little fire on it had gone out while he was on the stairs.

"Dark," she said.

"Osric says thank you."

"Osric says. Dunmore says. Vane says." She turned her head and looked at the boy on the bench, who looked up, and went red. "Everybody says." She looked back at Emeric. Her colour, in the Board's lamplight, was the faint clear brightness that Imani's card had down since September as pleased with herself. "My job. Sea watch. I watch the sea." She shrugged, a small movement of the shoulders under the cloak, a Kaldo shrug she'd picked up from Zale. "Tonight the sea does a thing. I watch it."

"You did more than watch it."

"I say when." She held up one long grey hand and turned it over. "They turn the keys. Not me. Two keys." She said it carefully, the way she said a word she was proud of having right. "I know the water. They know the gate. The boat knows the fire. Everybody knows one thing." She looked out of the window again at the dark shoal. "Then it is out."

"Yes," said Emeric.

She looked at him sideways. "You come down from the sky."

"I came down from the sky."

"Fast?"

"As fast as I could."

"And it is out." Her mouth went in at the corner. "Good," she said, kindly, the way you'd say it to a child who'd run all the way from school with something to tell and found everybody already knew. And then, because she was Ilenne: "Next time, stay up. Look at the stars. We are fine."



The Long Table was nine days later, on the twenty-fifth, and nothing was built at it at all.

Every lane in Kaldo put a table down the middle of itself, as every lane had since the first year Ferra wrote it in the book, out of doors, in the cold, with braziers between and lamps strung over on the tram wires and everybody in their coats; and in the upper ward, where the castle's lane met Gate Avenue, the castle put its table down with everybody else's and sat at it.

It was long that year. Ferra had measured it twice. The six, and the knights who weren't on the wall, and their wives and husbands and children. Harrold and Amara at the far end in the dark past the last brazier, a careful hand's width apart, her hood well forward. Tess and Osric, Osric's hands still pink in the creases from the soot. Wren, in her motorcycle jacket, toasted, because it was her decaday: twenty, and Ferra stood up on the bench and said so to the whole lane, and Wren went the colour of the braziers and drank her cup straight off so that nobody could make her stand up. Maeve, toasted too, because it was hers: thirty, which she said was a terrible thing to be told in public, and stood up anyway and bowed to the lane, from the waist, the way she'd bowed to the tower on the point. Zale, with his stick hooked over the bench behind him, the stone against his skin nearly six weeks now and Imani's day for swallowing it set for the first week of the new year. Ilenne beside him, hooded, at a Kaldo table for the first time in her life, eating everything Maren put in front of her and asking what it was afterwards. Maren up and down the length of it with dishes, refusing to sit. Doran at the head, who said the blessing over the bread, and kept it short, because it was cold.

And Corbin, three places down from the end, with his watcher beside him, because the watcher had wanted to come and Corbin had come with him. He had his foot on the bench's bar, and the gold leaves of the anklet showed above his boot in the lamplight when he leaned to pass the salt, and he passed it, and said nothing, and nobody at that table but the six and Emeric knew what was in the stone under the leaves.

Emeric sat between Ferra and Imani and ate Maren's goose and drank Zale's mulled wine and listened to the lane, which was very loud. He was given a small badly-carved wooden fish by a child he'd never met, who said it was for the king and ran away. He gave Tess, because he'd been meaning to for a month, a brass ring a size smaller than the one on her finger, with the same flat stone in it, which Vesk had moved across for him that morning without asking why; and she took the big one off and put the new one on and turned her hand to the brazier to see the stone, and said "It fits," and nothing else, and it was enough.

At some point, late, with the braziers burning low and the children asleep on their parents' coats along the benches, Ilenne leaned across Zale and said to him, quietly, under the noise:

"You look at him."

He knew who she meant. He hadn't known he was doing it.

"My brother."

"Your brother." She looked down the table at Corbin, who was listening to his watcher tell him something about a dog, with the face that gave nothing away. "You look at him like a man looks at the sea before a storm. Like he wants to see it come." She turned back to Emeric, and her eyes in the hood were a shade too large and very steady. "The sea does not know you look. It only is the sea."

Emeric didn't say anything.

"Eat," said Ilenne, and pushed Maren's last plate of gingerbread across Zale's lap toward him, and Zale said "Oi," and she ignored him, and the lane went on being loud, and the lamps swung on the tram wires overhead in the cold, and nobody built anything at all.
