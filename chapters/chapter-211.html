Chapter 211 - Satisfied

The fifteen went south on the twenty-eighth, on the ship, in the saloon, on mattresses laid edge to edge across the floor from the iron box to the door, with Imani and her two nurses sitting among them on the boards and the panes blacked so that nobody would have to look down.

Fletcher flew her low and slow the whole way, at a walk, on the push alone, so that the floor never tilted and the stones in the lift matrix overhead hummed no louder than bees; and when Imani told him through the speaking tube that Senna had asked whether they were in a cart, he took her lower still, and slower, and the trip that should have taken two hours took six. Nobody complained. Tansy slept for most of it with her head on her mother's lap. Robin sat up the whole way with his doll and his back against the iron box, listening to the hum, and at the end of the first hour said to nobody that it was not the cart, it was a bee, and went to sleep too.

Imani had the end room of the hospital ready for them. She had telephoned it ready from Wayford on the twenty-sixth, in the evening, between the litters and the stair: fifteen beds, fires lit, every light stone and warmer and bead taken out and locked in the dispensary, tallow in saucers on every sill. She went in ahead of the first litter and stood in the middle of the floor and turned round once, slowly, looking at the walls; and only then let them bring the litters in.

Wat walked in on his own. He stood just inside the door in his boots and his blanket and looked at the room, at the beds and the fires and the window over the river, for some while. Then he went to the bed by the window, the one nobody had chosen yet, and lay down on it with his face to the glass, and did not move again until morning.



The net had come down already, on the night of the eighteenth, the way it had gone up, quietly and in the dark, with Ferra standing over every part of it.

While Osric was still going from chimney to chimney across Wayford with his bag, Vesk and the two clerks who had sat by the bed all summer had unscrewed the office board from the wall of the room behind Ambry's, and coiled its needle and its wires into a crate, and lifted the bed of the mana printer out and packed it in straw; and the printer had gone south in the ship's hold at nine that night, and was home in the cellar under the keep before the eighteenth was out, as Ferra had said at the long table in May that it would be, on the day the knots were drained. Orrin charged its four push stones the next evening, and the school had its copybooks from it again by the end of the week. The two clerks were paid their summer's wage again on top of their wage, and sent home on the ship with a letter in Ferra's hand saying that the barony was in their debt and that the third condition had been kept, and that she knew what it had cost them to keep it. The bag of drained knots went into the strongroom at Kaldo in a box with a lid that Ferra screwed down herself.

She entered it all. The date, the crate, the wages, the box. At the foot of the last entry she drew a line across the page, the whole width, ruled, and under the line she wrote nothing at all, and turned to a clean page.

"That's the net," she said.

Nobody in Wayford knew it had been there. Nobody outside Kaldo would ever know. The Staple had been broken on what could be got openly: Sethe's boxes, and the five refusing a summons, and a cellar under a house, and a book taken out of a burning room, and fifteen people on a platform with a bell. Not one word that came off the knots had gone before anybody. The fourth condition had held to the end, and Ferra had held it, and she sat back from the clean page now and looked at it, with her pencil laid straight beside it, the way she looked at a sum that had come out and that she did not entirely like.



Keller wrote in the second week of September.

The letter came up from the post house by the ordinary rider, in an ordinary cover, with the crown's magistrate's seal on it, small and plain, and inside it a single sheet in a clerk's hand with two lines at the foot in Keller's own; and it said what Keller's letters said, which was the least that could be said to the purpose.

The crown's magistrate acknowledged the Holder's letter of the twenty-first of August, received on the thirty-first. He acknowledged also the report of the northern circuit's magistrate, received on the third of September, and the copy of the Holder's notice enclosed with it. He requested, for the crown's file, a full record of the matter of the Staple at Wayford, with the evidence on which the Holder had acted, and the names of the dead, and the date and manner of each. And at the foot, in the two lines in Keller's own hand, small and square and leaning nowhere:

The crown's file will be read. It is not mine to say by whom.

Emeric read that twice.

"He's telling you somebody will read it who'd like to hang you with it," said Ferra, across the long table, with her hand flat on her book. "And that he can't stop them. And that he would if he could, a little." She considered it. "That's warm, for Keller."

So he wrote the record.

It took him nine days. He wrote it at the long table in the evenings, from the ledger and Ferra's sheets and the parish books and his own notes, with Ferra across from him checking every figure as he wrote it, and Ward at the end of the table reading every page when it was finished, slowly, and handing it back without a word, which from Ward meant there was nothing in it he would have had otherwise. Every line of it was true. He made sure of that. The summons and the refusal, the thirty-eight men and the burning, the night, the strongroom and the book. The cellar, all of it: the chairs and the cradles and the ring and the racks, in the plainest words he had. Two hundred and ninety-seven. Sixty-two. Two hundred and twenty. A hundred and four, and more by every post, as the priests' letters came in from the rest of the barony. Every one of the fifteen, by name. The square. The five, by name, and the date, and the manner, and his order, in his own words: not by writ.

And not one word of how he had known where to look.

There was a way of writing it so that the gap did not show. He found it. It was not difficult; it was only a matter of starting each part of the story where the open evidence started it, Sethe's boxes, and Placit's summons, and the fortified house, and never once standing anywhere a reader might ask how he had come to stand there. Ferra read the finished thing through at one sitting, on the ninth evening, with her pencil, and did not mark it once.

"Every line's true," she said, at the end.

"It's not the whole."

"No." She squared the pages. "The fourth condition. Nothing heard goes before a court." She looked at the top sheet. "This is going before a court. Of a kind." She put her hand on it. "It's true in every line, and it doesn't lie by a single word, and there's a hole in the middle of it the shape of a summer, and nobody who reads it will ever see the edge of the hole." She was quiet. "I made the rule that made you write it like that. I'll not pretend I didn't."

It went to the capital by the ship, this time, in a sealed case, to Keller's own hand. There was nothing in it to be five days too late for any more.



Aldane's sheet came up the river on the fifteenth, in Saban's coat.

Saban had it from his man in the capital, who had it off the chancery steps, where it had been pasted at dawn on the ninth, the same morning as on three church doors in the old town and the door of the drapers' hall; and his man had peeled one off the drapers' door with a knife while it was still damp and sent it south folded in a bale of cloth, because Saban's man did not believe in sending anything south in a letter that could go in a bale. It was printed. Badly, on rag, with a worn black-letter fount and a border of black down all four sides like a funeral card. Saban laid it on the long table and smoothed it out with the side of his hand and sat down, and did not say anything, because the sheet said it.

It was a letter of condolence. His Grace of Aldane condoled, publicly, with the widows of the late Lord Placit and the late Lord Vennor, daughters both of houses that had sat at his table for three generations, upon the deaths of their husbands at Wayford on the twenty-sixth of August; who had been hanged in a market square, in the sight of a mob, without the crown's writ, without a trial, by the word of a prince who held their barony by grace and their road by none. His Grace made no comment upon the charge. The charge was a matter for the crown, which had not been asked. His Grace observed only that the lords of the north had long memories and old friendships, and that they did not forget their dead. And he gave the widows his word, and the word of his house, that whatever debts of honour were owed to the houses of Placit and Vennor would be paid, in full, by those who owed them.

Ward read it standing, at the end of the table, the way he read everything: once, slowly, with no change in his face.

He put it down.

"That is not a letter," said Ward. "That is a muster."

Nobody at the table said anything for as long as it took Saban to take out his pipe and look at it and put it away again unlit.

"Debts of honour," said Ward, "is what a man calls a war he means to start before he has a reason for it. He is not writing to two widows. He is writing to every house in the north that has a cousin at his table and a grudge against a southern prince with a press, and he is telling them where to stand when it starts." He looked at the sheet. "He will need a reason. He will find one." He looked down the table at Emeric. "Not before the spring, if he has sense. The roads are long and the harvest is in. In the winter, if he does not care what it costs him."

"How long have we got?"

"As long as it takes him to find the reason," said Ward. "Begin today."



They began that day.

Ward had the list. He had had it, Emeric understood, for a great deal longer than that afternoon, written in his small square hand on a sheet of the school's paper folded in four in the breast of his coat, and he unfolded it now on the long table on top of Aldane's black border and went down it, one line at a time, the way he went down the garrison roll.

Powder. Not seven cartridges; powder in quantity, by the barrel, enough for every rifle Kaldo could make to fire a hundred rounds and fire them again. That meant saltpetre, and saltpetre meant Saban.

Saban had seen it coming before Ward unfolded the sheet, and had priced it. There were nitre beds by the tannery and behind the dock quarter, which Emeric had had started in the winter for Fennick's blasting, and they were coming along; but a nitre bed took a year or two to ripen and Ward did not think they had a year. So: the old way, the slow sour way, which every powder maker in history had used before anybody thought of a bed. The earth of a cattle byre floor, where beasts had stood and pissed for a hundred winters, had saltpetre in it, and so did a dovecote's, and a stable's, and the floor of a sheep shed; and a man with a spade and a tub and a kettle could scrape the floor, and leach it, and boil the liquor down, and draw off the white crystals at the end, and put down a new floor for the beasts out of fresh earth and come back in five years. Saban had six men who would do it for the wage, and knew twelve more, and had already, he said, written to all of them, the day the sheet came up the river, on the expectation.

"Whose byres?" said Ferra.

"Everybody's in the four baronies who'll take a fresh floor and a Silver a byre and not ask what for," said Saban. "Which is everybody." He considered. "Milford's first. Milford has more cattle standing on old floors than the rest of the south together. Paxton's west byres haven't been dug out since his grandfather."

"Paxton will write," said Ferra.

"Paxton," said Saban, "will write an inventory of his byre floors, by the square yard, and object at the bottom." He looked mildly pleased about it. "I'll price his objection into the Silver."

Then Fennick. Fennick was at the foot of the table because Ward had sent for him, sitting very upright on the end of the bench with his maimed hand in his lap and the expression of a man who has been told he is not in trouble and does not believe it. Ward told him that he would have the powder house. A new one, in the old quarry beyond the works, where nobody went but Fennick; it would be built in stone, with a copper roof and copper hinges and not one nail of iron in it anywhere. He would make the powder and load the cartridges. He would have six men, chosen by Ward. And the powder house would have one key, which would hang on Ward's belt, and nowhere else, and Fennick would go in when Ward opened the door and come out when Ward closed it.

Fennick heard all of it with his face going slowly red, and at the end of it he laughed. It came out of him before he could stop it, the laugh he was known for, the one that always started a breath before the bang; and then he put his maimed hand over his mouth, and looked at Ward over it, and took it away.

"Yes, sir," said Fennick. "Your key." His eyes were wet. "I'll not ask for it. Not once."

"You will ask for it every day," said Ward. "And I will give it to you every day. That is what a key is for."

And then the rifles.

This was the line Ward had spent longest on, Emeric could see; it was the longest on the sheet, and the most crossed out. Not one rifle with a barrel cut in a night in the Forge. Three hundred. Single shot, breech loading, a brass case with the powder and the ball in it and a cap in its base, so that a farmer could put one in the breech and close it and fire it and open it and do it again, kneeling behind a bank, faster than any man had ever fired a weapon in this world; and the barrels and the actions and the locks made in the open works, by men, from gauges, the same today as tomorrow and the same as the man's beside him, so that a part off one rifle went into any other. Bram's work, and Ottoline's. Ottoline had cut the gauges with Bram in the spring, in Wayford and at the works, between the clocks, without being told what for, because Emeric had drawn them and asked; and Bram had had them on his wall since May, in a row in a felt-lined case, and looked at them every morning, and said nothing.

"Not from the Forge," said Ferra.

Nobody had said it would be. She said it anyway, with her hand on her book, and looked at Emeric across the table.

"No," said Emeric. "Not one barrel. Bram's barrels, on Bram's bench."

"Then why," said Ferra, "is there a forge run on Ward's list?"

There was. At the foot of the sheet, in Ward's hand, under the rifles: Cases. Dies. A run.

"Because the cases want drawing," said Emeric. "Brass, out of a flat disc, into a cup and then a tube with a closed end, in four steps through four dies, each smaller than the last. Bram's press can do it. It can do it a thousand times a day. But the dies have to be harder than anything Bram's forge can make and truer than anything Bram's lathe can cut, or the cases come out a hair wrong and split in the breech and take a man's eye." He looked at the sheet. "One set of masters. Four dies. Bram copies them for the press. No barrel."

Ferra looked at him a long breath.

"It's not a barrel," she said. "The rule says barrel."

"I know what the rule says. I signed it." He looked at her. "It's a weapon. I'll open the book."



Paxton wrote on the eighteenth, on the Milford line, and it took the clerk in the gate tower most of the morning.

It was an inventory. Of course it was. The steward of Milford sent his respects to the Holder and his compliments to the Chancellor, in that order, and begged to inform the Holder that the holding of Milford had upon its rolls, as of that morning, one hundred and forty tenants, of whom thirty-one were in arrears of rent, to the sums following; and the sums followed, all thirty-one, by name, to the Copper, with the date each had first fallen behind. It went on for most of a sheet. At the bottom, in the same flat clicking line as all the rest, the clerk had written down the objection.

The steward of Milford objected to the clause of the notice of the twenty-first of August that forbade the holding of any person for debt. He did not hold any person for debt and had never done so; he had never needed to. But the old law had stood behind every rent roll in the kingdom for three hundred years, like a dog in a yard, and nobody had needed to let it off the chain because everybody knew it was there. The Holder had taken the dog out of the yard. The steward of Milford asked the Holder respectfully what was now to stop thirty-one tenants in arrears from walking off their holdings across the border into the next barony with their rent unpaid; and begged to remain, et cetera.

Ferra read it standing at the back of the telegraph room with her book, as she read everything off the Milford line, and checked the thirty-one names against her own. They were right. She put a small mark beside two of them.

"He's not wrong," she said.

"No."

"About the dog." She closed the book. "Every landlord in the north reads that notice and thinks the same thing, and most of them aren't as honest as Paxton, and don't write." She looked at him. "What do you want me to answer?"

Emeric thought about it.

"That a tenant who walks off his holding with his rent unpaid can be followed to the next barony by a magistrate's order, and a part of his wages taken there, a Copper in the Silver, until it's paid," he said. "That his goods can be taken. That his name can be written in a book, and the book read by any landlord he goes to next." He was quiet. "And that the steward of Milford has thirty-one tenants in arrears in a year when the barony bought his whole harvest at his own price, and might ask himself why, and send me the answer, by the square yard."

Ferra wrote it down. She did not soften any of it. When it went down the wire that afternoon Paxton's clerk acknowledged it in four clicks, and the next morning, a little after the second bell, the Milford line clicked again; and it was a second inventory, of the byre floors of the holding of Milford, by the square yard, with the depth of each in inches, and the number of beasts that had stood on it last winter, and the date it had last been dug out, if ever; and at the bottom the steward of Milford objected to a man called Saban sending six men with spades into his west byres with a Silver a byre and a fresh floor, without anybody telling the steward what for.

Saban read it over Ferra's shoulder and took out his pipe.

"There," he said, with deep satisfaction. "By the square yard."



He cut the dies on the twenty-second of September, at night, in the cellar under the keep.

He opened the forge book first. He opened it on the bench at the end of the long rails, at the page after the last run, where Ferra's rule stood under the barrel's entry in her large clear hand with his signature under it; and he did what the rule said, though the rule said barrel, and this was not a barrel. He wrote the run's number. He wrote what it was: four cartridge-case drawing dies, a master set, for Bram's press, for the rifle line. And above it, before he had touched anything, in his own hand, the reason.

He sat with the pen over the line for some time before he wrote it.

Because Aldane is coming, and when he comes three hundred men of Kaldo will kneel behind a bank against knights, and a case that splits in the breech will take a man's eye; and I would rather make the dies true than tell a man's mother why I did not.

Then he ran it. It took the night. He felt the Forge take him, the way it always did, slowly and then not slowly, the long patient pull at the bottom of his well as the lattice went from red to cherry to the colour of noon and the shaping went on in the centring cage; and at the end of it, in the grey before dawn, he let the cage down and took them out, four of them, in a row, still warm, small and plain and grey and so hard that a file skated off them like a skate off ice. Four holes, each a little smaller than the last. That was all. They did not look like anything. Nobody looking at them on a bench would have known what they were for.

Ferra came down at seven, as she had said she would. She read the entry. She read the reason above it twice. Then she took her own pencil out of her pocket, and initialled the run, small, under his line, and dated it.

"That's a better reason than the barrel's," she said.

"The barrel's was I don't know."

"That's what I mean." She closed the book. "Go to bed."

He did not go to bed. He took the dies across the river to the works in a box under his arm and gave them to Bram, who looked at them on his palm one after another under the window, and turned them, and looked through each at the light, and put them back in the box, and said they would do; and by the first week of October, Bram's press in the long shop was drawing brass cases out of flat discs in four strokes, a thousand a day, and Tess at her bench under the north window was trying primers in the bases of them, one mixture after another, with Fennick at her elbow advising her loudly on what would happen if she got it wrong and her telling him to go and stand in the quarry.

The first rifle came off Bram's bench on the eighth of October.

Bram did not make anything of it. He put it on the long table in the long shop, on a piece of felt, at the end of the day when the men had gone, and went and washed his hands; and Emeric picked it up.

It was a plain thing. Plainer than his own, the one he had carried on his back over the Staple's gallery: no walnut from the old pews, only good ash, oiled; no sights but Bram's iron ones, a notch and a blade. The action closed like a door. The barrel was Bram's, drawn and bored and rifled on the bench Ottoline had built him a dividing head for, six grooves, a sixth of a turn, the same as the one barrel the Forge had made; and if it was a hair less true than that one, nobody but the two of them would ever know. And there would be three hundred of them, all alike, so alike that the action of this one would close on the barrel of the last one, because they had all been made by men from the same gauges and none of them had been made by him.

That was the thing. He stood in the long shop in the dusk with it in his hands and let himself feel it, once, all of it, because it was owed.

Everything he had made in this world, he had made so that he could hold it. A lamp, a pump, a press, a ship; he had kept the ship unarmed and would keep her so, and he had never yet made a thing that could not be stopped by the man who made it, if it had to be. This could not. When it left Bram's long shop it would go to a farmer's son in the levy, and the farmer's son would kneel behind a bank with it in the winter, and point it at a man on a horse, and pull; and whatever happened after that would happen whether Emeric was there or not, and whether he would have wanted it or not, and he would never be able to call it back, any more than he had been able to call back the weight in the cellar. Three hundred times. And then, in some year he could not see yet, when it had been copied, badly, by somebody else, a great many more times than that.

He held it a while longer. Then he put it down on Bram's felt, square, the way Ferra would have, and went to find Bram to tell him it was good, because it was.



Ottoline brought the column on the ninth of October.

She had come south on the ship's regular run the week before, and spent it in the census hall with her machine, and now she came across the square with a sheet of figures in her bag and her magnifying glass on its cord, and sat at the end of Ferra's table in the counting room, because that was where she sat when the work wanted her, and laid the sheet down, and put her finger on it.

She had run the census through her machine. Every card in the four baronies, every soul over seven years old, sorted by the three boxes Ferra had found on the old sheet: reads; reads name only; reads not. The machine had sorted them into three stacks in a day and a half, with Ottoline beside it with a pencil checking every hundredth card by hand, because she did not trust anything she had made until it had been wrong once.

"Kaldo," she said. "Thirty-one in the hundred read. Twenty, their name. The rest, nothing." Her finger moved. "Grayrock. Twelve read. Milford, nine. Wayford." She stopped. "Wayford, six."

Six in a hundred. He thought of the posting house door at seven in the morning, and the old woman with her basket, counting strokes.

"All four together," said Ottoline, "seventeen." She sat back. "Seventeen in a hundred, in the four baronies, with seven years of Kaldo's school behind them and the only printing works in the south. Kaldo's the best in the kingdom, Highness, I'd put money on it, and two in three of Kaldo can't read this sheet." She looked at him over the glass. "What d'you want it for?"

He did not answer at once. She waited, with her hands folded on the sheet. She was good at waiting. She had sat by a window for twenty years watching a cart cross a square, and waited, and had not known what she was waiting for.

"You didn't tell me what it was," said Ottoline. "In August. Strade's tallies. You knew, and you let me sit there by your lamp and read it out to you, line by line, Intake and Yield and Losses, and you watched me to see if I'd get there." She did not say it angrily. She said it as a woman states a fault she has found in a movement. "I told you I'd remember it."

"I remember."

"Why?"

He looked at the sheet with the three numbers on it, and thought about it, and found that he knew, and that he had known since the posting house parlour at Wayford in April with Aster's four years of letters on the table in front of him, barns and boats; and that he had not wanted to.

"Because I read ledgers," he said. "Not people."

Ottoline looked at him.

"I read Aster's letters every week for four years," said Emeric. "Every line. I knew the price of rope in Wayford better than anybody in Wayford. I never once went and stood in the square and looked at who was in it. Grey men at thirty, coughing, and their widows. They were walking past Sethe's hall every day. I'd have seen them in an afternoon." He was quiet. "I let you read the tallies because I wanted to see if the figures said what I thought they said, without me saying it first. I was checking the figures. You were a way of checking them." He looked at her. "That's what I do. I did it to you. I'm sorry for it."

Ottoline considered that for a while, with her glass in her fingers.

"Most people who can read a ledger do," she said at last. "Read the ledger. It's easier. A ledger doesn't look back at you." She picked up her sheet and gave it to Ferra. "Your chancellor reads both. I've watched her. She reads the ledger, and then she goes and looks at the man to see if he matches." She stood up and pulled her shawl round her. "And your healer doesn't read the ledger at all, as far as I can see. She just looks at the man." She went to the door. "You might ask one of them how it's done, Highness. You've got two."



Ferra took him off work the next afternoon.

She came down into the forge cellar at three in the afternoon, where he had been since six that morning with the Forge cold and the bench covered in drawings of the rifle line's jigs, and stood beside the bench with her book, and did not say anything. He felt her come down the stair. He did not look up.

"An hour," she said.

"Ferra, the jigs for the..."

"An hour." She opened her book and turned it round on the bench in front of him, on top of the drawings, and put her finger on the line.

Under the day's date, in her ordinary hand, between an entry for Bram's brass and an entry for the coal: E., one hour, not working, from three. F.

He looked at it.

And laughed.

It came out of him before he knew it was there, sudden and short and helpless, the first time since the cellar; since before the cellar, since August, since the night the house shook; and it surprised him so much that he laughed again, at that, at the sound of himself doing it, sitting on his stool in the forge cellar with his hands black to the wrist, looking at a line in a book in which the Chancellor of Kaldo had entered him as an expense.

Ferra watched him laugh with her finger on the line and her face perfectly straight.

"It's entered," she said. "So it happens." She shut the book. "Come on. You've lost a minute already."



She took him up to the orchard.

It was not much of an orchard: two rows of old apple trees on the slope below the keep's west wall, planted by somebody's grandfather for the keep's kitchen and left to the wasps for twenty years until Maren had had them pruned. The last of the late apples were on them, red and yellow and small, and most of the leaves were down, and the grass under the trees was long and wet and full of windfalls, and smelled of them, sweet and a little rotten. Nobody else was there. Ferra walked ahead of him up between the rows with her book under her arm, and stopped under the biggest tree, and looked up at it.

The best apples were at the top. They always are.

"Those," said Ferra, pointing.

He looked at the top of the tree, twenty feet up, and at her, and understood.

"You've brought me up here," he said, "to pick apples."

"I've brought you up here for an hour. The apples are while we're here." She held out her book to him. "Hold that."

He took the book. She put both her arms round his neck.

He lifted her.

It was the easiest thing he had done in months. The glyph came up between them, faint, violet, in the grey afternoon, the way it had come up over her table at the exchange three years ago, at night, with the shutters closed; and the weight went off both of them, and they rose, slowly, up through the bare lower branches, with her arms round his neck and her book in his hand and her skirt catching on twigs and her hair catching on more, until they hung in the top of the tree among the last apples, in the cold bright air, twenty feet over the long wet grass, with the whole of Kaldo spread out below them down the hill to the river: the works smoking, the slip, the ship on her cradle, the new streets going up in their grid on the far bank, the school bell going for the end of the day.

Ferra let go of his neck with one arm, and picked an apple, and looked at it, and dropped it.

It hit him on the head.

"One," said Ferra.

"Ferra."

She picked another, and looked at it, and dropped it on his head.

"Two."

He was laughing again. He did not try to stop it. He hung in the top of an apple tree with his chancellor's book in one hand and his chancellor in the other arm, being hit on the head with apples, while she counted them, in her table voice, with her face perfectly straight and her eyes not straight at all; and by seven she was laughing too, the laugh he had heard from her for the first time on the night of the first kiss, the one that came out of her as if she had not known it was in there; and she dropped the eighth apple and missed him altogether, and it went down through the branches to the grass, and she put her face against his neck and shook.

"You moved," she said, against his neck.

"I'm floating in a tree. Everything moves."

"Eight. Entered." She lifted her head and looked at him from a hand's breadth away, flushed, with a twig in her hair and her eyes wet with laughing; and kissed him.

The hold wobbled.

It always did, when she kissed him; she was the one thing left in the world that could still make it. It wobbled now, and they dropped a foot through the branches with a great rattle of twigs, and she gasped against his mouth and held on, and did not stop kissing him; and he caught it, and steadied it, and they hung there again, in the cold air at the top of the tree, with the apples swinging round them on their stems and her book pressed between them and her hands in his hair, for a good deal longer than it took to pick an apple.

When they came down it was slowly, and they lay down in the long wet grass under the tree among the windfalls, on their backs, side by side, without caring about the wet, with her head on his arm and her book on her chest; and looked up through the bare branches at the sky, which was going grey and gold over the river, and said nothing at all for a while.



"No more nobles," he said.

He said it to the branches. He had not meant to say it yet. It came out the way the laugh had, from somewhere he had not been watching.

Ferra did not move. Her head stayed on his arm. After a while she lifted her hand off her book and held it up against the sky, and counted on her fingers.

"No," she said. One finger. "More." Two. "Nobles." Three. She looked at the three fingers. "That's treason in three words. I've seen it done in more and hanged for less."

"I know."

"You're one."

"You said that in Wayford."

"I'm saying it again in Kaldo, so it's said in both." She put her hand down on her book. "Tell me what you mean. Not what you feel. What you mean."

So he told her. Lying in the wet grass, to the branches, slowly, as it came; and found as he told her that he had been building it since the mounting block without knowing he was building it, the way he built a machine in his head at night before he ever drew it, and that most of it was already there.

Not a war. A war would only put new lords on old seats. Not a law; a law could be unmade by the next man to hold the pen. Slowly. Over twenty years, or thirty, or as long as it took. Schools first. Every child in every barony he held, reading, writing, sums, not because it was kind but because it was the foundation, and nothing could stand without it. Seventeen in a hundred. Then fifty. Then ninety. And as the number went up, the offices: the steward's, the clerk's, the magistrate's, the keeper of the roads and the bridges and the books. Each one given to whoever was best at it, chosen by what they could do, set down on paper and tested and read by anybody who cared to read it; not to whoever's grandfather had held it. One office at a time. One barony at a time. Until there was nothing left in the whole of the four baronies that only a lord could do, because everybody could read what the lord could read; and then a lord would be only a man with a name and a hall, and the hall would be a building, and the name would be a name, and nobody would hold anybody's children against anything, because there would be nobody left with the right to.

"And Placit's everywhere," said Ferra, quietly. "Under every house."

"I can't reach every house. Not from a barony." He looked at the branches. "So I'll need more than a barony."

"How much more?"

"All of it, maybe." He was quiet. "I don't want it. I want to say that, to you, out loud, once, so it's said. I don't want it for itself. I want it so I can take it apart from the inside, slowly, without anybody burning anything down, while there's still somebody holding it who doesn't want it." He was quiet. "And if I don't take it, somebody will who does."

Ferra lay on his arm and thought about that for a long while. He let her. He could feel her think, the way he could feel the Forge pull, a steady patient working that he did not interrupt.

"A king who wants no kings," she said at last.

"Something like that."

"They'll kill you."

"They'll try."

"No." She turned her head on his arm and looked at him, very close, with the twig still in her hair. "Not try. They'll kill you. If they see it. Every house in the kingdom. Your father's included." She did not soften it. "So they mustn't see it. Not for years. Not until it's too far along to stop. It has to look like schools, and roads, and clerks who can add. Which is all it will be." She was quiet. "For a very long time."

"Yes."

"And you'll have to be a very good lord, all the while, to be let do it." She looked at him. "The best one in the kingdom. So they give you more. So they give you everything, in the end, because you're the best at it." Her mouth moved, a little, at the corner. "Which is your whole argument. Made with yourself."

He looked at her.

"You're the argument," he said. "Not me. A smith's daughter who could add. Who holds four baronies' books."

"Because you gave them to me."

"Because you could add. That's the only reason I gave them to you. And the next one, the one after you, gets them because she can add, and nobody has to give her anything."

Ferra did not say anything to that for a while. She lay with her head on his arm and her book on her chest and looked up through the branches at the sky going gold; and then she opened her book on her chest, and took out her pencil, and wrote something, lying on her back, at the top of a clean page, the way she wrote the headings of new columns. He did not try to see what it was. She closed the book.

"Your hour's done," she said. "Four minutes ago."

"You didn't say."

"I didn't want to." She sat up, and picked the twig out of her hair, and looked at it, and put it in her book between the pages, and shut it. "Entered as an hour. It was an hour and four minutes. That's the first false figure in my book in eight years." She stood up, and brushed the wet grass off her skirt, and looked down at him. "You're a very bad influence."



Ward was waiting at the top of the orchard, at the gate in the keep's west wall, with a letter.

He had not come down into the orchard. He had stood at the gate, Emeric understood, for some time, with his back to the trees, looking at the wall, as a man stands at a door he has decided not to knock on. When they came up between the rows, Emeric with his coat full of apples and wet to the shoulders and Ferra with her book and the grass on her skirt, he turned round, and looked at them both, and at the apples, for a breath; and held out the letter.

It had come up from the post house by a king's rider, who was waiting in the yard for an answer that Ward had told him would not come.

The seal was the privy seal. Not the crown's great seal, not the chancery's: the small one, the king's own, that went on letters a king wrote himself. Emeric broke it standing at the gate with the apples rolling in his coat. Inside there was one sheet of the king's own paper, folded once.

On it, in the middle of the sheet, in a large, slow, heavy hand that Emeric had seen perhaps four times in his life, and that Ward, he knew from Ward's face, had seen a great many more:

Satisfied.

And under it, an H.

That was all. Nothing else on the sheet. Not what with. Not with the record, or with the hangings, or with the notice, or with the son; not whether it was a judgment, or a pardon, or a warning, or a joke. One word, in a king's hand, from a man who had sent his son to a ruin in the south eight years ago in a plain carriage with six guards and had never once in eight years written to ask him how he was.

He stood at the gate with it.

"What does it mean?" said Ferra, beside him.

"It means what it says," said Ward.

"It doesn't say what with."

"No," said Ward.

Emeric folded the sheet again along its one fold. Twenty minutes before, in the wet grass under an apple tree, he had told Ferra that he meant to take his father's kingdom apart one office at a time over thirty years. He stood at the orchard gate with an apple falling out of his coat, holding his father's one word, and found that his hand was not quite steady, and that he did not know, at all, which of the two things it was steady about.

He put the letter inside his coat with the apples.

"No answer," he said to Ward.

"I told him," said Ward.
