Chapter 211 - Market Day

Sela read the notice the way she read everything that came off the prince's presses: she let somebody else read it to her first, and watched their face while they did.

It was Marta's girl who read it, on the soap counter, the week after the thaw, with her finger under the lines. Market days would move to the new city's square from the first of the spring. The island would keep a market one morning in the week, for the families who stayed. Any stallholder on the island who moved to the square would have a stall there at the same toll as now, for two years, and first refusal of a shop on the square's frontage, when the frontage was built. The girl read it all the way through and looked up, pleased, as if it were good news, because she was fourteen and had never had a stall in her life.

Sela thanked her and gave her a bar of the lavender for reading it and sent her home.

Then she stood behind the counter for a while in the empty shop, with her hands flat on the wood, and looked out of the door at the lower market.

It was a Tuesday, and not a market day, and it was quiet. It had been getting quieter all winter. She had noticed it the way you noticed the evenings drawing in, not on any one day but all at once, looking up from your work: that the lane to the water steps had fewer children in it, and that the chandler on the corner had begun opening late, and that there were doors along the back of the market with the barony's mark on them, the little painted K, that had not had it at the harvest. Whole streets. The fourth draw had taken the lane behind the tannery. The fifth had taken the one behind that. She had known every family in both of them. She had sold them their soap for eleven years. They had gone in the carts with their beds on top and their neighbours beside them, and they had come back once, most of them, on a market day, to buy their soap from her and tell her about their taps, and then they had stopped coming back.

There was one building on the island going the other way. At the top of the long street, round the old grain store where the healer kept her beds, the scaffold had gone up the week the frost came out of the ground, and there were masons on it taking the old slates off, and a gang behind the store digging out a footing that had been grass for as long as Sela could remember, and carts of the new glass coming over the Left Bridge in straw. Half the island had been up to look. It was the only thing on the island anybody was building instead of sealing. Sela had stood at the corner with the rest of them and watched a slate come down and break, and thought that it was a strange time to build up the ward, on an island everybody was leaving. She had not said so. The healer had been standing in the store's door with her sleeves rolled, watching the slates come down, and had looked like a woman nobody ought to say anything to that day.

She had heard the prince read the other notice at the cross in the winter, the one about the well and the lamps and the Copper a week. She had stood at the back of the square and listened with her arms folded. And when somebody behind the fish stall had called out and if we don't, and he had said then you don't, she had thought: well, that's true, as far as it goes. And then she had thought: and it goes as far as the market.

Because a woman could stay in her house till she died, with the lamps lit and the well mended and the watch going past her door. But a woman who sold soap sold it to people, and if the people were four miles off across the lake on a Saturday, she could stay on the island as long as she liked and sell soap to the lamps.

She did not trust it. She had never trusted anything that came off those presses on its first reading, and she had been right not to about as often as she had been wrong. So on the first market day of the spring, which was a Saturday, she did not open the shop. She put her good shawl on and took her basket and walked to the new city to look at it.

She had never walked there before. She had been across the bridge a hundred times, to the works and the foundry bank, and she had seen the new city from the island's wall a thousand, the long grey line of its wall on the flat with the towers going up in it. She had never been inside. There had been nothing inside for her. It was four miles, over the Left Bridge and past the works and along the Grayrock road across the flat, and she walked it in a little over an hour, and it was further than she had thought and easier than she had feared.

There were other people on the road. More than she had expected. Island women with baskets, like her, walking in to look. Carts. A boy driving six geese. And going the other way, toward the island, nobody at all.

She went in under the gate towers, which had scaffold on them still and a mason on the scaffold who whistled at the women going under and was shouted at by one of them, and walked up the long straight avenue toward the middle.

The square was on the bank of the outer canal, with the water along one side of it, and across the water, standing up on the open ground between the two canals, the first quarter's pair of towers, round and tall, with their cones of slate on their heads and their wind pumps turning. The square had been paved. The paving was new and very smooth, and there were iron posts at its corners for awnings, and a long stone trough at one end with three brass taps over it that ran when you turned them, and a crowd.

It was full.

She stood at the corner by the trough with her basket and did what she had come to do, which was count.

She counted stalls first. The island market on a Saturday had thirty-one, on a good week, in the lower market and behind the cross. This square had, this morning, nineteen, most of them faces she knew, island faces, the ones who had gone early: the fish woman from the water steps, and two of the bread stalls, and the old man with the knives, and Tobiah with his cloth, and a dozen more. And eleven she did not know. Farm people off the flat. A man selling ironware who was plainly from the works. A woman with cheeses who, Sela heard her say to a customer, had come in that morning on the train from Milford, with her cheeses in a basket on her knee, in an hour.

Then she counted buyers. That was harder, because they moved, but she had been doing it at the island market every Saturday for eleven years from behind the soap counter and she knew how. You counted the baskets. You counted how many baskets went past a stall in the time it took to say a prayer, and how many of them stopped, and how many came away heavier. She stood at the trough and counted baskets for most of an hour.

There were more than the island. Not many more. More. And there would be more next week, because there were more people living inside this wall every month than there had been the month before, and fewer on the island, and she did not need anybody's census to tell her that; she only needed to stand in a square and count baskets.

She went and bought an egg-cup of cider off the cheese woman's husband and thought about it.

The first-quarter women were at the trough. She talked to them because she talked to everybody, and because they were the ones she had come to see and had not known it until she saw them.

They were island women. That was the first thing. She knew half of them by sight and three by name: women from the lanes behind the market who had gone in the first and second draws, a year and more ago, and had not come back to buy soap. They knew her at once. One of them, a big fair woman called Hanne who had lived three doors from the tannery, put her arms round her in the middle of the square in front of everybody, and Sela let her.

They told her about the taps. She had known they would. Everybody who went told you about the taps. There was a tap in every house in the first quarter, in the kitchen, and it ran all day, clean, and you did not carry water up any stair, ever, and there was a room at the back of every house with a sluice in its floor and a pipe over it that you pulled a chain on and warm water came down, from the fire in the kitchen range, and you stood under it. Hanne said that the first week she had stood under it every night until the water went cold, and cried, she did not know why. There was a drain under every street. There were no middens. There was a school at the end of the street, with a woman teaching in it who was paid by the barony, and Hanne's two went every morning and came home reading.

Sela listened to all of it. She had heard most of it before, from the ones who came back once. She watched their faces while they told it, which was the part she had come for.

It was not all taps. It was mostly taps. But under the taps, when she asked them the thing she had walked four miles to ask, which was how they liked it, there was something else, and it came out slower, and it was the same thing in all of them.

"I don't know the people next door," Hanne said. "That's all. I'd lived next to Bet Farrow twenty years, on the lane, and I knew when she was ill by the smoke from her chimney, and she knew when mine had a fever by how long the lamp was lit. And she went in the draw after mine, and the draw put her in a different street, on the far side of the tower, because hers was the next lane over, and it went to the next street over." She shrugged, a big woman's shrug. "The woman next to me now's from Milford. Nice enough. I don't know her. I've been there a year and I don't know when she's ill."

"They draw a whole street now," one of the others said. "Neighbours with neighbours. Same left, same right. Since the winter. They didn't at the start."

"They didn't at the start," Hanne agreed. "At the start they were learning."

Sela thought about that on the walk home. Four miles back along the road in the early afternoon, with the sun on the flat and the island ahead of her across the lake, and the smoke of its chimneys, and the keep at its head. She thought about Bet Farrow, whom she had also known twenty years, and about the woman from Milford whose fevers nobody knew. And about the soap counter, and the row it stood in, behind the cross, six of them: her shop, and the cooper's widow's stall, and the Dobbs brothers with their rope, and old Nance with her herbs, and the tinker, and the candle woman, who had all stood in that row since before the plague and knew each other's business better than their own, and had lent each other money and minded each other's children and fought over the gutter between their pitches for twenty years.

By the time she came over the Left Bridge she knew what she wanted.

She went to the long room on the Monday. She went as she had gone the summer before with the spinners' petition: in her market apron and her good shawl, in the afternoon, and asked for the prince, and was shown up.

He was at the head of the table with his papers, and Ferra at her end with her book, and the grey knight by the door, as if none of them had moved since the summer. The prince looked up when she came in and put his pen down, which he did not do for everybody.

She did not sit. He did not ask her to. He had learned that the summer before.

"The market's going," she said. "I walked it Saturday. It's going whatever anybody does. I counted the baskets and there's more of them over there than there are here, and there'll be more next week, and nobody put a notice up to make that happen. It's where the people are." She held her shawl together at the front. "So I'll go. I'll take the stall and the shop when the shop's built. But I'll go on one condition, and it's not the toll."

He waited.

"My row," Sela said. "Behind the cross. Six of us. Me and the cooper's widow and the Dobbs boys and Nance and the tinker and Maud with her candles. We've stood in that row since before there was a plague. We go together, all six, the same order, the same side of the square, or I don't go, and you can sell soap to the lamps yourself. Same left, same right. I'll not have Nance put across the square from me with a stranger on either side of her. She's eighty. She'd be dead in a year." She drew breath for the rest of it, the part she had thought out on the road, about the gutter and the lending and the children, and what a row was and why it mattered.

"Yes," said the prince.

She stopped.

"Yes," he said again. "The whole row. The same order. The same side of the square. You can choose the side."

She looked at him. She had come up the stair ready to argue for an hour, and had got four sentences out, and he had said yes before the fifth. She did not like it. She did not like being agreed with before she had finished; it meant he had decided before she came in, and a man who decided things before you came in was a man who might have decided them differently.

"That's already the rule," Ferra said, from her end of the table, without looking up. "Since the winter. Whole streets together. Whole rows. It's the same thing."

Sela turned her head and looked at Ferra, and then back at him.

"Then why didn't you print it?"

"I did," the prince said. "It's in the notice. The winter one. The fourth line from the bottom. 'Whole streets move together, neighbour beside neighbour.' I read it out at the cross."

Sela thought about that. She had been at the back of the square with her arms folded. She had heard the part about the well, and the part about the Copper a week, and the woman behind the fish stall. She did not remember the fourth line from the bottom.

"I don't read notices," she said. "I read people. You can print what you like. I'll believe it when I see Nance's stall next to mine on the far side of that water."

She thought he might be annoyed. He was not. He looked at her for a moment in the way he had, as if she had said something he wanted to write down, and then he said, "That's fair," and Ferra, at her end of the table, did write something down, and Sela did not ask what.

"The far side," Sela said. "The side facing the water. The afternoon sun comes over the towers onto it. Nance feels the cold."

"The far side," said the prince, and wrote it himself.

She nodded once, as if that were more or less what she had expected, and turned to go. At the door she stopped, because there was a thing she had not said and had decided on the road she would.

"Hanne Pell says the woman next door to her's from Milford," she said. "And she's been there a year and doesn't know when she's ill." She did not look back at him. "You drew the first ones wrong. You fixed it. But you drew them wrong first, and they're still living wrong, and your rule doesn't reach them now. Somebody ought to go and knock on doors in the first quarter and tell people who their neighbours are."

She went out before he could answer.

On the stair she met Hobb coming up with a stack of paper, and asked after his mother's leg, and was told it was better, and said she was glad of it, and went on down.

They moved in the fourth week of the spring, all six of them, on a Friday, in two carts, with their stock in crates and their counters in pieces, over the Left Bridge and along the road, with Nance riding on top of the second cart on a chair tied to the rails because she would not be left behind to come in the afternoon.

The far side of the square, facing the water. Six stalls in a row under new awnings, on the iron posts, in the same order they had stood behind the cross: Sela at the end, then the cooper's widow, then the Dobbs boys, then Nance, then the tinker, then Maud. The frontage behind them was pegged out in lime for the shops that would be built there, and every one of the six had first refusal on the one behind its stall, and Sela had already walked hers and told the ganger where she wanted the door.

From the square, if she stood at the end of her stall and looked along the ring road, she could see past the second quarter's roofs into the third, where the frame the wallers called the Walker was going again now the frost was out of the ground, clicking along its rails over a row of houses, laying its grey out of its square iron mouth in bands, with the masons going ahead of it setting the corners. The Dobbs boys went to look at it the first morning and came back and said it was the strangest thing they had ever seen and the walls were dry. Nance said she did not care what laid a wall so long as it kept the wind out.

The first Saturday, a woman came along the row with a basket of eggs on her arm.

Sela did not know her. She was a first-quarter woman, by her apron and the way she walked, which was the way people walked who had lived a year on smooth paving and forgotten the cobbles. She stopped at Sela's stall because it was the end of the row, and looked at the soap, and then held up her basket and said she had eggs, a dozen, fresh that morning off her own hens in her own yard, and would Sela take them against a bar and a half of the plain.

Sela looked at the eggs. She took one out of the basket and held it up to the light, and put it back.

"A bar," she said. "The plain. Not a bar and a half."

"They're fresh."

"They're small."

"They're pullets' eggs," said the woman. "They're small because they're new. They'll be bigger by the summer."

"Then come back in the summer and I'll give you a bar and a half."

The woman looked at her with her mouth open. Then she laughed, suddenly, and put the basket down on the counter, and said that a bar and a quarter was her last word, and that her name was Anwen and she lived on the street behind the bakehouse, in the house with the blue door, and Sela could come and look at the hens herself if she thought she was being robbed.

"I might," said Sela. "A bar and a quarter. Where are you from?"

"Wayford. Before. My man's at the rail works."

"Hm," said Sela, and cut a quarter bar, and took the eggs.

It was how she had learned every neighbour she had ever had. Over a counter, over a price, arguing, until one of them gave a quarter and both of them went away knowing something about the other they had not known before. She had known the old ones that way for twenty years.

She stood in the doorway of the stall with the eggs in her basket and watched Anwen go off along the row to Nance's herbs, and start arguing there. And she thought that in a month she would know when Anwen's hens were laying and when they were not, and what her man earned at the rail works, and whether her children were well, and that Anwen would know the same about her.

It would do. It was a start.
