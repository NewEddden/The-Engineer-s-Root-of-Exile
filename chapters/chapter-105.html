Chapter 105 - Off the Water

Ferra settled it at the table in one sentence, before anybody else had finished arguing.

"I sign for her," she said. "So I ride in her."

There had been a good deal of arguing before that. Imani had wanted nobody aboard but Ward, since Ward was the only one who could work the levers, and had been told that a ship with only a man at her levers had nobody to read her stones, and had said that was the point. Armand had not argued, because Armand was not going, and had said so first, before Imani could. Saban had wanted to go, and had then raised the two keys, as he had raised them before the river last spring: the whole of the relay key in two heads, and both heads about to be in one hull. Ferra had entered it in her book as a risk accepted, with the date, the same as the river, and Armand would hold Kaldo with the sealed letter, the same as the river. But a risk accepted with two of the six aboard was not going to be accepted with three, and Saban had seen the sense of that and sulked for a day. Bram had wanted to be on the steamboat under her, at his own engine, and nobody had argued with Bram.

So it was four. Emeric, to read her. Ward, at the levers. Fletcher, because she was his, and because there would be a moment when she came down onto the water and somebody would have to know by the feel of her deck whether she had come down right. And Ferra, who had written the ninth clause's form herself and never used it, and who had written her conditions in a margin in the summer, and who now wrote nothing in any margin at all and simply put her name on the list.

"You don't have to," he said, after, on the gallery.

"I know." She did not stop walking. "I'm not going because I have to. I'm going because if it drops, I want to have been in it. I'll not sign for a thing and stand on a beach and watch it."

He did not have an answer to that, so he did not make one.

Vantic was in Kaldo for the autumn quarter, as she was every quarter, and he asked her.

He did it on the evening before they went down the river, at the foot of the keep stair, where she was going out with her book under her arm after the day's service. He had not asked her in the thaw. He had thought about it for most of a week then and not asked her, and had not been proud of the reasons, and he had kept thinking about the reasons since.

"We're going down to the lower water in the morning," he said. "There's something on the beach there that goes in the report whether you see it or not. I'd rather you saw it."

Vantic looked at him for a moment with her book held flat against her.

"Your Highness has not asked me to see anything before," she said.

"No."

"I shall come," said Vantic. "I shall stand on the shore. That is where I would stand in any case."

She came down in the steamboat in the morning with the rest of them, and sat in the stern beside Imani, and the two of them said nothing to each other for the whole of the river, which Emeric thought was probably the most comfortable silence either had had in a year. Armand stayed on the island, with the sealed letter in his coat and the relay under his hand, and stood on the quay to watch them go.

She was in the water when they came round the last bend.

Fletcher had launched her on the spring tide three days before, off the stocks and down the greased ways into the lagoon, the way he had launched every hull he had ever built, and she lay at her mooring in the lagoon behind the bar, black and long and very low in the water, with steam up already and a thin line of smoke going straight up from her stack into a still grey morning. The screw was down on its arm in the water behind her. The two battened sails lay folded along her sides. The steamboat came alongside her and looked, beside her, like a child beside its father.

Bram went aboard the steamboat's engine and stayed there. Imani went with him, with her canvas roll. Vantic went ashore, to the beach below the cottages, where Fletcher's six men were already standing in a row with their arms folded, not talking, and a good many other people from the lower water were standing a little behind them in a loose crowd, not talking either, because they had been told something would happen and not what.

Emeric went aboard her with Ward and Ferra, and Fletcher took the helm.

They steamed her out through the gap in the bar into the open water beyond it, where the lower water ran out wide and grey toward the sea and the coast went away east and west in a long low line of dunes and marsh. It was calm. There was a light air off the land, not enough to make a ripple. Fletcher took her half a mile out, until she had the whole of the water to herself, and put the engine down to a crawl, and looked at Emeric.

"The screw," Emeric said.

Fletcher had asked about it on the beach, in the nine days. He had asked what good a screw was going to be in the air, and Emeric had told him: none. A screw cut for water, turning in air, would move a ship about as far as a man could move one by fanning her with his hat. And a screw left down in the water when she began to rise would drive her forward along the top of the water with her keel coming out of it, and when the last of her came clear the screw would come clear all at once at full turn, and the shaft would go. So it came up. That was what the arm was for, and it had always been what the arm was for, and Fletcher had built it in the spring as drawn and had only been told it was for beaching her.

Fletcher stopped the engine. Bram's lad, below, swung the arm, and the screw came up out of the water dripping and locked against the transom, clear.

She lay dead in the water, rocking very slightly.

Ward put his left hand on the lift lever.

The cells in her hold began to thin. Emeric had them in the medium, a hundred and eighty small steady places in their rails in the dark, and the sixteen stations low in her frames taking the feed, and the reserve under her deck beams, cold, waiting, and the reading stone over its dish watching for her to lean. He watched her come up.

She came up out of the water the way a swimmer's back comes up out of a still pool. The sea ran down her sides. It ran off her plating in sheets and then in streams and then in threads, and then it was dripping off her keel, and then there was a gap between her keel and the sea with light in it, and the sea closed over the place where she had been and was flat again, and she was hanging over it.

Ferra's hand closed on the rail beside his.

She did not make a sound. She held the rail as she held her book, square and hard, and looked down over it at the water going away underneath them, a foot, a fathom, the height of a man, two, and her face did nothing at all, and her hand did not let go of the rail, and after a while he felt her other hand come to rest on the rail beside it, near his, not touching.

"She's lying," said Fletcher, from the helm.

He said it the way a man says the wind has gone round. His hands were on the wheel, which did nothing, and he did not take them off it. He was feeling her through the deck, as he had on the stocks, and she was lying on nothing, level, a little down by the stern where the engine was, and the deciding stone was catching it.

At the height of a mast Ward held her.

"Push," he said, and moved the right lever off its stop.

The housings at her quarters woke. It was the bellows sound, the sound Ward's back had made in the foundry at midsummer, but deeper, from two places at once, a long slow breath out behind her on either side, and the water under her quarters went dark with it and flattened in two long shivering patches. She began to go forward.

She went slowly. She went at about the pace of a man walking fast, and then of a horse trotting, and no faster, because Emeric had said no faster and Ward had heard him. Below them the steamboat came out through the gap in the bar under full steam, smoke pouring, Bram at the engine and Imani in the bow with her canvas roll, and fell in under her, twenty yards below and a little behind, and kept station there. The whole way, if anything went, there would be a boat in the water under them and a sea under the boat.

Ward leaned her, the way he had learned to lean himself, and she came round to the west, and ran along the coast.

The coast saw her.

There were boats. There were always boats on the lower water on a calm morning, crabbers and men at their lines, and the nearest of them was a quarter of a mile inshore with two men in it, and Emeric saw them both stand up at once in a small boat, which no man who has spent his life in a small boat ever does, and stay standing, and not sit down. There was a hamlet on the dunes, the first of the lower-water hamlets that had not been in the census until the second census, eleven houses and a drying frame for nets, and there were people coming out of every one of the eleven houses and standing on the dune top in a line against the grey sky, and one of them had a child on her hip and had put her other hand over the child's eyes, and then taken it away again. There was a dog running along the tideline under them, barking, keeping pace, and it ran until it came to a creek and could not run further, and stood on the bank and barked after them until they were out of hearing.

He had thought about this for a long time. He had thought about the lake in the thaw and the man in the skiff with his lines in his hands, and about the four versions and the fifth, and about Fletcher's six men walking her deck. He had thought that when it came to this he would feel something about it, about the coast, about being seen.

What he felt, looking down at eleven houses and a line of people on a dune, was that there was no longer anything to think about. It was not a rumour. It was a ship over the sea at the height of a mast in the middle of a still morning, and every soul on this stretch of coast had seen it with their own eyes, and there was no version of it that anybody could tell that would not be believed.

"By the new moon," said Fletcher, at the wheel, without looking round, "that'll be in every harbour on this coast."

"How far?"

Fletcher thought about it the way he thought about a price.

"The coasting boats go along as far as Emberfell," he said. "Where the mountain smokes. They take salt down and bring sulphur and pumice back, when they can get it. Those men'll be talking about this in the fish market there inside the month." He turned the wheel a spoke, which did nothing, out of habit. "Faster than your road. News goes faster by boat than by road on this coast. It always has."

At the headland below the second hamlet, Ward brought her round.

He did it wide and slow, leaning her, and she came round in the air above the sea with the headland going past under her port rail and the steamboat below turning with her, tight, trailing smoke, and then they were running back along the coast the way they had come, past the dog on its creek bank, past the eleven houses, past the two men in their boat, who had sat down now and were rowing for the shore as hard as they could pull.

Emeric was watching the cells.

They had thinned evenly the whole way, the way a lake goes down in a dry month, and the splinters in the rack in her hold, which Ferra had looked at through the hatch twice without being asked, had gone from bright to something less than bright. It was what the arithmetic had said. Ferra had halved the arithmetic, and it was very nearly what Ferra had said, too. There was enough to come down on, and some over. There was not enough for anything else.

"Down," he said. "On the water. Off the bar."

Ward brought her down.

He brought her down the way he had brought himself down in the foundry, a notch at a time, and the sea came up toward them grey and flat, and at the height of a man Fletcher said, "Now," quite quietly, and Ward held her there. Fletcher looked over the rail at the water. He looked at it for some time. Then he nodded, and Ward took her down the last of it, and her keel touched the sea.

It was the gentlest thing Emeric had ever felt a ship do. She did not come down onto the water. She settled into it, the way she had risen out of it, the sea coming up her sides as it had run down them, until she was lying in it and the water had forgotten she had ever been anywhere else.

Fletcher put his hand flat on the wheel for a moment and closed his eyes.

"Right," he said. "She's right."

Bram's lad swung the arm, and the screw went down into the water behind her. Fletcher rang for steam, and the engine came up under their feet, the old familiar knock of it, and she steamed home across the lower water and in through the gap in the bar as if she had never done anything else in her life, with the steamboat coming in behind her and Imani in the bow of it, with her canvas roll unopened across her knees.

The beach was full.

Fletcher's six men were still in their row at the front. Behind them, now, was most of the lower water. There were people on the beach and on the dunes and on the roofs of the cottages, and not one of them said a word as she came in through the gap and round to her mooring, and when her anchor went down, and her chain ran out, the only sound on the whole of the beach was the chain.

Vantic stood a little apart from them all, where she had said she would stand.

Ferra saw her first. She had come to the rail as they came in and had looked for her, Emeric realised, before she had looked for anyone else, and she was looking at her now across fifty yards of water. Vantic was standing on the sand with her book under her arm, shut. She had her pen in her other hand, uncapped, as though she had taken it out to write with, and had not.

As they watched, she capped it, and put it away, and stood with her hands folded over the book.

Ferra did not say anything. She looked at Vantic for as long as it took the steamboat to come alongside, and then she turned away from the rail, and he saw her face as she turned, and it was the face she wore over a column that had come out exactly as she had expected and not at all as she would have liked.

"She didn't write," Ferra said, very quietly.

"I saw."

"She took it out to write, and she didn't." Ferra went to the ladder. "That's the first time. In six years, that's the first time I've watched her decide not to."

They went back up the river that afternoon, and the story was ahead of them by the time they reached the island. It had come up on a salt boat. By the evening it was in the lower town, and by the morning it was in the upper, and it was, for the first time since the thaw, the true one, or near enough, because nobody on this coast was ever going to be able to tell it any other way. A ship had come up out of the sea at the lower water and flown along the coast at the height of a mast, and turned, and come back, and gone down into the sea again, and steamed home.

"There's only the one version," Saban said at the table that night, with something close to regret. "I've been down to the quay twice. I can't find a second. Nobody's even arguing about it."

"The rumour's over," said Emeric.

"The rumour's over," Saban agreed. "Now it's news. News is worse. People act on news."

Nobody disagreed with that.

It was Imani, of all of them, who brought it round to the next thing, and she did it without looking up from the sheet in front of her, which was Ferra's log of the flight.

"You have a room," she said, "above this town. In the other place. We were all in it. You told us it was wrong, because you'd never seen the town from above, and you'd built it out of maps and streets you'd walked, and the parts you hadn't walked were vague." She put the sheet down. "Now you have a thing that goes above the town."

"It went to the height of a mast."

"It went where you told it to."

Saban had begun to smile. He had been wrong about a great deal in that room, confidently and in detail, on the first day, and he had been wrong about most of it ever since, because nobody could prove otherwise.

"I should very much like," he said, "to be told I'm wrong from a great height. It'd make a change from being told it from across a table."

Ferra did not look up from her book.

"High," she said. "Not along the coast. Over the island, over the lake, up. High enough to see the whole of it." She wrote something. "That's what the next one's for."

Nobody at the table argued, and Emeric, looking down it, understood that the argument he would have to have now was not whether, but how high, and with whom, and that he would have it with Imani, and would lose most of it, and that this was right.
