Chapter 105 - Harder Steam

The engine that was going to run on the iron had been building in the engine shed since the thaw, behind the same shut doors as the first one, and at midsummer Tobe came up to the long room and said it was ready to be tried with water.

It did not look like the engine in the shed at the start of the winter before last. That one had been a house with a fire in it. This one was the size of a cart. It sat on a frame of rolled iron on four wheels as tall as a man's waist, and the boiler lay along the frame like a barrel on its side, and there was no beam. The two cylinders lay flat along the frame under the boiler, one on each side, and the rods out of them went straight to cranks on the wheels, and the front wheels and the back were tied together by a long iron bar on each side so that the four turned as one. The fire went in at the back. Its heat went forward through the water in a fat iron tube and came back through the water in a second one before it found the chimney, so that nothing left the boiler hot that had not done its work twice.

Tobe and Wick had built it. Bram had watched them build it, when he could, which was less often than any of the three of them would have liked, because Bram was also building the engine that was going to go down to Fletcher's yard in the summer, and whatever Ward's harness wanted next, and the racks for Orrin's cells. Emeric had drawn it in the winter, sheet by sheet, and given the sheets to Tobe one at a time, and Tobe had asked about every one of them before he began, and Wick had read every one of them twice.

"With water," Emeric said.

"With water first," said Tobe. "You said."

He had said. It was on the first sheet, above the drawing, in his own hand: before steam, water, at twice what she will ever carry. Water did not burst. A boiler full of steam that let go put the whole of what it held into the air at once, and the air of a shed was where men stood. A boiler full of water that let go let out a cup of water and a noise, because water did not care to be squeezed and gave nothing back when it was let off. So they would fill her brim full with cold water, every tube and every corner, and pump her, and watch.

They did it the next morning with the doors shut. Wick had the force pump, a brass barrel on a bench with a long handle and a pipe to the boiler's lowest cock, and a gauge beside it on a board that Emeric had drawn and Jorrin had made, with a needle that moved round a painted arc against a spring. Rudd stood by the gauge. Tobe went round the boiler with a lamp, looking at every seam.

"Pump," said Rudd.

Wick pumped. The needle climbed off its pin, slowly, and went round the arc past the mark that was the old engine's working pressure, the pressure of a man leaning on a door, and past it again as far, and then past the mark that was this engine's, which was three times the old one, and Emeric watched it go past that mark and felt it in his stomach, though there was nothing in the boiler but cold water. Wick's arm went steadily. The needle went on to the red line Emeric had painted at twice the working mark, and Rudd said stop, and Wick stopped.

Nothing happened for a count of twenty.

Then Tobe, at the back of the boiler with the lamp, said, "Here."

It was the corner of the firebox, low down on the left, where four plates met and the riveting had been hardest to reach. A line of wet along one seam. Not a spurt. A slow shine on the iron, gathering into a bead, and the bead running down, and another gathering after it.

"She weeps," said Tobe.

"Let her off," said Rudd, and Wick opened the cock, and the needle fell back to its pin, and the bead stopped.

It took four days to put right. Tobe cut out eleven rivets along the seam with a chisel and a hammer, working in the dark of the firebox on his back with a lamp on his chest, and Wick heated new ones in the forge and passed them in through the fire door one at a time on tongs, and Tobe drove them hot, and they shrank as they cooled and pulled the plates together harder than any hammer could, the way they had in Fletcher's hull. On the fifth morning they filled her again and pumped her to the red line, and held her there for as long as it took Rudd to walk three times round the shed, and nothing gathered anywhere. Tobe went over every seam with the lamp and the flat of his hand.

"Dry," he said.

"Again tomorrow," said Rudd. "Then I'll believe it."

It was dry again the next day.

Rudd came up to the long room that evening with his sheet.

He did not sit. He stood by the table with the sheet in his hand, the one he kept inside his shirt, and did not unfold it, because he did not need to.

"It says nothing on the lever," he said. "No weight added. No hand on it. It screams because it's working." He looked at Emeric. "You're going to run her at three times what that sheet was written for."

"Yes."

"Then the lever's got three times as much on it before it lifts."

"It has. It's set by Tobe, against the gauge, before she ever sees a fire, and then it's sealed." Emeric had the drawing ready. The safety valve on the new boiler sat under a little iron cage, and the spring that held it down was set to lift at the working mark and not a breath above, and a wire ran through the cage and the adjusting nut, and the ends of the wire were pressed into a disc of lead. "Tobe sets it. Ferra seals it. Anybody who breaks that seal has put a hand on the lever, and you put the fire out and ask him why afterward, the same as always."

Rudd looked at the drawing for some time.

"That's one," he said.

"That's one. Here's the other."

He turned the sheet over. On the back, he had drawn the firebox from the side, the fire under it and the water over it, and in the crown of the firebox, the iron roof of it with the water lying on top, a single hole with a plug in it.

"Lead," he said. "A plug of soft lead, screwed in there. As long as there's water over the crown, the water keeps it cool, and it doesn't care how hot the fire is underneath. If the water ever falls below the crown, the crown gets hot, and the lead melts, and the plug goes, and the steam comes down through the hole into the fire and puts it out." He put his finger on the plug. "If a man forgets his count, or a pump fails, or somebody's been clever. She puts her own fire out before the crown can soften, because a crown that softens is what kills men."

Rudd was quiet for a long moment.

"She tells on herself," he said.

"She tells on herself."

"Even if nobody's listening." Rudd had a look on his face that Emeric had seen once before, the day the first engine's gland blew out steam across the shed and Rudd had put the fire out without being told. "I'll want a new sheet. Both of those on it. And I'll want to watch Tobe set the valve, and I'll want the lead in my own pocket till it goes in."

"You'll have it."

"And it binds you."

"That's what it's for."

Ferra wrote the new sheet out fair that night from Emeric's draft, and read it through, and signed it under his name, and sealed a disc of lead with the Chancellor's seal in front of Rudd the next morning in the shed, and gave it to him, and Rudd put it in his shirt with the other sheets and did not say anything else about it at all.

They raised steam on her for the first time in the last week of the midsummer month, on the jacks.

Tobe had her up on four timber stacks so that her wheels hung clear of the floor by a hand, and she was chained down to rings in the floor at all four corners, and the shed doors were shut. The lower town was beginning to decide that the outside foundry made noises sometimes and that it was nobody's business, and Emeric was counting on that, and did not much like counting on it.

The fire took a long time. It was a smaller firebox than the old engine's and it burned the same Grayrock coal, and the chimney drew slowly, and for most of the morning the shed was full of yellow smoke and the needle sat on its pin. Wick fed the fire and watched it and fed it again. When the needle finally moved it moved very slowly.

Tobe opened the regulator a crack, and steam went into the left cylinder, and the piston moved, and the rod pushed the crank, and the wheels went round a quarter turn in the air and stopped, and the cylinder breathed out.

It breathed out through a pipe that went straight out of the side of the frame, and the noise it made was a flat hard bark like a very large dog, and everybody in the shed took a step back from it. Tobe opened the regulator further. The wheels went round. They went round in the air at a walking pace, and the pipe barked at every half turn, bark and bark, very loud in a shut shed, and the needle on the gauge, which had climbed so slowly all morning, began to fall.

"She's using it faster than she's making it," said Rudd.

Wick was looking at the fire door. Then at the chimney. Then at the barking pipe.

"That's a lot of noise to throw away," he said.

He said it to nobody. Then he went to the bench and came back with a length of iron pipe and a bend, and asked Tobe to shut her off, and when the wheels had stopped he took the barking pipe off the side of the frame and ran it forward under the boiler and turned it up with the bend into the bottom of the chimney, where it pointed straight up the middle, and made it fast. It took him most of an hour. Nobody asked what he was doing. Emeric knew what he was doing and did not say so, and it was one of the harder things he had ever not said.

"The noise'll go up the chimney," Wick said, "instead of in our ears."

Tobe opened the regulator.

The first bark went up the chimney, and the fire went white.

Everybody saw it. The fire door was open a crack and the glow from it went from orange to a white so bright it lit the whole back of the shed, and with every bark the chimney drew a great breath through the firebox and the tubes and threw it out of the top, and the fire roared. The needle stopped falling. It stopped, and hung, and began to climb, while the wheels went round in the air faster than they had gone yet, and the barks came quicker, and the faster she ran the harder the chimney pulled and the hotter the fire went.

Wick stood with his mouth a little open, looking at the fire.

"She blows her own fire," he said.

"Write it down," Emeric said. "In your book. Your name on it."

Wick looked at him, and then went and got the book he kept at the bench, and wrote it down, and his name under it, and the day.

It was later, when she had been let off and the fire was dying and the wheels had stopped, that Wick came and stood by the frame and looked at the wheels hanging off the timber.

"She's heavy," he said. "Four tons, Tobe says. The rails are rolled for wagons. Four tons on one pair of wheels on a rail rolled for a wagon."

"It'll carry her. Jorrin rolled them for her, not for the wagons. The wagons are light for them."

"But if she were lighter." Wick said it slowly, as though he was not sure he should. "The old man lifted the boat. The tower sells stones that make a carriage lighter. If she weighed half, the iron would never know she was there."

Emeric looked at the wheels on the timber stacks.

"Open her up again," he said to Tobe. "Just a crack. There's steam enough left."

Tobe opened the regulator a crack. The wheels went round, slowly, in the air, a hand clear of the floor, and turned and turned and did not go anywhere, and after a moment Tobe shut her off again.

"That's what a wheel does with nothing pressing it down," Emeric said. "On the rail, the only thing that stops her wheels going round like that is her own weight on them. The rail takes hold of her because she's leaning on it. Make her half as heavy and she has half the grip, and she stands there in the dew spinning her wheels with a train of ore behind her going nowhere." He put his hand on the warm iron of the frame. "She wants to be as heavy as the iron can stand. And not a pound heavier. That's the whole art of her."

Wick thought about that for some time.

"Then the rails are the thing," he said.

"The rails are always the thing."

Bram came into the shed at the end of the day, after the forge was drawn, when everybody else had gone home. Emeric was still there. Bram did not say anything to him. He took a small hammer out of his belt and went round the boiler seam by seam in the last of the light, tapping each rivet head once and listening, the way a man listens at a wall for where the timber is, all the way round, every seam, every rivet, and it took him the better part of an hour. At the firebox corner where the eleven new rivets were he tapped each of them twice.

Then he put the hammer back in his belt.

"Good," Bram said, and went home.

Emeric sat on in the shed for a while after that, on an upturned box by the door, with the engine standing on her timbers in the dark and the smell of hot iron and coal smoke going out of the place slowly. Two miles north, and a little more, the iron stopped at a stake in a field. She would reach it in a quarter of an hour, if the rails held and the fire drew and nobody was clever. She had never been further than a hand off the ground.
