Chapter 105 - Seven Days

Imani dressed his wrists in the morning, at the desk by the ward's side door, with the lamp still lit because the December light had not come up enough yet to see by.

He had not wanted to come. Ward had brought him, at first light, without asking, by the elbow, up the hill from the keep in the cold, and put him through the side door and said "Here," to Imani, as though he were delivering a sack of something, and gone back out to stand in the street.

She looked at him once from the door to the desk, the whole of him, the way she looked at everything that was put in front of her. Then she sat him down on the stool and took his hands and turned them over under the lamp.

The wrists were the worst. The cord had cut into them where it crossed, behind, and the wet had made it worse, and under the new red weals were the old shiny ones from the bridge, the burn scars he had carried since the war, and the cord had found those and opened two of them. There was a cut across the heel of his left hand where Rylan's knife had slipped in the dark, and another on the knuckle of a finger. There were the bruises from the hatch and the bilge and the fall into the shallows, which she did not ask about, and found anyway, one by one, by pressing.

She cleaned the wrists with spirit. It hurt the way spirit hurt. She did not tell him it would, and he did not say anything, and she watched him not say anything.

His hands would not keep still.

He had been warm since the middle of the night. Ward had put him in front of the fire in the guardroom wrapped in two blankets and Ward's cloak and a third blanket over that, and fed him hot spirit and water until his teeth stopped, and that had been hours ago. He was not cold. His hands shook anyway. They lay in hers on the desk under the lamp and they shook, a fine steady tremor from the wrist, and every time she let go of one of them to reach for the linen it went on shaking on its own, on the wood, as though it belonged to somebody else.

He knew what it was. It was the man in the hold.

It was the thing he had felt through his own legs in the dark, the man's breath going in and not coming out, and the weight of him, and then the stop. He had killed men before, with fire, across a river, over a wall. He had never felt one stop. His hands had felt it. His hands had been tied behind him at the time and had not been part of it at all, and it was his hands that would not let go of it.

Imani did not ask what it was.

She wound the linen round his right wrist, over the spirit, three turns, firm and flat, and tied it off, and did the left. She did it clinically and quickly and well, as she did every wrist that was put in front of her at that desk. And then she did not let go.

She held his left wrist on the desk, over the new linen, in both her hands. Not hard. Her fingers were round it, and her thumbs lay along the inside of it, over the pulse, as they had lain on his pulse the first autumn in the ward with the glass in her other hand, counting. She was not counting. She held his wrist still against the wood, so that the hand at the end of it, for as long as she held it, did not shake.

It went on a long while. The lamp burned. Somebody in the ward behind her coughed and was quiet. Out in the street he could hear Ward's boots, two steps one way, two steps back.

She did not look at him. She looked at his wrist in her hands.

"The ward's full of it this morning," she said, at last, to his wrist. "Half the lower ward came up in the night with the ones who'd been in the water to the knees. They've all seen the boat." Her thumbs did not move. "They're all telling each other how far it is from the deep water off the east wall to the stones under the stair."

He did not say anything.

"Nobody can agree," she said. "Some of them say it's a long way."

She looked up then.

It was the look from the laundry yard, in the spring before the war, over the basin, when she had come up out of the ether grey and sick and listed for him every single thing about him that did not add up, and asked him what he was. She did not ask it now. She looked at him with the whole of that question in her face, and he watched her decide not to ask it, and put it somewhere, carefully, the way she put her father's razor back in its cloth.

She let go of his wrist.

His hand lay on the desk, and did not shake.

"Keep them dry," Imani said, and got up, and went into the ward.



On the second day the burned boat was aground on the gravel bar below the east wall, a black hull with its back broken, half in and half out of the water, and Vantic was on the bank looking at it.

Emeric saw her from the water gate. He had come down to look at the stone under the top step, because he could not keep away from it, and from the head of the stair he saw her on the stones below, alone, in her grey coat, with her book under her arm.

She was not looking at the hull. She was looking at the river. Out in the deep water, where the boat had lain at anchor, the anchor was still down, and the end of its rope was still tied to a float, a little cask, burned black on one side, bobbing in the current to mark the place. She looked at the float. Then she turned round and looked at the stones at the foot of the bank, where the marks were still in the mud from three people coming up out of the water in the night, and a long scrape where Ward had dragged the gate crew's skiff off its rollers and back.

Then she walked it. Along the bank. From the place opposite the float, down the stones, to the marks in the mud. She walked it at an even pace, a long careful pace, and her lips moved, and Emeric knew she was counting, because he had watched Ferra count the same way all his life in Kaldo.

She stopped at the marks.

She looked back at the float, out in the deep water. She looked at it for a while. Then she opened her book and wrote something, short, and closed it.

He came down the stair. She heard him on the steps and turned.

She looked at him. At the linen at his wrists. At his face. It was a long look and a very exact one, and he felt it go over him the way he had felt her eyes go over the gate yard on the day she came, once, all of it, missing nothing.

She did not ask.

She did it the way she had done it at Ferra's trestle in August, over twelve stones that were on nobody's books: a pause the exact length of a question, in which the question was considered, and set down, and left where it lay. He watched her set it down. He did not know what she had written in her book. He did not think he was going to be told.

"The House," Vantic said, "did not lend against a prince at the bottom of a river."

"No."

"It would be obliged if he did not go there again." She put the book back under her arm. "It would be very much obliged."

And she went up the stair past him to the water gate, and through it, and he heard her steps go away up the lane, even, unhurried, toward the keep and the east tower and her week's letter to the House.



Armand knew before Emeric said anything. He knew from the door.

"You went out," he said. "Alone."

"Yes."

Armand was in his chair by the fire. He did not get up. He put the book down on the board across his knees, and folded his hands on it, and looked at Emeric with an expression Emeric had not seen on him before, in the tower or in Kaldo, and it took him some time to understand that it was fear.

"How far?"

"The hold. The deck. The water." Emeric sat down on the stool. "Across the water. To the reeds on the far bank. A man in a skiff."

Armand closed his eyes.

"And back," he said. "How long, back?"

"I don't know. More than two breaths. I had a cord on my wrists. I went to that."

Armand opened his eyes. He looked at the linen.

"Good," he said. His voice was not quite steady. "Good. That was right. That was exactly right." He reached out across the board and took Emeric's bound wrist in his old dry hand, not hard, and held it. "Next time you won't have a cord. Nobody will have been kind enough to tie you up. So you carry a stone. Always. In your pocket. A small one, with an edge on it. And when you go out you close your hand on it until the edge bites, before you go, not after, so that it's already hurting when you need it." He held on. "You come back to the hurt. Not to the stone. To the hurt."

"I know."

"You know now." Armand let go. "You were lucky. You went further than I would have let you go for a year, on your third time, alone, in the dark, frightened, and you came back." He looked at the fire. "The man in the tower was not frightened. He had all the time in the world. I have thought since that it might have been better for him if he hadn't."

Emeric told him about Garrin. About the voice in the dark, and the note, and the way his body had known it before he did, from a fire on a road three years ago, when he had not known there was anything to know.

Armand listened to all of it.

"Then you'll know him," he said. "In a crowd. In a market. Across a room, the day he walks into it, whatever coat he's wearing." He was quiet. "For the rest of your life. That's the gift. And you'll know him in your sleep, some nights, when he isn't there. That's the rest of it."



The garrison hated the order. It kept it.

Osric hated it loudest. He said so in the guardroom on the first night, and the second, to anybody who would listen and several who would not, that a man who walked the prince out of the water gate at one in the morning into a boat full of knives should be hanging off that same gate by now, and that any garrison in the kingdom would have hanged him, and that he did not see what was different about Kaldo. On the third night Ward came into the guardroom while he was saying it, and stood inside the door, and said nothing at all, and Osric stopped, and did not say it again.

Declan said nothing. He walked his turns on the wall and ate at the knights' table and did not look at the empty place at the end of the bench where Rylan had used to sit.

Nobody spoke of the night. Nobody touched Rylan. Nobody went to his house. Rylan stayed in it, and did not come out, and the lower ward walked past his door with its eyes on the cobbles.

On the third morning Nessa came to the keep.

She came to the gate passage, not further, with Wren holding her hand, and a loaf in a cloth under her other arm. She gave the loaf to the gate crew man on the passage and said it was for the prince. She did not ask to see him. She did not say anything else, and when the gate crew man asked whether there was a message she said no, there was no message, and stood there a moment longer in the passage as though there were one after all, and then took Wren home.

The gate crew man brought it up. It was still warm.

Finn was back at school on the fifth day. Sela let him in without a word and put him in his place on the bench beside Arthur, and at the noon break Arthur heard him tell two of the smaller boys that he had flown. Over the river. In the dark. Higher than the boat. Arthur, who was nearly fifteen and very large and knew everything, told him that it had been his father's jump, that knights could jump a long way, everybody knew that, and Finn said that he knew what he knew and he had flown, and Arthur shrugged, and the smaller boys went back to their bread, and nobody thought about it again.



On the seventh morning Rylan came up to the keep.

Ward brought him into Emeric's room and stood by the door.

He had shaved. He was in his harness, the whole of it, the breastplate Bram had raised for him before the war and the bracers and the belt, polished, as he would have worn it for an inspection, or a burial. He had no sword. He had left it in the guardroom with the gate crew, and Ward had it. He stood in the middle of the room on the boards with his hands at his sides, very straight.

"I've come to be hanged," he said.

Emeric was at the table under the window. He did not get up.

"Sit down, Rylan."

"I'd rather stand."

"Then stand. Tell me."

Rylan told him. He told it in order, plainly, the way he had told the yard fire about the room in April. Garrin had come to his door in the first days of December, at dusk, with two men behind him who looked like wool buyers, and had said Rylan's name, and Nessa's, and Finn's, and Wren's, all four, in the doorway, in a voice Rylan had last heard on a road three years before, and then said that Finn was not coming home from school that day. That he would come home when the prince had been taken out of Kaldo on a night of Garrin's choosing, by the knight who sat the board, and not before. That if the knight told anybody, the captain or the prince or his priest, the boy would go into the river with a stone on him, and Garrin would know, because Garrin had people in Kaldo who would tell him. Rylan had not known whether that was true. He had not been able to find out without it being true.

He had done what he was told. He had sat the board. He had asked Declan the questions. He had given them his own lamp stone off his own belt, to swing in the water. He had left a letter in Doran's box for Nessa, the night of the fault, in case.

"They told me I'd have him back on the stair," Rylan said. "When they had you. I didn't believe it. I did it anyway." He looked at the boards. "I'd do it again."

"I know."

"I'd do it again tomorrow. If it was Finn." He made himself look up. "That's why you have to hang me. Not for what I did. For what I'd do."

Emeric looked at him for a while.

"If they'd taken my son," he said, "I'd have done what you did. Every step of it. I'd have put a prince in a boat for him." He put his bound hands flat on the table. "You put your family first. That's the right answer, Rylan. It's the right answer for every man in that guardroom who has one. I'm not going to hang a man for giving it."

Rylan did not move.

"And you put me in a boat," Emeric said. "And then you broke a man's neck in the dark with your hands still half tied, and carried me across a river on your back, and the boy in your arms, and stood in the water in front of twelve crossbows and didn't lift your hands." He looked at Ward by the door. "The garrison doesn't have to like it. They have to keep it. You keep your post. You keep the board, if Ward will have you on it. And you'll prove it, every night you sit there, for as long as it takes them."

"They'll never forget it."

"No. Nor will I." Emeric was quiet. "That's what you'll be proving it against."

Rylan stood there. He stood in his polished harness in the middle of the floor, and Emeric watched him try to find something to do with his hands, and fail, and leave them where they were.

"There's a debt," Emeric said. "It's not yours. It's the man at your door." He held Rylan's eyes. "That's twice now. The road, and the river. Twice he's put us both in the dark to die, you and me. I'm going to collect it. I don't know when. I'd like you to be there when I do."

"I'll be there," Rylan said.

"And there's the other thing." Emeric did not look at Ward. "What you saw on the boat. What you felt, at the rail. I'd ask you to keep it."

Rylan thought about that. He thought about it seriously, the way he had thought about the card, a word at a time.

"I didn't see anything I'd know how to say," he said.

Ward, at the door, did not move at all.

When Rylan had gone down, Emeric went up to the line room. He did not know why, except that he had not been there since the night, and he wanted to see it.

The board was as it had been, twelve shutters up and twelve needles still, and a garrison man on the stool, who stood up. Beside the board the card was where he had nailed it in October, at the height of a sitting man's eyes. But somebody had been at it. The third line, the one Ward had put his finger on, had been scored through with a single straight stroke, and beside it, small and square, in Ward's hand, a new line.

A shutter that drops and lifts and will not settle: a stone at fault. Wake the prince and the captain. The prince does not go down alone.

He looked at it for a while.

Ward had come up the stair behind him, as Ward always came up stairs behind him, and stood in the doorway.

"You said it was one man," Emeric said.

"I did."

"You were right."

Ward considered that.

"I was not right," he said. "I did not like it. That is different." He looked at the card. "I have changed it. You may change it back."

Emeric did not change it back.



Doran brought the letter up in the afternoon.

He brought it himself, up the keep stair, which he had never climbed before in Emeric's time, in his old cassock, a little out of breath, and gave it to Rylan in the hall, where Rylan was sitting at the end of the knights' bench, alone, in his harness still, with nobody sitting near him.

It was a single sheet, folded small, sealed with candle wax and a thumb, and the name on the outside was Nessa's, in a hand that sloped downhill and pressed too hard.

"It wasn't opened," Doran said. "It's the rule."

Rylan took it. He looked at it for a while. Then he put it inside his harness, against his shirt, and said thank you, and Doran said that it was nothing, and went down the stair again slowly with one hand on the wall.



The midwinter pay was on the last Friday of the year, at the exchange window, as it always was, with the garrison in a line across the square in the cold and Ferra at the window with the pay sheet and the strongbox and Hale at her elbow counting the coin out in piles.

Emeric was in the back room behind her, at the trestle, because he always was on pay day, to sign the sheet at the foot when the last man had put his mark.

The line went past. Each man came to the window and gave his name, and Ferra found it, and Hale counted out his pay, and the man put his mark against his name with the pen on its string, an X, or a circle, or a shape of his own that he had used since he first drew pay, and took his coin and went. Declan signed his name, as he always had, small and neat. Osric made his mark, a big careless cross. Ward signed in his square hand without looking at the sheet.

Rylan came to the window.

The line behind him went quiet. Ferra found his name, and Hale counted, and Ferra turned the sheet round on the sill and held out the pen on its string, as she held it out to every man, and Rylan took it.

He did not make his mark.

He bent over the sill with the pen in his big hand, holding it as though it might get away from him, and his tongue in the corner of his mouth, and wrote. Slowly. One letter, and then the next, pressing too hard, the pen scratching. Emeric could see it from the trestle, upside down, past Ferra's shoulder. R. Y. L. The A, which came out too big. The N.

The name ran downhill. It started on the line and ended a good way under it, sloping toward the foot of the sheet like a man walking down a lane. And the Y was the wrong way round. It faced backward, the way a child makes it the first week of school. Rylan looked at it. Then he crossed it through, carefully, with one stroke, and wrote it again above, the right way round, smaller, and put the pen down.

Ferra looked at it. She did not say anything. She blotted it, as she blotted every line, and turned the sheet back, and Hale pushed his pay across the sill.

Emeric said nothing.

He sat at the trestle in the back room and looked at the name on the sheet past Ferra's shoulder, running downhill, with its one letter crossed out and done again, until Rylan had taken his coin and gone back across the square, and the next man had come to the window, and Ferra had turned the sheet round again, and the name was face down on the sill under somebody else's mark.

Rylan had not done it for him. He had not done it for anybody in that line. He had done it in Doran's vestry at night with a slate since August, and not told anybody, and the first thing he had written with it that mattered had been a letter to his wife, in case. This was the second.
