Chapter 54 - Tess's Feed

He did not answer her that night, and in the morning he went back to his wheels.

He sat at the dyehouse bench with the regulator in pieces in front of him and a covered lamp at his elbow, and drew it again, and widened every gap between every pair of teeth by a hair, and put a brass cover over the whole train with a felt lip to keep the soot out, and a long iron neck between the works and the arc so that the heat would have further to climb. It was a better regulator by the end of the day. It was also a heavier one, with more parts in it that Bram and Tess would have to cut by hand, and it still needed every one of its teeth.

Tess did not come up to the dyehouse that day. Bram said, when Emeric asked, that she was in bay three, in the corner by the river door, and that she had asked him for an hour of the hammer and a bar of the river iron and the use of the corner, and he had given her all three, and that he had not asked what for, and that if the prince wanted to know he could go across the bridge and look.

Emeric did not go across the bridge. He told himself it was because he had the cover to finish.



She came up the next afternoon with a nail and a washer in her hand.

It was an ordinary nail, one of Bram's, a long one, and an ordinary iron washer with a hole a little bigger than the nail. She did not say anything. She held the nail upright between her finger and thumb, point down, and dropped the washer over the head of it. The washer slid down the nail, loosely, and stopped against her fingers.

Then she pushed one edge of the washer up with her other thumb, so that it tipped.

It jammed. The tipped washer bit into the nail at two points, top and bottom of its hole, and held there, and when she lifted it by its raised edge the whole nail came up with it, stuck fast. She let the edge go. The washer fell flat, and the nail slid down through it, and stopped against her fingers again.

Tip it, and it grips. Let it lie flat, and it lets go.

"I've known that since I was small," Tess said. "Everybody in a forge knows it. You put a ring on a rod to hang it up and it jams if it hangs crooked." She tipped the washer again, and lifted the nail, and let it fall. "That's your carbon. The nail. It wants to go down. The washer's what stops it."

Emeric watched her do it three more times. He saw the whole of it before the second, and he went on watching anyway, because he wanted to be sure that what he was feeling was what he thought it was.

It was. Two things, at once, side by side, like the two holds of the tumble. The first was the plain pleasure of a good idea, which he had always felt, all his life, whoever had it. The second was smaller and meaner and he did not like it, and it said that he had spent nine days of Tess's fingers on brass wheels he had not needed.

He let the second one sit where it was, and did not feed it.

"The magnet tips the washer," he said.

"The magnet tips the washer. When the current's on." Tess had her bench book out now, open at a page she had drawn on in bay three, and she turned it round to show him. She turned the pages from the front to get there, and kept her thumb on the ones before, as she had for Armand. "Current comes on, the coil pulls up, the washer tips and grips the rod and lifts it, that's your light struck. Carbons burn back, the gap gets wider, the current gets weaker, the coil lets go a bit, the washer goes flatter, the rod slips down a hair. Gap gets shorter. Current comes up. Washer tips again. Grips."

"No teeth."

"No teeth," said Tess. "Nothing for the soot to get in."

There was a drawing on the facing page, of a rod and a guide and a coil with an iron plunger in it, and a lever from the plunger to the washer, and on the end of the lever, hanging, a small weight. Beside the weight she had written: like Harl's stone.

He looked at the drawing, and she began to take the book back.

"Build it," he said.



Armand was on the keep stair when Emeric came up it that evening, halfway between the hall and his own rooms, sitting on a step with his back to the wall and one hand inside his coat, where he had sat down to wait for his breath.

He did not get up. Emeric did not ask him whether he was well, because he had asked once in January and been told, at length and in the voice Armand kept for first-year boys, that he was seventy-eight and on a stair and that both of those were facts and not symptoms. He sat down on the step below instead, and waited with him.

"You look," Armand said, when he could, "like a man who has lost an argument he was not having."

Emeric told him about the nail and the washer.

Armand listened with his eyes closed and his head against the stone, and when it was done he did not say anything until his breathing had evened out.

"In the tower," he said, "a first-year girl who showed her master something like that would have been thanked very warmly. And the thing would have gone into the tower's books under the master's name, because she was the master's apprentice, in the master's room, using the master's iron, and the master would have believed that was fair. He would not even have had to try." He opened his eyes. "I did it myself. Twice. I was quite sure, both times, that it was fair."

"It's hers."

"I know it is hers. I am asking whether it will be." Armand put a hand flat on the wall and began, slowly, to get up. "Help an old man up a stair. Then go and decide."

The next morning Emeric went down to Ferra's table and told her to strike out the brass for the other seven regulators.

She found the line, and struck it, and wrote the date. Then she turned back two pages.

"You'll want to know what the first one cost," she said.

"No."

"Nine days of Tess. Three of Bram. Two sacks of charcoal for the casting. The candlesticks, at what Saban would have given for them in Wayford, which is more than you'd think." She ran her pen down the column without looking at him, and wrote a total at the bottom, and drew a ring round it. "And hers?"

"A rod and a nail and a washer."

Ferra wrote that down too, under the ring, exactly as he had said it. Then she looked at the two lines, one above the other, and blotted them both, and turned the page.



She built it in bay three over ten days, in the corner by the river door, in the hour a day Bram gave her and most of the hours he did not.

The first one dropped its carbon in jerks. The rod was the river iron, drawn under the trip hammer and filed, and it was rough, and the washer gripped and slipped and gripped, so that in the powerhouse the light jumped and steadied and jumped like a man stammering. Tess took the rod back across the bridge and spent most of a day polishing it with sand and oil on a strip of leather, until she could see her own eye in it, and wrote in her book how long it had taken her, and what the sand was, and where she had got it.

The second one held the light so hard that the arc went out. The coil was too strong; when the current came on it tipped the washer right over and lifted the carbon a full finger instead of a fingernail, and the light came up, and stretched, and broke. Tess did not take any turns off the coil. She moved the weight on the end of the lever, a little lead weight that had been a sinker on one of the dead boats' nets, a hair's width along at a time, exactly as Harl had moved his stone along the belt lever, until the coil had to work against just enough to lift the carbon just enough and no more.

Emeric came down to the powerhouse for every trial and stood by the wall with his hands in his pockets. He did not touch it. Twice his hands came out of his pockets of their own accord, and twice he put them back.

The third time, she asked him something.

"If one of them goes out," Tess said. "On the wall. If a carbon burns right down, or the rod sticks. What happens to the rest?"

"They all go out."

She looked at him.

"They're all on one wire," he said. "One after the other, like beads. The current has to go through every lamp to get back to the machine. One gap anywhere, and it can't. Eight dark walls."

Tess thought about it, standing in the powerhouse with the dead lamp hanging on its beam and her book under her arm.

"Then when it's burned right down," she said slowly, "the rod's all the way down. As low as it can go." She put her hand up flat under the lamp's works, where the bottom of the rod's travel would be. "Put a piece of copper there. When the rod gets that low, it lands on the copper, and the copper takes the current round the lamp instead of through it, and the rest stay lit."

"Yes," Emeric said.

She wrote it down. She went back across the bridge with the lamp's works under her arm and came back two days later with a strip of copper riveted under the guide, and a spring under the copper so that the rod would press on it hard when it got there, and nothing else changed.



They tried it all night on the twenty-sixth of February.

The lamp hung on its beam in the powerhouse, in one of Bram's hooded brackets, with Tess's works under the hood and two new carbons in it, a forearm long. The coil of iron wire on its frame stood in for the other seven. Harl was on his stool between his buckets. Nobody else had been told, because Tess had asked that nobody else be told, and Emeric had understood why.

Tess pulled the lever. The machine came up.

There was a click as the coil took hold, and a crack, and the light came up white in the powerhouse, and everybody lifted their black glass. And then there was nothing to listen for. No catch. No clicks. Only the hiss of the arc, steady as a kettle, and every so often, so slight that Emeric only knew it was happening because he was watching for it, the faintest whisper of the rod slipping down a hair through its washer, and the light not changing at all.

Tess sat down on the floor with her back against the wall and her book on her knees, and wrote the time.

At midnight the light was steady. At two it was steady. The upper carbon had burned down by more than half and come down to meet the lower, a hair at a time, and nobody had touched it. Some time after three Tess fell asleep where she sat, with her head against the stones and her pen still in her hand and a long black line across the page where it had slid. Emeric took the pen out of her hand and did not look at the page.

He sat on and watched the lamp. Harl sat on and watched it too.

At a little after five the upper carbon came down to the last finger of its length, and the rod went on down through the washer to the bottom of its travel, and landed on Tess's copper strip with a small definite click, and the light went out.

The machine's note hardly changed. The current had gone round the lamp, by the copper, and back to the machine, as if the lamp had never been there. On a wall with seven other lamps on it, the seven would have gone on burning.

Harl looked at the dark lamp, and then across at the girl asleep against the wall, and then at Emeric.

"That's hers," he said.

"That's hers."

Harl nodded, once, as if that were a thing worth knowing about a machine, and picked up his buckets.



In the morning Emeric took his own regulator apart for the last time.

He did it at the dyehouse bench, slowly. The cover with its felt lip. The long iron neck. The train of brass wheels, nine days of Tess's fingers, every tooth filed by hand. He laid them out on the bench in a row, and looked at them, and they were good wheels, and there was nothing whatever wrong with them except that nobody needed them. Then he put them in the box under the bench where the candlesticks had been, and closed the lid.

Tess came in at noon with her eyes still swollen from the powerhouse floor, and stopped when she saw the bench empty.

"Seven more," Emeric said. "Can Bram spare you?"

"Bram can't spare me." She said it without any pride in it. "He'll do it anyway."

"Then seven more. Your drawings. Your weights. You show Bram's boys how, and they make the rods, and you set every washer yourself." He nodded at the book under her arm. "And put your name on it."

Tess looked down at the book.

She opened it, at the page with the nail and the washer and the coil and the little weight like Harl's stone, and looked at it. There was nothing written at the top of the page. There was nothing written at the top of any page in the book, as far as Emeric had ever seen. It was a book of measures, not of names.

She took her pen out of her apron and wrote at the top of the page, small, in her careful hand, and turned it round so that he could see.

Tess's feed.

Bram had come to the door behind her. He read it upside down from the doorway, over her shoulder, and looked from the page to his apprentice and back.

"She'll want paying for that," he said.

"She will be."

"More than me, I suppose," said Bram, and went back to his forge.
