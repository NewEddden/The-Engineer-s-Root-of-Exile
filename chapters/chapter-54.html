Chapter 54 - Winter Air

There were three things Emeric could do about Sethe, and by the end of the first week of winter he had done all three, and not one of them touched Sethe.

He could read. Saban's people sent it in every month and it came in dated, by carrier, with names, and he read it the way a man reads a barometer and put it in the drawer. He could keep the ledger black, which was Ferra's curve and eighteen months long and depended on nothing going wrong. And he could change his own watch, which was the only one of the three that was actually his to change, and so he changed it, and knew exactly how small a thing it was while he was doing it.

The board had run for a year by then. The walk had not altered by a man or an hour, which was the rule and remained the rule. What altered was the chair.

He put the four capital men on it in rotation, on the long nights, one in four.

His reasoning was not complicated and he gave it to them in the gatehouse with the door shut. A lamp at the second bell used to mean a runner and an otter. If a man six days north had three companies on retainer and was asking after mages with lineage, then a lamp at the second bell might one day mean something that had to be decided in the time it took to cross a room, and he did not want that decision made by a nineteen-year-old from the lower divisions who had been told to sit still and send runners.

They took it without complaint. Osric asked whether the chair came with a cushion. Declan asked for the roster in writing, which he always did. Rylan said the board had taken half his nights and said it the way a man says a thing he is pleased to have to complain about.

He checked it with Ferra first, because the covenant had trained him to check things he would once have simply done.

"Who sits a chair is a roster," she said, without looking up. "A roster is operating. You are not building anything, you are not buying anything, and you are not asking the factor for anything. Go away."

The other change that winter came out of Armand's bench and arrived on a slip of paper in handwriting nobody in Kaldo could read at speed.

The constant Emeric had derived in his second winter was wrong. It was wrong the dangerous way, which was low, by about a sixth, which meant that every margin he had ever calculated off it was thinner than he had believed and always had been. Armand had been at it for a month by then, cheerfully, taking the barony apart number by number with the satisfaction of a man who had not had anything worth checking in fifteen years, and he sent the corrections up as he found them, with the working shown.

Emeric applied it to the drying shed, the furnace gauges, the main and the four suits of harness, in that order, in front of Armand, and thanked him for it out loud.

He applied it to the board alone, at night, in the gatehouse, with the door barred.

The nodes had been on a three-week swap because three weeks was what his own figure gave him with a margin. The true number was nearer seventeen days, and seventeen days with no margin at all is not a schedule, it is a countdown. He rewrote the card's schedule to twelve, told Ferra to make the box hold twenty spares instead of eleven, and did not tell Armand what he had corrected, because to tell him what he had corrected would have meant telling him what was behind the backing of a board in a gatehouse, and every route to that sentence went through the same country he had already failed to cross three times over a cup of wine.

So the box emptied faster. More swaps meant more entries in Ferra's column, and more men learning that a node is a stone in a wall and not a piece of the prince's private business, and more nights in the chair where something actually had to be done.

It was, on any honest accounting, an improvement. He wrote it up as one.

The frost came in hard in the second week and the island started coughing.

It did that every year. It came off the water and it went into the children first, and Imani had been saying the same six sentences about it for four winters: boil the water anyway, keep them off the wet stone, and if you have anywhere dry to send a chesty one for a month, send him, because dry air does more in a fortnight than anything she could put in a cup.

Finn had it by the third day of the frost. Emeric heard about it the way he heard most things about that house, which was sideways, at the gatehouse brazier, from a man who had spent four years keeping his family out of the keep on purpose and had lately stopped bothering.

"He is up half the night with it," Rylan said. "You can hear him through the wall. Nessa is for sending him to her sister."

"Milford."

"Una, yes. Dry as a bone out there and cattle in every direction, which he will hate for four days and then he will be out with the man at first light and we will not get him home." He was cleaning the inside of a lamp housing with a rag while he said it, not looking up. "There is a cart going out on Friday anyway with the first of the contract on it. He can ride with Tam's lot as far as the hall and walk the last of it."

"Take the day," Emeric said.

"I will take the morning."

He took the morning. The cart went out through the Western Gate on the Friday in the first grey of it with three barrels, two crates of the foundry's small stuff, a drover's boy going home, and a ten-year-old in a coat too big for him who had been told fourteen times to keep it buttoned. Wren stood at the gate in a temper that lasted the rest of the week, because Finn was going to see cattle and she was going to see the lane, and no adult in the barony had given her one satisfactory answer about why.

Emeric watched them go from the wall for about as long as it takes a cart to reach the bridge, and then went in and did the day's work, and it did not cross his mind again for eleven days.

Rylan changed over the following week.

It was not sudden and it was not dramatic and Emeric watched the whole of it happen from four feet away, in pieces, and had no frame to put the pieces in.

He went quiet. Not the old quiet, the cold one from the year after the names, which had been a door shut in a man's face every morning for nine months. This was a held quiet, careful, the walk of somebody carrying a full bowl across a room and not wanting to be spoken to until he has set it down.

He stopped coming to the fire. The four of them had eaten at the same fire every night since the road down from the capital, six years of it, in a yard, in the rain, in a stable once; and Rylan ate in the gatehouse instead, standing, and was gone before the pot came round. Osric made a joke about it on the second night, something about married men and the quality of the company, and Declan did not laugh, and Osric did not make another one.

He was at the board on nights that were not his. Twice. Both times he had taken the chair off a line man who was glad to be taken off it at that hour, and both times he said he could not sleep with the house quiet, and both times that was a perfectly good reason, because half the garrison could not sleep in winter and the other half slept too well.

And on the fourth evening, at the brazier, with nobody asking him anything at all, he said that Finn was at his aunt's in Milford and the air was doing him good.

Emeric heard it, and put it where a man puts a pleasant small fact about somebody else's child, and said that was good.

The other thing he saw twice and did not think about once was the horse.

It was four inches of pine with one ear longer than the other, carved on the road south by a man who had needed something for his hands to do after the fire on the sixth night, and given to a boy who had been six years old at the time. Rylan had it in his hands in the chair, turning it, the way another man would turn a coin. Emeric recognised it and felt nothing except the small pleasure of recognising a thing he had watched being made, and went on with the swap sheet.

What he did notice, and was pleased by, was the questions.

They came over three or four nights and they were good ones. They were the questions a man asks when he has been handed a chair with something in it he does not fully understand and has decided, at thirty, to understand it rather than sit there hoping.

If the board faulted at night and the prince was not in the keep, who did the chair wake, and where was the prince likely to be?

How did you tell a starved node from a real fault in the dark, and was there anything in between?

What did a man do if it was neither, and the lamp was doing something the card had no word for?

Emeric answered all of it, and enjoyed answering it, and was aware of enjoying it.

A starved node goes dark and stays dark, he said. There is nothing to see and nothing to decide, and a dark lamp is nearly always a dead stone now that the schedule is twelve days: swap it out of the box, log it, and tell whoever has the morning that it wants the strain taken off. Anybody can do that. A boy can do that.

A true fault flickers first, because the working is half there and the stone cannot make up its mind what it is being asked for. That is the one you do not touch. That is the one you wake me for, wherever I am, and if I am on the coast then it waits ten days, and the walk covers it, because the walk always covers it.

And the only reason it has to be me, he said, is that a stone has to be learned by hand before it can be spoken to at any distance at all, and a stone that has gone wrong has to be learned over again, at the stone, with a hand on it. That is not an arrangement I am fond of. It is an arrangement I have.

He told him which nights he sat the chair himself and which nights he did not. He told him he slept in the keep and where in the keep, because Rylan had been standing outside that door two nights a week for six years and knew perfectly well.

Everything a man needed in order to write a false summons in Emeric's own procedure was said out loud in the gatehouse over four nights, and not one word of it was a secret. It was the card on the wall. Ferra had hung it herself, level, with two nails, where anyone sitting in the chair could read it.

Declan brought it to Ward, and Ward brought it to Emeric, and that was the whole chain and it took nine days.

Ward came up to the keep at the end of a shift with his gloves in one hand, and stood, and gave it the way he gave everything, which was flat, in order, with no verb doing any more work than the facts could carry.

Rylan had sat two nights that were not his.

Rylan had asked Cobb's boy on the west file, and then Hollis, and then a line man whose name Ward supplied without effort, which nights the prince came down to the gate.

Rylan had stopped eating with them.

"That is the lot," Ward said. "I am not telling you what it means. I am telling you I do not like it."

Emeric put the sheet down and made himself sit with it, because the first thing that came up in him was that it was nothing, and the first thing that comes up is the thing to be suspicious of.

Two of the four had wanted to go home since the autumn and had not said so, and Ward knew that and had told him, and neither of them had done anything about it because there was nothing to do about it. Men went strange in their sixth winter away from where they were born. Except that Rylan was the one of the four who was not away from where he was born, which cut the other way, and Emeric noticed it cutting.

"Say the rest of it," he said.

"There is no rest of it. Three facts. They sit next to each other and I have been doing this for twenty years and they sit badly."

"And if I do something with three facts that sit badly," Emeric said, "what do I do exactly? Take him off the chair? Put a man on him? Have Saban's people ask in his own lane what his wife has been buying?"

"I did not say any of that."

"No. I did." He stood up and went to the window, which was cold, and stayed there. "I hung a man's name in a yard once on four confirmations and a column. Better evidence than this by any measure you like. And you stood in that yard afterward and told me that I had asked the ledger and then asked the man to agree with the ledger, and that those were the same question twice."

Ward said nothing, which was what he did with things he had already said once.

"A column records what moved," Emeric said. "The reason lives in the man. And if I set this garrison to watching one of its own because he asked three questions that a good guard ought to ask, then the next man with a good question about a fault keeps it to himself, and the next fault kills somebody, and that will also be on paper somewhere with my name under it."

"Then ask him," Ward said.

Emeric turned round.

"That is fair," he said.

He asked him the next night, at the board, with the door shut and the brazier low, which was the only way he knew to do it that did not turn it into a proceeding.

He did not dress it up. He said that Ward had brought him three things, told him what they were, and asked whether something was wrong at home.

Rylan took a while over it.

That was the only true thing in the room, and Emeric watched it happen and read it as what it looked like, which was a man deciding how much of his own house he wanted to hand over to his employer at half past eleven at night.

"The house is quiet," Rylan said at last. "You have never had a house go quiet. It is not restful, it is just quiet, and I do not sleep in it. So I come down here, and there is a lamp and a brazier and somebody to say two words to, and that is the whole of the sitting nights." He turned the cup round. "As for your hours. I am in that chair one night in four now, and the stones are dying a week early since the bench started sending numbers up, and the card says a flicker wants the prince. So I would rather know where the prince is before I need him than find out I do not know at the moment I do. That is all it is. If that is a thing I should not have been asking, tell me and I will stop."

Every word of it was plausible. Some of it was true.

"It is not," Emeric said. "You are the man I would trust with that board."

Rylan looked at the lamps for a moment, all twelve of them dark and in order.

"Aye," he said.

He told Ward the following morning, the whole of it: that he had asked, what he had been told, that he was choosing to believe it, and why.

Ward was quiet long enough for the quiet to be the answer.

"As you say, my lord," he said, and went to walk his wall.

Emeric signed the week's swap sheet in Ferra's room three days later with the lamp log lying open beside it, because she kept them in the same book and would not be talked out of it.

Two lines that week on the east run. Otter, nine. Otter, nine. Both in Rylan's hand, which was still new enough at letters to press too hard, so the nine had gone through to the next page.

He read it and thought about the autumn the board went up, and the runner coming out of the stair swearing that it was the same otter, and about Ferra being right in the way she was usually right, which was two years early and without any triumph in it.

He signed the sheet.

The new roster went up on the gatehouse wall at the end of that week. Rylan read it standing, with his thumb on the line, and then asked whether he could have the new moon.

"It is Declan's."

"Declan wants the eighth, for his knee. He has been at the fire about it for a week."

The new moon was a dark night and a cold one and nobody sane wanted it. Emeric took the chalk and swapped the two names on the board himself, and thought nothing of it at all.
