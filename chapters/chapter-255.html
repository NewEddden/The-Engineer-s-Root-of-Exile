Chapter 255 - The Second Count

Ottoline had the box of cards on her trestle by half past five on the morning of the nineteenth of July, a Monday, under the same awning by the armoury door, with the same punch and the same pot of ink and three pencils sharpened to needles, in the same grey coat buttoned to the neck on a morning already warm, as if a year and a month had not happened to her at all.

It had happened to everybody else.

Emeric watched them come in from the keep stair, where he had watched them the first time. Fifty-nine; a levy man had gone back to his father's boat, and a Commencement girl had found she liked strapping ankles under Imani's awning better than falling down beside them, and gone down the hill to the ward. They came in at six straight across the cobbles to their lines, and stood in them without being told.

He had seen them three days a week for thirteen months and not seen it, because you do not see a thing grow that you look at every day; and then you look from a stair with the other morning in your head beside it, and it is a different yard. The school's children were not children. The levy men had lost the soft round the middle that a winter on the stream ground had not touched. Arthur was Arthur, only more so. And in the fourth line, by the rail, where he had been put on the first day and nobody had told him to move, Noll Finch stood with his hands behind his back like Ward, and was not the smallest in his line any more, and Emeric could not have said when that had happened either.

Somebody came up the stair behind him with two cups. He knew who before he turned.

"You were early," said Ferra, sitting down on the step above him with her book on her knees, as she had the June before.

"I was on time."

"You were on time last night as well. To the minute, by the counting room clock." She drank her tea. "I'm not complaining. I'm noting it."

"Who told you?"

"I was there," said Ferra, and opened her book to a page that had nothing to do with him; except that her pen had stopped above it, and she knew he had seen it stop, and he looked at her hand for longer than he should have, and she said without looking up, "The yard, Emeric," and he looked at the yard.



No rite. Ward said it first, from the well's step, as he said it at every count; and some of them looked at the board by the armoury door when he said it, where something had been pinned on the Saturday beside the place where the sheet would go, and then looked away.

There were four stations, as there had been: the run, twice round the measured track, with Declan at the corner holding the works' chronometer and calling the seconds in a voice like a clerk's; the bar, with Bram's iron plates; the standing jump from the chalk line, which Osric ran and could not run without talking; and the rail. The same rail: Bram had offered a better dial in June, and Ward had said no, so that nobody could say afterward the yard had changed the stick it measured with.

Emeric stayed on the stair. Ferra left him the second cup at seven and went back to the counting room, and some while later he was holding it in both hands, the way a man holds a cup at a sickbed, and could not have said when he had picked it up.

Mira went round the track third and did not look at who was ahead of her, which was new. She took the bar a plate past the best of last year's levy men, and jumped two feet past her old mark, and Osric told her nothing at all, which in Osric was a speech. At the rail she pushed past a hundred and thirty, where she had stopped the year before, and past two hundred, and the needle stopped at two hundred and eighty-one, a finger short of the pin; and she took her hands off and did not sit down hard on the bench. She stood with her chest going and watched the needle sink back.

"Two hundred and eighty-one," said Rylan, in the voice he used for the price of fish.

Bren came after her, which he would not have chosen. He had run the track a breath behind her this year instead of a breath ahead, and Emeric watched him see it and set his jaw; and at the rail he pushed for two hundred and sixty-four, and looked at the needle, and then at Mira's card in Ottoline's box, which he could not see from there and did not need to.

Arthur went to the rail after the jump, and nobody in the yard was doing anything else by then.

He put his hands on the iron without any ceremony and pushed. The needle went up the dial the way water goes up a glass from a jug, steadily, and reached the pin and stayed there, trembling a little, as it had stayed for nobody in that yard but Rylan; and Rylan counted. A hundred. Two hundred. The yard was quiet enough that Emeric could hear Declan's chronometer ticking at the corner of the track. Three hundred. Arthur's face did not change. He breathed slowly through his nose, as a man breathes on a long walk, and Rylan's voice went on, flat, unhurried, through four hundred.

"Arthur," said Ward, from the well.

Arthur took his hands off.

A year and a month ago Ward had said Rylan's name at that rail, in that voice, to stop him, and every one of the fifty-nine had been there. Arthur looked at Ward across the yard, and went red from the collar up, all at once, as he went red at nothing else, and looked at the cobbles.

"Held," said Rylan to Ottoline. "Four hundred and twelve. Stopped."

Armand had come down for the rail, as he had the year before; in his chair this year, carried down the outer stair by two of the garrison, because Imani would not let him do the stair since the spring and he had stopped arguing after a week, which was how Emeric knew what the spring had cost him. When Arthur took his hands off the iron he made a small sound in his throat, and Imani, behind the chair, put two fingers on the side of his neck without looking down and took them away again.

"Nine months," said Armand quietly. "September to the ditching. Saturday nights, at the mark. He's been living a night a week in what the rest of them get in a month."

Noll Finch came to the rail near the end, when the line had thinned, as he had the year before.

He had run the track in the middle of the field, red, his mouth shut, and had not come in grey. He had taken the bar to five plates and jumped a hand past his own height, and Osric had told him four things anyway, out of habit, and he had listened to all four. Now he put his hands flat on the rail, as Rylan had taught him, not with his arms but with whatever was inside him, and pushed.

The needle went past the place where it had stopped for him the year before, which was hardly off the pin, and past a quarter, and past a third; and Rylan counted in exactly the voice he had used for nine; and it went a hair past the middle of the dial and stopped there.

"A hundred and seventy-three," said Rylan, and wrote it on the card.

Noll did not sit down. He stood looking at the dial with his hands open at his sides, as if he had been given something and was waiting to be told he could keep it; and then he went and got his bread.



Ottoline pinned the sheet at noon, with Ferra beside her, which Ferra did not do.

It was the same sheet: fifty-nine names in Ottoline's small hard capitals, four figures after each, every column in order from best to worst. And a fifth column ruled down the right-hand edge that had not been there the year before.

GAINED.

Ferra's column. A sheet that said where everybody stood, Ferra had told Ottoline in June, said only half of it; the fifth was the places climbed in every column since the first sheet, added together, so that a boy who went from the bottom to the middle of everything stood above a boy who had stayed at the top and had nowhere to climb. Ottoline had said it was not how the tabulator liked to be asked things, and had made it like it by Thursday.

Emeric came down off the stair for this, and stood at the back of the crowd beside Ferra, and read it with them.

Most of them had gained in everything. He had expected it and it still went into him like a drink of something hot: seconds off the track, a plate or two on the bar, and at the rail eighty and a hundred more than the June before, the middle of the yard pushing now for what had been a still-crew man's figure. The stones in every kitchen had done some of it, and the water since November more, and the yard the rest, and there was no telling which from a sheet.

And the ones who had started poorest had gained the most. The GAINED ran almost upside down against the first sheet, as if it had been pinned up the wrong way. Arthur was at the top of the rail, held, with a mark beside it in Ottoline's pencil that meant off the dial; Mira second, Bren third; and because the three of them had been near the top of everything the year before, in the GAINED they sat low on the sheet, below boys they had beaten at everything. Emeric watched Bren read that, and read it again, and not understand, and then understand.

At the top of the GAINED, by more than the next two together, was Noll Finch.

He was in the middle of every column. Thirtieth on the track, twenty-eighth at the bar, twenty-sixth on the jump, thirty-first at the rail, of fifty-nine. Not the top of anything. But he had been fifty-ninth in all four, alone at the bottom of every list on the board, and he had climbed further than anybody in the yard, and the tabulator, which did not care who anybody was, had added it up and put him first.

The middle, Rylan had said on the first night, from the well's step, is where a company stands.

Noll read the sheet once. Not four times, as he had the year before; once, top to bottom, and his own line, and then he went to eat his bread on the well's step with Mira, who said something that made him look at his boots.

Kit Bastow read the fifth column aloud with his finger to three levy men who could read it perfectly well, and got to the top, and said "Finch!" across the whole yard in a voice that carried to the gate, and lifted his cup, which on a yard day held water; and the levy men lifted theirs, and Noll on the well's step put his bread down on his knee and did not look round.

Then the crowd thinned, and the other thing on the board was there for anybody who had not read it on the Saturday.

It hung a hand's width from the sheet, where nobody reading one could miss the other. Rylan had pinned it himself and nobody had asked him to take it down. One of Ivo's photographs, on its glass, in a frame of four laths Rylan had nailed together: a young man's forearm on a white sheet, elbow to wrist. Not a knight's lines. Lines that began at the wrist as a knight's did and went up pale, the width of a thread, for a hand's length; and then stopped, and did not close, and went on as something else, darker, spreading along their own paths under the skin toward the elbow like ink run in wet paper. Under it, in Imani's hand, three lines. An age, which was young. A date, the same in all three. And one word, Forced.

No names. The families had asked for none on the board, and Imani had asked Rylan, and Rylan had said that was right: the names were theirs, and the arm was everybody's.

Ferra had not looked at the sheet since she came down. She was looking at the three lines, and her lips moved very slightly, and Emeric knew she was saying the dates from her own book, where the same three lines stood beside a column of every warning that had gone out in May, the day and the place and how many had heard it. She had read that column to the table the morning after, flat, in order, and nobody had interrupted her.

She shut her book.

"I've to get back," she said, and did not go.



It was Rylan's idea to use the stones, and Rylan's bag of them.

Most of the fifty-nine were along the north wall in the shade with their bread when Emeric came across the yard at two, because Ward had asked him to, without saying why. Rylan was by the armoury with a mason's tool bag at his feet, full of river stones, round and grey, the size of a child's fist, off the gravel below the water stair.

"Armand says you've a plate," said Rylan.

"Armand says a good many things."

"Armand says you've stood in front of Orrin in the long shed twice a week since March, letting him throw fire at you, and you've stopped flinching, and you're very pleased with yourself." Rylan weighed a stone in his hand. "Ward says the class should see a shield. A real one, in a real yard, so they know what it's for." He tossed the stone up and caught it. "And what it isn't."

Emeric looked at the bag, and at Rylan, and down the yard at fifty-nine young people who had stopped eating.

"You've been planning this," he said.

"Since March," said Rylan.

It was the simplest thing he had learned in a year: your own mana told to be nothing but a surface, held where your hand said, like a door held shut against a wind; and you paid for whatever arrived. Orrin had paid ten times over to make his fire out of empty air, and all the plate paid for was the heat and push that reached it, so the fire broke on it like a wave on a quay, and Orrin swore, and Emeric had stood there untouched and been, as Rylan said, very pleased with himself.

He held his hand up, open, toward the far end of the yard, and made the plate.

There was a glyph for the space of a breath as it formed, the shield's own faint sign in the air before his palm, that a knight with a charged edge could have cut if he had been quick; then it was gone into the plate and there was nothing to see. The north wall saw the glyph. He heard them see it, a sound along the wall like a breath taken.

"Ready," he said.

Rylan did not throw as a boy throws. He threw as a knight throws, with the whole of his body behind it and the rite's lines going taut along the insides of his arms, and the stone came down the yard faster than the eye could follow and hit the plate a foot from Emeric's hand.

The plate held. The stone stopped dead in the air, as a stone stops against a wall, and dropped and rolled. And the cost went into him at the same instant, the whole of what Rylan's arm had put into it, out of his own mana and through his arm into his chest like a hand on the breastbone; he went back half a step without meaning to, and the breath went out of him in a grunt the whole yard heard.

"That's one," said Rylan, and threw again.

A stone every few breaths, with the same easy swing, and every one cost what the first had cost. By the sixth there was a ring of river stones round his boots and he was breathing like a man who has run the track.

"Orrin's fire," said Rylan conversationally, with the seventh in his hand. "What did that cost you?"

"Next to nothing," said Emeric, through his teeth.

"And this?"

"Everything you put into it."

"And what did I put into it?" Rylan threw. Emeric went back another half step. "A stone off the river." He turned to the north wall. "A mage pays ten times over for his fire, so it breaks on a shield cheap. Nobody paid for this." He held up the eighth. "So he pays for all of it. And I've a bag full."

He threw the eighth, and the ninth, and the tenth.

Emeric held, because the class was watching and because he was too proud to put his hand down in front of fifty-nine people and a bag of gravel; and his arm had begun to shake at the shoulder, and the plate in his own sense had gone thin, like ice on a pail in the first week of frost.

He felt the tenth come, as he felt everything come: a little low, a little to the right, for the edge of the plate where his arm had drifted a hand's width. He could have dropped the shield and stepped out of its road, and the class would have learned that a man steps out of the road. He kept the plate where it was and let it come; and it caught the edge, and went past, and took him on the point of the right shoulder.

It hurt a great deal. He said so, in one word, which the whole yard also heard.

"That's the side," said Rylan, and put the eleventh back in the bag. "A plate's only where your hand is. A skin costs more and a bubble costs most, and a stone's a stone whichever you've got up." He looked at the north wall. "A shield's for things that cost the other man more than they cost you."

"And what it isn't for," said Ward.

Emeric had felt his note cross the yard from the armoury door, as he felt every note in the yard, and had been too busy holding a plate against a bag of river gravel to think anything of it. Ward came across the cobbles with the ash blade from the mornings, and stopped two paces off, an arm and a blade's length.

"Up," said Ward.

The plate went up badly, after ten stones; the glyph shook as it formed and came out thin. Ward laid the blade's edge against it, slowly, as a man lays a straightedge on a board, and Emeric felt him charge it, the lines in Ward's arm sending what was in them down the hilt, so that the ash carried a thin bright line of Ward's own mana along its edge. It went into the plate as a hot wire goes into wax. It did not slow. It stopped of its own accord an inch from Emeric's shirt, over the breastbone, where Ward had meant it to; and where it had passed, the plate was simply not there any more, and that cost him too, small and sharp, like a thread pulled through a seam.

The yard was absolutely silent.

"A mage behind a plate laughs at fire across a field all day," said Ward, to the north wall. He took the blade away. "Then a knight walks up to him."

He went back to the armoury.

Emeric put his hand down. His shoulder throbbed and his shirt was soaked through.

"Next time," he said to Ward's back, "warn me."

"No," said Ward, without turning round.

It began with Kit Bastow, who could not help it, and went down the whole north wall until Arthur had his face in his hands and Mira her head back against the stones; and Emeric stood in his ring of river gravel and found he was laughing too. Imani was by the awning with her bag, looking at his shoulder across the yard as if it were a symptom.

"I'll want that after," she said.

"It's a bruise."

"It's a stone off the river at a knight's arm. How many?"

"Ten," said Rylan, tying his bag.

"Ten," said Imani, and wrote it down.



Rylan read the numbers at the end of the day.

He stood on the well's step in his shirtsleeves, with the lines going up the insides of his forearms where all of them could see, and read the GAINED from the top, every name and what it had climbed, without looking up, and the yard sat on the cobbles in the long light and listened to their own names go past as if he were reading out the tide.

"Finch," he said, at the top. "A hundred and twenty-one places."

He did not let it sit. He went on, in the same voice. Holt and Tallis came a long way down, with a handful of places each. Arthur came near the bottom, where the ones who had started at the top of everything had to be, and Rylan read his name exactly as he had read Noll's, and went on to the last.

Then he put the cards in his shirt.

He did not say anything about them. He stood on the well's step and lifted his hand and pointed across the yard at the board: at the sheet with its five columns, and held his hand there a breath; and moved it a hand's width to the left, to the photograph in its four laths and Imani's three lines under it, and held it there until everybody in the yard had looked.

Then he put it down.

"Bread," said Rylan, and stepped off the well.



Ned Tallis came up from the shed when the yard had mostly gone to the long room, with the Hod's crew book under his arm in its oilcloth.

He came in by the little door from the canal walk in the lighterman's coat he wore even in July, and read the sheet from the top, and stopped at the third name in the rail column. Bren was at the well, washing. He saw his uncle and went on washing, with great attention.

Ned opened the crew book on the board's ledge at the page with the crew's names, his own and Tobe's and Cade's, and Arthur's under them, and Mira's, under the line he had written across its head in June, NEVER FEWER THAN FOUR ABOARD; took the pencil from behind his ear, looked at the sheet once more as if checking a bill of lading, and wrote the sixth line.

"Bren," he said. "Come here."

Bren came, with the water still running down his neck.

"Count's up," said Ned. "On the board, where anybody can read it." He held out the pencil. "Read the page first. All of it. Line two, and what Imani wrote at the foot." He looked at his nephew. "Nobody'll say it was the family boat. Nobody can."

Bren read the page with his lips moving, and at the foot of it, where Imani had written in June what the racks did to a body over a year, he read it twice. Then he signed, badly, because his hand was wet.

"February," he said, not to Ned; to nobody. "The end of it. There was ice at the edges. Nobody was there." He gave back the pencil. "I didn't tell anybody. I wanted it on the board."

"It's on the board," said Ned, and wrapped the book, and went back to the little door without saying anything else, which was how Ned said most things.

Mira was at the foot of the outer stair, looking at the sky. Bren walked past her without looking either; then stopped on the bottom step and said something, and she said something back, and he hit her shoulder with his fist, not hard, and she hit his back much harder, and they went up to the supper together with their heads down, both talking at once.

The Hod had five chutes, and there were six names in her book now. Somebody had told Abloh. Bren's had been folded in its pack on a shelf in his workroom since the first week of July, with no name on it, waiting.



The crates had been in the armoury's back room for three years under a stack of old pikes: two of them, long, iron-cornered, stencilled on the lids STOVE PARTS. Seven suits had gone into crates like them in the week the Visitor walked the town, and five had come out after, and two had stayed in, because there had been nobody yet to wear them.

Bram opened them on the Tuesday with a bar, as if he were opening a coffin he had built himself and had doubts about, and the sixth and seventh came out of the straw as they had gone in, oiled and wrapped, and lay on the long table in their pieces in the light from the high window, like two men taken apart to be mended.

Mira and Bren stood at the end of the table and did not say anything at all.

"They weren't made for you," said Bram, turning the first casing to the window. "They were made for a big man with long arms. The casing comes in at the chest for you," to Mira, "and the harness wants a place for that plait that isn't down the back of the shoulder rig where it'll catch. And you're long in the leg and narrow in the hip," to Bren, "and you'll want the knee let down a finger." He put the casing down. "The duke'll measure. I'll make them to what he writes. He's never once been wrong on the slate." He looked at Emeric. "Mind you're not wrong this time."

So Emeric measured them himself, with Abloh's tape and a slate, as he had measured Arthur in the winter and the four knights before him, because a suit a finger wrong in the leg pulled the man in it sideways every time it lifted, and he gave that to nobody else.

Mira stood in her shirt with her arms out and her chin up and stared at the far wall with her jaw set, and did not say a word until the last figure.

"Is it near enough?" she said then. "To what it was made for?"

"It's nowhere near what it was made for. That's why Bram's taking it apart." He held up the slate. "This is what it'll be made for."

She looked at the slate, and at the suit in its pieces, and put her shirt back on very quickly and went out into the yard; and from the window he saw her stand with her back to the armoury wall in the sun with both hands over her face for the space of three breaths, and take them away, and go back to the well as if nothing had happened.

Bren had grown two fingers since the spring and his body had not caught up with the news. He stood with his arms out at the wrong angle and his shoulders round his ears and his face set like a man having a tooth drawn.

"Hold still."

"Sorry, sir. Your Grace. Sir."

"Emeric, in here, when nobody's looking but Bram, and Bram's counting rivets." He took the stride. "If your arm's at the wrong angle because you're stiff with nerves, I'll write it at the wrong angle, and Bram'll build it at the wrong angle, and the first time you lift you'll go sideways into the canal in front of the whole town." He looked up. "And Mira will see it."

Bren's arm came down to the right angle at once.

Abloh came in at the end with the two chute packs under his arm and his mouth full of pins, and fitted each to its owner, strapping and letting out and taking in, until each pack sat square between the shoulders and did not ride up when they lifted their arms. When Bren asked how he had known to make one before there was a name for it, Abloh took the pins out of his mouth and said he had never once in thirty years waited to be told who was going to need clothes, and put them back.



The lot was on the Gate Avenue, on the east side, between a block that was finished and a block that was not.

It had been a lane of four plague-empty houses in the spring, taken down in May beam by beam onto the builders' yard; now it was a raked oblong of earth with Linus's pegs at its corners and a string line round it, and the new avenue running past its front with the young limes in their iron cages and the two bright lines of the tram rails down its middle, waiting. The retrofit had finished in the kept wards the week before, and Ferra had ruled off that column at the table and said it was the first of its size she had ever ruled off on its date and she would like everybody to notice; so the crews had come to the avenue, and it was noise from end to end, scaffolds and mortar mills and hot tar and cut stone.

Bram stood on the bare lot with his hands in his apron pocket.

"Well," he said.

"Give me a breath."

He had the house in his room. They had drawn it there in March, in the slow time, at full size, over four nights: four houses in a terrace, three storeys and a cellar, the standard plan with the bay Bram wanted and Ferra had costed; and walked it there until Bram said it would do. But a house in the room stood on nothing. He had never put one down on its own ground.

He found Bram's note beside him, slow and warm, a smith's note, with the small bright fleck in it of the council band on Bram's wrist, and opened the door the width of a hair. He felt Bram feel the quiet that came a moment before.

"Open your eyes," he said, and opened his own.

The house stood on the lot.

Faint, a drawing on a window with the world behind it; but standing at its true size where it would stand, the four fronts along the avenue's edge with their doors and bays and three ranks of windows and the line of the roof against the sky. The scaffold of the next block showed through its second floor. A mason's boy walked through its drawing-room wall with a hod on his shoulder, not knowing.

Bram walked into it. He went in at a front door that was not there and stood in the hall and looked up the stair. He went out round the back and followed the drain line on the drawing down to the sewer under the avenue with his eye, and nodded. Then he went in at the back door and up the back stair, and stopped on the first landing, and stood there.

He stood there long enough that the door, held open with a whole house in it, began to cost: the overlay's price, a dull ache behind the eyes, coming in slowly as a tide does.

"Come here," said Bram.

Emeric went round and up the drawn stair, which his eyes could see and his feet could not, his boots going up raked earth and his eyes going up treads; and stood beside Bram on the landing, a man's height above the ground with his feet on it, and looked where Bram was looking.

The back stair turned on the landing toward the outside wall, and the first floor's back window was set over it; and where a man turned on that landing to go on up, the stone head of the window came down across the stair, so that anybody of any height going up in the dark would walk his forehead into the lintel at the turn, every night, for the rest of his life in that house.

"It's the wrong way round," said Bram. "It wants to turn toward the party wall. Then the window's over the bottom flight and you're a yard clear." He looked at the lintel. "We walked it in your room. Four nights. You can't walk into a lintel in there. There's no head on you in there to do it with."

Emeric could find nothing to say to that, because it was exactly true.

"Turn it."

"I'll turn it on the paper. And the other three, because they're the same." Bram looked round at the faint house, at the rooms with the scaffold through them, at the mason's boy coming back through the kitchen with an empty hod. Then at Emeric.

"If you can put it in front of my eyes," said Bram, "the whole of it, on its ground, where I can walk up the stair and find the lintel before a stone's laid, why do I need forty masons to put it in the ground?"

Emeric shut the door. The house went out of the air like breath off a cold glass, and the lot was raked earth again, and the ache went with it, slowly.

"A machine," he said. "And a winter."

Bram looked at the bare lot a long time.

"I'll want the drawing on paper," he said, "where I can put a pencil through it. Not the picture." He walked back toward the avenue, and stopped at the string line, and said without turning round, "And I'll want to be in the room when you draw the stairs. You drew those on the Thursday. I was at the works."

Which was also true. Emeric stood on the lot by himself and remembered the Thursday in March, the four back stairs drawn in ten minutes at the end of a long night because they were the standard plan and he knew the standard plan; and that he had not walked them again; and that he had not remembered it until Bram said it, and Bram had known since the landing.



Sela had asked for the name in June.

She had not asked Emeric. She had stood at the works gate in her shawl one Saturday until somebody fetched Bram, and asked him whether the fourth engine would be a good one, and he had said the best they had built; and then she had said she knew the crews named them for Kaldo's dead, and she would like to give them a name if they would let her, and told him which. Bram had gone in and told the crews. The crews had not argued.

They brought both engines out on the Wednesday morning, the twenty-first, under their own steam, very slowly, with the whole works out on the ballast and Wick standing in steam on the next road with her driver leaning out of the cab to see.

The fourth was the biggest thing Kaldo had ever built that moved. She came out of the great doors black and enormous in the July sun, higher in the boiler than Wick by a head and longer by half her length, with six driving wheels where Wick had four, coupled with rods as thick as a man's wrist, so that when she moved they all moved together, rising and falling like a man walking with a load. She was built to pull Grayrock's coal to a power station that was still a set of pegs in a field, and she looked like a thing that would not stop.

Behind her came the fifth, short and squat and very pleased with itself, its water in two tanks along its sides and no tender at all, built to shove wagons about the yard and never go a mile from home. A tank engine: Emeric had called it that in the drawing office in the spring, and the crews had kept it, as they kept anything short and plain that meant what it said.

The crews had chalked the fifth already, as they had chalked Wick and Dunnel and Harl, in a fireman's big careless capitals on the side of the cab so that it would not go out of the shop a single morning without a name. WAT.

He had sat on a stool by Wat's bed in Imani's end room most of a week, two Octobers ago, and watched his hands, a hedger's hands on a man of thirty-one with a grey head, lay a hedge on the blanket, stem after stem, close and even, until they stopped. He had not known the crews knew who Wat was. He did not ask who had chalked it. His hand had gone to Bram's arm without asking him, and Bram let it stay, and he took it away.

The fourth had no chalk on her.

Sela came down from the town at half past seven in her good shawl, with Arthur beside her a head and a half taller and walking at her pace; and the works, which had been talking, stopped. She did not look at Emeric. She walked along the ballast to the big engine where it stood breathing in the sun and stopped under the black round face of the smokebox, which was higher than her head.

The fireman, a dock-quarter boy who knew who she was, climbed down without being told and held out his chalk.

She took it, and looked up at the smokebox door, and then at her son.

Arthur did not say anything. He put his hands round his mother's waist the way a man lifts a child onto a cart and lifted her, easily, and held her there at the height of the door with her good shoes off the ground; and Sela wrote on the iron, slowly, in the school capitals she had taught every child in Kaldo to make on a slate, three letters.

TAM.

Then she put her flat hand on the iron beside the name. It was warm; the engine was in steam. She left her hand there a breath.

"Down," she said.

Arthur put her down. She gave the fireman his chalk back and turned round and looked along the ballast at the works, the fitters and the boilermakers and the drawing office boys and the old men from the forge, standing in their aprons with their caps in their hands, which nobody had told them to take off; and at Emeric last, at the end of the line beside Bram. She nodded to him once. It was the nod she gave a child at the end of the day who had done his letters and would do them again tomorrow.

Then she took Arthur's arm and went back up the ballast, and the works did not put its caps on until she was in the lane.

The driver opened the big engine up very gently, and the six wheels turned together, once, and once more, with a long slow sigh from the cylinders like a horse let into a field; and Tam went down the yard road toward the empty coal wagons on the siding, with her name on her in chalk.



The trainees went home on the afternoon train: Grayrock's thirty-one, after a season in Kaldo.

Ferra gave them their papers on the platform below the works: a sheet off the mundane press with the gold rose at its head and the trade in capitals, MASON or SMITH or PUMP MAN, the name written in, the shop lead's signature and Ferra's, and her seal in red wax. A big slow Grayrock mason with lime in his knuckles did not go back to the coach, but stood reading his, and turned it over to read the empty back, and turned it again.

"That's my name," he said. "On a paper with a seal on it." He looked up at Ferra. "What's it worth? At Grayrock?"

"Whatever Bryda Renner pays a mason who can set a drain to a fall of one in forty without a man from Kaldo standing over him," said Ferra. "Which is more than she paid you in the spring." She closed the box. "Put it somewhere dry."

The mason buttoned it inside his shirt, flat against his chest, and got into the coach.



Tam went up the Grayrock line at five behind the people's train, with twenty empty coal wagons and Emeric on the footplate.

He had asked Bram rather than the driver, which was cowardly, and Bram had said the driver would no more want the duke on his footplate than a goat, and that Emeric was to keep his mouth shut on the curves. He did. The driver listened to her with his head on one side, as a carter listens to a new horse; and in the first ridge's cutting he said, without looking round, "She's a good one," and it mattered to Emeric more than he would have admitted. At the halfway water the fireman held the shovel out to him with no expression whatever, and he fired her badly for a mile and then less badly, and when the smoke at the chimney went from black to grey the fireman took the shovel back, and that was the whole of his opinion.

They came into Grayrock at dusk.

From the air, at night, there had never been anything at Grayrock to see but one lamp at the winding house and a thread of sparks on a black hillside, which he looked for every time he flew over and was sorry every time he found.

There was a powerhouse at the pithead now, a long brick shed with a tall stack, built since March on Grayrock's own coal by Grayrock's own masons, half of whom were on the train ahead, with Bram's dozen beside them since June and Tess's two line men up the poles; one of Bram's small steam sets in it, and a dynamo, and lines on poles going out two ways, up to the pit and down to the rows.

And the rows were lit. They had been knocked through and rebuilt over the winter, the old pit rows that had been two rooms and a midden to a family, and in every window of every new terrace, as Tam came round the last bend into the valley with her empties rattling, there was a light that was not a candle, steady and white and the same in every window, so that the valley side from the landing to the pithead was ruled across in lines of light like a page of Ferra's book.

The driver shut her off and let her drift the last half mile so that she would not be loud coming in. Emeric did not ask him why.

The shift was coming up. The men came off the cage in a crowd, black to the eyes, as the men of Grayrock had come up out of that pit at dusk for as long as there had been a pit; and on the front of every leather cap there was a lamp, small and brass with a glass, a cord over the cap to a box on the belt, so that the crowd crossing the pithead yard in the dusk was a crowd of small white points of light moving together, like a field of fireflies come up out of the ground. Cap lamps, two hundred, charged in racks at the powerhouse every shift. And behind every glass, where a man in the dark could reach it with his thumb, a dumb light stone, because Emeric had said at the table that a pit was the last place in the kingdom to trust a thing that could stop, and nobody had argued.

The men did not go to their rows. They went, black as they were, into the long building at the side of the yard whose roof vents were steaming, the pithead baths, hot water off the powerhouse's spent steam, so that a man went home in a clean shirt and his wife did not boil his clothes in her one copper every night of her life. Bryda had put it at the bottom of her list in one line, a place for my men to wash.

Bryda was on the landing. He had felt her note before the train stopped.

She stood at the end of the staithes in a dark coat with her hands in its pockets, watching her men go into the baths; and when he came down off the footplate with coal on his face she looked at him without any expression for long enough that he became aware of the coal.

"Your Grace. You came up on the coal train."

"I wanted to see it lit."

"You'll have seen it from the air."

"Not lit."

"No." She looked at the rows. "My husband said a collier's wife didn't need to see to sweep. She needed to sweep." She was quiet a while.

He had the two lines in his head, Ilsa's square hand at the foot of a page of carts and powder, and Ferra on the end of his bed saying let her keep it. He kept his mouth shut on them.

"I sat at Ferra's table in February," he said instead, "and told you I couldn't trust you."

"You told me to my face, with my daughter beside me. That was the part I liked." She looked at him at last, long enough that he wondered what Ilsa had told her, and knew he would never ask. "Trust's slow, in a pit. You find out who's beside you when the roof talks." The corner of her mouth moved. "Your Chancellor's teaching my Ilsa columns. Ilsa says nobody wins an argument with her."

"Nobody does."

"Then come and eat," said Bryda. "My cook made a pie when she heard the coal train had the duke on it, and if you don't eat it she'll never make me one again. And wash your face. You look like one of mine."

He slept in the landing house, in a room with a lamp that was not a candle, and lay a long while with it off looking at the lines of light across the valley; and went down in the morning on Tam's footplate with twenty wagons of Grayrock coal behind him, the first, for a power station that was still pegs in a field.



Ferra had been waiting all week to tell him the thing she actually wanted to say about the two lines, and she told him on the Thursday night, in the cellar, after the second bell, sitting on the bottom step of the cellar stair with her book on her knees and her feet tucked under her skirt because the floor was cold even in July.

She had been in a very good mood since the find, in a way that had made Saban ask him on the Monday, carefully, whether the Chancellor was ill.

"Off the books," said Ferra, "isn't the same as hidden."

He was on the floor with his back against the barrel rack, because the stair was hers. "No?"

"No. Hidden is when you keep a thing from the person it's owed to. Wexler hid. Sethe hid. The Staple hid. There's somebody on the other side of the column with a right to see it." She held up one finger. "Off the books is when it isn't owed to anybody. When it's yours. Bryda had a debt of honor from Aldane that wasn't Grayrock's and wasn't the crown's; she received it and sent it back unopened, and would have had it written nowhere. Ilsa wrote two lines at the foot of a page against her mother's word, because she couldn't bear a book that lied, and put no figure against them." She put the finger down. "Not in a column. Because it wasn't anybody's column. It was theirs."

"And you found it."

"And I found it, and I didn't put it in a column either. I told you to let her keep it, and I've let her." Her face was doing the thing it did when a figure came out exactly, a stern delight she would have denied under oath. "I've been thinking about it all week," she said, "because I've got one."

She did not look at him when she said it. She looked at the cellar's far wall, where Bram's chalk marks stood on the stones in a rough arch for the door into the canal shaft, with figures beside them; and went on looking at the wall with great dignity.

"One. In my whole life. The first thing I've ever had that wasn't in a column somewhere. It isn't hidden. It isn't owed to anybody." She put the book down on the step. "It's mine."

He got up off the floor. She watched him come and did not move. He put a hand on the step either side of her and she put her hands flat on his chest and did not push, and said from a hand's width away, "You came down at the bell exactly, by the clock. I watched you," and he said, "I'll go back up and come down again," and she said, "Don't you dare," and pulled him in by the shirt.

It was some time before either of them said anything else.



They went up a little before one, by the cellar stair, to go round by the passage and the keep stair to her room; with no lamp, because they both knew the stair in the dark, every step, and the fourth best of all.

He felt Bram's note at the third step.

It was in the meeting room off the passage. It had been there all evening, he understood with a small cold drop in his stomach, steady and slow, and he had felt it as he felt every note in the keep at every hour and not thought about it once, because his mind had been on the cellar and the person in it. Bram, at the long table at one in the morning, turning four back stairs the other way on paper.

And the note was moving. Out of the meeting room, into the passage, toward the head of the cellar stair.

Emeric stopped dead on the third step. Ferra, on the fifth, having stepped over the fourth as she always did, felt him stop and turned in the dark.

"What," she whispered.

He tipped his head very slightly upward.

She could see nothing; there was nothing to see. But she knew his face in the dark by now, and he felt her understand, and felt her note do a thing he had felt it do only once before, on the lift, the first time, when she had learned his gravity: it went up bright and quick, like a fire when somebody opens the door on it.

She was delighted.

Lamplight was coming along the passage floor toward the stair's head. There was nowhere to go on a stair but up into it or down out of it, and down was the cellar, and the cellar had one door, and it was this one. Ferra came down off the fifth step in one long stride over the fourth, onto his step, against him, and put her back to the wall and pulled him in under the curve of the stair where its turn threw the deepest shadow, and held still.

And he, to make room for her on a step not made for two, put his heel back on the fourth.

It went down under him and came up the other side with a sound like a plate dropped on a stone floor, a flat loud clack that went up the stairwell and along the passage and, as far as he could tell, round the whole keep and out over the town.

The light stopped.

Ferra did not breathe. He felt her not breathing, with her face in his collar and her fists in the back of his shirt. He went perfectly still, as he had in a boat's hold with Rylan's son, and felt the note at the top of the stair, and waited to see what it would do.

The light came to the top of the stair, and Bram stood in it, with the lamp held out a little at the height of his chest. Its light went down over the first step, and the second, and the third, as far as the turn; and stopped a hand's breadth short of the toe of Ferra's shoe.

He stood there.

He did not hold the lamp any further out. He did not take a step down. He stood at the head of the cellar stair at one in the morning in his shirtsleeves with a pencil behind his ear, and looked at the place where the light ended, at the fourth step, with no expression whatever; and he said nothing.

He said nothing so long, and so steadily, and with such complete attention, that it became a kind of sound.

Then Bram looked at the fourth step a moment longer.

"Wants mending, that," he said, to the stair.

He turned with the lamp and went back along the passage the way he had come, not quickly, and the light went with him round the corner, and the meeting room door shut.

Ferra breathed out, all of it, into his collar, and then she was shaking against him, and for a breath he thought she was crying; and then he understood that she was laughing, with her whole body and without a sound, holding his shirt in both fists with her face pressed into his neck, laughing so hard her knees went and he had to hold her up.

"He didn't see," she whispered, when she could. "He was looking at the step. He's said that about that step for five years. Every time he comes down. Wants mending. To nobody." She put her forehead against his chin. "That was the nearest. That was the nearest there's ever been."

"We could tell him," said Emeric.

He said it before he had decided to. He had been not saying it since the autumn, since a sleeve and four words over a lamp, and it came out of him now as a thing comes out of a man who has carried it so long he has forgotten it is heavy. Not the thing he knew. Only the door to it.

"No," said Ferra, and he heard that she was smiling in the dark, that it had not touched her at all except to make her happier. "If we tell him, it's his. Then it's a question. Then he has to have an opinion about it, and say it, and I have to answer it, and it's a column." She put her hand flat on his chest. "It's off the books. I've only got the one." She pushed him gently down off the fourth onto the third, and stepped over it herself, and stood on the fifth looking down at him. "We'll go round by the yard."

"That's twenty minutes."

"That's twenty minutes in the dark by the yard wall, with the night watch on the far gate." She held out her hand. "Come on. Duke."

It took twenty-five, because she stopped twice by the wall to laugh again and once by the well for a reason that had nothing to do with laughing; and when he came up the keep stair behind her he looked along the passage, and there was a line of light under the meeting room door, and a smith's note behind it, bent over a table, turning a stair.



Ferra told Imani at breakfast, because nobody had a thing the other did not. She told it at the little table in her room at seven, in order, flat, as she read a column to the table; except that she had to put her cup down twice, at the step and at the lamp.

Imani ate her bread and listened to all of it and did not laugh, and at the end she looked at Ferra across the table with her doctor's face, the one for a symptom she had not seen before and found interesting.

"Your father," said Imani, "has been going up and down that stair with a lamp since the night of the oath." She picked up her bread. "One day he'll mend that step. And then where will the pair of you be."

Ferra opened her mouth, and shut it.

She looked at Imani. Then, quite slowly, at Emeric, who was buttering a piece of bread with great care and giving it a good deal more butter than it wanted.

"He was looking at the step," said Ferra firmly. "He says it every time." She opened her book. "Wants mending."

"Wants mending," Imani agreed, and caught Emeric's eye across the table for exactly as long as it took Ferra to turn a page.

Ferra wrote something small on the inside of the back cover, where she thought he did not know she kept a column with no heading: dates, and against each a number of steps, or a door, or a time. Against last night she wrote the date, and the fourth, and after it, in square small capitals he could read upside down from across the table, one word.

NEARLY.

She closed the book, very satisfied, and finished her tea, and went to work.

There was a note under his own door when he went up for his coat, folded in three, in Tess's quick slanting hand.

Tram. Saturday. Eight. The South Gate. Osric says I'm to tell you to bring a coat because it'll be cold at the works end and you never do. T.
