Chapter 4 - The Vouch

He worked the persuasion problem most of the night, which was easier than working the other one.

There are creatures too small to see was a dead end and he had known it before he finished thinking it. Nobody in Kaldo was going to accept the invisible on his word, and they shouldn't. He wouldn't have. On Earth that sentence had taken two centuries, a microscope, and several thousand corpses to become obvious, and he was proposing to skip all three by being confident about it in a market square.

So don't argue the cause. Argue the outcome. Make a prediction they can watch come true, and back it with numbers they already trust, which meant numbers they had written themselves.

That was the plan and it had two holes in it. He needed a person in this town who would talk to him honestly, and he needed a way into records nobody was going to hand a prince because he asked nicely.

Two doors down, Declan was still sitting outside a room with a traitor in it. Emeric noticed that the persuasion problem had kept his attention for four straight hours without effort, and that he had not spent thirty seconds on Petram, and that he was in no danger whatsoever of accidentally doing so.

He put out the candle.

"Who keeps the death records here?" he asked Rylan on the way down the hill in the morning. "Baptisms, burials, whatever the parish logs."

"Priest keeps the parish book. Has for years." Rylan said it like it was obvious, then caught up. "You're not thinking of asking him for it."

"Not yet. I'm thinking about who'd vouch for me first, so that asking him doesn't go the way everything else has gone since I got here."

He needed a name this town trusted more than it would ever trust his title. Somebody whose word carried because it had been earned across years instead of inherited in an afternoon.

"Bram," Rylan said, before Emeric had finished the thought. "Blacksmith. People bring him more than horseshoes, if you take my meaning. He's fair all round, damn near incorruptible, and he's been here longer than the war took anybody. If he says you're worth listening to, some of them will listen." A pause. "If he says you're not, it won't matter what you actually do."

That was either the best lead he was going to get or the fastest available way to lose this before it started, and it was the only one on offer.

The walk cut past the edge of town, where the buildings thinned into what should have been farmland and mostly wasn't. Fields sat fallow in long stretches, soil gone hard and pale where nothing had been turned in a season or more, weeds doing considerably better than any crop would have. Further on, a mill wheel sat dead still over water with more than enough current in it to turn the thing.

He didn't stop for either. He put them in the same column as the wells and the burial ground. Unsorted, unexplained, waiting on data before they meant anything. The column was getting long, and he noticed with some interest that the length of it was doing something to his mood that a decade of court had never managed.

The forge took up a good corner of town and the air around it was thick with iron and smoke. The man at the main hearth was short and broad, thick through the neck and shoulders, with forearms corded from a lifetime of hammer work and a heavy dark beard over most of a weathered face. He had the dense build of someone who had picked up a hammer the first day he could lift one and had never once put it down for long.

Bram didn't look up. Emeric didn't make him. He knew what real focus in a craft felt like from the inside and he knew exactly what it cost to be pulled out of it, and he had recently acquired more respect for that than he had any right to.

So he waited, and watched the man work.

The metallurgy was good. Better than good for a town with no money in it. There were a dozen places he could improve it given tools and permission and about four he could improve given a stick and an afternoon, and he catalogued all of them and said none of them, because a man who opens with corrections is a man who leaves without anything.

Six hours went by. Bram spoke without looking up.

"If you're here to be measured for armor, I'll want you to stop holding your stomach in."

"It isn't held in."

"It isn't, sir," Rylan agreed, with the flat respect he would have given actual royalty. "I've watched him eat."

Bram's mouth moved fractionally inside the beard, which Emeric decided to count as a landslide.

"Noble of some kind," Bram said, setting the piece down. "You've been at my back since morning. I worked extra hard hoping you'd get bored. Near burned myself stalling. If it's all the same, I've nothing to sell and I'm not buying. Something needs fixing, bring it here and I'll sort it."

"Straight to it. Good." Emeric let the smile go, enough to signal that the next part wasn't banter. "I'd like you to hear the whole thing before you decide what you think of me."

"Go on, then."

"The priest keeps the parish records. Births and deaths. I want to see them, back as far as they go. I'm looking for the shape of the plague. When it started, who it took, whether there's a pattern in it nobody's had a reason to look for."

Bram's eyebrows did something. "The dead."

"The dead."

"Nobody comes here about the dead. They come about taxes, and they come about levies, and once a man came about a bell."

"The dead are the only thing in this barony nobody's had a reason to lie about." Emeric heard himself say it and knew it was true as it came out. "Every other ledger in Kaldo has somebody's interest in it. The tax roll's been shaved. The grain count's been shaved. A death book gets written by a man with no motive whatsoever, one line at a time, by candlelight, for forty years. It's the only honest document in the building and nobody's ever looked at it sideways."

"I can't walk up to a priest I've never met and ask for his death ledger," he went on. "He's no reason to hand it over and every reason not to. You have. Rylan tells me when you vouch for a man here, people listen."

"And what do you offer me for it?"

"Nothing. I'm not going to insult you with money and I've nothing else worth taking. I haven't earned a thing from you. I got here three days ago and I know exactly what I look like, a title with no track record attached, walking around asking questions." He shrugged. "All I've got is the reason. The kingdom doesn't look twice at towns like this one. Either nobody's reporting what happens here, or somebody's making sure they don't. I want to know which. That starts with knowing what killed who, and when, and I can't get there without those books, and I can't get the books without you."

Bram studied him the way he would study a piece of metal he wasn't yet sure had a flaw in it. Most nobles who wanted something out of a smith didn't ask. They told, and they didn't explain themselves to a commoner in either direction. This one had just done both, at length, standing up, in a hot room.

"I'm not vouching for you," Bram said. "Not yet. I'll walk you to the priest myself and tell him you're worth hearing out. What he does after that is his business, and what this town makes of you after that is yours to earn, not mine to hand over."

"That's more than I came in with."

"It isn't the speech." Bram picked the hammer back up, already half-turned to the hearth. "The speech was fine. Speeches are cheap and I've heard better from worse men. You stood at my back six hours and didn't open your mouth once while I had work in my hands." He set a piece on the anvil. "Nobles don't do that. Don't make me regret the walk."

Behind him, Rylan caught Emeric's eye and gave the smallest nod available to a man in uniform.

Neither of them said much walking out of the forge district. Rylan seemed to work out that Emeric had spent most of what he had left and didn't fill the quiet with any of his own. There was one house left before the light went.

"The healer," Emeric said. "You said this street."

"Imani." Rylan slowed without being asked. "She'll be in. She's always in."

The house had a low door and a smell coming out from behind it that wasn't the town's smell. Vinegar, something bitter under it, herbs hung to dry in a room with no spare room to hang anything. The woman who opened it was somewhere in her middle twenties, holding a strip of boiled linen, and she looked at him the way this town had looked at him for three days.

"Whatever it is, I'm out of it."

"I'm not sick."

"Then you're worse." She did not move out of the doorway. "Sick I can do something about."

"I want to know what killed the ones who died."

That earned a second look, longer than the first, and no invitation.

"The priest keeps a book," she said.

"I'm getting the book. The book only has the ones somebody paid to bury."

He had said it as a deduction and heard, a half second later, that it was also a hole in his own knowledge. He had no idea what happened to a person in this kingdom whose family couldn't pay. None. Twenty-two years of memory and the drawer was empty, because nobody had ever thought it worth telling him.

What he could produce instantly, whole and unasked, was the other end of it. A noble of sufficient standing was encased in mana stone and kept, body and face intact, indefinitely. He knew the sizes. He knew what the good ones cost. He could have named four houses that had commissioned one in his lifetime and told you what the crystal had been cut from.

So that was the shape of what he'd been raised to know. The rich are kept forever in stone. The poor go into the ground somewhere uphill of the drinking water and are not written down at all.

Imani stood a moment with the linen in her hand. Then she stepped back from the door, which was closer to a welcome than anything he'd been offered since he arrived.

Her front room was the only orderly space he had seen in Kaldo. Everything hung where it could be reached one-handed without turning around. That was the tell. The room had been arranged by somebody who had once needed to work faster than she could think.

"How many?" he asked.

"Past nine hundred. I stopped counting the winter it got past what I could carry." She sat down, which she plainly did not do often. "So the number's wrong, and it's wrong low. Start there and you'll be closer than the book gets you."

"You've been doing this how long?"

"Since I was nineteen. It was Mother Sabel's before it was mine and then it wasn't hers anymore." She said it the way people in this town said everything about that year, which was quickly and once.

"What kills them?"

"The ground." Flat. The voice of a woman saying a sentence she has had five years to test. "It comes up off the ground where the water sits. You'll see it in which houses go first, if you stand here long enough. The low ones. The wet ones. The ones out by the burial rows."

She was right about every house and wrong about every reason, and something in Emeric's chest came loose that he would have called relief if he had been honest enough to name it. She had the map. She had had the map for five years. She had a bad name for the thing drawn on it and nobody in the world to correct it with.

"Did anyone ever come?" he asked. "From the capital."

Her hands stopped for the first time.

"I sent word three times the first year. Through the castle, because that's the road it goes down." She picked the linen back up. "I don't know that anyone read it. I know nobody came. The Circle keeps healers, Your Highness. I've never met one. I've met the idea of one."

Through the castle, he thought, and put it somewhere he could find it later.

"And if one came now?"

"Then I'd have somebody to hand the ones I can't keep."

He let that land where it landed, then said the thing that had been bothering him for two days. "There's a guard of mine with a knife wound eight days old. I told him to come to you."

"Big one. Won't sit down." Imani made a sound that was not quite a laugh. "He walked past my door twice today and didn't knock. Send him again if it makes you feel better. He'll come when it goes red and not an hour before, and probably the same for every one of you."

At the door she looked at him properly for the first time.

"You're going to tell me it's the water," she said.

He hadn't said a word about the water.

"I'm going to show you. Not tell you. There's a difference and I'd rather you held me to it."

"Everyone's going to show me something." The door was already half shut. "Bring numbers or don't come back."

The light had gone further than he'd meant to let it. The town settled into itself the way it did before dark, less a place with people in it than a held breath waiting to be let out, and Emeric let the quiet sit, because for the first time since he had arrived it was something he didn't have to solve.

The pull found him on the road up from town, stronger than any pass before it, close enough that he stopped and looked directly at the low wall running along the road's edge.

Nothing. A gap in the stonework, an old drainage cut, shadow doing what shadow does at that hour.

Then Ward stepped out of a side lane thirty feet ahead and stood at the gatehouse as though he had been there since noon. Forearm still wrapped in the same linen. Face giving away precisely nothing.

Emeric stopped for the second time in a minute.

He looked at Ward, and back at the wall, and at Ward again, and the column finally sorted itself. The alley yesterday. The street corner before it. The wall just now. Every one of them with a direction, and every direction pointing at somewhere a man could have been standing if that man could move through a town without being seen.

"You were on the roofline back there," Emeric said. Not a question.

Ward didn't confirm it. He didn't deny it either. "You're inside before full dark. That's the part that matters."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the answer you're getting tonight." Something crossed his face that wasn't quite a smile, closer to the specific satisfaction of a job done clean. "Rest. Tomorrow will want you sharper than today did."

Emeric let it go and turned it over the whole last stretch of road anyway, because he had a mind that would not leave a repeatable pattern alone once it had noticed one.

Not imagination, then. Not exhaustion. Something with a source and a direction, and the source was a man carrying a charged crystal in a bracer, which was a mechanism, which was the thing he had been missing. Whatever the Kinetic Rune did for Ward it was doing considerably more than the fight at the campfire had shown, because six and a half feet of muscle has no business on a rooftop and every business announcing itself when it gets there.

He was pleased with the solve. He was pleased enough that it took him until the gatehouse to notice the residue.

The banquet hall. Ward had been at his shoulder the entire evening, in plain sight, for four hours, and the thing had come once, for about a second, when a stranger passed close behind him.

He turned that over, and found he could account for it, because Ward had shifted position several times that night and any of them could have been the one. It fit. It fit the way a slightly wrong answer fits, snugly, with a little pressure at the edges, and he was tired, and he had a blacksmith and a healer and a walk to a priest in the morning, and the file went into the drawer marked solved.

Up in his room he pulled the paper across, and the ink, and the bad quill the steward had dug out for him, and sat looking at a blank page with the particular appetite of a man who has finally been handed a problem worth the tools.

It was the most alive he had felt since a diner counter and a napkin, and he was some way into enjoying it before he heard Declan shift his weight in the corridor, two doors down, outside a room with a man in it who knew who had paid him.

Tomorrow, then. Probably.
