Chapter 4 - Night Five

The carriage was waiting in the lower yard at dawn, and it had been chosen to say nothing. It could say nothing all it liked. His eyes would say the rest to anyone who looked at him, so he'd keep his hood up at the inns.

It was large and plain, dark wood with no crest on the doors and no color on the wheels, the kind of carriage a wool merchant might hire to move his family. Emeric walked around it once while the trunks went up, because walking around it was something to do. Then he crouched by the rear wheel and looked underneath, and stayed there long enough that a groom asked if he'd dropped something.

Four black stones sat where the springs should have been. Each was the size of a loaf, set into an iron cradle at a corner of the frame, flecked through with tiny points of light like grit in coal. Their faces were carved in a lattice so dense it looked like cloth. When the porters heaved a trunk onto the roof, the carriage dipped a finger's width and came back level, without a creak.

Gravity stones. He'd known they existed in the way he knew the capital had sewers. He had never once in fourteen years thought to look at one.

He stayed crouched there with the cold coming up through his boots, and something stirred in him that he hadn't felt since he was a boy. It took him a moment to name it. Curiosity. Plain and unreasonable, wanting to know how a thing worked for no better reason than that it worked.

"Prince Emeric." Ward, above him. "The horses."

He stood up and dusted his knees.

There were six riders. Ward led. Behind him rode three men Emeric knew from years of half-watching the training yard: Rylan, quick to laugh; Declan, who talked to his horse; Osric, who talked to everyone else. The other two were strangers. One was a broad, easy man called Petram. The other was lean and quiet and gave his name as Garrin, and then gave nothing else. Extra men, Emeric assumed. His father's additions. He nodded to them and stopped thinking about them.



Randillia City climbed its hill in rings, each wall higher than the last, with the castle at the top and the river curling round the outer works like an arm around a shoulder. The carriage went down through all of it, ring by ring, through gates whose guards came to attention for a plain carriage because somebody had told them to.

The streets didn't smell. He noticed that first, because Marcus would have expected a medieval city to stink and this one smelled of bread and wet stone and a little of horses. Utility mages saw to the water and the waste. It was how cities worked, if you were the capital and could pay for it.

High over the third ring, as they passed below, he could see the mage tower, and beside it the Circle's gallery hanging in the air with nothing under it at all.

Armand had taken him up there once, when he was small. He remembered the room under the gallery better than the gallery itself: ten men sitting in a ring, each holding a crystal the size of a melon in both hands, and ten more along the wall waiting to take their place. They held it up all day in turns. Twenty men a day, every day. He'd asked why there wasn't a staircase, and Armand had said, quite seriously, that the Circle answered to no staircase.

Small craft hung over the inner rings too, the sky-boats the great houses used, and every one of them had a man aboard feeding it, hour after hour.

The carriage turned a corner, and he pictured the stones under his own feet, and saw the difference.

The stones under the carriage had been told once what to do. They'd keep doing it for anyone until their charge ran down, and then a mage would top them up for a fee and they'd go on again. That was a machine. The gallery was something else. It worked only while a man poured himself into it, and it emptied him while it did. That was a job.

The kingdom had built its wonders as jobs. That was why none of them ever left the capital.

He didn't know what to do with that yet. He knew it was important, the way he'd known the stack-up on the drawing was important before he'd worked out why.

The crowds thinned toward the outer gate, and for the first time he looked at them with Marcus's eyes instead of Emeric's. Faces of every shade from near-black to freckled white. Broad noses and narrow ones, hair straight and tight-curled and every grade between, a porter with narrow, heavy-lidded eyes arguing with a carter whose cheekbones belonged somewhere else entirely. Every kind of person Earth had, and a few combinations Earth didn't.

And nobody else. No pointed ears. No fur, no scales, no one too tall or too small to be a man. A world with magic in it, and only people.

He'd half expected otherwise, from books. He filed it with something like relief and let it go.

The carriage passed under the last gate. He leaned back against the seat.

"Goodbye, sweet Randillia City," he said, to nobody, and Ward, riding by the window, didn't turn his head.



On the second day they ranked the gifts.

It started because Emeric was bored, and Ward was riding close to the window, and the road was long and empty.

"The coat," Ward said, when asked. "The coat was good."

"Better than fifty Gold?"

"Gold doesn't keep the rain off."

"The soldiers?"

Ward considered the wooden soldiers. "Well made."

Emeric laughed, and Ward's mouth moved the small amount it ever moved.

"Am I the worst prince you've guarded?"

"You are not the worst prince I have guarded."

"How many have you guarded?"

"One."

Later, when the road had climbed out of the river valley and the farms were getting smaller, Ward mentioned without being asked that he had requested this posting. It had been granted the same hour.

Emeric turned that over for a mile. A request from a knight to follow a disgraced prince into exile, approved within the hour, the day after a sentence nobody had known was coming. Either someone with authority had been waiting for Ward to ask, or someone had arranged for him to ask. Both meant there was a hand in it above Ward's.

He didn't ask which. Ward was riding beside him, and the question would still be there tomorrow.

Past the last of the outer farms the road dipped through a hollow where spring rain had torn it out, and there were men working in the gap.

There were perhaps twenty of them, cutting the bank back with mattocks and hauling stone in hods. They wore leg irons. Two guards with short spears sat on a cart at the top of the hollow, eating. The men in irons didn't look up as the carriage passed.

Emeric's stomach turned over.

He'd been waiting for this since the hall, since the moment he understood where he was. A king, a court, swords and banners and a peasantry, and he knew from every history he had ever read what usually stood underneath all that, doing the work.

"Ward." He kept his voice level. "Who are they?"

Ward glanced down at the hollow as though it were weather. "Crown prisoners. Thieves, mostly. Debtors who ran."

"Whose are they?"

"The crown's, for the length of their sentence." Ward looked at him. "This land's lord won't pay a mage to shape the road. So he asks for convicts, and the crown lends them. When their time's done they walk."

"Nobody owns them."

Ward frowned faintly, as though the question had come from a long way off. "Nobody owns anybody, Your Highness."

Emeric sat back.

He worked at it for the next mile, because working at it was easier than looking back. Magic did a great deal of the work here. It held up galleries and carried boats and kept the capital's water clean. If enough of the work that was hardest on bodies could be bought from mages, perhaps nobody had ever needed to buy the bodies themselves. Perhaps the need had never come, so the thing had never grown.

It was a guess. He knew it was a guess. It held together better than he'd feared, and it didn't make the men in the hollow look any less like what they were.



On the fifth night they camped at the edge of a long wood, with the fire built up high because the cold came in fast once the sun went.

Emeric sat on a saddle with his back to the carriage wheel and told Ward the truth.

He kept the whisper back; that was his father's, and it stayed where it was. He told him the rest, in order. The rooms moved twice in a week. The food taster. The four attempts. His father on the end of the bed, and the game.

And the act itself, which he'd spent fourteen years perfecting and which Ward had watched every day of. He told him about the ambassador from Velmoor, the year he was fifteen: how he'd asked her, in front of forty people, whether it was true her country's ships were towed by fish. How he'd spent two days choosing those words so the story would travel, and how it had travelled further than he'd hoped.

Ward listened. He was oiling his blade, as he did most nights, long slow strokes from guard to point.

"Somewhere it stopped being an act," Emeric said. "By nineteen I hadn't opened a book since I was twelve. I couldn't tell you when it went."

He looked across the fire.

"When did it stop, Ward?"

Ward's hand stopped on the blade.

Then he did something Emeric had never seen him do in fifteen years. He set the sword down on the cloth beside him, out of reach, where it was no use to anyone.

"Your fourteenth summer," Ward said. "A tutor brought you a book on crystal lattices. A good one. You'd asked for it, the year before. You said you'd look at it later." He looked at the fire. "I carried it up to your rooms myself. It sat there. You didn't."

Emeric said nothing.

"I knew then." Ward picked up the cloth and folded it once, carefully. "I was given you to keep alive. I was not given the rest of you to keep."

The fire cracked. Somewhere back in the trees an owl tried a note and gave up.

After a while the others drifted in from the horses and the talk went easier. Osric told a long story about a bandit camp outside Grayrock that got bigger every time he told it, and Declan said it had been eleven men and a goat. Declan had missed his brother's wedding for this posting. His brother had written him four lines about it, and none of them were a complaint, which Declan said was worse.

Rylan talked about Kaldo. He'd grown up there. When he was a boy the harbor had real coast trade in it, he said, ships from down the coast and up the river, until the war took the men, and a couple of years after that the plague took most of what was left. He had a wife there, Nessa, and a son, Finn, who was six, and a daughter, Wren, who was four and had opinions. He hadn't seen any of them in four months.

Petram, who had said very little in five days, said his cousin lived in Kaldo. Corliss. In the same quarter as Rylan's family, as it turned out, and the two of them worked out the streets between them, which lane, which corner, which house had the blue door, while the fire burned down.

Emeric listened, and slowly realized he was comfortable. Nobody at this fire worked for him in any way that mattered tonight. Nobody was watching him for a story to carry back. He tried to remember the last time that had been true, and couldn't, and named the feeling anyway.

At the edge of the trees, something brushed past that sense again, the one he had no word for. Faint and cold, there and gone.

He looked. Dark trunks, darker spaces between them. An animal, he decided. Something with eyes, watching the fire. He let it go.

Petram had a kettle on by then, from the carriage stores. Tea, he said, for the cold.



It came on as a heaviness first.

Emeric had drunk half his cup when the fire went a little further away than it had been. His hands were slow. Across from him, Declan had stopped talking in the middle of a sentence about his horse and was staring at nothing. Osric's cup was tipping in his fingers. Rylan was already lying down, as if he'd simply decided to.

Petram hadn't drunk. Garrin hadn't drunk. They were both on their feet.

And in the part of his mind that had come with the trial, something cold and clinical lined up what was happening and named it: a heavy sedative, fast onset, in a dose meant to put a man under and keep him there. His tongue was thick. He had perhaps a few seconds left in which it would work.

"Ward," he said. "Poison."

Ward was already turning.

Petram had come round the fire with a knife low in his hand, and he went for Ward's back, and Ward turned into it instead of away. The blade went through the leather of his bracer and into the forearm under it. Ward made no sound at all. He closed his other hand on Petram's wrist, stepped in, and put him on the ground hard enough that the breath went out of him in one long noise. Then Ward knelt on him.

"Alive," Ward said. His voice hadn't changed. "A dead man tells nothing."

Garrin was gone. Emeric had seen him go: one moment by the horses, the next a shape moving fast into the trees, and then not even that.

Ward didn't follow. He stayed where he was, knee on Petram's back, blood running down his wrist into the dirt, and watched the treeline. Later he would give his reasons, and they were good ones. The rune on his bracer needed a clean line to the thing it was meant to push against, and the woods gave him none. Four men lay helpless at the fire, and one living traitor was under his knee. You don't leave four for one.

There was a second reason. Emeric saw it in Ward's face in the firelight, and Ward never said it.

The heaviness was still coming. It should have closed over Emeric's head by now. Declan was out. Osric was out. Instead something in him that had always run a little hotter than it should was burning. He could feel it, a slow heat moving outward from the center of him, and the fog in his head thinned as it went. He didn't understand it. He didn't try to.

He got onto his knees. Then he crawled to Declan and rolled him onto his side, and pulled his top knee forward so he'd stay there, and tilted his head back so his airway was open. He did the same for Osric. He did it for Rylan. His hands knew the positions, and they shook only a little.

"Still breathing," he told Ward. "All three. They'll sleep it off if they don't choke."

Ward looked at him for a long time. "You drank it."

"Half."

"You should be lying down with them."

"I know."

Ward didn't answer that. Then, quietly, almost to himself, "A mage for a mother and a knight for a father." He turned back to Petram before Emeric could ask what he meant.



Later, with Petram bound hand and foot by the carriage wheel and Ward's arm wrapped in a strip of shirt, Emeric sat by the fire and took the night apart.

Petram had suggested this campsite. Petram had brewed the tea. And Petram, over five days of easy talk, had drawn out of Rylan the names of his wife and his children, their ages, and the street they lived on, so naturally that nobody had noticed it being done.

He'd given his cousin's name, too. A real cousin, Corliss, in a real street. Emeric kept coming back to that piece. A careful man lies about everything. A careful man gives a true name like that only if he expects no one at this fire to live long enough to repeat it.

And Emeric had sat across from him for five days and called him his father's man.

He'd been so sure. He'd spent two days taking his father's plan apart, the exile, the forest, the code, and he'd been so pleased with how well it fit that he'd never looked twice at the two strangers riding behind his carriage.

There was another reading, past that one. He could feel the edge of it, the one that began with who had drawn up the roster of his escort. He stopped there.

Late was worse than early. It was infinitely better than never.

He reached into the trunk behind him, by feel, and found Armand's notebook where he'd packed it. The fire was low but still giving light. He opened the book on his knee, turned to the first page of thirty years of an old man's careful handwriting, and began to read.

Across the fire, Ward watched him do it, and didn't say a word.

Three men asleep, one bound, one bleeding. Seven days of road left.
