Chapter 4 - The Vouch

He wasn't going to walk into the town square and explain why. He'd settled that before he'd finished the walk.

He didn't need anyone here to believe in things too small to see. He needed the water moved and the outcome to speak for itself. Nothing he said out loud was going to convince a single person in this town of anything. A prediction that came true might. So would a set of numbers pulled out of records they already trusted, which meant their own.

That was the plan in theory. In practice he was missing two things. Somebody here who would talk to him honestly, and a way into records nobody was going to hand a prince just because he asked nicely.

"Who keeps the death records?" he asked Rylan on the walk back. "Baptisms, burials, whatever the parish logs."

"Priest keeps the parish book. Has for years." Rylan said it like it was obvious, then caught up to why he'd been asked. "You're not thinking of asking him for it."

"Not yet. I'm thinking about who'd vouch for me first, so that asking him doesn't go the way everything else has gone since I got here."

What that meant in practice was a name this town trusted more than it would ever trust his title. Someone whose word carried because they had earned it across years instead of inheriting it.

"Bram," Rylan said, before Emeric had finished the thought. "Blacksmith. People bring him more than horseshoes, if you understand me. He's fair all around, damn near incorruptible, and he's been here longer than the war took anybody. If he says you're worth listening to, some of them will listen." A pause. "If he says you're not, it won't matter what you actually do."

That was either the best lead he was going to get or the fastest way to lose this before it started.

The walk cut past the edge of town where the buildings thinned into what should have been farmland and mostly wasn't. Fields sat fallow in long stretches, the soil gone hard and pale where nothing had been turned in a while, weeds doing better than any crop would have. Further on, a mill wheel sat dead still over water with more than enough current left in it to turn the thing.

He didn't stop to look closer at either. He added them to the same column as the wells and the burial ground. Unsorted, unexplained, waiting on more data before they meant anything.

The forge took up a decent corner of town, and the air around it was thick with iron and smoke and sweat. At the main hearth stood a man built like a dwarf out of an old story, though there was nothing inhuman about him. Bram was short and broad, thick through the neck, with heavy shoulders and arms that seemed slightly too large for the rest of him. His hands were wide and scarred, his forearms corded from years of hammer work, and a dark heavy beard covered most of a weathered face.

He had the dense build of a man who picked up a hammer the moment he was strong enough to swing one and never put it back down.

He never looked up, and Emeric didn't interrupt him. He knew what real focus in a craft felt like from the inside, and he knew exactly how much it stung to be dragged out of it.

So he waited, and watched the man work through the end of the day.

The metallurgy was good. Emeric could still see a dozen places he'd change it given the tools and the go-ahead, and every one of those places was a thing Bram would have found himself in a year if anyone had ever handed him a reason to look.

The hammer stopped somewhere in the last of the light. Bram set it down, looked at him for the first time all day, and said, "You've been standing there since morning."

"I have."

"I worked an extra two hours hoping you'd get bored." He wiped his hands on the apron. "Guessing you're a noble of some kind. I don't have anything to sell and I'm not buying. Something needs fixing, bring it here and I'll sort it. Otherwise say your piece, because I'd like my supper."

"Straight to it, then." Emeric let whatever was left of the smile go, enough to signal that the next part was real. "I've got a request. I could tell you I'm not like other nobles and that you should trust me, but we both know talk is cheap and you've had cheap talk before. I'm not here for your trust today. I'm here because I need something and you're the only person in this town who can get it."

"The priest keeps the parish records. Births and deaths both. I want to see them, going back as far as they go. I'm trying to find the shape of what's been killing people here. When it started, who it took, whether there's a pattern nobody's had reason to look for."

"I can't walk up to a priest I've never met and ask for his death ledger. He's got no reason to hand it over and every reason not to. You do."

"And why would I."

"Because the kingdom doesn't look twice at towns like this one, and either nobody's reporting what's happening here or somebody's making sure they don't. I'd like to know which. That starts with knowing what actually killed who, and when." He shrugged. "I'm not going to offer you money. I don't think you'd take it, and if you did I'd have learned something about you I didn't want to know."

Bram studied him the way he'd study a piece of metal he wasn't sure had a flaw in it yet.

Most nobles who wanted something out of a smith didn't ask. They told. And they didn't explain themselves to a commoner either way. This one had just spent two minutes doing both, which was either a sign of something or a better-than-usual performance of one.

"I'm not vouching for you," Bram said finally. "Not yet. I'll walk you to the priest myself and tell him you're worth hearing out. What he does after that is his business, and what this town thinks of you after that is yours to earn, not mine to hand you." He picked the hammer back up, already half-turned to the forge. "Don't make me regret the walk."

Behind Bram's back, Rylan caught Emeric's eye and gave the smallest possible nod.

Neither of them said much walking out of the forge district. Rylan seemed to sense that Emeric had spent most of what he had for the day and didn't try to fill the space. There was one more house before the light went.

"The healer," Emeric said. "You said this street."

"Imani." Rylan slowed without being told to. "She'll be in. She's always in."

The house had a low door and a smell coming out from behind it that wasn't the town's smell. Vinegar, something bitter underneath it, herbs hung to dry in a room with no spare space to hang anything. The woman who opened it was somewhere in her mid twenties and holding a strip of boiled linen, and she looked at him the way this town had been looking at him for two days.

"Whatever it is, I'm out of it," she said.

"I'm not sick."

"Then you're worse." She didn't move out of the doorway. "Sick I can do something about."

"I want to know what killed the ones who died."

That got him a second look, longer than the first, and no invitation.

"The priest keeps a book," she said.

"I'm getting the book. The book only has the ones somebody paid to bury."

He knew that the way he now knew most things, arriving whole and unasked. Down here a burial was a fee, and a fee was a thing a family could fail to pay. Up where he'd come from, nobles with the means went into mana stone instead of ground, sealed and preserved and visitable, which he had never once thought about as a boy and had a very hard time not thinking about now. His mother was in one somewhere. That fact had been sitting in his head for weeks with no particular weight on it until this doorway put a price beside it.

Imani stood a moment with the linen in her hand. Then she stepped back from the door, which was nearer to a welcome than anything he'd been offered since he arrived.

Her front room was the only orderly space he had seen in Kaldo. Everything hung where it could be reached one-handed without turning around. That was the tell. The room had been arranged by somebody who once needed to work faster than she could think.

"How many?" he asked.

"Past nine hundred. I stopped counting the winter it got past what I could carry." She sat down, which she clearly did not do often. "So the number's wrong, and it's wrong low. Start there and you'll be closer than the book will get you."

"What kills them?"

"The ground." Flat, the way a person says a sentence they have had five years to test. "It comes up off the ground where the water sits. You'll see it in which houses go first, if you stand here long enough. Low ones. Wet ones. The ones out by the burial rows."

She was right about every house and wrong about every reason, and something in Emeric's chest went loose that he would have called relief if he'd been honest enough to name it. She had the map. She'd had it for five years. She just had a bad name for the thing drawn on it.

"Did anyone ever come?" he asked. "From the capital."

Her hands stopped for the first time.

"I sent word three times the first year. Through the castle, because that's the road it goes down." She picked the linen back up. "I don't know that anyone read it. I know nobody came. The Circle keeps healers, Your Highness. I've never met one. I've met the idea of one."

"And if one came now?"

"Then I'd have somebody to hand the ones I can't keep."

He let that sit where it landed, then said the thing that had been bothering him for a day and a half. "There's a guard of mine carrying a knife wound six days old. I told him to come to you."

"Big one. Won't sit down." Imani made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh. "He walked past my door twice today and didn't knock. Send him again if it makes you feel better. He'll come when it goes red and not an hour sooner, and that's most of them."

At the door she looked at him properly for the first time.

"You're going to tell me it's the water," she said.

He hadn't said a word about the water.

"I'm going to show you," Emeric said. "Not tell you. There's a difference and I'd rather you held me to it."

"Everyone's going to show me something." The door was already half shut. "Bring numbers or don't come back."

The light had gone further than he meant to let it. The town was settling into itself the way it did before dark, less a place with people in it than a held breath waiting to be let out. Emeric let the quiet sit. It was, for the first time since he'd arrived, something he didn't have to solve.

The pull found him one last time on the road up from town, stronger than any of the earlier passes, close enough that he actually turned and looked at the wall along the road's edge. Nothing. A gap in the stonework, an old drainage cut, shadow doing what shadow does at that hour.

Then Ward stepped out of a side alley thirty feet ahead of them and waited at the gatehouse like he had been standing there all afternoon. Forearm still wrapped. Expression giving away nothing at all.

Emeric stopped walking. He looked at Ward, then back at the wall, then at Ward again, and three data points went into the same column for the first time.

"You were on the roofline back there," he said. Not a question.

Ward didn't confirm it. He didn't deny it either. "You're back at the castle before full dark. That's the part that matters."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the answer you're getting tonight." Something crossed his face, not quite a smile, closer to the specific satisfaction of a job done clean. "Rest, Your Highness. Tomorrow's going to want you sharper than today did."

Emeric let it go and kept turning it over the whole last stretch of road anyway. Not imagination. Not exhaustion. Something with a source and a direction, tracking a man who could apparently move through a town without being seen or heard, which six and a half feet of muscle had no business being able to do. Whatever that bracer was doing for him, it was doing more than the fight at the campfire had shown.

He walked the last of it pleased with himself, the way you are when a stubborn set of readings finally resolves into one clean cause, and he tidied the older entries into the same column on the way up. The alley this afternoon. The road. The treeline at the camp.

Ward had been at the camp, after all. Sitting six feet from him, working a blade.

It was well past dark by the time it occurred to him that this made the treeline reading fit worse rather than better, and by then he was already asleep.
