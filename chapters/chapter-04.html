Chapter 4 - Gifts

They held it in the same hall.

He assumed at first that this was scheduling rather than cruelty, a room being a room and a castle having only so many of them. Then he watched the pages set the tables along the same wall where the nobles had stood ringed the night before, and he reconsidered, and settled on the answer that it was both, and that the man who chose it had not needed to think about it either way.

Two hundred and eleven people came to say goodbye to him. He counted. He did not decide to count. That reflex had come with the rest of Marcus and it had not once asked permission for anything.

Two of them were related to him.

He worked out the arithmetic of that while a herald was still getting through his titles, and then he put it down, because he had decided in the corridor on the way here that tonight was a job and not an ordeal, and he intended to do the job.

The job was this. Every man in that line was going to walk up to him, hand him an object, and take away an impression, and the impression was the only thing on the table that Emeric controlled. So he decided, before a single hand was shaken, who got the full wastrel and who got a fraction less of it, and then he adjusted per person, live, the way you trim a fit at the bench.

Some of them got the whole thing. Slack mouth, half a beat late on every response, eyes going to the wine. Some of them got a version with the edges filed down, because there is such a thing as overselling and a man who is stupid in exactly the same way to two hundred people is performing.

He gave Aldane the full idiot and enjoyed it more than he had enjoyed anything all day.

He gave Varek almost nothing, because a soldier reads posture before he reads a face and there was no version of the act that survived a man who had spent thirty years watching how people stand.

It was, he thought, in the gap between two barons whose names he already had, an information problem rather than a humiliation. That reframing held for most of the evening. It did not hold all the way to the doors.

Waylon did not come.

A page produced the answer before Emeric had finished asking the question, which was the first thing wrong with it, and produced it with the particular brightness servants use when they have been given a line in advance and are pleased to have an occasion for it. Hunting. Gone since yesterday morning.

The alibi was unnecessary and that was what stung.

Waylon had been three the year their mother was killed and four the summer his brother became a drunk, and nobody had explained either one to him. Nobody sits a four-year-old down and tells him that the brother who used to build things with him on the floor has been given a part to play. So Waylon had drawn the only conclusion available to a child, which was that he was not worth staying for, and had spent most of his adolescence proving he no longer cared, and that was precisely how Emeric knew he still did.

There had never been a single moment in fifteen years when he could have said one word about it.

It came due at intervals. It came due now, in a receiving line, with a viscount approaching. He had gotten very good at paying it without moving his face.

Maeve did not come either, and for Maeve there was no prepared answer at all.

He asked twice. The first time he got a bow. The second time he got a different servant, which is not a coincidence in a house this size, it is a procedure.

Absence was not her. She had taught him to swim by pushing him into a river. She had lied to their father on his behalf so often that she had once done it by reflex about something he had not actually done. If she had decided he was finished she would have come and told him so to his face, at length, with examples, and then made him repeat them back.

Two readings. Either she had stopped caring in a way that broke pattern hard enough to be its own event, or somebody had made certain she was not in this room.

He could not tell them apart. He made himself stop trying, failed, and picked at it through most of the gift line.

The queens made three.

Sabina, the first queen, was in the palace and did not come down, and not one person in two hundred found it remarkable. Rowena, the second, mother to Maeve and Zale, had gone home six years ago, south down the coast to Emberfell. She had come to Randillia as a treaty and left when the treaty stopped needing her in a room.

There had been something about a brother. Emeric had the shape of it and not the middle, a death and then a queen of Randillia sitting a foreign seat, and the court had turned it over for a week and then stopped, because she was gone and it was not their coast. He had been sixteen. He had filed it and never once gone back for it.

Amara, the third, was dead, and nobody said her name either.

Three queens and three kinds of absence, and no page had been briefed with a bright little answer for any of them, because nobody had thought to ask.

He stood in the middle of that pattern shaking hands and noticed, distinctly, that he was standing in it.

His father gave him fifty gold Rands in a pouch and a chest of uncut mana crystals.

The pouch was pressed into his hands with more formality than warmth, which the room read correctly as a king discharging an obligation and incorrectly as the whole of it. The crystals were unremarkable stock, varied elements, the sort of thing an affluent tutor hands a promising student, and the room read a father who had never quite given up on the boy who once loved crystals, and made the small approving noise that rooms make.

Emeric read a delivery mechanism. No crest. No seal. Nothing entered anywhere. A manasmith will buy them in any town in the kingdom and ask nothing.

He said thank you at exactly the volume the room required.

Corbin took the longest, and Emeric had the shape of it ready before his brother reached him, because he had worked the shape out years ago and it had never once needed revising.

He had spent an embarrassing amount of his adolescence looking for the reason his brother did not like him. He had stopped, eventually, not because he found it but because the shape turned out to be more informative than the cause.

Corbin had never been cruel to him in private. Not once. Not a word in a corridor, not a look on a stairwell, nothing in fifteen years that happened where there was nobody to see it. What there had been instead was a steady rhythm of small precise humiliations, every one of them performed in front of an audience of at least three.

That is not hatred. Hatred leaks. Hatred gets you in a corner when nobody is looking.

What Corbin did was maintenance. Regular, scheduled, public, the way a man paints a fence before it rots. He was not wounding his brother. He was reminding a room where his brother stood, at intervals, so that the room never had to work it out on its own and never had occasion to wonder whether it had been wrong.

It was extremely good statecraft and Emeric wished that understanding it made him feel better about it.

The gift was a set of seven Hollow Crystals. Dull grey, carved over every face in a fine repeating lattice, and worthless, because nobody in recorded history had ever made one do anything at all.

"Something to bring back the old passion." Corbin set them down with both hands, unhurried. "Unchanging. Just like you. Keep staying true to you, little brother."

Emeric took the word apart out of old habit, the way he would once have taken apart a clause that compiled clean and ran wrong.

Unchanging is not a compliment for a gift. It is a diagnosis. His brother had not handed him a hobby. He had handed him a sentence, wrapped warm enough that two hundred people heard the wrong one.

Then the second line, softer, still carrying.

"Your mother gave you one of these once. Thought it might mean something, you having some others."

His hand went to his pocket before he told it to.

It closed on a smooth grey stone he had pulled out of the back of a drawer the night before for no reason he had been able to produce when he asked himself for one.

Not a rock a boy had liked. A Hollow Crystal his mother had put in his hands, kept through fifteen years of not remembering why, carried out of a room he was being thrown out of because his fingers had declined to let go of it.

Something in his chest went quiet and heavy. No fear in it and nothing sharp, just the specific ache of a grief filed somewhere he could not get to on purpose.

Corbin was already moving to the next pleasantry.

Every word of it had been delivered at carrying volume. Emeric noted that, and did not let it help.

Aldane came next, old blood, from a house said to be as old as the main Randillia line, and handed him a set of carved wooden soldiers.

The kind you give a boy playing war on a bedroom floor. The kind you give a boy half his age.

He offered them plainly, with a smirk so small that most of the room would have missed it, and he did not bother dressing the insult as anything else, because he did not consider Emeric worth the effort of a decent lie.

Emeric found that he respected it more than he had expected to. There was no cost in it for Aldane and no craft either, and something about being insulted by a man who could not be troubled to do it well was almost restful after Corbin.

He made a note of the name.

He was mildly interested to discover that the note felt less like wounded pride than like bookkeeping. It went into the same place the two men by the second pillar had gone, and the woman in blue, and it went in cleanly and without heat, and it stayed there.

Varek gave him a coat.

Heavy, insulated, collar reinforced, stitched the way gear gets stitched for a man who will be in it for months rather than for a season at court. There was no speech. There was a short nod.

"You'll need this more than a crystal where you're going, Your Highness."

It was the first gift all evening that assumed he would be actually cold, in an actual place, with actual seasons.

Armand held out a slim leather-bound notebook with both hands, as though it might bruise.

His own notes. Crystal theory, thirty years of it, in a hand that had gotten smaller and worse as it went. He said he had never finished teaching him properly and that perhaps Emeric would get further with it than he had.

He was not quite meeting his eyes.

It was not an apology. It sat close enough to one that Emeric did not know where to put it, so he thanked the man and let the line move before either of them had to stand in the space that neither of them was going to fill.

Augustine came with nothing in his hands, which from a man of the church is its own kind of statement.

He was old and did not look it, and he moved through the room like a man who had watched a great many princes come and go and fully expected to watch a great many more. He stopped, and considered Emeric the way a physician considers a case already decided, and offered a blessing in place of a gift.

"May the Light find you where you are going, and make of you something better than you have managed to be."

The room heard gentleness.

Emeric took it apart on the spot. Where you are going is not comfort. It is placement, a man being filed somewhere out of sight and told it is providence. Better than you have managed to be is not encouragement. It is assessment, a verdict on the whole of him handed across as prayer, the failure named first so that the grace could be made to sound like generosity.

Augustine did not smile. He had no need to. He had reduced a prince to a soul in want of correction and called it mercy, and two hundred people had heard only the mercy.

Emeric registered, and registered nothing else, that a churchman he barely knew had taken the trouble to come and be disappointed in him.

The rest of them were not worth recording, and he did not record them, and then he was at the doors and the actual subject of the evening arrived all at once.

Nobody had said her name.

Two hundred and eleven people, four hours, a receiving line. Not one joke. Not one barb. Not a single one of the sly little condolences that the capital specializes in and takes real pride in. Aldane had handed him toys rather than say it. Augustine had blessed him rather than say it.

He had come down those stairs braced to spend the evening being reminded, and the subject had gone around him all night the way water goes around a stone, and it took him until the doorway to work out why that bothered him.

A scandal everyone is willing to discuss is a scandal.

A scandal that nobody will say out loud in a room of two hundred is something else, and he did not have a word for the something else.

Then the colder one, arriving behind it.

Nobody had whispered to him all night. Two hundred mouths, every one of them pitched to carry.

Which meant that by the only rule he had, he had not heard a true sentence since his father's office.

Crossing toward the doors a man passed close behind him, near enough that the small hairs on his neck stood up without being asked to. He turned. There were backs and shoulders and a page with a tray, and nothing at all.

Something had brushed the edge of a sense he did not have a name for, and had been gone before he could put a wall around it.

He put it down to the wine and the nerves and the airless dread of a room full of people who had quietly agreed he was finished. It sat beside nothing and belonged to no column, and a single stray number with nothing to enter it against is a number a careful man sweeps off the page.
