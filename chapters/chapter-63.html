Chapter 63 - The Most Lawful Man in the Court

The city had not changed and Emeric had, and the second of those was the only interesting one.

He came in through the outer works at midday on the eleventh day with mud to the knee and a satchel under his coat, and nobody at any gate asked him anything, because a man on a tired horse with one servant is not a thing a gate is for. He had come up this road once before in a carriage with four outriders, six years ago, to put a baron in front of a magistrate. He had been twenty-three and had believed that closing a case closed something.

He took a room at an ordinary inn in the third ring and sent a note to the judiciary asking for an hour, and did not say what it was about, and did not use his title, and put his name at the bottom.

The answer came back the same evening, in a clerk's hand. Tomorrow. First hour. Not longer than an hour.

Keller's room was the same room or one indistinguishable from it, and Emeric could not afterward decide which, and decided it did not matter.

A plain table. A window at the wrong height. Two chairs. No gallery, because there was nowhere to put one. The stack of paper squared at his right hand with the edges true.

He was six years older by the calendar, and Emeric could not find a year of it on him. The grey had stayed at the temples. The jaw had not gone soft. He had the same flat stillness, and he did not stand, and it was not an insult then either.

The capital kept its people. Everybody who lived in it said so, and put it down to the physicians and the markets and the good water, and to not spending a life behind a plough. Emeric had spent six years watching Kaldo men weather on Kaldo work, and was inclined to believe them.

"Sit down," he said. "Before you begin. You brought me a good case once. That was six years ago and it is spent. It does not purchase a second hearing and it does not purchase the benefit of the doubt on anything you are about to say. If what you have is thin I will tell you it is thin and you will have ridden eleven days for a sentence."

"Understood."

"Then you have until the hour is finished."

Emeric had spent four of the eleven days on the road building the argument and the other seven taking it apart, and what he laid out on that table was the cleanest piece of thinking he had ever done.

He did not begin with Wayford. He did not begin with the dead rider. He began with the only thing in the satchel that was not about Kaldo, which was the behaviour of roads.

"A road that loses letters loses them at random," he said. "That is what loss is. Water gets into a bag. A rider drowns at a ford. A bag goes into the wrong pile at a crossing and is found in a year or is not found. All of that happens and it happens constantly and it is the ordinary friction of a kingdom that moves paper on horses."

He put the first sheet down.

"Random loss has three properties and every one of them can be checked. First, it takes everybody's post. A ford does not know whose bag it is. Second, it takes it in proportion. A route that carries four hundred items and loses four loses roughly one of mine for every hundred of somebody else's. Third, it does not care what is in the letter, because a road cannot read."

Second sheet. Ferra's two columns.

"In two years this road has lost twenty-one items out of my barony. In the same two years and out of the same gate it has lost nothing at all belonging to a merchant who moves four times the paper I do. It has lost nothing belonging to my creditor's resident factor, who writes weekly and is answered weekly. It has lost none of the Grayrock schedules, none of the Milford correspondence, and none of the Duvrenne letters."

"You are telling me it takes yours."

"No. That's the part that matters." Emeric turned the sheet. "It doesn't take mine. It takes the crown's. Every item on that list was addressed to the crown or to an office of the crown, and every item not on it was addressed to somebody else, and there is no item anywhere in four years that breaks the rule in either direction. My tallow contracts go through. My petitions do not. The road cannot read, and something in the road is reading."

Third sheet.

"And when I tested it, I tested it dully, because I knew what you would say about a clever test. Three ordinary dispatches, four months apart, each one paired with something that went by a route the crown post does not touch. All three crown copies died. All three pairs arrived, and I have the confirmations with dates, and I have waypoint receipts attesting that each satchel existed and was moving north on the day it was supposed to be moving north, taken at a waypoint that does not belong to the crown post. Every one of those receipts is south of the crossing at Wayford, and the crossing at Wayford is the single point on six hundred kilometres where everything I send to my king is out of a bag and on a table in a man's hands."

He put his own hand flat on the three sheets.

"Loss with a selection rule in it is not loss," he said. "It is a hand."

Keller read all of it. He read it the way he had read the Wexler accounts, which was slowly and twice, and he asked four questions in the course of it, all of them about the waypoint receipts and none of them about anything else.

Then he put the sheets back into the order he had found them in and squared the stack.

"That is persuasive," he said. "It is more than persuasive. If you asked me as a man what I think is happening on that road, I would tell you that I think somebody at that crossing is taking your letters to the king out of the bag, and that he has been doing it for two years, and that he is being paid for it."

"Then."

"I will not touch it."

Emeric had been ready for a great many responses and had not been ready for the speed of that one.

"You just said you believe it."

"I said I am persuaded. Those are different faculties and I keep them apart on purpose, because the day I stop, I become a man who convicts people he has formed an impression of." Keller's voice did not change at all. "What have you brought me? A pattern of absence. A set of things that did not happen. Twenty-one letters that nobody at this end can say were ever received, because nobody at this end received them, and nobody at your end can say were ever delivered, because nobody at your end delivered them."

"The receipts prove they existed."

"They prove a satchel of a stated weight with a stated seal was in a man's hands south of Wayford. They do not prove what was in it, and they do not prove what happened to it afterward, and the man who would have to say what happened to it afterward is the man you are accusing." He let that sit. "Your duplicates arrived. Very well. They travelled a different road, in different hands, under different conditions. A road that is worse than another road is not a criminal. You have compared two routes and found one of them worse and you are asking me to conclude that the difference is a man."

"It is a man."

"It may well be a man. I am telling you what the paper does, and the paper does not put a name on anything, and there is no name on any document in that satchel." Keller sat back. "I have spent thirty-one years convicting people on evidence. I have never once convicted anybody on a pattern, and the reason is not squeamishness. A pattern is a thing a clever man makes out of the world by choosing what to count. You have chosen well. I have watched three men choose badly and I have watched one of them be entirely sincere while he did it."

It was, Emeric understood, not obstruction. It was the job being done the way the job had to be done or it was not a job. He also understood, sitting in that chair, that it was the most expensive answer he had ever been given and that it was correct.

He did not argue.

That was the thing he was afterward most pleased with, and it took him no effort at all, because the argument was already over and he had been in enough rooms by then to know when one was.

"Then I'd like to ask for something else," he said. "And it's harder."

"Ask."

"Tell me what would be enough."

Keller looked at him properly for the first time since he had sat down.

"Not what would help," Emeric said. "Not what you would find interesting. The standard. What would you need in front of you to open a matter and sustain it against a baron with money and a hall and a reason to fight it."

There was a silence of some length.

"Most men do not ask that," Keller said.

"Why not?"

"Because they believe the standard is a door I am choosing not to open for them. It is easier to be refused by a man than by a rule." He pulled a sheet of his own toward him, and a pen, which surprised Emeric considerably. "A magistrate who will not tell a man what the standard is has no business holding it. Write this down, because I am not going to repeat it and I am not going to put my hand to it."

Emeric got out the notebook.

"Four things," Keller said. "All four. Three of them is nothing."

"One. Dated. Every item bearing a date, and the date fixed against something outside your own house. Your own book saying you sent a letter on the fourth is your own book. A stamp, a toll entry, a lodgment, a clerk's mark from a man who has never heard of you. If the only person who can date your paper is you, your paper has no dates."

"Two. Paired. Every item with a twin, and the twin's fate known and demonstrable. Not a copy in a drawer. A copy that went somewhere, by a different route, and either arrived or did not, and can be shown to have done the one or the other by somebody who is not in your employ. A letter that vanishes proves nothing. A letter that vanishes while its twin arrives, on the same day, out of the same building, is a comparison, and a comparison is the only thing in this world that makes an absence say something."

"Three. Independently attested. By parties with no interest in the outcome and nothing to gain by lying for you, and ideally by parties who do not know what they are attesting to. A merchant's correspondent. A waypoint clerk. A stabler. The more of them who could not describe your case if asked, the better your case is."

"Four, and it is the one men always shortchange. Produced over long enough. Long enough that weather cannot account for it, that a bad season on a road cannot account for it, that one dishonest rider cannot account for it, and that bad luck cannot account for it. Four months is a season. A season is weather. I would want a year and I would rather have more, and I will tell you now that when you come back to me with eleven months of it I will send you away, and you will think me a pedant, and I will be right."

He put the pen down.

"Do that, and bring it to me, and I will read it. I am not telling you I will open a matter. I am telling you that is the ground on which the question can be asked."

"One more thing," Keller said, "and you should hear it now rather than work it out later and feel deceived."

"Go on."

"I am not your ally. I will not advise you. I will not warn you if something crosses my desk that touches you. I will not receive anything from you privately, and if you send me anything that is not formally lodged I will return it unread and record that I did. And if you bring me paper that does not hold, I will rule against you in front of whoever is in the room, without hesitation and without regret, and I will not soften it because you rode eleven days."

"You've said that twice now."

"I have said it twice because men in your position hear it once and file it as modesty."

Emeric found, walking out of that building an hour later, that he liked the man enormously. It was not a complicated feeling and it was not reciprocated in any degree whatsoever, and he did not need it to be, and he noticed that he did not need it to be and thought that was probably the healthiest thing that had happened to him in a year.

The other thing happened at the door, and it took about forty seconds, and it reorganised six years.

Keller was putting the sheets away, which involved a drawer and a key and a certain amount of squaring, and he said, in the tone of a man remarking on weather he has no opinion about:

"I know that waypoint."

Emeric stopped with his hand on the door.

"The crossing marker. The one on your receipts." Keller did not look up. "It was in the Cobb records. In your baron's matter, six years ago. There was a routing marker on the dispatch schedule and it was that one."

"You remember a marker from six years ago."

"I remember everything I have ruled on. It is not a virtue, it is a disability with a use." The drawer closed. "I mention it because it is a coincidence and coincidences are worth stating out loud once so that they can be dismissed properly. I am not drawing a conclusion from it. Drawing conclusions is not what I do."

"But."

"But the marker was already in use for that purpose at a date when your barony was a plague town with eighteen hundred people in it and no baron worth the name." Keller looked up at last. "I have no view about what that means. I am telling you because you will find it yourself in three years and waste the three years."

Emeric walked back to the inn through the second ring and did not notice any of it.

Sethe had begun taking Kaldo's letters after the war. That was not in doubt. Every item on Ferra's list was dated and the pattern started when Kaldo started being worth silencing, and the reason was obvious and squalid and entirely sufficient: a beaten baron with a war to hide does not want the crown reading the winner's account of it.

And the crossing at Wayford had been a place where dispatches quietly went somewhere other than where they were addressed, four years before Emeric ever came south. When Kaldo was a name nobody in this city could have placed on a map. When there was no war, no foundry, no ore, no prince, and nothing in that barony worth the cost of a single bought clerk.

Two operations. One piece of ground. And the older of them had nothing to do with him at all.

He worked at it for three days in the capital and the whole eleven days home and he could not get a grip on any part of it. Cobb had been dead or gone for six years and had never been found. The marker was a marker. Wexler had paid somebody through a dispatch route and Wexler had been executed in a yard and the men who had paid Wexler had never been named, because Emeric had walked into a conclusion and closed the case around it.

There was no thread. That was the thing he kept arriving at and kept refusing. There was no name, no living witness, no account with a balance on it, no standing matter, and no instrument anywhere in the kingdom that reached whoever had been buying a crossing before Kaldo was worth buying it for.

He was going to spend the next several years assembling an extremely good case against a baron, and he understood, somewhere around the sixth day of that ride, that when he won it he would have cut off a hand at the wrist and that the arm would still be there.

On the eighth day he took out a thing he had been carrying for six years without once looking at it directly.

A bookkeeper, in a cold room, in his first winter, with her finger on a line, telling him that the large payment to Cobb was a discharge and not a fee, that the rhythm did not bend around the date, and that what the ledger actually said was that Wexler had paid people who were already coming.

She had been right. She had been right in the specific and she had been right in the general, and he had thanked her for flagging it and then explained to her, patiently, why this one was different, using four sentences he would have demolished in under a minute if anybody else had offered them.

Ward asked him twice that day what he was chewing on and got nothing both times.

He came in through the Western Gate on a wet evening with eleven days of road on him, and did not go up to the keep, and went to the counting room instead, where there was a lamp on.

"Your objection about Cobb," he said. "The one you wrote in your own book. I want it."

Ferra did not ask which one. She did not ask why, and she did not ask what had happened in the capital, and she did not ask whether Keller had taken it.

She went to the shelf, took down the third book from the left, opened it without hunting, and put it on the table in front of him with her finger already on the page.

It had taken her under a minute, and he stood there looking at it and did the arithmetic on that, which was that she had known exactly where it was for six years.

The entry was four lines. It was dated. It had her initials against it. It said that the holder had been advised that the Cobb payment was a discharge against a standing account and not a fee for the attempt, that the retainer rhythm did not support the reading he had adopted, and that the holder had considered the advice and had directed otherwise.

It did not say she had been right. It did not say anything about him at all.

"Do you want it out of the book?" Ferra said.

"No. Leave it where it is."

He read it twice more and gave it back, and she put the book on the shelf in the place it had come from, third from the left, and turned the lamp down.
