Chapter 63 - The Dynamo House

He was through the water gate before Ward could say anything, and Ward did not try.

Ward sent Rylan after him, and four of the bowmen from the carts, and that was all he could send. Emeric heard him say it behind them, flat, to the men at the carts: close up, nobody else goes, the bridge is the bridge. Then the gate was behind them, and the path went down from the bridge foot along the outside of the wall, steep and stony, toward the mill yard, and he was running down it in the half light of the lamp overhead with the crossbow in his hand, spanned, and a bolt in it, and no idea at all how he had come to be holding it.

The lamp over the path dimmed as he ran under it. It came back. It dimmed again, further, to a sullen orange, and he could hear it ticking in its hood as the carbons cooled.

Behind him, up on the bridge, the shouting changed.



The mill yard was full of men.

They had come up from the reeds along the foot of the bank, thirty of them, perhaps, off the two barges that had gone under the bridge, and the light was going, and in the light that was left he saw them all at once: round the river door of the powerhouse, which hung off one hinge, split down the middle; in the doorway; in the yard between the powerhouse and the mill, where the race ran black under its plank bridge. Some of them were lying down. Two of the six Ward had chosen were lying down too, by the powerhouse wall, one of them with his bow still in his hands. The coast man Ward had put at the river door was sitting against the doorpost with his chin on his chest, and Emeric knew from how he sat that he would not be getting up.

From inside the powerhouse came the sound of the machine, slowing. It was a sound he knew in the dark, in his sleep, the hum that told him everything was well, and it was going down, note by note, like a top losing its spin.

And Tess's voice, again, and this time he heard the word. It was "No."

Rylan went past him into the yard without stopping. The four bowmen behind him knelt on the path, as Ward had taught them, and shot down into the yard, and two Grayrock men by the race went over, and the rest turned, and Rylan was among them.

Emeric went for the door.

A man came out of it to meet him, a levy man in a leather coat with an axe, and Emeric shot him in the chest from three paces and did not stop to see him fall, and went past him into the dark of the powerhouse.

The machine was slowing on its bed in the middle of the floor, and the belt was off it. It had been thrown off the pulley, or cut half through and thrown off; it lay in a long loop across the floor, writhing a little where its far end still ran over the pulley on the wheel's shaft, coming in through its slot in the wall, slapping on the boards. There was a Grayrock man standing over the machine with a sword, a big man in a mail shirt, looking down at it as if he were trying to decide where to hit it first.

Tess was on the floor between him and the machine.

She had come out from behind it. Emeric saw it in a single look, and understood it: she had been behind the machine, as she had been told, with the machine between her and the door, and the man had come in and gone for the belt, and she had come out from behind it to stop him. She had her hammer in her hand still. She was lying on her side on the boards with her face toward the machine, and there was a great deal of blood on the boards under her, black in the bad light, and more coming.

The man with the sword looked up and saw Emeric in the door.

Emeric had no bolt in the bow. He had shot it into the man with the axe. He threw it, the whole crossbow, at the man's face, and the man put his arm up and it broke against his arm, and in the time that took Rylan came through the door behind Emeric and went past him, and the man with the sword did not have time to bring it round.

He did not remember crossing the floor. He was on his knees on the boards beside her with his hands on her back, both of them, flat, pressing down, and the blood came up between his fingers, warm, and did not stop.

"Tess."

She was awake. Her eyes were open. She was looking at the belt.

"The lamps," she said.



The machine had nearly stopped. He could feel it through the floor, through his knees, the long slow grind of its bearings running down. In the slot in the wall the belt's far end flapped and slapped on the turning pulley, and outside, along the whole length of the wall, the lamps would be going down to red, to nothing, all of them at once, the river going dark under them, the bridge going dark, and on the bridge the levies.

He could not take his hands off her back.

"Rylan."

Rylan was at the river door, with his back to the room, watching the yard. One of the bowmen was beside him. He turned his head.

"Here. Your hands. Here, and press. Hard. Harder than you think." Emeric took Rylan's hands and put them where his own had been, on her back, and pressed them down with his own, and Rylan knelt and leaned his weight onto them and did not ask anything. The bowman took his place at the door.

Emeric went to the belt.

The cut went a third of the way across, near the lacing, and the rest had held, and it had been thrown off the machine's pulley and lay on the floor in its loop with the far end still running round the wheel's. He picked up the slack end of it. He had put this belt on that pulley twenty times, with the machine still, by daylight, with Tess holding the lamp. He had never once done it with the shaft turning.

"Not your fingers," Tess said from the floor.

He looked round. She had turned her head on the boards so that she could see him, and her face was grey, and her eyes were on his hands.

"Roll it on the loose one. From the side. The edge first, onto the rim, and let it take it. Then the lever." She stopped, and breathed, and went on. "No twist. Look at the arrows. Look at the arrows before you put it on, or it runs backward and makes you nothing."

He looked at the arrows. The one he had painted on the machine's yoke, and the one she had painted on the post beside it, the second one, white, the same way, that she had painted for whoever had the lever. He laid the belt flat along his forearm, the right way up, with the arrows, and brought the edge of it to the rim of the loose pulley, and pushed it on, and the loose pulley took the belt's edge and pulled it round and over and on, and he threw the lever, and the belt walked across onto the fast pulley and went taut with a snap that jumped through the whole floor.

The machine caught.

He felt it come up through the boards. The hum came back, low at first, rising, the note he knew, climbing up through itself until it was where it should be, and above them on the wall, he knew without seeing, the lamps would be going from red to orange to white, all together, all the way along, and the river coming back out of the dark.

Outside, up on the bridge, the shouting changed again.

He went back to Tess and took Rylan's place, and pressed down.

"That's it," Tess said, to nobody, and shut her eyes.



The yard was quiet when he looked out. The Grayrock men who could still walk had gone back down along the bank into the reeds, toward their barges, and Rylan's four bowmen were shooting after them into the dark.

They had set the mill alight on their way.

It was the roof. Somebody had put a torch to the thatch at the downstream end, where it came down low over the race, and the thatch had caught and was going up the slope of it toward the ridge, slowly at first, with the dew on it, and then less slowly. The smoke rolled off it along the ridge. Sparks went up and came down on the powerhouse tiles and lay there, harmless, and went out.

Harl came out of the dark at the back of the powerhouse with his two buckets.

Emeric had not seen him. He did not know where Harl had been through the fight, whether behind the machine or in the corner or out in the dark of the yard. He came out with a bucket in each hand, full, and stood in the yard and looked up at his roof.

"Not a spark in my mill," he said.

Emeric had heard him say it in December, in his back room, about the machine. Harl said it now in the same voice, as a plain fact about the world that the world would have to be made to agree with, and he went to the mill door and opened it and went in.

"Harl." Emeric could not leave Tess. He shouted it. "Harl, not inside."

Harl did not come back out.

Emeric understood what he was doing before any of the others did, because he had stood in that mill in December and heard Harl tell it: a mill that went up all at once, when he was a boy, and the roof came off, and the miller came out without his eyebrows, and they found the boy under the stones in the morning. Harl's fear had always been the dust: the fine white dust that lay on every ledge and every beam in the stone room under the floor, round the shaft that drove the belt to the machine. Fire on the roof was fire. Fire that got down into the stone room with that dust in the air was the whole mill at once, and the shaft with it, and the wheel, and the lamps, and very likely the powerhouse and everybody in it.

Harl was in the stone room keeping the dust down. Emeric could hear him through the belt slot: the slosh of a bucket thrown, and then the scrape of a bucket dipped in the trough under the shaft, where the race came in round it through the wall, and thrown again. Wetting the floor, the walls, the beams, the ledges. Laying the dust the only way it could be laid.

"Buckets," Emeric said. "Rylan. The race. Get them on the roof."

Rylan was already up. He took the four bowmen and what was left of the six, and they found whatever would hold water, helmets, a sack, the dead coast man's tarred bag, and went at the roof from the race, throwing up at the low end of the thatch where it came down over the water. It was not enough. Emeric could see that it was not enough from the floor of the powerhouse with his hands on Tess's back. The fire had the ridge. It went along the ridge from end to end in a long running line, and the smoke came down into the yard so thick that the men at the race were coughing, and the light of it lay on the water red under the white of the lamps.

Then the bucket line came down the path.

He never found out who had sent it. Ferra, or Ward, or nobody: people from the bridge foot, the dock quarter's own, with their buckets, who had seen the mill alight from the gate and come down the path without being told, a dozen of them, more, and fell in along the race beside Rylan, and the water went up onto the roof in a steady chain, bucket after bucket, hand to hand, as they had been doing at the bridge foot all night.

The roof fell in a little before dawn.

It went at the downstream end first, where it had started, the burning thatch and the rafters under it coming down together into the mill with a long soft roar, and a great breath of sparks went up out of the hole into the grey sky. Everybody in the yard stopped. Emeric stopped breathing. He was waiting for the other sound, the one Harl had heard as a boy.

It did not come.

The fire burned on in the mill's upper floor, on the boards, where the roof had come down, and the bucket line went at it through the doors and the windows, and it burned lower, and then it did not burn at all, and the mill stood in the grey light with its walls black and its roof gone and smoke coming out of every opening it had, and down in the stone room under the floor, through the wall, Emeric could hear the shaft still turning, and outside, in the race, the wheel.

The lamps had not gone down again. Not once.

They found Harl in the stone room when the smoke had thinned enough to go in, sitting on the wet floor with his back against the wall by the shaft, with a bucket between his knees, half full. Every ledge in the room was dark with water. There was not a grain of white dust left dry anywhere in it. The smoke had come down through the floor into the room and he had stayed in it, throwing water, until he could not, and then he had sat down. He was dead.

The shaft went round beside him, dripping, as it had turned every day of his life.



Across the river, downstream, below the burning dock quarter, bay three was still standing.

Emeric saw it from the powerhouse door, when the smoke off the mill thinned, in the first grey of the morning. The fire had come down the lanes as far as the old works and stopped there, against the old works' stone, and smoldered in the last of the houses above it. Bay three's door was open. There was a figure in it, big, in an apron, with a bucket, throwing water out of the door at the burning shed next to it, and then going back in for more. He was too far off to see the face. He did not need to.



They carried Tess up to the ward on the river door, off its last hinge now, with a plank across the split, through the water gate and up through the lower ward in the grey light, with Emeric walking beside it with his hands on her back, pressing, and Rylan and three of the bowmen carrying.

She was not awake. Her breathing was quick and shallow, and her lips had gone a color he did not like, and he talked to her the whole way up the hill, about nothing, about the arrows on the post, about the belt, telling her it had gone on clean, as she had said, first time, no twist, that the lamps were lit, that they had not gone down again, and she did not answer him.

Imani met them at the side step. She looked at Tess once, and at his hands, and at the blood on the door.

"In," she said.

The ward was full. Declan was in the bed by the window with his leg bound up to the hip, white, asleep. There were men on pallets on the floor all down the room, and Imani's women going between them, and the smell of the spirit over everything. Imani had a table cleared by the time they got Tess through the side door. They laid her on it face down, and Imani took Emeric's hands off her back with her own, and put her own down in their place, and leaned on them, and looked.

"It's in her," she said. "Deep. Something big's cut, inside, and it's not stopping." She did not look up. "I can hold it like this. Not forever. I can pack it. I can't close what I can't see."

He knew what she needed. He knew exactly what she needed, and how he could have given it to her, and it was on the other side of a wind that had come over from the far end of the bridge and blown through him and taken everything outward with it, and would not give it back until the place filled in again.

"When it clears," he said. "Hold her till it clears."

Imani looked up at him then. She did not ask what he meant. She gave him the look she had given him in the laundry yard, from the pallet, with the basin on her knees, and she kept it, and set it aside, and looked down at Tess again.

"Then I'll hold her," she said. "Go and end it."

Outside, over the roofs of the lower ward, the sky in the east was going from grey to white, and somewhere at the bottom of the town, from the South Gate, a horn was blowing.
