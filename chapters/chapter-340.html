Chapter 340 - Seeds

The Church's men went north on the second of April, in four of Saban's wagons, under a white flag that Abloh had hemmed the night before and said was the plainest thing anybody had ever asked him to make, and he had been asked to make shrouds.

There had been thirty-one of them taken on the ground in the city in December, and none of them in the air: the brothers in grey off the towers, with their bags and their boxes; two of the temple guard from the arch behind the drain net; and the temple knight Ward had carried up a stair by his collar and belt. They had come south in the K Endeavor's hold and been kept since February in the rooms under the keep that Hale had aired for them, seen by Imani's people every morning and asked nothing by anybody. Doran had sat with them most afternoons, talking about bells.

Four had asked for rooms, and were not in the wagons: a brother in grey who wanted to be put anywhere there were no stones at all, and had been put in Fletcher's sail loft; two of the temple guard, who wanted the railway; and a breaker who wanted the charging room, because it was the only work he knew, and had been told by Imani that he could have it six hours a day and not a minute more, and had cried, which nobody had expected, least of all him.

The nine bleeders off the cloud were in the charging room already, at a wage, on a clock, with Imani's card over their benches and in her hand at the foot of it, and then you go home.

So twenty-seven went. They stood in the yard below the north gate in their own clothes, washed and mended, with a blanket each and a loaf each and a twist of salt, and Ferra's clerk read them onto the wagons by name, or by number for the ones who would not give one, and ticked them, and Emeric stood at the tailboard of the first wagon with the letter.

It was short. Ferra had read it twice and cut a line out of it.

These are yours. Twenty-seven. Fed, doctored and asked nothing. Their names and numbers are on the back in our clerk's hand; the four not on it asked to stay and are staying. We have kept nobody who wished to go home. Emeric, of Kaldo.

He gave it to the carter, Saban's man, who would drive them as far as the queue at Pryor's Gate, where the pilgrim road began; and past that, Saban said, it carried itself.

Doran climbed up onto the first wagon's bench with his bad knee and his patched coat, because he was going as far as the first night's halt on the Wayford road and coming back on the morning train.

The temple knight was the last man at the second wagon.

He was a big man, very dense, silver to the jaw on both sides, with his steel cap gone and the bruise long since faded off his temple; and he had not given a name in four months, and was on the clerk's board as temple knight, the stair, the crown wall. He stood by the tailboard with his loaf under his arm and looked at Emeric, and then down at Emeric's right hand, and back up.

"It's mended," said Emeric.

The knight did not say anything. But something in his face moved a little, as a man's face moves who has wondered a thing for four months and not asked.

"I want you to carry something," said Emeric. "Not a letter. A sentence. To the holy father."

"I'll not get within a mile of the holy father."

"Then tell whoever you get within a mile of. It'll get there. Things do, in your house." He waited until the man's eyes were on his. "Tell him Kaldo doesn't keep people in stones."

The knight looked at him a long time. Behind them the first wagon creaked as the carter took up the reins.

"That's all?"

"That's all of it."

The man said it back, under his breath, once, the way a soldier says back an order so as to have it right, and climbed up into the wagon among the brothers in grey, who moved along the bench for him without looking at him. The flag went up on its pole. The wagons went out under the north gate at a walk with Doran on the first bench already talking to the carter about axles, and down the road toward Wayford in the April mud, and Ferra's clerk ruled a line under twenty-seven and wrote the hour.

"You didn't put the two handlers on it," said Ferra, at his elbow.

"Saban said not." He watched the last wagon go over the rise. "He said it in one sentence, and I couldn't think of one back."

"No," said Ferra. "Nor could I." She closed the board. "Was that wise? The sentence."

"He knows already. He had her in his fist in the square and lost her." Emeric turned back toward the keep. "I wanted him to hear we'd said it."



They planted two of Amara's stones the next evening, in the bed on the east side of the trunk, where Bram had lifted the flags in March and put down a yard of the dome's own soil and said nothing about why.

The twelve lay in the treasury's deal box on Bram's crate at the bed's edge, in their white paper and their red tape, every parcel saying Of no value in a clerk's brown hand forty years old. Armand opened them one at a time. He held each stone in his palm a while with his eyes half shut, as he had held his own once on Long Table night, and set it on the paper, and went on to the next; and when he had been through all twelve he went back and took up two, and gave them to Tess.

"Those want to wake," he said. "The others are asleep further down. I'd not trust them yet."

"How can you tell?" said Bram.

"I can't. I was a farm boy. You pick the seed that looks like it means it."

Tess sat on the crate with a stone in her left hand and a small flat file from her apron in her right, and took the file across one end of each, three short strokes, through the grey coat to the pale under it; the way farmers filed hard seed so the water could get in, and the way the chip in Bram's brooch had done it for the first tree by accident. She did not hurry. When she had done the second she held them both up to the lamp, and looked at the little bright scratch on each, and nodded at them as she nodded at a weld.

Bram put them in. He knelt on the edge of the bed with his sleeves rolled and made two holes with his thumb, a hand apart, a knuckle deep, and laid a stone in each, scratch upward, and closed the soil over them with the side of his hand and pressed it, once, as he would have pressed a patch of clay round a mould. Then he stayed kneeling, as if he expected it to do something.

Amara was there. She had come out of herself at dusk, as she did most evenings now, and stood at the bed's edge with her arms folded and her head on one side, breath on glass with the trunk showing through her; and Bram looked up at the place where she was and away, because Bram did not like being seen to look at things.

"They were in a drawer longer than I was in a stone," said his mother. "Somebody wrote on them that they weren't worth anything. I'd like to see his face."

Ferra had sat down on the curb of the old bed with her book on her knee and was doing the sum, because the sum was why she had come. She did it aloud, the way she did a sum for the table, to the bed and nobody.

"A lone tree stalls when it's dropped. That's the ladder. Somebody up it for two hours with a stick hooked on a rung." She did not look at Tess, and Tess did not look at her. "Two trees out of step do it for each other. One drops and the other flowers off it. These two will come up together, so Tess shades one a season and puts it behind. So. These come up this year. First pod a year after, if they're like the first one, and lean. Seven. Then three or four a cycle while they're young. Then six." She wrote. "Six pods of eight. Two trees. Every year, and nobody on a ladder."

She turned the book round.

Ninety-six. Under it, smaller, from about year eighteen. If they take.

Armand looked at the figure for some time with his hands in his coat pockets.

"That's not a tree," he said. "That's a farm."

Amara laughed, the laugh Emeric had heard go through him on the nineteenth of February and known at once; and Bram got up off his knees in two goes and looked down at the two places in the soil he had pressed with his hand, as if he had been told he had just planted a field.

Ferra had not written the farm down yet. She was looking past them at the trunk, at the lowest tier, where a thin dark shoot came out of a braid at about the height of Bram's head; and she had the face she wore when a column would not foot.

"What if it was a twig," she said.

Nobody answered, so she said it to Emeric. "The clause says seeds. Every lord in the north is going to read it with a lamp, looking for the hole. If a man can walk out of here with a cutting up his sleeve and put it in a pot at home, I've written a charter about the wrong thing." She tapped the book. "Does it grow from a cutting? A willow does."

"No," said Emeric.

"How do you know?"

He opened his mouth and found that he didn't. Vey's pages had the trees and the pods and the people carrying them, and nobody carrying a stick. The groves had been planted by dragons. Nobody in the tower's records had ever had a tree to cut.

"I'd expect not," he said.

"I didn't ask what you'd expect." Ferra wrote something and drew a line under it. "I'm not signing a clause on an expect."

Bram looked up at the shoot the way a man looks at a horse somebody has suggested he sell.

"You're not taking a knife to that," he said.

"A finger's length," said Tess. She was already up off the crate. "It can spare a finger."

She took it with the little saw from her apron: the tip of the shoot, with three violet leaves on it. The tree's note did nothing anyone could feel, which Bram seemed to take personally. Then a root, from where the strands spilled over the curb of the old bed, as thick as a pencil; a leaf; a fallen flower off the flags; and with the point of her knife, a chip out of one of the bright grooves in the trunk. It came away the size of a fingernail and went out in her palm slowly, like an ember, until it was a flake of blue glass with nothing in it.

"And a bit of pod," said Ferra.

"Pods go on Imani's heap," said Tess, and went round behind the herb beds, and came back with a curved grey plate the length of her hand, dull and dusty, that had been a lit shell in February and had been lying under the gardeners' clippings since.

She put them in along the outer edge of the east bed, a hand apart: the shoot, the root, the leaf, the flower, the glass, the plate. Ferra wrote a label for each on a strip of card and pegged it in with a matchstick. Then Tess made a second set, smaller, in a seed tray of plain soil from the castle garden, and took it out through the pass-box and set it on the sill outside, in the town's air, where the tree couldn't reach.

"That's the control," she said. "If they both do the same thing, it isn't the dome."

"How long?" said Ferra.

Armand had watched all of it with his hands in his coat pockets. "A willow would tell you in a month," he said. "This will tell you in a summer, if it means to tell you at all. Somebody has to look at them."

"I'll be awake," said Tess; and she would be. She and Armand were the only two at that bed who would.

Ferra wrote the line in the stone book that night. 3 April. Two of A.'s twelve, planted, east bed. T. filed them. B. put them in. Ten to the box. And under it, after a while: farm. A. Because it was his word and she wanted it down as his.

And on the line under that, smaller again: Cuttings, east bed, outer edge. Six, and six on the sill. T. to watch. Clause waits.



Bram and Tess had fitted the meter the week before, and it was the first thing Ferra looked at when she came into the pass-box's outer room on the afternoon of the fourth.

It sat on the reservoir line under the old brass wheel: a brass drum the size of a loaf with a little wheel of vanes inside that the mana turned as it went by, and a dial Tess had painted herself, in cartridges a day. A flow meter, Emeric had called it at the bench, and Bram had said he supposed it would have to be called something. The wheel above it still had Bram's chalk on the rim from Armand's winter: FULL. DON'T. Nobody was going to clean it off.

"There," said Ferra, and put her finger on the glass. "That's the whole of it. That's the end of guessing."

It was Armand's rule from the Band's foot, Emeric thought, made into a pipe: give a man his number and you need not guess what he has room for. Nobody would stand at a wheel all night with a hand on it, hoping.

She did her sums at the shelf with ten people standing round her in a room built for four.

"Holding the dome at the tree's line costs what it costs," she said. "It costs the same for one of us as for nine. That's the dome. What adds is us, drinking. Armand's rate was four a day. Nine of us at four is thirty-six; and the queen, and the tree's one, and the two in the east bed, which are nothing yet." She wrote. "Forty a day the first week, falling as we wake. The K Meridian's topped the reservoir twice since the fleet came home." She looked up. "One at a time would have been nine times the holding and a council half asleep till Christmas. So it's once."

She turned the page. The list was there: nine names, in the order Imani had given her by who would wake first, Emeric at the top and Saban at the foot; and under Saban's, ruled off on its own line, her own. F.

"You're not last to wake," said Imani. "Saban is."

"I'm last to swallow," said Ferra. "So nobody can say I didn't."

Imani let that go. She had the jar out of her coat pocket, and she put it down on the shelf beside the feed gauge book without a word: Ivo's squat jar with the back of an old man's left hand in it between two squares of glass, clear as a window, the age spots still in it.

Armand, in the door, looked at the ceiling.

"In the last weeks your skin will go dull," said Imani. "Cloudy. Like that. Your eyelids will go grey. The first time I saw it I wrote down that I thought he was dying." She tapped the jar. "He wasn't. It comes off in sheets, and there's new under it, and nothing that comes off is alive. Bettany will lift it with a damp cloth and she'll not scream, and nor are you." She looked at the four knights. "Your bands go. All of it. The tracery's scar, and it all goes. I'll not pretend I know what that's like for you."

Ward said nothing at all. He looked at the silver laid into his forearms, that he had carried out of the rite when most of his class did not, and pulled his sleeves down over it.

Rylan looked at his burned arm. "And this?"

"That too."

"Huh," said Rylan.

Ferra was looking at the jar. "Do you mean to keep one of everybody's?"

"Only the interesting ones," said Imani.

"Which are the interesting ones?"

"I'll know when I see them." She put the jar back in her pocket. Then she pinned her card on the outer door under the old ONE AT A TIME, Armand's rule in the same upright hand: arms and neck every hour; lines that came and went were the work; a line that stayed an hour meant the mending had stopped, and that one came out. Under it, new: Nine of us. Read each by name. Not the room. I.

Tess had come in at the end with her stick and stood against the wall, because she wanted to hear the sums, and because Osric was in the room.

"I'll have the tiles up from the island while you're under," she said. "The whole table. I'm not going out there with..." She stopped, and went on. "I'll do it in the back bay."

"Then put something on it for me," said Imani, without turning round. "Every keyed wall takes a little out of the air. I've read the works district against my induction numbers. One wall's nothing. A street of them you can measure." "Write it in the square under what each one does. What it costs to stand there."

Tess wrote it on her hand.



Bram had made seven more sets of cuffs over the winter from the two in the iron press, so that there were nine, and Ward's book was on its nail, and Ward wrote the line in it himself at a quarter to seven in his square hand before he went in: 4 April. Stone cuffs, nine sets, out. W. Then he stood and looked at the book, and handed the pencil to Arthur.

"You'll do mine," he said. "Armand does the rest. You do mine."

Arthur took the pencil and did not say anything. He had been sworn to the dome the night before, on Ward's word and the six's vote, which was the only way anybody but the six and the three knights had ever been let near what happened under the glass. Emeric watched him put it in his breast pocket and button the pocket over it, the way Ward put things away.

The boards lay round the trunk on the north and west sides like spokes, nine of them, new oak, with a straw mattress and a jug on each; and on the south side Amara on her old mattress with her hands on the sheet. The dome was full, every breath of it heavier than air had any business being; and the four sentries Tess and Merrin had finished by the end of March stood two at the outer door and two at the inner, seven feet each in their dark plate with their shields grounded, and let them all through because every one of them was on the register.

Nobody made anything of it. Saban lay down first, on the end board nearest the door, because he was going to be there longest and he said he would like to be near the way out. Bram lay down and folded his hands on his stomach and looked straight up into the violet, and said, to the canopy, "Hm." Declan lay down on his side as if in a barracks and had to be told to lie on his back. Rylan lay down and sat up and took his boots off and lay down again. Osric lay down on the board beside the place Tess could see from the glass, and did not look at the glass, and went red to the ears anyway.

Ward lay down last of the knights, square, with his arms at his sides; and Arthur knelt on the paving with the cuffs and did him, ankles first over the stockings, then the wrists, as he had seen Ward do it for others, and turned the key in the last one and did not leave it in the lock. He put it on its cord, and the cord round his own neck, and tucked it in his shirt.

Ward looked up at him from the board.

"Good," said Ward, and shut his eyes.

Emeric's board was between Ferra's and Imani's, because Ferra had drawn up the plan and nobody had argued with it, least of all Emeric. He lay on the straw and Armand cuffed him, briskly, the way a man does a thing he has had done to him, and he unbuckled his band.

The root bead lay in his palm. Small and grey and faceted, the end of his little finger: cut small from the first of his mother's stones, and worn ever since as the latch of his own door. It was warm. He thought of the fear he had had once in a week of agony, that somebody would eat a thing bound to him and walk away with him in them; and that after tonight there would be nothing on his wrist for anyone to take.

He looked across at the mattress by the trunk. His mother was standing at the foot of it, out of herself, very faint in the lamplight, watching him. She did not say anything. She nodded once, as if he had asked.

Ferra was counting. She sat on the edge of her board with her book shut on her knee and counted them in under her breath as they went, as she had counted the fleet off the quay, so quietly that only he could hear it. Saban. One. Bram. Two. Declan, three. Rylan, four. Osric, five, who swallowed his and said "That's horrible," aloud, and was told by Imani to be quiet. Ward, six. Imani lay down on his other side and took his wrist, two fingers inside it, the way she always found it; and swallowed hers, dry, with the face of a woman taking a medicine she had prescribed herself and did not approve of; and Ferra said, "Seven."

He put the bead on his tongue and drank from the jug and swallowed it.

"Eight," said Ferra.

It went down like a warm coal. Then the door in him opened, the one he went through every night of his life, and kept on opening; and the dome came into him from every side at once, not hard, not the agony of the pod stone in the year eleven, but the way the tide comes over a flat, everywhere together, so that there was no edge of him it was not already at. He felt Imani's fingers on his wrist go slack as she went under. He felt Ferra's hand come across and take his other hand, and hold it.

"Nine," she said, somewhere a long way off; and he felt her hand stay on his, and stay, and then go still.



The room ran slow. That had always been the gift of it.

He stood in it. The long table, the shelves, everything he had ever read lying open on them in the slow clean light; and it was the same as it had always been, and it was not. The floor under the table felt now the way a floor feels over a cellar you have only just been told about. And the door, which he had always had to open, stood open, and there was nothing on the other side of it holding it shut, because the latch was in him now.

Somebody knocked on it anyway.

"You'll want to keep that," said his mother, from the doorway. "Knocking. Even now nobody can make you. Especially now."

She was quite clear in here, as she had been when he brought his father in: a slight dark woman in a plain dress, younger than her face on the mattress, with her arms folded. At the far end of the room, past the stair, there were shelves he kept in the dark, the ones from somewhere else; she glanced that way once, the way a guest glances at a room she has already been shown, and did not walk toward it.

"You've been down," she said. "I can tell. You went down once and came up in a hurry."

"Once."

"Then you went down like a man jumping into a well to see how deep it is." She came to the head of the stair and looked down it. "Put your hand on the rail."

There was no rail. He looked at her.

"There's a rail if you put one there," said Amara. "It's your stair. Put something of yours on it you'd know in the dark."

So he did. He put the rail of the K Discovery's ladder on it, the brass one worn smooth on the inside of the curve where every hand took it; and it was there, under his palm, cold, exactly; and they went down.

It went a long way. She talked as they went, not about the stair: about the tower kitchen, and the porter's cat. When he looked back up, she said, "Don't. Count them if you like. That's how you lose a stair; you look at where you've been and forget you're standing on it." So he counted. He was in the four hundreds when it ended.

The Root lay under them, the dark floor, and the lights.

He had seen it once, for a few breaths, with Augustine going out like a lamp in front of him. Now he stood with his hand on the ladder's rail and looked, and the lights were rooms seen from underneath, near and far, like the lamps of a town at night from a ship's rail.

"Find one," said his mother. "Not by looking. You'd be all night. By feel. The way you'd know somebody's step on a stair."

He found Ferra's before she had finished saying it. Close, and small, and very steady, like a lamp on a desk at the end of a long room; he would have known it anywhere in the world. He did not move toward it. He only knew where it was.

Amara looked at him sideways.

"You didn't need me for that one," she said.

The others came slower. Imani's, warm and quick and moving about; Bram's like banked coals; Ward's, which was square, if a light could be square. The three knights' were small and new and very bright, Osric's much too big for itself and going at the edges.

And very far off, so far that far was not the word, a light so large and so old and so cold that he did not look at it.

"No," said his mother quietly. "Not that one. Nobody knows what that one is."

"Not even you?"

"Not even me. So you learn how to stand down here. Small. A lamp with the wick down. You don't hide; you can't hide; it's all one floor. You just don't burn where it can see the flame." She was not looking at it either. "My father taught me that before I could read. He made it a game. Who can be the smallest candle."

They did it at the foot of his stair until he could hold the wick down without thinking. Then she took him back up.



The standing up was the strangest of it.

"You're lying in the thickest air in the kingdom," said his mother, "and you've a stone in you that's turned you inside out for a door. You'll never have it this easy again. Stand up."

He did not know how. She showed him, in the room, by doing it herself and letting him feel her do it: not a push, not a casting. A leaning. The way a swimmer who has stopped kicking finds that the salt water holds him anyway, and lets it. He leaned out of himself into the dome's mana, and it held him; and he was standing by his own board with the trunk showing through his hands, looking down at a man on the straw with his face quite empty, and Ferra's hand on his.

It was not a pleasant thing to see. He looked away from it.

The others were up too, or getting up. Declan stood by his board as if he had been called on parade. Bram had got as far as sitting up out of himself and stayed there, looking with great interest at his own hands, which he could see the canopy through. Imani was on her feet, bent over her own body with two fingers at its throat, and found she could not feel the pulse with fingers that were not there, and said something short. Osric stood up out of himself too fast and kept going, through the side of the next board and halfway into the trunk, and came back out of it looking like a man who has walked into a door in a strange house.

"Smaller," said Amara.

"I know," said Osric. "I know. Smaller."

She walked them to the glass. The mana thinned toward the panes as water thins toward a shore, so that by the glass he felt himself stretched like a voice going down a corridor. She did not let anybody go through it. "That's the edge," she said. "In here it's thick. Outside, in Kaldo's streets, it's thinner, and past the walls it's nothing. We'll find out where your edge is when you've bodies that can stand up on their own. Not now." She looked at them all, nine faint shapes at the glass in the green dark, and he saw her mouth move in a way he knew from his own. "I've had a class of boys in a tower kitchen that stood straighter."

Afterward, in his room again, she sat on the end of his long table as if it were a kitchen table and swung her foot, and asked him the one thing she had come for.

"What did you build?"

He opened his mouth to tell her. The dam, the furnace, the ships, the trains, the glass over her head. It came up all at once, and none of it was an answer.

"Soap," he said.

She looked at him.

"It was the first thing. The town stank. Nobody would come within a mile of the port." He found he was smiling. "I boiled it in a yard with a man who was a sailmaker. It was the first thing that worked."

His mother laughed until she had to put her hand on the table.

"Your father," she said, "will tell everybody I raised a soap boiler."



Imani came to his room because his had the table.

She knocked, too. He asked her first, at the door, because she had told him once with the ends of her fingers on his face to ask next time, and he always had since: there was a way to lay what he knew in front of her that was faster than talking, a read given and not taken, the things he carried from the world only she and Ferra knew about. She could have all of what bore on bodies, or none of it, or a piece at a time.

"A piece at a time," said Imani. "And I'll stop when I like. Go on."

So they sat at the long table in the slow light, and he gave it her a piece at a time.

That everything she had ever put her hands on was built of small living bricks, too small to see, each one with the whole plan in it and doing only its own part; and he watched her go quiet over it, and stop thinking about wounds, and start thinking about bricks. That some animals could grow back what they lost: a newt a leg, a salamander a tail, the stump waking, the bricks at the cut forgetting what they were and turning back into builders, a little heap of them, a bud. Blastema, he said, and she said it after him, carefully, as if it were a drug she did not yet trust. That every part of a body knew where it was: a hand's bricks knew they were a hand and the left one and the palm side, and a shoulder's knew shoulder, and the bud listened to them to know what to be. That without that, growth went wrong. And that nothing was made from nothing; that an arm was meat and blood and bone, and every grain of it had to come from somewhere, and the somewhere was the plate.

She stopped him at the stump.

"No," she said.

He waited.

"You've got it the wrong way round. You said ours don't grow back because they don't wake. The stump scars and shuts and that's the end." She had her hand flat on his table, as she put it on a patient. "Ours wake. I've seen them. You've seen them; you've read my book. Every healer who ever poured mana into a stump woke it. That's what the lump is. It's the bud, woken, with nobody telling it what to be. It grows what it likes. A spur. A knot. Three fingers out of a wrist. That's not a stump that wouldn't wake. That's a stump that woke and had no map." She took her hand off the table. "Where you come from, maybe they don't wake. Here they wake fine. Waking's the easy part. The trouble's the map."

He thought about it for a long time, in the slow light, with the shelves all round them.

"Yes," he said. "You're right."

"I know," said Imani. "Go on. The food."

He went on.



Arthur slept two hours on the bench in Ward's guardroom the first night and woke at four with the cuff key on its cord stuck to his chest.

Ward's sheet was pinned over the desk in his square hand: who held what wall, who walked the inner ward and when, who rang Armand if the dome rang. At the foot, under a ruled line: Nothing large will come. If it does, you're three of the best I have. A. B. M. Read this twice and then stop reading it. W.

He read it twice. Then, because nobody was in the room, he read it again.

Ward's chair was behind the desk, the plain oak one with the arms worn pale where Ward's hands went. Arthur had not sat in it. He had stood beside it all evening.

Mira came in at half past four with two cups and sat in it.

She put her feet up on the corner of the desk, carefully, because of her side, and drank her tea and looked at him over the rim.

"It's a chair," she said.

"It's his."

"He's under a tree in his socks with his hands cuffed." She drank. "He'll not mind. He'll mind that you stood up all night beside it, though. That he'll mind." She held the second cup out to him. "The walls are quiet. Bren's on the inner ward with the big ones and he says the one on the left looked at him."

By eight there were children on the inner ward's wall, looking at the two sentries by the pass-box's outer door, which did nothing at all, very large and very patient, and let the pigeons walk on their feet.

At nine Arthur's mother came up the path with a basket on her arm and walked straight past the rope.

The left-hand sentry's slot lit, a faint violet line across the helm, and its head turned a hand's breadth on its neck toward her with a small exact sound of gears; and the children on the wall made a noise like a flock going up. Sela did not stop. The light went down again. The head turned back. She walked between the two of them to the door where Arthur stood and put the basket in his hands.

"It knows me," she said. "I've been on Ferra's register since before that thing had a head." She looked up at the small dark shapes on the boards, and her mouth went straight in the way it went for a boy who had not done his sums. "Eat that. There's an egg in it. You've not eaten since yesterday dinner, I can see it on you."

"I've eaten."

"You've drunk Mira's tea," said Sela, and went back down the path past the sentries without looking at either of them, and the children on the wall watched her go as if she had walked through a fire.



The king came up the hill on the first night at eleven and stayed until three, and on the second night at ten, and stayed until it was light.

He sat on a crate in the pass-box's outer room, in his brown coat, where he could see through the inner door's glass when Bettany opened it for the hour; because an unchanged man could stand a quarter of an hour in that air and no more without a mask, and he said he would rather see his son through a door for six hours than beside him in a mask for ten minutes.

Armand sat the nights, as they had sat with him. He sat on the other crate with a book he did not read, and when Bettany went in on the hour with her watch and her lamp, he sat with his eyes half shut and his sense in through the glass.

"There," said Armand, a little after one.

Arthur looked. He saw the tree, and the nine on their boards with their cuffed hands on the wool, and Bettany bent over Rylan's arm. Silver came and went on the arms in the lamplight, faint threads that rose and branched and sank. That was the work. He saw nothing else.

"What?" said the king.

"They're up," said Armand. "All nine. She has them standing." He tilted his head to one side. "She's walking them to the glass. Declan's at attention. Bram's sitting up and won't stand. Your son's looking at his own face and doesn't like it." A pause. "Osric's gone into the tree."

Harrold sat very still.

"He's come out again," said Armand. "She's cross with him. He's apologising." He shut his eyes. "Now she's lining them up at the glass. That's the north pane. She's telling them that's as far as they go. Ferra has her hand up."

"Why has she got her hand up?"

"She has a question," said Armand. "Ferra always has a question. Your wife is pretending not to see her."

The king put his face in his hands. Arthur thought he was weeping, and looked away at the meter on the line, and then heard that he was laughing, very quietly, the way a man laughs who has not been sure for a long time that he would again.

"Tell me what she looks like," said Harrold into his hands. "Standing."

Armand opened his eyes and looked through the glass for a long while.

"Like a woman teaching school," he said. "In a kitchen. With her arms folded. Very pleased with herself and hiding it badly."

"Yes," said the king. "That's her."



The needle went down at twenty to four on the second night.

Bettany saw it, because Bettany saw everything on that shelf, and said Armand's name once in the voice she used for a thing that was not yet anything. The needle had sat since the swallowing a hair past the forty Tess had painted red. It was at thirty-four, and as Arthur watched, it went down a hair more, and trembled.

Armand had his hand flat on the inner door's glass.

"It's thinner," he said. "Not much. A breath. It's not them drinking less." He did not take his hand away. "Arthur. Get Tess."

He ran. Tess was at her door before he had finished knocking, in her nightdress with Bram's old coat over it and her stick, because, she said afterward, she had not been asleep, because who could.

She stood in front of the meter and looked at it the way she looked at a bench somebody else had left untidy, and did not touch Bram's wheel.

"It's not the reservoir," she said. "If the reservoir was low it'd go down slow over a day. That went down in an hour." She put her ear to the pipe where it came up through the floor. She listened a long time. "It's singing wrong."

"Wrong how?"

"Higher. Somewhere it's going out that isn't in here." She held out her hand without looking. "Lamp. And Bram's big spanner off the hook. And come with me."

The line ran under the inner ward from the plant's house in a covered trench with a plate over it every ten yards. She had Arthur lift them one after another along the path, while she knelt at each with the lamp and her bare hand held flat a finger off the pipe; and at the fourth plate, under the corner of the old pear's shadow, she stopped and held her hand there, and said, "Feel that."

He held his hand where hers was. Cold. Not the cold of iron in an April night; the cold of a held stone, thin and sharp, coming off a union in the pipe where two lengths met, in a fine fan, as a joint weeps steam.

"The gasket's gone," said Tess. "The packing. It's been sitting there since Bram fitted it, sweating, and tonight it gave." She sat back on her heels. "Bram did these. I watched him." She sounded almost offended.

She could not get the purchase on the spanner kneeling, with her legs. So she told him: which way, how far, and stop when she said stop. He put his weight on it, and the union took a quarter turn and then another eighth, and she said stop, and he stopped, and held it; and she put her hand back where the cold had been, and kept it there, and nodded.

"That'll hold till he wakes," she said. "Then he can do it properly and be cross with himself. I'll put it in his book." She looked down the trench at the pipe going away under the plates toward the dome, where the violet came through the glass and the tree's light moved on the boards. "It's nearly always a joint," she said, to the pipe.

When they came back into the outer room the needle was going up, slowly, past thirty-eight, and Armand was standing at the inner door with his hand still on the glass and his face turned toward Tess; and Arthur saw something go across the old man's young face and stay there, and Armand did not say what it was. Bettany wrote it in the feed gauge book: the fall, the joint, the rise, and T. found it.



The tiles came up from the island on the K Endeavor's Monday run, in eleven crates of straw marked TESS, FRAGILE, TESS, and by Tuesday noon the Motion Shop's back bay looked like a pottery that had been very carefully burgled.

Arthur went down at two because Armand had sent for the night's book, and found the Seam Table on the back wall: eight squares across and eight down, every kind of dust against every other, fire and water and air and earth, light and lightning, gravity and shard, chalked on a board the size of a barn door. Half the squares were empty. Some had a word in them. Two were red. In the square for fire over lightning, which was the first, Tess's chalk said warm cell, and in a smaller hand under it, Imani's line, copied off Tess's palm before it washed off: what it costs to stand here.

Tess was on her high stool at the bench with her stick hooked on its edge, and Armand stood beside her in his cardigan with a tile in his hand, and they were arguing, which was how Arthur knew it was going well.

They had four new squares since Sunday. Light over lightning, on the windowsill with two wires to Ambrose's meter, whose needle sank when a cloud came over and came back a hair when it went: sun seam, and weak, underlined twice. Air over water, in a dry jar with the lid on, beaded overnight as if somebody had breathed on it: dew seam, and the island. Gravity over shard, under a cup of water Armand had struck sideways with a ruler, hard; the cup had not moved, and Arthur had to strike it himself to believe it: still seam. And gravity over light, flat, lying on a page and making the words under it twice their size with no curve in it anywhere: flat lens.

"The dockhand," Armand was saying. "Look at the dockhand."

"I've looked at the dockhand. I cut it."

"Then look at what you cut."

The twenty slices of it lay on the end of the bench in a row, each a finger thick and grey and banded like an onion, every band following the curve of the old golem's back. Armand put a slice on the flat lens, and the bands came up under it twice their width, and he laid his finger along them from the outside inward and read them off, one, two, three, the way Ferra read a column.

"Gravity. Shard. Gravity. Shard. All the way in through the outer skin. Forty lifts of it, I make it, give or take." He looked at the board. "Your still seam. Forty times over. On the outside, where you'd be hit." He took his finger off. "They poured seams. On purpose. They knew."

Tess looked at the slice for a long time.

"I found that," she said. "On Sunday. On my own."

"You did."

"Somebody found it a thousand years ago and poured a man out of it."

"Several thousand, I should think," said Armand. "And used it for a dockhand."

Tess put the slice down very precisely and did not speak to anybody for most of an hour, and drew a line under still seam so hard the chalk broke. By the time Arthur went back up the hill she had the slice under the lens again with her nose almost on it, saying to Wren, who had come in from school, "Look at their lifts. They're thinner than ours. How did they get them that thin," in the voice of a woman who has been told the answer to a puzzle and found three better puzzles behind it.



On the third night Armand read the sleepers' hands.

He went in after the two o'clock hour, without a mask, because nothing in the dome could hurt him now, and knelt by each board in his slippers with his palm on the back of each cuffed hand, and moved on. Arthur watched him through the glass. The king had gone to bed at midnight because Arthur had told him to, which had surprised Arthur more than the king.

Armand stayed a long time at Emeric's board. He had both hands on the right one, the cuffed one, folded round it as a man folds his hands round a cup.

When he came out he sat down on the crate and did not open his book.

"The bones are done," he said. "His. All of them. The stone's laid them down again from what it knew of him, and it's laid in what you've got." He looked at Arthur. "The packing. In the bone. You three were born to it, near enough; the rest of us never had it. They'll all wake with it now. Ward too."

Arthur's hand went to his own left forearm, where Emeric had once tapped it in the cart shed in front of the other two, and he had felt nothing but a tap and had no idea what he did.

"And his right hand," said Armand, "has something more in it."

He held up his own right hand, smooth and young, and closed it, and looked at the knuckles.

"Rings," he said. "Down into the bone and the flesh over it, laid ready, waiting. Like a cut in a stone that waits for one thing and does nothing till it comes. Three hundred a morning in the cart shed with Ward counting. The stone read that as part of him, the way it read his height and the colour of his eyes, and it's written it in." He let the hand open. "He won't have to think about it any more. When that hand meets something, it'll go off on its own."

"Then it won't break."

"It'll break less," said Armand. "It'll still cost him, every time, like a small spell." He looked through the glass at the long shape on the board between the two women. "He'll be furious. A year and a half of practice, and the stone's done it for him in a night."

Arthur thought about that. He thought about Ward counting in the dark in the cart shed, one, two, three, all winter, three hundred a morning.

"Ward'll be pleased," he said.

"Ward will say he knew," said Armand. "Which is the same thing, in Ward." And opened his book at last, and wrote in it, and did not read it out.

Arthur went back out onto the inner ward. It was cold and clear, the town dark below the wall but for the lamps going down toward the river and one window lit in the forge house where Tess was not sleeping either. The right-hand sentry's slot lit as he passed, faintly, and went down. Up on the wall walk Bren was a dark shape against the stars with his hands in his armpits, and Mira's voice came along the parapet from the east tower, saying something to him that made him laugh out loud and then tell her to be quiet.

Arthur stood on the path between the sentries with the cuff key on its cord against his chest, and looked up at the dome. The violet came through every pane. Under it, round the braided trunk, nine people lay on boards with their hands on their blankets; and two seeds lay a knuckle deep in the east bed with a scratch on each where Tess had filed them; and a woman stood somewhere in there with her arms folded, teaching school, that only the old man on the crate could see.

He did not go in. It was not his to go into. He walked the inner ward once more, the long way round, and went back up onto the wall.
