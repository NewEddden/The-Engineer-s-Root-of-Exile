Chapter 38 - Sites, Not Towns

He had been carving the lids since the ninth of January and by the end of May he had six of the seven.

The working was one he had read off a wall when he was eight years old, in a cellar under the west tower where the crown kept things that had stopped being interesting, and what he had retained of it for twenty years was the shape and not one line of the reasoning. It had taken Armand's notebook and four failures to get the shape to mean anything. The first three had done nothing at all. The fourth had held for two days and then let go all at once with a noise like a stick breaking, which had frightened him considerably more than it had frightened Bram.

The fifth had worked. He had put a cut apple under it on the eleventh of March and had gone to look at it every morning for nine days like a man checking a trap, and on the ninth day it had still been a cut apple, wet at the face, with the cut edges pale and unbrowned, and he had stood in the workshop by himself for a while feeling something he had no proper word for.

Grain does not rot in a good dry bin. Grain gets into it, in the third and fourth month, weevil and mould and mice and heat in the middle of the heap, and a barony that puts eleven months of food in one building loses a portion of it every year and has always lost a portion of it every year and has never called it a loss because it has never had a name for the part that did not happen.

Seven bins, each with its own floor and its own lid. Lose one to fire and you lose a seventh. Lose the working in every one of them and you have seven perfectly ordinary stone bins with slate over them, which is what the barony had had anyway, so nothing got worse. That was the rule he built everything to and he had built this to it without thinking about it, which was how he knew the rule had taken.

It was good work. He was doing it eleven hours a week and had been since January and had done nothing better all year, and he would go on thinking that afterward, which was part of the trouble.

Vantic wanted the camps itemised for the June return.

That was ordinary. He wanted everything itemised for the June return, and had been asking for pieces of it since the middle of May, and this piece was the works: the second granary was in the schedule with a completion date and the schedule was a covenant term, and a covenant term with a date on it gets a column of its own.

So on the second of June, Emeric sat down after supper with the wage book to take the four camps out of it and turn them into a number, because Ferra had been on the return for nine days and he was not going to hand her a tenth.

The wage book ran by camp and by week. Men, days, rate, days not worked.

He got to the fourth column somewhere in the third week of April and stopped.

It ran along at three and four and six, which was what a work camp does, and then in the last week of April it was nineteen, and in the first week of May it was thirty-one, and in the second week of May it was forty-four across the stone camp and the timber camp together, and there were three lines in the book with a rule drawn through them and the rule meant what a rule through a name in a wage book means.

Bern Ostry, timber camp, the eleventh of May.

Wick, timber camp, the fourteenth of May, who was one of the twenty-six families, and who was one of Nessa's brother's cousins, and who had come here in the spring of the year before on a letter read out three times in a room in Wayford.

Tulley, road camp, the nineteenth of May.

He sat with the book open for some while.

Then he got the map out, because he already knew and wanted to be wrong.

Four camps. The stone camp at the outcrop on the eastern arm, thirty-one men, quarrying footings. The lime camp a mile below it, nineteen men, burning for mortar, which he had gone to Vantic for in writing in February and got in writing in February. The timber camp up in the hangers, thirty-eight men, felling for the frame. The road camp on the western arm, fifty-five men, mending eleven miles of river road and the old landing so that a Duvrenne barge could come up in the autumn.

A hundred and forty-three men. Living out since the second week of March.

He put his finger on the stone camp and worked out where the huts would be, and where a man would put a latrine trench, and where a man would go for water.

You sleep dry. Nobody argues about that. Dry ground is high ground, so the huts go up the slope.

You dig the trench where nobody has to smell it, which is downwind and out of sight of the huts, and the wind on that side of the valley comes up off the river in the afternoon, so downwind is further up the slope.

You draw water where a man can get a full bucket without breaking his back, which is a shelf of easy bank, and there is one of those on that stream and it is below the huts.

Three rules. Every one of them correct. He could not fault a single one of them and would have given the same three to any foreman who asked.

He did the timber camp and got the same answer. He did the lime camp and got the same answer with a spring line in it instead of a stream, which was worse. He did the road camp and got the same answer twice over, because the road camp had moved once in April as the work moved, and had rebuilt itself in the identical shape eleven miles further down.

Four foremen who had never met. Two of them could not read. None of them had ever seen the other three camps.

"That's not four mistakes," he said out loud, to a table. "That's one rule nobody wrote down, working perfectly."

Ferra found him there at some point past eleven and did not ask what was wrong, because the book was open at the fourth column and she had ruled the three lines herself.

"You entered these," he said.

"On the twelfth, the fifteenth and the twentieth of May, out of the foremen's returns, which come to me because wages come to me." She sat down. "I entered them as deaths at the camps with the date and the camp and I did not go further than that, because a man dies at a timber camp and it is a tree, and a man dies at a road camp and it is a bank coming down on him, and I have kept a wage book for eleven years and there is a line through a name in it most years."

"There are three in nine days."

"Yes." She did not defend it. "And the fourth column is in the same book on the same page and I did not lay one against the other, and I have been looking at that page for the last four minutes and I cannot tell you why not. I would like to be able to."

"Because you were sent a wage book," Emeric said, "and a wage book has a column for days not worked, and the column did what the column does, which is get filled in."

"That is a generous account of it."

"It's the true one. You'd have caught it in October." He shut the book. "In October you'd have had eleven days and nothing else on your desk. In May you had the return, because I gave you the return."

He told Vantic in the morning because Vantic was going to be told and there was no version where hearing it later improved anything.

Vantic listened without a word, which took four minutes, and then asked two questions.

"Does it delay the granary."

"I don't know yet."

"How many men are at the four camps."

Emeric opened his mouth to say a hundred and forty-three, and found that he only had a hundred and forty-three because he had counted it off a map at eleven o'clock the previous night, and that if the man had asked him a week ago he would have said something in the region of a hundred and fifty and would have said it in the tone of a man who knew.

"I found that out last night," he said.

"You have four settlements of between nineteen and fifty-five persons standing on your ground since the second week of March," Vantic said, "and the number was not in your head."

"No."

"That is the entry, then. Not the fever." He got his book out. "I will have to write it and I would rather you heard the sentence from me than read it in August."

"Write it," Emeric said, and then heard the rest of it come out of his mouth before he had inspected it. "Write it, and put a line in there about how much of my week goes on producing a return for a man who has never once put a spade in the ground in this barony, and see whether your house finds that useful."

Ferra was in the room. That was the part he thought about afterward.

Vantic did not react at all, which was worse than if he had.

"That was unearned and I withdraw it," Emeric said, immediately, out loud, in front of her. "You asked me a correct question and I did not have the answer and I went for you because you were the nearest thing in the room. It's the second time I've done that this year and the first one was also you."

"It is a common reaction to me," Vantic said. "I am generally in the room when a man finds out something he did not want to know, and there is no way to be in that room and not be part of it." He turned a page. "For the record, since you have raised it. The return takes about six hours of your week and it is the reason your Mistress Ferra had a fourth column in front of her at all, and I would have gone to my grave without pointing that out if you had not obliged me."

"Get out of my long room."

"Yes," Vantic said, and did, and stopped at the door in the way he had. "It will not delay the granary. I have looked. Stone is ahead and lime is ahead and the frame is the only thing on that critical path and the timber camp is behind by nine days out of forty. You may spend the ride worrying about something else."

He sent for Imani at first light and got the answer from the vestry, from one of the four women she had spent the week of the battle teaching, who gave it to him without any sense that she was giving him anything.

Imani was at the timber camp. Imani had been at the timber camp since Thursday. Before that she had been at the lime camp for six days and before that at the stone camp, and she had been out at the camps for the better part of a month, and had come back into town twice in that time, and had gone out again.

"Who did she tell she was going," Emeric said.

The woman looked at him with polite confusion, the way you look at a man who has asked something that does not have an answer in it.

"The camps," she said.

Ward had a horse in the yard before Emeric had finished getting his coat, which meant Ward had had it saddled since about the time the wage book had gone quiet upstairs the night before, and Emeric did not ask him about that.

He got up and then sat there a moment with the reins in his hands.

"I put it to Wexler in April of last year," he said. "Move the latrines and the burial ground below the wells, or move the wells above both, whichever's cheaper to dig. I fought him for eleven days over it and I won and it was the first thing I won in this place."

"Aye."

"I wrote it as a victory," Emeric said. "A victory's about a place. A rule's about anywhere. I've spent a year standing in front of people telling them the difference between the two, and I never wrote the sentence down, so it stayed a story about a town, and four men who have never been to that town built four camps out of ordinary good sense and every one of them came out the same shape."

"It's eleven miles," Ward said. "You'll be there by noon."
