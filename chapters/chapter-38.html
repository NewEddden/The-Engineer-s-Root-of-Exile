Chapter 38 - Porous Pots

The potter's yard was behind the market, on the slope below the canal ring, and it was the only yard in Kaldo with smoke coming out of it every day of the week.

He was a short broad man with clay to the elbows and a kick-wheel under a lean-to roof, and he did not stop the wheel when Emeric came in. The kiln sat at the bottom of the yard like a beehive made of brick, banked and ticking. Along the wall beside it, on planks, stood the pots that had not come out right: lopsided jugs, bowls warped into ovals, crocks with a crack in the rim, and a long row of plain brown cylinders that had been fired once and never glazed.

"Seconds," the potter said, when he saw where Emeric was looking. "Half price. Quarter, if you'll take them all."

"Why weren't those glazed?"

"Because the glaze costs more than the pot, and those were for the brewer, and the brewer died." The wheel went round. "They hold water, more or less. Sweat like a horse. Fine for keeping it cool, no good for keeping it."

Emeric picked one up. It was the height of his forearm and as wide as his hand, flat-bottomed, open at the top, with walls about as thick as a finger. He held it up to the light at the top of the yard and could see nothing through it, and when he put his wet thumb against the outside and blew into the mouth he felt his breath come through the clay, faintly, everywhere at once.

"I'll take them all," he said.

The potter stopped the wheel then.

"You want my mistakes."

"I want those ones. And then I want more of them, the same shape, fired the same way and left the same way. Not glazed. Hard enough to stand, not so hard they stop sweating."

"Water jars I fire soft and leave bare on purpose," the potter said. "Those were brewer's pots. They were meant for the glaze." He looked at the row of cylinders, and back at the prince, as a man looks at a customer who has asked for a loaf with no bottom crust. "You want a brewer's pot made like a water jar."

"Yes."

"Same price as the good ones."

"Same price as the good ones," Emeric agreed, and the potter, who had plainly expected to haggle and was now unsure what he had won, started the wheel again.



The pot was the wall. The zinc was the other thing, and the zinc was harder.

The brown stuff out of the spoil, the stuff the miners threw away and the old man had no name for, was zinc ore. Emeric had been sure of it on the hill, by the weight and the shine and the way it broke, and surer when he scratched it and the streak came out pale. But zinc was a metal that did not want to be a metal. Heat its ore with charcoal hot enough to give it up and it came off as a vapor, not a liquid, and the moment that vapor touched the air it burned to a white ash. In an open hearth it went up the chimney and fell on the lane as snow.

So it had to be heated shut in. Roasted first, to drive off the sulfur, which made the lane hate him for another two days. Then ground, and mixed with charcoal, and packed into a clay retort, a thick round-bellied pot with a long neck that the potter made him to a drawing and charged him extra for, sealed at the mouth with clay, and the neck run out of the fire into a clay pipe that ended in a pot set in hot ash outside the fire. Heat the belly till the zinc boiled off inside. Let the vapor run down the neck into the cooler pot, away from the air, and come out of it as metal.

The first retort did nothing for a day and then cracked.

The second did nothing for most of a day and then, in the late afternoon, a thin white smoke came out round the seal at the mouth, and the pot at the end of the pipe, when they opened it, held a fluff of white flakes like wool off a sheep's back and nothing else.

"That's it burned," Emeric said. "It got as far as the pot and found air."

Bram had come up to watch the second one with his arms folded, because he had built the hearth it sat in and wanted to see it fail with his own eyes.

"Your fire's wrong," he said.

"I know."

"Hot here, cold there, never the same two minutes running." He nodded at the hearth, where the charcoal was settling and flaring as charcoal did. "It's a forge fire. It's built to go up and down. You want a fire that sits still at one heat for a day, you want a different fire."

"There isn't a different fire."

"Then you want a different man to tend it," Bram said, "and there isn't one of those either," and went back to his forge.



The third retort he held himself.

He built the charcoal fire round it as before, and let it come up as before, and then, when the belly of the retort was glowing a bright orange in the shade of the hearth, he put his hand out to the fire and took hold of it, and did not let go.

He did not drive it this time, as he had driven the flame against the rock face on the hill. He did the opposite. He spread it. He laid it round the belly of the retort like a hand round a cup, and when it wanted to climb he held it down, and when the charcoal under it slumped and the heat started to fall he drew the flame in closer and fed it, and when it flared he let it out a little and gave it room, so that the orange of the clay stayed the same orange, the same depth, hour after hour. It was not hard, any one minute of it. It was only that there were no minutes off.

The fire glyph sat in the air above the hearth all afternoon. Tess, on the bellows, stopped pumping in the first hour because he told her to, and after that she sat on the floor against the wall with her knees up and watched the glyph and the retort and his face in turn, and did not say anything, and did not go home.

In the fifth hour a bluish flame began to flicker at the end of the clay pipe, very small, where it went into the pot, and he knew what it was and did not let himself hope.

In the seventh hour he let the fire go.

He sat down on the floor beside Tess, because his legs had decided it before he had, and the hearth burned down by itself to an ordinary fire, untidy and going down and going up, and he sat with his back against the wall and his eyes shut and his head pounding like a forge, and let Tess open the pot.

She tipped it into her hand.

Grey beads, a little bluish, the size of lentils and smaller, some of them run together into a crooked twig. A small heap of them in the cup of her palm. She held her hand still and looked at them, and then she weighed them, the way she weighed everything, by moving her hand up and down an inch.

"Zinc," he said, without opening his eyes.

"Out of the stuff they threw on the heap."

"Out of the stuff they threw on the heap."

She closed her hand round them, carefully, and did not open it again until she found a jar to put them in.

It worked every time after that, while he was holding it. Bram tried a retort on his own, with his own fire, built his own way, which was better than Emeric's way in every respect but one, and got white wool. Tess tried, with Bram watching her, and got white wool and a cracked neck. The fire had to sit still for seven hours, and the only thing in Kaldo that could make a fire sit still for seven hours was Emeric, sitting still beside it with his hand out.

Tess cast the beads. She built a mold in a box of damp sand, pressing a peg into it to make a row of holes as long as her finger and as thick as a quill, and melted the zinc in a ladle over Bram's fire, which it did at a heat that surprised her, far below iron's, below copper's, barely past lead's. It poured like dull silver and set at once. She knocked the rods out of the sand still warm and laid them on the bench in a row, and measured each one against her thumb, and took a file to the one that came out long until it was not.



The dyehouse became the battery house in the second week of June, and nobody decided it. The bench was there and the vats were there and the lime pit was dug, and things that stank or ate wood went there already, and so the jars went there too.

A jar was this. One of the potter's good glazed crocks, the kind a household kept its pork in, with a plate of copper bent round inside it, standing in a blue water. The blue was copper too: the trimmings off Bram's plates, put into the oil of vitriol and warmed on the hearth until the oil had eaten them, and then thinned, when it turned the color of a summer sky. It took a long day and gave off a choking smoke, and when it was done Tess held the jar up to the window and did not say anything for so long that Abloh asked her if she was praying. Inside that, standing in the blue, one of the potter's sweating brown cylinders, the bad ones made on purpose. Inside the cylinder, the acid, thinned with water, and standing in the acid, a short thick rod of zinc that Tess had cast in a sand mold from Emeric's beads.

Two waters in one jar, kept apart, and touching through the clay.

The first one went together on a Thursday, at noon, with Bram in the doorway and Abloh on his stool and Rylan leaning on the doorframe eating an apple. Tess joined the wires.

The needle swung off north and stopped there, and stayed.

It did not creep back. There were no bubbles on the copper. When Emeric lifted the copper out at the end of the hour to look, the face of it was clean and pink, without a bubble on it. The copper in the blue water was going onto the plate instead of the gas, which was the whole of the trick, and the zinc in the pot, with the wire off, was eaten so slowly that a rod would see out the summer, which was the rest.

At dusk the needle was still off north.

Rylan bet Declan two coppers that it would be back at north by morning. Declan, who had never once in Emeric's hearing taken the other side of anything Rylan said, took it.

In the morning the needle was off north. Rylan paid Declan in front of the whole battery house, slowly, one coin at a time, and asked whether there was any way at all of betting on a thing like that and winning, and Tess said, without looking up from her board, that he could bet it would still be there tomorrow.

It was there the next day. And the day after that.

On the third evening Bram came up from the forge with his apron still on and stood at the end of the bench looking at the compass.

"How long?"

"Three days."

Bram grunted. He looked at the jar, and lifted the lid of the brown pot an inch to look at the zinc, and put it back, and looked at the needle again.

"Hm," he said, and went back to the forge.

The potter brought the first of the made-on-purpose pots up the hill himself, in a handcart, a dozen of them packed in straw. He set them out on the dyehouse floor in a row and stood over them with his hands on his hips.

"Fired them soft," he said. "Soft as I dared. Thirty years I've been firing brewer's pots, and the first time anybody pays me full price for one, it's to leave it half done." He picked one up and blew into it, the way he had seen Emeric do in the yard, and felt the outside with his thumb, and grunted. "They'll sweat. They'll sweat like a carter in June."

The old dyer from the end of the lane came to see what had been done to her vats. She walked down the bench past the jars and the blue water and the brown pots, slowly, with her stained hands behind her back, and stopped at the fourth vat from the end, where the copperas grew.

"Still the black," she said, satisfied, and then she sniffed. "It smells worse than when we had it."

Nobody argued with her.



By the end of June there were twelve jars on the dyehouse bench, wired in a row, copper of each to the zinc of the next. The needle in Abloh's compass did not twitch at them any longer. It swung its full quarter turn and stayed there.

Ferra came up to look at them. She stood at the end of the bench and read the row of jars from one end to the other, as she read a column, and asked what each one had cost.

He told her. Copper in every jar, and the copper was Grayrock's, and the next of it would be a fifth over, and some of it went into the blue water and stayed there. The potter's crocks. The potter's seconds, at the price of the good ones. Charcoal. The oil of vitriol, which was spoil and a fortnight of the dyehouse and Bram's useless iron. And the zinc, which was seven hours of him on the floor of Bram's hearth for every handful.

She wrote it all down. Then she looked at the last line.

"How do you price that?" she said.

"I don't know."

"Then I won't." She wrote, in her small plain hand, against the zinc: Seven hours. No price.

She closed the book on her finger and looked at the twelve jars and the pinned needle.

"Does it do anything yet?" she said. "Apart from that."

"Not yet."

"When?"

He looked along the bench, at the copper going bright in the blue and the brown pots sweating quietly in their crocks, and at the wire that ran off the end of the row and nowhere yet.

"When there's a wire long enough to put it at the other end of," he said.
