Chapter 38 - The Lane

He did not give a speech.

He thought about it, in the way a man thinks about a thing he already knows he is not going to do, and got as far as composing two sentences and then discarded both of them because neither was true enough to say out loud to a hundred and thirty people who were going to be shot at in the morning.

What he did instead was walk the line, which is what he had done before every other piece of work in three years.

Down the flank, along the firing positions, past the whitewashed post and the painted stone. He checked the bolt supply with Abloh, who had it stacked in three places rather than one and had worked out for himself that a cart is a single point of failure, and who was mildly offended at being asked.

Imani was awake. Doran was awake.

Ferra had the count of everything in the barony written in a book she was carrying and did not open once, because she knew all of it and the book was for afterward.

Ward found him at the top of the flank at some point after midnight.

"You stand behind the second stake with the reserve. You do not move forward of it for any reason. If the flank goes, you go over the back of it to the ford path and you do not stop to look at anything."

"And if the lane goes."

"Then you have already gone over the back of it," Ward said, "because I will have told you to."

It was the tone of a man giving directions to a market. Second turning, past the cooper's, you cannot miss it.

The bridge came up an hour before light and Emeric settled it in four words.

Four men had been standing on it for three weeks with an axe each and a stack of fuel, and the sergeant of them wanted to know the order for the day, and it was a fair question and it had a wrong answer sitting right in the middle of it.

Drop it and the town was safe. Eighteen hundred people behind water and rock and nobody could reach them, and everything the barony owned was already inside, and he could stand on the wall and watch the field burn and survive the winter.

And the coalition would sit on the west bank and eat, and be in no hurry, and Kaldo would come out in the spring with less of everything onto ground they had had four months to choose.

"The bridge stays."

He gave the rest of it to Ward in front of the four men.

"He decides. Not me. If he tells you to drop it you drop it and you do not look at me first, and I will not countermand him and I am saying that now in front of witnesses so that nobody has to work it out at speed later."

Ward accepted it with a nod and nothing else.

Emeric went up onto the wall and stood in the dark for a while and did not particularly enjoy having said it, because handing another man the authority to seal you out of your own town is a thing you can only do once.

They came down the river road at the middle of the morning.

The crop stood green on both sides of it, waist high and moving, and the column came through it the way a thread comes through cloth, and from the wall of Kaldo the first thing anybody saw was dust and the second thing was that the dust did not stop.

Two hundred men is not a large army. It is a small one. In the histories Emeric had read in another life, two hundred men was a footnote in a paragraph about a road.

It is also more armed men than any living person in Kaldo had ever seen in one place, and the wall was full of people who had come up to look, and the sound on the wall changed as the column came into view and then stopped altogether.

A woman near him said her son's name, once, quietly, not as a call. Her son was on the flank. There was nothing to be done about it and Emeric did not try to do anything about it.

They deployed in the order their politics demanded and it told him everything.

Grayrock went forward. Not a vanguard, a commitment, the bulk of Renner's strength pushed to the front of the road with their own colours up and no interval behind them, which is what a man does when he means to collect personally and does not intend to share.

Wayford spread wide on the right, filling in across the open ground toward the water, in a formation that looked like support and was actually a man keeping his options.

And Milford came on at the back and kept coming on at the back, at a pace that was not quite slow enough to be an insult and was nowhere near fast enough to be anything else, in a column that was noticeably longer and noticeably worse dressed than either of the others.

Emeric stood on the wall and read the whole of it in about a minute.

They thought this was going to be short. Everything in the shape of them said so. There was no reserve. There was no second line worth the name. Nobody had sited anything against being shot at, because you do not site against being shot at by forty men with bad steel.

Their picture of this barony was eighteen months old and their headcount in it was correct, which was the part he found genuinely unpleasant. Forty-odd men. That was right. It had been right when Wexler's people walked out of the Western Gate and it was right now.

Every single other thing in it was wrong, and they had no way to know.

The ground started working before anyone was brave.

There was no wall out there and nothing that looked like a fortification. There was a spoil bank where spoil had to go, a ditch where a ditch made sense, a line of stumps nobody had pulled, three brush piles, a broken cart, and four hundred feet of ground along the low side that had been under a foot of water since the sluice was opened at dawn.

A column of two hundred men coming off a road onto open ground spreads out, because men spread out. And this column spread out, and found the wet on its left, and found the stumps and the bank on its right, and did what every human being does when presented with easy walking next to difficult walking.

By the time Grayrock's front was properly committed it was ninety feet wide.

Nobody had chosen it. That was the thing Emeric stood there and watched and could not find a name for. There had been no trap and no trick and nothing anybody could have pointed at. Two hundred men had each made a free and sensible decision about where to put their feet, one at a time, for four hundred yards, and every one of those decisions had been arranged six weeks earlier by two men with a rope.

He watched it happen and felt something that was not triumph and was not guilt and did not correspond to any word he had in either language.

The first volley was ragged.

It was ragged because a hundred and thirty people who have never shot at a man do not shoot at a man tidily, and because the first one is the first one and there is nothing to be done about that.

The second was not ragged. The second went out inside eleven seconds of the first and went into the same ground, and the reason was that these people had stood on this exact hill in the dark and done this exact thing until it stopped requiring them to think.

Nobody estimated a distance all morning. Not one person. There was a whitewashed post in the hedge and a painted stone below it and a scratch on a fence rail, and the crew captains called a marker rather than a number, and every crew on the flank knew what the markers meant because they had been walking past them for a month.

Hollis loaded faster than anyone had any right to expect. He had a rhythm inside four minutes, foot in the stirrup, crank, seat, and he did not look at what he was shooting into once, all morning, not one time, and he was sick behind the bank afterward for a long while and nobody said anything to him about either thing.

Colm's hands went on the fourth bolt. They simply stopped working, the way hands do, and he stood there for three or four seconds with the crank in them doing nothing at all.

Marit, who ran the Tuesday shift on the soap rota and had six children and had never in her life held a weapon before that spring, took the crank out of his hands without a word and without looking at him and shot the bolt.

Colm fed her for the next two hours. Neither of them ever discussed it and both of them were still alive forty years later.

The inversion was the quiet thesis of the whole war and nobody on that field had time to notice it.

Kaldo's bolts went through coalition harness. Not through the good stuff, of which there was almost none out there, but through the ordinary mail and the light plate and the boiled leather that two hundred provincial levies were standing in, at ninety paces, downhill, off a machine that did not care how strong the person holding it was.

And coalition arrows came back and skated off black steel.

The kingdom's entire armour doctrine was mediocre plate over good crystals, and it was not stupid and it had not been stupid for four hundred years, because what a magic knight fights is other magic and other magic is what the crystals are for. Against a working, that arrangement is excellent. Against a machine that puts a steel point through the plate at ninety paces because nobody in the chain has thought about the plate in a century, it is a man wearing an expensive shirt.

Two or three people on that field worked it out. All of them worked it out inside the lane, and none of them got the sentence out of the lane.

The mages opened at about the twenty-minute mark and that was the dangerous part.

Five of them, grey-robed, hired out of the backcountry through Sethe's people and paid with Renner's money, because Renner had money and would not spend it on better.

They were not good. Emeric would learn that later and it did not matter at all in the moment, because five men working together against people standing in the open is not a question of quality.

The flank came apart for about ninety seconds. Fire went along the top of the bank and two crews stopped existing as crews, and the noise that came off the hill was not a noise Emeric had heard before and he did not want to hear it again. People went down. People rolled. Somebody was screaming in a way that did not stop.

Kaldo had no answer to it in the ordinary way. There was no counter-mage. There was one man behind the second stake who could pull water thin across a flame.

So he threw the stone.

It was not an explosion. There was no flash and no sound worth the name, and half the field never knew anything had happened at all.

What there was, at the point where it landed, was a wrongness. A pressure behind the teeth. A note that was not a note. Every person on that hill felt it and not one of them could have said what they had felt, and two of the volunteers threw their hands up over their heads for no reason they could afterward explain.

And then there was a patch of the world about a hundred and forty feet across in which nothing worked.

Three of the five died in it. Emeric did not watch that part and did not need to. A man holding a great deal of moving power at the instant the world stops permitting it does not get a chance to put it down.

He had been carrying those stones for a year knowing exactly what they were. He had written down what they were, in the book, in plain words, and had used the word vile about them, and had made three anyway.

What he noticed, standing behind the second stake with two more of them in a satchel against his hip, was that he did not feel as bad as he had expected to.

And what disturbed him, for the rest of the morning and for a considerable time afterward, was not the act. It was that.

Declan's suit died eleven seconds later.

The falloff was wrong. It had been measured three times on empty ground at the far end of the ninth division with stakes at intervals and Rylan walking out from the centre calling the edge, and the figure had been consistent across all three throws and Emeric had underlined the radius in the book because it was the number men would have to stand outside of.

That ground had been empty. There had been one charged crystal on it and nothing else.

This ground had ninety men in harness on it, half of them with charged steel on their bodies, and it went over all of them and kept going, and it went a great deal further than a hundred and forty feet.

Bram's threshold breaker fired the way Bram had built it to fire, took the coupling apart and dumped the crystal out of circuit before it could be wrung to nothing, and Declan went from an Enhanced knight to a man in extremely good plate between one step and the next.

He was not hurt. He was not even knocked down. He was, for the remaining three hours, absolutely furious, in a controlled and entirely functional way, and he killed four men in the lane with an ordinary sword while being furious.

Ward worked out what had happened before Emeric did.

He came back along the bank with his visor up and blood on him that was not his, and he did not slow down, and what he said as he went past was four words.

"It rolled over us."

Then he was gone down the line and there was nobody to say anything to.

Emeric stood behind the second stake and filed it, because there was nothing else available to do with it. His own weapon, his own design, his own year of work, had come off his own hand and taken the crystal out of one of the four men in the barony he could not replace. The radius in his own book was wrong. He had measured it himself. He had measured it three times.

He could not do one thing about any of that for the next hour.

He cast three times all morning and none of it was impressive.

Water pulled thin across a flame, first, which put out a fire on the bank that would otherwise have taken four crews off the hill, and which was the first drill he had ever run in the room nobody knew about.

A blade of moving air, second, twice, into the mouth of the lane where the press was thickest, which did not kill anybody outright and which knocked eleven men off their feet into ground that other men were walking through.

And a slab of stone raised a hand's breadth and dropped, third, late, under the front rank at the narrow point, which was the cheapest thing he did all day and which cost the coalition more than either of the others.

Three drills. The same three, in the same order, that he had been running for two years on a bench in a place nobody had ever seen him enter.

A court mage would have watched all three and been bored. Four grey-robes had come onto that field with a wider repertoire than his and more raw strength than his, and by the middle of the morning three of them were dead and one had stopped being able to do anything at all, and Emeric was still standing behind a stake with more left than he had started the hour with any right to expect.

The difference was not talent. It was that he had done these three things several thousand times and had never once done a fourth.

Renner came forward at the back end of the morning.

Grayrock's front was bleeding in the lane and had been bleeding for an hour, and there was a point at which it was going to come apart or somebody was going to hold it together, and the man came forward himself to hold it together.

Mounted. Visible. Colours up, with two men beside him and no attempt whatsoever to be difficult to see.

He was not a coward. Emeric understood that immediately and never revised it. Whatever else was true about Baron Renner of Grayrock, he had put nearly everything he owned into that lane and he came forward into it himself when it started to fail, and there were a great many lords in that kingdom who would not have.

And he pulled them back together. It took him about two minutes. The bleeding front stopped being a thing coming apart and started being a thing standing still, and then started, slowly, to be a thing coming forward again.

He did all of it standing inside the dead zone, where no working of any kind could reach him, and where he believed, entirely correctly, that whatever Kaldo had just done to his mages had also switched off everything Kaldo had.

He was fifty yards from the mouth of the lane.

Emeric could see him perfectly well, and could not touch him with anything at all.
