Chapter 362 - The Word Room

He lost the word for fish on the second of July, at about half past five in the morning, sitting on an upturned crate in the Cradle's watch room with a pad on his knee.

He had meant to write the sixty-one out fresh for the first session, in a clean column, so that whoever sat with her first would have them in front of them. He had written water, and the sound beside it, and hand, and stone, and hurt, and they had come up the way everything came up for him, whole, with the light on them. Then he had reached for the heavy fish on the cook's board and got the board. He got the rice under the slices, and the saucer, and the trough's edge where she had set it down. He got her face as she gave him the sound. He didn't get the sound.

He sat there and tried again, and it was like putting his hand into a coat pocket for a key he'd felt there all week.

By six he had gone down the column twice. Twenty-two of them were gone. Not blurred; gone, as if they'd never been handed over. Another nine he had the sound for and nothing with it, a noise in his head with no picture behind it, and four he had the picture for and no sound at all. The rest were there. He wrote those down, and looked at what he had, and it was a good deal shorter than sixty-one.

Armand came up the iron stair at a quarter past with two cups, because he had seen the lamp from the yard, and read the column over Emeric's shoulder without being asked.

"Twenty-six," he said.

"I've read every book I ever opened. I can give you the page. I can give you where the page was torn." Emeric put the pencil down. "I can't keep a word for fish."

"You didn't read it." Armand gave him a cup and sat down on the bench under the window. He sat as though he had been sitting on benches for ninety years, and his body hadn't been told, and was too polite to mention it. "You didn't see it, you didn't hear it. She lent it to you. It's one of her memories and it's sitting in you behaving exactly like one of hers. She forgets things she doesn't use. So do you, it turns out, if they aren't yours to begin with."

"Zale kept his."

"Zale said every one of them to her every morning for six months and got shouted at when he was wrong. That's using it." Armand drank. "And she never stopped, I suppose. She hasn't had the choice. She's been living on the ones she took off us."

Emeric looked at the pad. It made a kind of sense he didn't like. The delve could put a thing into a man's head. It couldn't make him keep it, any more than a good dinner could make him strong if he lay in bed for a month afterward.

"So it's no good," he said.

"It's very good. It's just not enough on its own." Armand took a watch out of his waistcoat, an old silver thing with a tower's mark worn nearly smooth on the case, and set it on the windowsill between them, face up. "Short reads. Forty minutes, no more, and I'll call it. She gives, we give, both ways, so she's getting ours while we're getting hers. Then whoever sat goes straight out and says every word aloud to somebody with ears, the same day, and keeps on saying them. The read's the lesson. The talking is the homework." He looked at the column again. "You'll be the worst at it."

"Thank you."

"You'll know more words than any of us and you'll sound like a man trying to sing with a mouthful of bread. You'll see."



Fletcher gave them the loft over the mess hut, where he'd been keeping sailcloth and two broken oars and a box of something he said was valuable and wouldn't open. It had a window over the bay and a table and, once the cloth was out, a smell of tar that never quite went. Wren came over on the Hod's Thursday run with a crate of Tess's spares and chalked the name on the door in letters a foot high, WORD ROOM, without asking anybody, and then stood back and looked at it and added a fish underneath. Nobody rubbed it off.

Ferra put the rota on a nail beside the chalk. Monday Emeric. Tuesday Imani. Wednesday Armand. Thursday Ferra. Friday whoever had lost the most. Saturday nobody, because Ilenne had told her, through Zale and then more firmly without him, that she wanted one day in seven when no one went into her head, and Ferra had written Saturday: hers and underlined it.

The sessions were quiet to watch. Two people at the table with their hands together and their eyes half shut, and Armand on the bench under the window with the watch open, and nothing happening that anyone could see. At forty minutes he said "Time," not loudly, and they let go, and whoever it was went down the outside stair to the slab where Zale sat on a coil of rope with his stick across his knees and Wren sat on the edge with her boots in the air, and said everything they'd got.

That was the part Armand had been right about.

Emeric came down on the first Monday with forty new words, clean, the picture and the sound together, and said the first one, which was a kind of rope the shallows families used for tying kelp to a frame, and Zale shut his eyes.

"Again."

He said it again.

"You've put the stop in the wrong place," said Zale. "It's after the lift, not before. You're saying it like a man going up a step and falling off the top."

"I've got her memory of it. I can hear exactly how it goes."

"Aye. And then your mouth gets hold of it." Zale opened one eye. "Wren. Say his rope."

Wren hadn't been in the room. She hadn't been in anybody's head. She had been sitting on the slab for twenty minutes listening to Ilenne say the word through the loft window, in between other things, and she said it now with her chin in her hand, not even looking round, and it came out with the stop after the lift and the little fall at the end, the way it had sounded in his head.

Up at the loft window, Ilenne laughed. It came down onto the slab with the click under it.

"She's got the ear," said Zale. He didn't say it kindly. He said it the way Fletcher talked about a rival yard's welding, and it was the warmest thing Emeric had heard out of him since June.

"I listen to bearings all day," said Wren. "Tess says a bearing tells you it's going a week before it goes, if you've got the ear for it."

"It's not a bearing."

"Everything's a bearing," said Wren, and said the rope again, to make her point, a little better.

It went on like that for most of July. The four who sat with her came out with the most words and the worst mouths. Zale and Wren, who had never had a word put into them, sounded like the shallows by the end of the month, and knew a third as much. And Ilenne, who was getting all four of them, every day but Saturday, and then spending what she got from the minute the read let go, came down the outside stair at the end of each session talking. She was talking at the cook. She was talking at Fletcher's men and at the seals. She ran out of words at least once a sentence and walked straight through the gap on her hands.

"She's better at us than we are at her," said Imani, on the third Tuesday, sitting on the bottom step with her boots off. "That's not fair."

"She's got more reason," said Armand.

"I've got plenty of reason. I'm going to be the only physician in a city of fish. I want to be able to say where does it hurt without drawing it."



Imani's sessions were the body. Everybody had known they would be. She had sat down at the table on the first Tuesday with her hands out and said, before Armand had even opened the watch, "Not fish. Not rope. I don't care about rope. I want her bones, and her blood, and the inside of her ear, and every word she's got for pain, and how her people tell one sort from another, because I've been guessing for a year and a half and some of my guesses have been stupid." Then she had stopped, with her hands still out, and looked at Ilenne across the table. "Except first."

Ilenne waited.

"Two words first," said Imani. "Before anything. The one you say when you want somebody to stop. And the one you say when it's all right to go on." She put her own hand flat on the table, palm down, the way Ilenne had put hers on the slate in Fletcher's cabin and on the water for the captain. "This is good. I've worked with this. I've worked with you shutting your eyes, and going slate, and the noise you make. I've done the best I could with all of it. I don't want to do the best I could anymore. I want you to tell me."

Ilenne looked at the hand for a while. Then she reached across and took Imani's wrists, both of them, and Armand opened the watch.

She gave both words in the first minute, as it turned out. Imani said afterward that they'd come before anything else in the read, before Imani had properly asked, as if they'd been kept at the front of a drawer. The word for stop had the stop in it, the lock gate Zale had heard on the first morning. The other was short and had a lift at the end like a question, and was the same word, nearly, as the one Zale had been calling again since January.

"Go on," said Imani, on the slab. "Again. It's both. It's more of that." She said it to Wren, who said it back, and then to Zale, who corrected the vowel. "I've been using it to make her do her breathing exercises. For six months. I've been saying more of that at her and pointing at her chest."

"Did it work?"

"That's not the point."

She used them the next morning at the check, sitting on the iron rim of the tank with her sleeves rolled and her sight open, narrow, the way she opened it for a child. She asked before she put her hands on, in Ilenne's word, with the lift at the end, and Ilenne gave her the other word back before she'd finished asking. Imani went over the gills, and the long muscles of the back, and the healed place low on the left of her back, where the bident had gone in and Imani's knife after it, slowly, the way she always did. Halfway down her back Ilenne said the word with the lock gate in it.

Imani took her hands off at once. Both of them, in the air, open.

"What is it? Is that sore? Where..."

"Cold," said Ilenne, in Kaldo, with enormous dignity. "Hands."

Imani looked at her own hands. Then she blew on them, and rubbed them together hard, and held them out for inspection, and Ilenne felt one of them with the back of her fingers and said the other word, and Imani went on.

Emeric, in the doorway with the coffee, had stopped walking.

"Don't," said Imani, without turning round.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were standing there feeling something. I can hear it. Put the cup down, I'll want it in a minute." She went on down. "Eighteen months," she said, quieter, to nobody in particular, and then nothing else about it.



Ferra's book was a ledger. Of course it was. She'd bought it from the stationer under the arch in Kaldo, a long brown one with a cloth spine, and ruled it in five columns before she wrote a word: the Kaldo word; Ilenne's word, in Ferra's own spelling; a column of small marks for how it was said, which she'd made up herself, a tick for the stop, a little hook for the lift, a dot for the click at the end of a thing that was meant; who it had come from, and on what day; and a fifth column, very narrow, with nothing in it yet and no heading.

"What's that one for?" said Emeric.

"I don't know yet. There's always one."

She took the words off everybody. She sat on the slab in the evenings while they said them for Zale, and took them down, and when two people had come out with different spellings for the same thing she made them both say it until Zale picked one. The first Kaldo-merfolk dictionary was being made on a coil of rope, mostly by a woman telling her husband that he didn't say it like that, and Emeric thought, watching her, that most first things probably had been.

The counting was hers alone. She hadn't asked for it. She had simply taken it.

On the night before Zale's run she had sat up in the mess hut with the reckoning shell, while he slept, and laid a sheet of paper over the inside and gone over it with the flat of a pencil until every groove came up white on grey. The shell was at the bottom of the thick water now, somewhere a long way west. The rubbing was in the back of her book, folded in four, soft at the creases from being unfolded. She had brought it to her first Thursday session and laid it on the table between them and pointed, and Ilenne had looked at it with an expression Emeric had seen on Ferra's own face when somebody showed her their household accounts.

It took three Thursdays. He wasn't in the room for any of them. He saw the results at the mess table afterward, in pencil, on scrap, with a good deal crossed out.

"It's not tens," Ferra said, on the third. "I thought it would be tens. Everyone thinks everything's tens. It's not anything I've got a name for yet, and it doesn't matter, I'll have it by August. That's not the interesting part." She turned the scrap round. "This is."

It was the rubbing, with rings drawn round four of the marks on it.

"She said the shell told you what was overhead in a season. I thought she meant the sun. Or stars." Ferra put her finger on the first ring, then the others. "It's the moons. All four. The big one and the far one and the two small ones. You can't see the small ones from under the water, you'd think, but you can, from the shallows, at night, when it's still. They time everything by where the four of them sit against each other. It's a date. Every one of these is a date." She sat back. "Every date she's given us since she got here, she's given us in moons, and we've been writing down the day we heard it."

"That's why you drew them," said Emeric. "In June. At the bottom of Saban's page."

"I didn't know why I drew them." She looked at the rubbing. "I do now."

He knew what it meant before she said it, and she said it anyway. Every depth Ilenne would give them, every distance, every date, the length of the voyage and where the wall began and how long to wait at the door, would come to them in that counting, and somebody in Kaldo had to be able to read it without a land man on a stick in the middle doing sums in his head and getting them wrong. That somebody was going to be Ferra. She was already halfway there and pretending she wasn't.

He leaned over and put his mouth against the side of her neck, under the ear, where the pencil smell was.

"Bed," he said.

"After."

"It's nearly eleven."

"Four moons," said Ferra, "and I can't make two of them sit still." She didn't move her neck away, though. She tipped her head an inch further, in fact, to give him more of it, and went on writing. "Ten minutes."

Imani, at the other end of the bench, didn't look up from her own notes. "She said ten minutes at half past nine."

"Different ten minutes."

He gave it twenty. Then he took the pencil out of her hand, very gently, and put it in his own top pocket, and she looked at him with her hand still in the shape of holding it, and said, "Right," in a voice that was going to cost him something, and got up so fast the bench went over.



Armand's Wednesdays were the grammar, and Armand liked it more than he would admit. He came out of the loft each week looking younger than when he'd gone in, which, Imani pointed out, wasn't easy for him anymore.

He found it on the second Wednesday, and he found it by watching rather than by reading.

"She says the same word twice," he said, on the slab, "and I get two different things off her. The same word. The same sound, I'd swear to it, I've got her memory of both. And one of them's a polite thing you'd say to your mother's friends, and the other one's what you'd say to a boy who's left a gate open." He had a stick of chalk and was turning it over. "So either they've got two words that sound alike, which every language has, or there's something else in it that I'm not hearing."

"You're not hearing it," said Zale, "because it isn't a noise."

Armand looked at him.

"Look at her," said Zale. "Next time she says it. Don't listen. Look."

They did it that afternoon, the four of them on the slab and Ilenne on the edge with her feet in the bay. Armand had her say the polite word, and then the other. Emeric watched her throat. The first time, a little pallor went down it, grey-white going paler, the shade the guards had gone in the wash when they'd seen her face in the captain's lamp. The second time it went the other way, a quick darkening at the collarbone like a shadow going over.

"Again," said Armand.

She said them again. Pale. Dark.

"It's in the skin," said Armand. He said it slowly, the way he'd said things in the tower when he had the floor and wanted the back row to hear. "The colour's part of the word. Not how she feels about it, or not only that. It's grammar. It changes what the sentence means." He looked up at Zale. "You've been talking to her for a year and a half without any."

"I've been talking to her for a year and a half in black and white," said Zale. "Aye. I'd worked that out. I didn't know there was a name for it."

"There isn't. I've just made one up." Armand turned to Ilenne. "What happens," he said, carefully, in Kaldo, with his hand doing the shape of a mouth, "when a man says words with no colour?"

She understood him. Emeric watched her understand, and watched her decide how much to say, and then her eyes went to Zale, and stayed on him.

"Cold," she said.

"Cold how?"

She thought about it. Then she put her two hands together in front of her in the shape of something with a mouth, and opened them slowly, and closed them, and made it come toward Zale along the stone. She didn't make a sound with it. That was the point, Emeric saw. It came without a sound and without a colour.

"In dark," said Ilenne. "Deep. Thing come. Say..." She said it in her own tongue, the few short words Zale had called across the water to the guards at dusk on the twenty-fourth of June. She said it with no colour at all, grey, flat, and the skin along Emeric's arms tightened before he knew why. "Like that," she said. "Thing say this. Before eat."

Nobody said anything.

"What does it mean?" said Armand. "With colour."

She said it again, with the pale going down her throat. Then with a warmth Emeric hadn't got a word for, a faint brightening along the sheen. "I come to you," she said, in Kaldo, slowly, getting each one right. "Friend say. Guest say."

"And with none?"

She looked at Zale.

"Hungry," said Ilenne.

Zale sat very still on his rope for a moment. Then he put his face in his hands, and his shoulders started going, and it took Emeric a second to see that he was laughing.

"I had the right word," he said, through his fingers. "I had the right word. I said it right. I stood up in front of twelve soldiers with spears and said I come to you as perfectly as any man ever said it, in a voice like a shark." He took his hands down. His eyes were wet. "No wonder they went for me."

"You said you told them you were hungry," said Wren.

"I did tell them. I was right." He wiped his face with his sleeve. "That's the worst of it."

Ilenne had been watching him laugh, and something had happened at her throat while she watched, that she didn't turn into a word, and that Emeric took care not to look at. She looked down at her own hands instead. Then she held one up, flat, palm down, and lowered it slowly, an inch, two inches, like a woman bowing with her hand.

"This," she said. She touched her own throat, where the pale had gone. "Is this."

Armand stopped turning the chalk.

She showed them three more before the light went. The dark at the collarbone was a hand closed sharp, a snap. The warmth was two fingers drawn up the inside of the other wrist. The slate, which they all knew, which Imani's card had on it, was a hand put across the eyes. She made them up as she went, Emeric was nearly sure. She'd try one and look at it and change it. Wren had them all by the third go and was doing them at the seals.

"Land mouth," said Ilenne, to Armand, and tapped her hand. "No colour. So. Hand do colour." She did the bow with it again. "My mother's house. Teach. All." She looked round at them. "Then land man say I come to you, and nobody..." She did the mouth with her two hands, coming at them, and then let it fall open in the middle, harmless. "Nobody afraid."

"You'd teach that to the court?" said Armand.

"Yes."

"They'll do it? A queen's court? Sign with their hands for foreigners?"

She considered him, with her head on one side, and Emeric saw her find a word she'd had off Ferra on a Thursday and was proud of.

"I am daughter," said Ilenne.

Ferra wrote it down. Then, after a moment, she wrote a heading at the top of the fifth column, the narrow one, in small capitals, and turned the book round to show him. COLOUR. Under it, against the first entry in the book, which was water, she drew a little flat hand.



Saban came over on the eighteenth with nothing in his hands but his hat and one question, and he waited until the Monday session was done, which was like him, and asked it in the loft with the door shut.

He'd been asking it, Emeric knew, in one way or another since the twenty-fourth of June. He'd asked Fletcher, who didn't know. He'd asked the cook, who fed the seals scraps off the end of the pier and said the seals had been off their food for two days before the lights came and back on it the morning after. He'd had two of Fletcher's men take a punt and a lead line out past the reef on a calm day, and got nothing but rock at forty fathoms and then no bottom. He'd put a second lamp on the pier and a third on the headland and two men in a hut with a glass. He had not, until now, asked her, because a month ago there had been no way to get an answer out of her that he'd have trusted.

He sat on the bench under the window with the hat on his knee. Ilenne sat across the table from him with her hood down. She'd had Emeric in her head for forty minutes and had come out of it brighter, the way she did now, and full of words and short of patience.

"The night your mother's guard came," said Saban. "Something waited for them. Out past the white water." He drew the reef on the table with his finger, and the gap, and then a long way out past it, a mark. "Big. The king felt it. What was it?"

She looked at the mark. Then at Emeric, who nodded. Then at Saban again, and he saw her go into her own head for it, and gather, and come out carrying more than she'd ever carried in Kaldo at one time.

"Big one is my mother's," said Ilenne. "Old. It swim with soldiers when they go far, so nothing in the deep eat them, because it speak to the deep things and say no, go, and they go. It wait out there, past white water, in deep, because the reef is small and your water is thin and it hate your water, very much, and it was angry all the time it wait. And when soldiers go home, it take them home."

She stopped. She seemed slightly surprised at herself. Then she counted it off, silently, her lips moving, and Emeric realised she was going back over it for mistakes.

Nobody in the loft said anything for a moment.

"That's the longest thing she's ever said," said Ferra, from the door. She had come up the outside stair with the book and stopped there. "In Kaldo. To anybody."

"Yes," said Ilenne, and the click went at the end of it.

Saban hadn't moved. "Is it a soldier? An animal?"

She thought about the difference, and didn't seem to think much of it. "Friend," she said. "Of house. Long. Very old." She held her hands apart, and then further, and then gave up on hands and pointed out of the window, down the length of the works pier to the Hod at the end of it, and then made a small gesture beyond the Hod, out over the water, as if to say, and then some more of that. "My people speak. It hear. Many deep things, we speak, they hear. This one... best."

"Your people speak to animals."

"Speak," she agreed. Then, with some care, as if correcting a child who was nearly right: "Not to. With."

Saban looked at the mark on the table for a while. Then he did something Emeric had never once seen him do. He held his hand out to Ferra, without looking round, and Ferra, without a word, put a pencil in it, and he took his own small book out of his coat, the one he never wrote in, and wrote in it. It wasn't much. Two lines, perhaps. He blew on them as though they were ink.

"Will it come again?"

"When called."

"Who calls it?"

She smiled at him. It was not a smile Emeric had seen on her before, and he thought it was probably her mother's.

"My mother," said Ilenne.

Saban put the book away. "Then I'll stop paying two men to look at the sea through a glass," he said. "And start paying them to look at it more carefully." He put his hat on. At the door he stopped and turned it a quarter turn on his head, which nobody had known was possible. "Thank you," he said, to her. "That's the first straight answer I've had to anything since June."



Ferra called the six over for the twenty-ninth, a Tuesday, because she had two hundred and eleven words in the book by then and wanted them read through by everybody who would be standing in that hall at the door, before she copied a page of it clean. Ward came on the Hod's early run in his shirtsleeves with his coat over his arm. Bram came with him and spent the hour before the reading in the yard with Fletcher, looking at the Challenger's new valve chest and making noises about it. Saban came because he'd been asked. Zale was down on the slab with Wren, being taught the colour signs by a girl of twenty with no patience, and they could hear him through the window losing.

They sat round Fletcher's table in the loft with the window open and the bay coming in on the air. Armand had the chair by the window. There was no fire, it being July, and he looked faintly put out about it.

Ferra read. She read each word in Kaldo, and then in her own spelling, and looked at Ilenne, who said it properly, and then whoever had brought it in said it, badly, and then Ferra wrote a mark or crossed one out and went on to the next. It was slow and dull and nobody minded. Ward had his arms folded. Bram leaned his chair back on two legs, which Fletcher had asked people not to do on that floor.

They were in the hundred and forties, which were mostly trade, when Ferra came to iron.

She read it. Ilenne said it, a heavy word, low, with a sour little twist at the end that Emeric thought at first was the colour and then realised was her face. Ferra wrote the mark.

"Where does iron come from," said Ferra, mostly to herself, turning the page, "if you can't make it under the sea?" It wasn't really a question. It was the kind of thing she said aloud at the accounts to keep her place.

"Buy," said Ilenne.

Ferra stopped turning the page. "From who?"

Ilenne made a shape with her hands. She made it low, near the table, a broad flat shape like a man with his shoulders up round his ears, and then didn't have the word, and looked round the room for something to help her, and found it.

She pointed at Bram.

"Short," she said. "Like Bram. Strong arm. Hair here." She drew a beard on her own chin with two fingers, generously. "Make iron. Under mountain. Bring to top city. Dry city. We give fish, salt, pearl." Her face did the sour thing again. "Cheat. Always cheat. Price, price, price." She looked at Bram, kindly. "Bram not cheat."

Bram's chair came down onto four legs.

Ferra had not written anything. Her pen was lifted off the page a little and staying there.

Emeric heard the room go still around him. Ward's arms had come unfolded. Saban had put his hat down on the bench, very carefully, the way a man puts down something he's suddenly not sure of the weight of. Armand had turned in the window chair, and Imani, at the end of the table, had put her hand flat over her own mouth.

Ilenne looked from one to the other of them, mildly, and then down at Ferra's book, as if she might have got the word for iron wrong and that was what all this was about.

"They're alive," said Ward.

He didn't say it loudly. He said it the way he'd said the door figures faced out, five years ago at the table in the delve, with his back to the wall, as a thing he'd seen and was going to stand behind.

Ilenne turned to him. Emeric saw her not understand the question, and then go back over it and still not understand it. There wasn't anything in it to understand. It was like being asked whether the sea was wet.

"Yes?" she said.

"The short ones. The smiths." Armand's voice had gone strange. "You've seen them. Yourself. Living."

"Yes. Top city. Many." She frowned at him a little. "Two live there. In house, by the trade water." She held up two fingers, to be clear. "Two. They do not like the wet. They complain."

"And the tall ones," said Emeric. He heard his own voice from somewhere a little way off. "Tall, long faces. Long hands. In the trees."

She knew at once who he meant. Her mouth went flat. "Far," she said. "They send wood. Not come. Not let us come." She brushed something invisible off the back of her hand, which Zale would have known and none of them did, and Emeric thought it was probably not polite. "Cold people."

"The winged ones."

"Yes." Her face cleared. "They tell storms. We give fish. Fishing on the..." She made a cliff with her hand, high, and the sea at the foot of it. "Good." She looked round the table. "And more. Many more. Far. Some I don't know, only story." She shrugged, and it was a Kaldo shrug; she'd had it off Imani. "Many."

Nobody said anything for long enough that Zale's voice came up through the window from the slab, cross, saying something about his thumb.

Emeric sat with it.

He'd stood in the swept city under the Isle with his lamp up. He'd read every one of Vey's pages twice. He'd sat in this kind of quiet in his own study in the August of year eleven with the barley water going warm on its tray and the names he wasn't saying going round in his head, and he'd told himself then that a carving was a carving, and a wall was a wall, and a man who'd grown up on stories had better be very careful what he let himself want. Then a woman had come up out of the sea onto his beach. He had spent the better part of two years learning to treat her as a guest. He had not once, in all that time, let himself put the next thing on top of her. If her people were alive, who else was. He'd kept it shut on purpose, the way you keep a drawer shut that you know has something in it you'll have to deal with.

And she'd opened it at the table to explain where iron came from, and complained about the price.

"The tower taught there were none," said Armand. He was looking out of the window. "Seventy years. Eighty. I taught it. Not even taught it. You don't teach a thing that's too obvious to need teaching." He laughed, a short dry sound that wasn't much like one. "I'd have failed a boy for asking."

"Then the tower was wrong," said Imani. Her hand had come down from her mouth. "It's been wrong before. It was wrong about her density. It was wrong about the stones."

"It was wrong on purpose," said Ferra. She still hadn't written anything. "Somebody took it out of the books. Vey said so. Somebody did that, and kept doing it, for long enough that the man who ran the tower thought it was too obvious to teach." She looked at Ilenne. "And it's not even a secret. Not out there. She didn't think it was worth mentioning."

Ilenne had begun to understand. Emeric watched it come, slowly. It wasn't the slate and it wasn't the dark wash. It started somewhere behind her eyes and went nowhere on her skin at all, and he didn't know what it was, and he wasn't sure there was a word on her side or his.

"You don't know?" she said.

"No," said Emeric.

"Nobody? Here?" She turned her hand palm up, taking in the loft, the island, the bay, Kaldo across the water, the whole of the cursed land behind it. "Nobody know?"

"We thought we were the only ones left."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she said something in her own tongue, very soft, two words with a fall at the end, and her hand came up as she said it and did a sign she hadn't shown them on the slab: two fingers laid across the palm of the other hand, and held there, the way you'd hold something small that had fallen out of a nest.

Ferra wrote the word down. She didn't ask what it meant. In the narrow column, against it, she drew the sign as well as she could, two little lines across a little hand.



Saban said it, in the end. Somebody was always going to have to, and he was the one who'd said it on Hollow Night.

"Not the city."

"No," said Emeric.

"Not the school. Not Hollow Night, not Sela's class, not the News." Saban picked up his hat and looked into it. "There were other peoples here and they were driven off by men. That's this year's notch, and it's taken the better part of a year to get the town to swallow it without choking. I'll not put the next one on top of it in the same summer. If the lanes start saying the old peoples are alive and coming back, I'll have three different churches by the first frost, and one of them will be the real one, and it'll have a sermon ready." He turned the hat. "And not from her. We agreed. Not the cave, and not her."

"Not her," said Imani.

"Not her," said Ward.

Ilenne followed perhaps half of it, and of the half she followed she didn't like all of it, and said so with her face. But she had been in the long room on Hollow Night with her legs folded under her on the floor, and she had followed notch then too.

"Slow," she said.

"Slow," said Saban. "Yes."

"Crew must know," said Ward. "Before we sail. I'll not put one man over the side into the middle of somebody else's country without telling him it's somebody's."

"The crew before we sail," said Ferra, and wrote it, at last, on a fresh line at the bottom of the page, under iron: 29 July. The others live. Smiths (iron, her top city, two live there), tall ones (wood, far, cold), winged (storms). Many more. Her word for us knowing nothing: and the word, in her spelling, and its little drawn sign beside it. And under that, smaller: The six. Not the city. The crew before we sail.

She blew on it. Then, because they still had sixty-odd words to get through and a Hod to catch, she turned the page.

"One hundred and forty-seven," she said. "Salt."



Bram stayed behind after the others had gone down the stair.

Emeric only knew because he came back up for Ferra's pencil, which was still in his top pocket from a fortnight ago and which she'd suddenly decided she needed. Bram was sitting where he'd been all afternoon, at the corner of Fletcher's table, with his big forearms on it, looking at his own hands. He had them turned over, palms up, the way a man looks at his hands after a burn, to see whether it's as bad as it felt.

Ilenne was at the window, watching Zale on the slab below, who had his thumb in his mouth.

"Cheats on the price," said Bram, to the table. "Does he."

"Always," said Ilenne, without turning round.

Bram was quiet a while. Then something went across his face that Emeric had seen before, at the start of things, when there was a new machine in pieces on a bench and nobody yet knew how it went together. He closed his hands.

"Then he's never had to sell to Ferra," said Bram, and got up, and went down the stair to see about the valve chest.
