Chapter 215 - Dead Field

Ward said it on the thirteenth of November, two days after the declaration, at the long table in Kaldo with the shutters still closed against the rain.

Nobody had asked him. They had been talking about horses. Declan had the muster on a sheet in front of him, the figures that had come south with Isolde and Jabari and the figures from Saban's capital man, and none of them agreed; two thousand, or seventeen hundred, and nearly half of it horse whichever it was. Osric had said it did not matter whether it was nine hundred horse or a thousand, because either way it was a great many more than Kaldo had, which was forty. Rylan had said that was very helpful. Saban had sat with his small book shut under his hand and said nothing, as he had said nothing since the gazette came with the news that a host had been mustering in the north for three weeks and his man in the north had not sent him one word about it.

Ward stood at the window end of the table, as he always did, with his hands behind his back.

"He will not use the mages to fight," said Ward.

The table stopped.

"He has hired fourteen. Perhaps more. Gray mages, from the east workings, the kind who work for money and do not belong to a tower. The lady says so." He did not look at Isolde, who was sitting very straight at the far end beside Jabari in a borrowed Kaldo coat, but she nodded once. "Fourteen gray mages are not enough to fight Kaldo with. He knows what the K Discovery did on the capital road. He knows about the masks, or he knows enough. He knows we have the wire and the lamps and His Highness." Ward was quiet. "So he will not fight us with them. He will use them to break stones."

Emeric had known it since the quarry, with Vesk's grey face and the cheap clouded walnut of a stone in Vesk's hands. He had not let himself put that beside Saban's page. Ward had, the same night, and said nothing for three weeks, and drilled for it every Thursday in the rain.

"Saban," Emeric said. "How many stones?"

Saban opened the small book. "My man had four hundred and some by the end of October. Bought in the capital and three coast towns. He's had word of more since, but that's hearsay, and I'll not swear to hearsay."

"Size?"

"A fist. Bigger, some. Clouded. Cracked. The ones the lamp makers send back." Saban turned a page. "Paid a third above the price. Never bargained once. That's what first made my man look."

Emeric got up and went to the slate on the wall, because he could not do it sitting down.

He knew what one walnut stone had done in the quarry, and what Vantic's had done in the yard at Wayford in August. He put the figures up on the slate in chalk, the size of the stone against the reach of the dead air, cellar and yard and north road and quarry, and drew the curve through them, and it was not a straight line; a stone twice the size did more than twice. Then he put a fist on the end of the curve. Then he put four hundred fists beside it.

He stood back.

Only Ferra could read the curve. She put her pencil down.

"A field," Emeric said. "Not a yard. Not a road. A whole field. A band of stones, broken all together or near enough." He touched the chalk. "Miles of it. Everything in it dead. The lamp stones. Bram's suits on the masks; they're plate and leather after that and nothing else. The bands on the levy, the tattoos. The ship, if she's over it, and she'd fall." He stopped, and then said it, because Ward was looking at him. "And me. I'll stand in the middle of it and not feel a man of ours a yard off."

"And his knights keep what is inside them," said Ward. "As ours do. He has a great many more than we have. He has looked at Kaldo and seen a barony that fights with stones and wire and a prince who can feel a man through a wall. So he will take the stones and the wire and the prince away, all at once, in one field, and then it will be knights against farmers." He was quiet. "In every war there has ever been, that is a good bet."

Osric laughed. Nobody else did, and he stopped.

"It's not a bad plan," said Rylan after a while, to the table in general. "I want that said, before anybody gets cross. If it was us, it's what I'd do."

"It is an excellent plan," said Ward. "It is the best plan anybody has made against this house." He turned from the window. "It was made by somebody who has not stood in the quarry."



Fennick had been in the corner the whole time, on the bench under the map, where Ward had put him; and when the others had gone out to their work he was still there, turning his cap in his maimed hand, and looking at the slate.

"Highness," he said.

Emeric was rubbing out the curve. He stopped.

"Tobe's pots." Fennick got up off the bench. "On the capital road. In August." He came and stood closer to the slate than he needed to. "Clay, and a fuse through the lid. I was cross for a week that my sausage never went off, and Tobe's pots all did, and they were only smoke." He turned the cap. "I've been thinking about them since. Not in a bad way."

"Go on."

"Well. What if it wasn't clay." He looked at the cap. "And what if it wasn't smoke."

Emeric looked at him.

Fennick laughed: a short breath of a laugh through his nose, the kind he gave before the bang; and then he looked worried, as though he had been caught at something.

"I know Ward has the key," he said. "I'm not asking for anything. I'm only saying, a pot a man can throw. With powder in it. In a field where nothing else works." He held up his hands, the good one and the one with the two fingers gone, as though measuring something the size of a small loaf between them. "That size. Iron. And then it's not smoke when it goes over the chain."



Emeric drew it that night, in the end room of the drafting floor, with the rain on the skylights.

He drew it badly, the first three times. He was used to drawing things that had to work; this had to work and then come apart, and it came apart wrong on the page, in long splinters and one great lump that would go a hundred yards and kill a cow. He knew the answer, which was the trouble. He had seen it behind glass in a museum, in another life, with a card under it. He put the memory aside and drew the thing again so that it could be made here, by these people, out of what they had.

An iron shell, about the shape of an egg and a little bigger than a fist, scored on its inner face in a lattice so that it broke where it had been told to, into pieces of a size, and smooth outside so it sat in the hand. A threaded hole at the top for the filling and the plug. The plug was the part that mattered. A fuse that wanted a match was no use to a farmer kneeling behind a bank in the rain with a horse coming at him, and in a dead field nothing that wanted a stone would light at all.

He drew the plug four times. Then he took it across the river to Tess.



Tess looked at the drawing under her north window for a long while with her chin on her square hand and did not say anything, which in Tess was not a good sign or a bad one, only Tess.

"You've drawn a spring," she said at last.

"A spring and a striker. You pull a pin, the spring drives the striker down onto one of your primers. The primer lights the fuse. The fuse burns down to a count of five and then..." He put his finger at the bottom of the drawing. "No match. No slow-match. Nothing that wants lighting. Nothing that wants a stone. It goes in the rain. It goes in a wind. It goes in a dead field."

"It goes in a man's hand, if the pin comes out in his pocket."

"That's why there's a lever." He showed her. "You hold the lever down against the body when you pull the pin. Nothing happens till you let go. It's the letting go that starts it. When you throw it, the lever flies off."

Tess looked at the lever.

"Who drew this?" she said.

"I did."

"No. Who drew this first." She did not look up. "You draw like a man copying. Your lines go straight to where they're going, the first time, as if you've seen it on a wall." She turned the sheet a little under the lamp. "When you make something new you rub it out twice. You've not rubbed this out once."

He said nothing.

"All right," said Tess, after a while. She took her pencil from behind her ear. "The spring's too stiff. A boy with cold hands won't hold your lever down against this, not for long, and if he's frightened he'll grip and then he'll not let go when he should." She drew on his drawing, lightly, in the margin, in her square hand. "And your striker wants to come down square on the cup, not at a slant, or it'll dent it and not fire, one in ten, and one in ten's a dead boy. I'll make you one."

She made him six, over the next two days, and threw four of them away.



Bram cast the first shells on the fourteenth.

He had made the pattern himself from Emeric's drawing, in pear wood, and the core box with the lattice raised on it in strips so fine that his apprentices stood round the bench to watch him set them; and he had changed the drawing twice in charcoal before he started, because he could see where the iron would not run and Emeric could not. He laid it on the bench and waited, and when Emeric said yes, he went on.

The first pour gave him nine good and three with the lattice blurred. He broke one of the bad ones on the anvil and it went everywhere in long ragged shards; then one of the good, and it came apart along its scoring into neat thick squares like a slab of toffee. He picked a square off the floor and turned it over in his black fingers and put it in his apron pocket.

"How many?" he said.

"As many as you can. Before the end of the month."

"Twenty in a pour," he said. "If I take the second floor off the brass. Four pours a day, if the iron holds." He looked at the piece of shell in his palm. "Less what goes wrong."



They tried the first one filled on the morning of the fifteenth, in the quarry, in the rain.

Ward opened the copper door of the powder house at seven and walked down with Fennick to the bottom of the quarry, and did not take his eyes off Fennick's hands while Fennick filled it. Nobody else was allowed to watch. The rule was the rule.

Then Fennick screwed in Tess's plug, and stood up with the thing in his good hand, holding the lever down against the iron with his thumb, and grinned at everybody along the quarry rim the way a boy grins on a frozen pond.

"Behind the bank, if you please," said Ward. "All of you."

They had built a rubble bank across the quarry floor in the night, chest high, and set old hay sacks beyond it at twenty paces and thirty and forty, with a straw man in a borrowed breastplate among them. Emeric crouched behind the bank with Tess and Bram and Tobe, who had come up from the foundry because nobody could have kept him away.

Fennick pulled the pin. He held the lever down a breath longer than he needed to, looking at it.

"One," he said, and threw, overarm, not well, like a man who has never thrown anything heavier than a stone at a crow; and the egg went up in a short lob over the bank and came down among the sacks at about twenty-five paces and rolled, and Fennick dropped behind the rubble, still counting aloud. "Three," said Fennick, and laughed, a breath, through his nose. "Four..."

The quarry jumped.

It was not loud like the rifles. It was heavier than that, a short flat hard thump that came up through the stones under Emeric's knees before it came through the air; and then the air came, and something went over the bank with a sound like a hand tearing cloth, and something else hit the rubble a foot from Tobe's head and stuck there, and Tobe looked at it.

When they stood up the smoke was going off sideways in the rain. The sacks at twenty paces were in rags. The sacks at thirty had holes in them, a dozen in each, some no bigger than a thumb. The straw man was on his back with his breastplate punched through in three places and the straw coming out of him.

Nobody said anything.

Tobe reached out and worked the piece of iron out of the rubble beside his head with his fingers. It was a square, thick and dark, with one edge bright where it had broken along Bram's scoring. He held it on his palm.

"That's my pot," he said. "That was my pot, that."

"It's Fennick's egg," said Osric, from the quarry rim, where Ward had put him to keep people off, and where he had evidently been watching over the edge. "Look at it. It's an egg. Fennick laid an egg."

Fennick went red to the ears. By noon the whole works was calling it that, and by evening so was Ward, flatly, like a name on a muster, which was worse.



The cup came out of the same afternoon, because Rylan could not throw.

He could; he threw better than Fennick. But at thirty paces, he said, standing in the quarry with a sand-filled egg in his hand, a man was well inside the reach of a horse, and the man with a horse at thirty paces had already lost. "I want it at them while they're still coming. When they're here I'll be using a sword like a gentleman. Or running like one."

Emeric had the cup already, in the back of the same notebook. He had not wanted to draw it. He drew it then, on his knee, with the rain spotting the paper: a short iron cup that locked over the muzzle with a quarter turn and held the egg by its lip until it was thrown; a cartridge with no ball in it to throw it; the rifle's butt on the ground and braced against the knee, pointing up and out like a man pointing at a bird.

Bram came up again in the evening with two cups rough off the lathe, and Fennick made up blanks with Ward at the door, and at dusk Rylan knelt in the quarry with a rifle braced on its butt and a sand egg in the cup, and fired.

The egg went up out of the cup into the dark in a long, slow, lazy curve, much slower than anybody had expected, so slow that Emeric could follow it with his eye against the last of the light, and came down at the far end of the quarry floor, past the painted marks, past the end of the quarry floor altogether, and went into the brambles at the foot of the north face.

Tobe paced it, with a lantern. A hundred and forty-eight.

"Again," said Ward, at the rim. "With the sights set."

By the second evening they were putting sand eggs into a ring of stakes at a hundred and fifty paces one time in four. By the third, filled ones; and Emeric stood at the rim with Ward and watched the floor at a hundred and fifty paces go up in short flat grey bursts, a breath apart, and the stakes go down.

"Three hundred paces," Ward said then. "The volleys. Every rifle on the bank. A hundred and fifty paces, the cup, every third rifle, into wherever they are thickest. And for the ones who reach the bank, the egg, by hand, over the top." He looked down into the quarry. "Every man in the levy will know which ring he is standing in. He will not have to be told where to look." He was quiet. "In a dead field, nobody can tell him."

"The rings," said Rylan, beside them, as if trying the word.

"Three rings," said Ward. "Rifle. Cup. Hand." He turned and went down the quarry track toward the gate, and called back over his shoulder, without turning his head, "Every third rifle. Tell Bram."



The forward depot went up on the night of the fourteenth, while they were still throwing sand.

The news came south on the K Discovery the next morning, because nothing else could bring it; the wire was dead at Harrow under Lisle's padlock. Cade brought it, with Declan's note in his coat. The depot had been a stone barn on Sethe's land, three miles north of the bridge, where they had been putting by powder and cartridges for the column since October, cart by cart, with receipts at the toll: forty-two casks, the cartridges in their crates, and the lead. Lisle's men had come across the fields at two in the morning with oil and a lantern. Nobody had been killed. One of Sethe's men had been knocked on the head and woken in a ditch with the barn going up a hundred yards off, and lain there with his hands over his ears while the casks went, one after another, half the night.

"How do you know they were Lisle's?" Emeric said.

"Green coats," said Cade. "The man in the ditch saw them by the light of it. He says they weren't even running. He says they walked." He looked at his boots. "Declan says to tell you there's nothing left north of the bridge but what the column has in its pouches. Sixty rounds a man. He says that's one good morning."

Ferra had taken Declan's note and read it twice.

"It's Lisle's land they crossed," she said. "Ours they burned. And there's a state of war between this house and the house that's sworn to protect his." She folded the note. "Nobody's going to court about a barn."

"No," said Emeric.

"And everything we send north by road goes over his bridge. Under his bar. Past his men in green." She looked at him. "They'll not burn the next lot. They'll just stop it. Lawfully, by his charter, for the duration."

"Then it doesn't go by road," said Emeric.



Ward's objection came to him that evening in writing, which Ward had never done before.

It was a single sheet in Ward's own hand, square and plain and unhurried, laid on the drafting table in the end room while Emeric was at supper, folded once, with nothing on the outside. Emeric read it standing up, with his coat still on.

Ward objected to the flight of hull two to Wayford on the night of the sixteenth with powder in her hold. He set out why, numbered, like a muster. First, that she would be flying at night over country now at war, with forty miles of Lisle's land below her. Second, that she was one of two, and a hull lost was not a cart lost. Third, that if anything went wrong aloft with that cargo, a spark, a dropped cask, a stone failing over a dead patch nobody knew was there, there would be nothing to bury of her or her crew. Fourth, that hull two was still Kaldo's secret, and that a hull landing three miles from Lisle's land in a war would be seen by somebody, and could not then be unseen. Fifth, that he did not think the powder was worth any one of the four.

And at the foot of it, under the five, a line on its own.

I know you will fly her. I ask that this be kept.

Emeric read it twice. Then he took it into the counting room and gave it to Ferra, and Ferra read it, and looked up at him.

"Keep it," he said.

Ferra took a new folder from the cabinet behind her, wrote the date on its spine and one word, Objections, and put Ward's sheet in it, alone, and locked the cabinet.

"He's right about the fourth," she said.

"He's right about all five."

"And you're flying her anyway."

"There are three hundred men going north to stand behind a bank," said Emeric, "and I'll not send them with sixty rounds each."



Hull two went up from the secret dock at the far end of the lake at nine on the night of the sixteenth, with no lamp on her anywhere, and thirty-one casks in her hold.

They had loaded her by hand at dusk, Ward at the head of the chain with the key on his belt, Fennick in the hold setting the casks in straw on the racks and lashing them as if they were sleeping children. Thirty-one, because that was what the hold would take with an arm's width clear of the lift stones, and Ned Tallis would not have one more, and nobody argued with Ned Tallis about his own hold.

Ned was a bald, slow, square man from the river, a lighterman since he was twelve, who had looked at hull two the first time he saw her in the shed and spat, and said she was a barge with ideas. He flew her as if she were a barge still, feet apart, his weight a little forward as though the deck might lurch and he would take it in his knees, and he talked to her under his breath in a lighterman's endearments that Emeric would not have repeated to anybody.

Garrick was on her racks, and young Wes, and Emeric beside Ned at the helm, because Emeric had said he would go and Ward had not said he would not; Ward had only looked at him across the dock in the dusk, and then gone on watching the casks.

She went up off the black water without a sound, nothing but the stones in their racks doing what he had told them to, and the dock and the lock-keeper's lamp going away below until the lake was a dull shine in the dark. Then Ned leaned on the levers and spoke to her, and she turned north.

It was cold aloft. They came up through a thin cloud into stars, with the cloud lying under them grey and lumpy in the moonlight like snow on furrows, and flew north over it three hours, nobody speaking much.

Once, past midnight, Ned took his eyes off the compass and spoke.

"They're quiet," he said. "Back there." He meant the casks. "I don't like a quiet cargo, Highness. Barrels of nails, now. They talk. They shift, you hear them, you know where they are." He looked at the compass again. "These don't say anything. They just sit there, thinking."

"Fennick lashed them."

"I know he did. I watched him." Ned moved a lever a hair. "I'd sooner he'd come along. I'd sooner the man who packed it was on top of it." He was quiet. "He wanted to. Ward said no."

The cloud thinned south of Wayford, as Linus had said it would on the card he had given Ned that afternoon, with the barometer reading and the wind at a thousand feet and his name at the bottom in a hand like a copperplate engraving; and they came down through the last of it with the country lying dark and silver under them, the river, the road, the long shadows of the woods, and at the river's bend a single light, a lantern held up on a pole and moved twice side to side, and then held still.

Declan's lantern. Sethe's lower meadow, half a mile from the hall, with the hedges all round it and the river on the fourth side.

Ned brought her down onto the wet grass as softly as a man setting a sleeping child in a cot, and she settled, and her stones went quiet in their racks, and the column came out of the hedge at a run with the carts.

They had her empty in half an hour. Thirty-one casks, hand to hand in the dark, nobody speaking, nobody running with a cask, Declan at the hold door counting them out under his breath and a corporal of the column counting them again on the carts. Thirty-one, said Declan. Thirty-one, said the corporal. Declan nodded, and went to Emeric at the helm, and gripped his arm once, hard, above the elbow, which was a thing Declan did not do; and then he went back to the carts.

Emeric stood on the grass with his hand on her cold wet side and let his sense go out, as he always did at night: the meadow, the column, the horses, Ned's note slow and ox-like at the helm; the hedge, the river, the field past it, and the wood past the field.

There was a note in the wood.

A man's. Alone, very still, at the edge of the trees on the rise past the river, three or four hundred yards off, with the slow warm note of a horse beside him. Not a poacher; a poacher at night was all nerves, and this was patient. It had been there before the lantern.

He turned his head toward the wood.

The note moved. Not like a man who had been seen; like a man who had seen what he came for. It drew back into the trees with the horse's note beside it and went away over the rise, a walk, then a trot, then a long steady canter north along the river road, past the edge of Emeric's reach, and gone.

Declan had come up beside him in the dark.

"Highness?"

"A rider," said Emeric. "In the wood. On the rise." He did not take his eyes off the place. "He's gone north."

Declan said two names over his shoulder, quietly, and two of the column were across the ford in a splashing rush before Emeric had finished turning round. They came back at dawn mud to the girths, with a lame horse and nothing else. Nine miles up the river road, the corporal said, and then the tracks went off onto the hard, and the hard went north.

By then hull two was home in her shed at the far end of the lake, and Ward had come down to the dock at four in the morning with a lantern to count her casks out of the empty hold, and found none, and walked back up to the keep, Garrick said, without a word.



Saban came north on the K Discovery on the eighteenth, though he did not like heights, and sat on a coil of rope the whole way with his eyes shut. At Sethe's hall he asked the corporal three questions about the tracks; then he took the posting house's gig up the river road, and was gone all day, and came back at dark and asked for a room with a lock on it, and Emeric.

He had not taken off his coat. He laid the small book on the table between them, under Sethe's lamp, and did not open it.

"There's a farrier at Lowcote," he said. "On the hard, past where your men lost him. He shod a horse for a man going north on the seventeenth, at first light. Bay mare, cast a shoe off the near fore in the night. In a hurry. Paid in Silver and did not wait for change." He looked at the book. "I know the mare. I've paid for her oats. For three years."

Emeric waited.

"She belongs to a lad named for nobody, from a village nobody's heard of, who carries letters south for my man in the north. Has done since the first year. My letters." His voice did not change at all. It was his commercial voice still, flat and dry and exact, the voice he priced cloth in. "Ansley Dunmore's runner. Sat in a wood on the Wayford side at midnight on the night we flew, and watched it, and rode north."

"Saban."

"Let me finish, Highness, while I've got it in order." He turned the book over under his hand, once. "Dunmore never sent word of the muster. Three weeks of two thousand men, and not a line. I thought he was dead in a ditch and I'd be paying his widow." He looked at the lamp. "The posting house at Lowcote keeps a post book. He's sent four letters south since September to a house in Wayford that isn't mine, under another name." He was quiet. "And in September I wrote to him myself. The day after the long table. I told him a northern man had been asking after Fennick two summers back, and to watch for another; and so he'd know what he was watching for, I told him Fennick was no use to anybody who took him, because Ward had the only key to the powder house." He stopped. "In my own hand. To him."

Emeric said nothing.

"So that's where the key came from," said Saban. "Not the long table. Me." He opened the book to a page, Dunmore's page, Emeric supposed, three years of it. "Selling both ways. Me one week and the duke the next. Very sound. And the one thing I'd have wanted from him, the muster, he sold the duke the silence of." He shut the book. "Best price there is, silence. Nobody can prove they paid for it."

"It's not your fault," said Emeric.

Saban looked at him over the lamp, mildly surprised, as if a customer had offered above the asking.

"With respect, Highness, it's entirely my fault. I hired him." He stood and put the book away inside his coat. "A network's people, and people talk both ways. I've always known it. I've never had to pay for knowing it before." At the door he stopped with his hand on the latch. "I'll want Ward to say what's done about him, after. I'll not decide it myself. I don't think I'd decide it well."



Ward read both reports standing, in Sethe's counting room, and gave them back.

"Then Aldane knows we can bring powder in by the air," was all he said. "He is not a man who has had to think about the air before. It will take him a day or two." He turned from the window. "Where is the surveyor?"



Linus Vance had been in Wayford since the eighth, sleeping over Sethe's stable with his instruments locked in a chest under the bed, because Emeric had sent him up to walk the country between the bridge and the north road and set down everything a man would need to know about it if he had to stand on it and be shot at.

He came into Sethe's counting room on the twentieth with a roll of paper under his arm and his boots wet to the knee, and spread the paper on the table without a word, and put Sethe's inkwell on one corner and the lamp on another and his own two hands flat on the other two, and stood back.

It was a map, the finest Emeric had seen in this world, and he had seen the crown's. Every field and hedge and ditch in a fine brown ink, the woods stippled and the wet ground hatched blue, the rise and fall of the land in contours a foot apart. The river down the left, the bridge at the bottom with the toll-house a square dot, the north road coming down from the top. And across the middle, between the road and the river, running east to west, a stream.

"There," said Linus, and put his finger on it.

Ward bent over the map.

"It goes into the river here, two miles and a half above the bridge," said Linus. "North of it the ground's open. Stubble and pasture, on a long slow slope down from the north road; the best riding ground for ten miles." He moved his finger. "But look at the sides. On the east, where the stream comes off the high ground, there's a beech hanger on a bank, too steep for a horse to keep a line in. On the west, by the river, the stream spreads out before it goes in. Rushes and alder and black water. I went into it to the waist on the twelfth." He looked at his boots as if they were evidence. "A horse would go in to the girth."

His finger moved across the open ground, from the top of the sheet down toward the stream.

"So a man comes down the road with a great many horse, and he sees half a mile of open ground in front of him, and he forms up across it as wide as it goes, and starts down. And as he comes, the hanger comes in on his left and the marsh on his right, and by three hundred paces from the stream his half mile is a little over a quarter. At a hundred and fifty, less." He put his finger on the stream. "Whatever front he started with, he arrives with it all in a lump in the middle. He can't help it. The ground does it to him."

Nobody said anything.

"The stream's nothing," said Linus. "A man could jump it. But it's cut deep into its bed, four feet, five, with a bank on the south side higher than the north, because the water's always been taking the north side away. And on the south side, here, the ground comes up again." He moved his finger south of the stream. "Not much. A rise. Six feet over three hundred paces. Enough to see from." He straightened up. "That's all. That's the ground."

Ward did not look up from the map.

He had a pencil in his hand, Emeric saw; he did not remember Ward taking it from the table. Ward put the point of it on the south bank of the stream, at the marsh end, and drew a line along the bank, eastward, slowly, a little back from the water, along the top of the rise, all the way across toward the hanger; and short of the hanger he turned the line, a little, south, and stopped it short of the reeds at its foot, and lifted the pencil.

"The earthwork," he said. "On the rise. Chest high, with a ditch in front of it into the stream bank, so a horse must jump the stream and go down into the ditch and come up the face. A horse will not do that at a charge. Not twice." He looked at the end of the line, short of the reeds. "This end is open. The reeds will hold horse, not men on foot. That end will want something on it."

"Tobe's pots," said Emeric. "There are six left."

Ward looked at the open end. Then he nodded.

"Three hundred paces," he said, and put the pencil on the open ground north of the stream, where Linus had said the half mile had narrowed. He drew a short mark across it. Then another, closer, at a hundred and fifty. Then nothing at all at the stream itself; he did not need to. He looked at the three lines on the map for a long breath: the bank, the mark, the mark.

"Will he come here?" said Emeric. "He's got the whole north to choose from."

"He has to come to the bridge," said Linus, before Ward could answer. "It's what the war's for, on paper. He has to be seen standing on Lisle's bridge." He ran his finger down the road. "There's no other way down for a host with carts. The road crosses the stream here, at the ford. He can come any way he likes as long as it's this way."

Ward looked up from the map at Linus for the first time.

"You walked all of it?"

"Twice," said Linus. "Once on the eighth, once on the twelfth. And I've got the weather for the last forty Novembers, if you want that too. It'll rain on the twenty-fourth and twenty-fifth and freeze on the twenty-sixth, more likely than not. The ground will be hard on top and soft under. Bad going for a heavy horse." He looked at the map. "Good going for a man on his feet."

Ward was quiet.

"Thank you," he said.

Linus looked at him as if Ward had said something in another language; and then rolled up his map, very carefully, and tied it, and gave it to Ward, and went out to dry his boots.



They began on the twenty-first. The earthwork went up along Ward's line like a long low brown wall, the ditch cut into the clay of the bank in front of it and turves laid along the top so it would not wash. Linus walked it every evening with his level and made them take it down a foot at the hanger end, where it stood too high to see over. The levy slept in Sethe's barns and in tents behind the rise, and in the evenings went down to the stream in twos to fill their canteens and looked across at the long empty slope running up to the north road, and came back quiet.

Ward drilled them on the bank every afternoon. Three ranks. Kneel, fire, back, load. At a word every third man in the front rank set his butt to the ground, gave the cup a quarter turn onto the muzzle with a sand egg in it, and fired it out over the stubble at a ring of stakes at a hundred and fifty paces while the rank fired past him. Then Ward said hand, and the whole rank dropped behind the face, came up with a sand egg each, pulled a dummy pin, counted, and threw. Ward made them count aloud the first day. By the fourth, Rylan said, they hardly counted at all; their arms knew.

"Which ring?" Ward would say, walking along the back of the bank in the rain.

"The rifle, sir."

"Which ring?"

"The cup, sir."

"Which ring?"

"The hand, sir," from the boy at the end; and Ward would nod, and walk on.



Ward sent him home on the twenty-second, because there were three hundred men on the bank who could dig and one who could tell Bram what was wrong with a striker. In Kaldo the eggs were coming off the sand floor twenty at a time.

Bram took the second floor off the brass and doubled the shift on the cupola, and taught four more boys to set the lattice with his black fingers over theirs. Tess had nine girls from the school at the long bench making strikers, each with a gauge, and walked up and down behind them taking springs out of their fingers to try them and putting them back without a word, the girls not breathing until she had gone past. Fennick filled them in the powder house with Vesk at the door and the key on Vesk's belt, because Ward was at the stream; and Vesk, who had been handed the key with a look he said he would remember on his deathbed, did not let Fennick out of his sight from the opening of the door to the closing of it.

The lathes in the long shop turned the cups, a hundred of them, one for every third rifle. The blanks went in crates with a red band painted round them, so nobody would ever load one behind a ball.

Emeric went from one to the other. He did not build anything; he looked. In the yard behind the long shop the main's pipe lay where the frost had stopped it, the lengths stacked under tarpaulins with the derrick folded beside them, and the roller's boiler cold. He broke a shell from every pour on the anvil to see that it went into squares, and once it did not, and Bram went still, and went into the core boxes himself with a lamp at midnight and found a boy had set a strip of the lattice upside down.

By the twenty-fourth Bram had three hundred and some; by the morning of the twenty-fifth, when the K Discovery took the last of them north in her hold under Cade's eye, nearly six hundred. Five hundred and ninety-four, Ferra wrote in the book; she had counted them herself on the dock, crate by crate, with Vesk counting beside her. She would not have a figure on the book she had not seen.



Imani's note was on his drafting table on the twenty-third when he came up at two in the morning, folded under the corner of Tess's gauge. She had been on nights in the end room all week and so had he, a quarter of a mile apart, and they had not been in the same room awake since he came home.

He read it standing, among Tess's springs and Bram's broken shell.

One of Tess's girls had come into the dispensary with a spring through her thumb, and Imani would like it known that she did not approve of whatever they were making over there, and that she had stitched it, and that the girl had asked to go back. And she had heard from Ferra, who said things very exactly, that he had flown north on hull two with the casks; and if he ever did that again without telling her first she would not finish her sentence, not this winter and not the next.

And at the bottom, under her name, in a smaller hand, as if she had come back to the letter after she had sealed it and broken the seal again to write it:

The buttons are still there. I've not undone a single one. You may come back and see to them yourself when you've finished your war, if you've the time, which you won't.

He read it twice, standing. Then he folded it and put it inside his coat, on the left, where Ferra kept her pencils in hers, and went down the stair to the sand floor to watch the night pour, and found that he had been smiling at Bram for some time, and that Bram had noticed and had decided, gravely, not to ask.

Ferra found him on the dock at the lake end before dawn on the twenty-fifth, when the eggs were going up the plank into the K Discovery's hold, in the cold, with her ledger under her arm and her hood up and her breath going white.

She did not say anything. She stood beside him on the planks and counted the crates, and wrote, and when the last crate had gone up and Cade had put his head out of the hold and nodded to her, she closed the book, and turned, and reached up and set his collar straight; and her fingers stayed on the edge of it a breath longer than a collar needed.

"Come back," she said, "with all of them."

"I'll try."

"I'll not have try." She took her hand away. "I've the figure in the book. Five hundred and ninety-four. I want to know what came of every one." She looked at the ship, at the hold, at Cade, who was very carefully looking at the lake. "And three hundred men. And you." She put the ledger under her arm again. "I'll take them in that order, if you like, so you'll know which I counted first."

He went up the plank. At the top he looked back, and she was still on the dock with her ledger, very straight, in her hood, and did not wave; and then the ship lifted, and the dock went away.



Ward was on the earthwork when the K Discovery came down behind the rise in the dark of the twenty-fifth, at the hanger end, among the beeches, where the levy had cut a clearing for her two days before and Linus had paced it.

He was standing on the top of the bank with his hands behind his back, looking north over the stream, over the black ribbon of the ditch below him and the paler stubble running up beyond it to the north road, where there was nothing to see at all. The wind came down the slope at him off the stubble, cold, with a smell of frost in it. Linus had been right about the frost.

Emeric climbed up beside him. Behind them, along the back of the rise, the levy's fires were going out one by one, and a man was singing somewhere, badly, and stopped.

He let his sense go out over the field: the sleeping camp behind him, the levy and the column and Declan's knights and the five masks in their bivouac by the hanger, a field of notes in the dark like a town's lights seen from the ship; the stream, the stubble, the empty slope to the road. He tried to imagine it going out, all at once, between one breath and the next. Standing on this bank among three hundred men and not feeling one of them.

"You will not feel anybody," said Ward. He did not turn his head. "When it comes. You will stand where I put you and listen. You know the drill."

"I know it."

"The rifles do not need you to feel them. Nor the cups. Nor the eggs." Ward looked out over the stubble. "Nothing on this bank needs a stone to work, except you." He was quiet. "That was the point."

The wind came down the slope.

"Ward," said Emeric. "Your sheet. The objection. Ferra's kept it."

"Good."

"The rider saw the ship. You were right. About the fourth."

"Yes." Ward did not move. "And there are thirty-one casks of powder in this bank, in the levy's pouches, that there would not be." He looked down at the ditch. "Both of those are true. I wanted it written down that I said the first one. Somebody should have, in writing, before you did it." He was quiet. "Next time it will be something else."

They stood on the bank together a while longer, in the cold.

Away to the north, at the top of the long slope, the north road came over the rise between its hedges, and there was nothing on it at all.

Ward looked at them.

"Go to bed," he said. "When he comes, it will be early."
