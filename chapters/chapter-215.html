Chapter 215 - The Ampoule

Rylan's front heart went thin at the fourth hour after midnight, on the western flats, with a bale on his shoulder and a quarter mile of sand between him and the line.

It did not fail. The suits did not fail that way; the cells gave what they gave through their necks and went on giving it until they were nearly empty, and then gave less. Rylan felt it as a heaviness coming back into his legs, the way a man feels the end of a long day's walk, and said nothing, because Rylan never said anything until he had to, and walked the bale to the line on Lighten with the rear heart carrying the suit alone. It was Emeric who noticed, because he had the sense out over the flat as he always had on the island, and among the five small hard knots of the suits' hearts sliding about in the dark, one had gone dim.

He sent Rylan up first, that night, ahead of the casks. Rylan went without arguing, which was how Emeric knew how low he had been.

In the ship, on the way home, Ward sat on the bench against the hull with his helm on his knees and said what they had all been thinking for a month.

"We're flying a night's work on a night's charge," he said. "There's nothing over. If a man had to run on the way back to the line, run properly, in the air, he'd be walking by the end of it." He turned the helm in his hands. "And if we lost the ship, and had to glide for the coast, we'd be in the sea."

"Carry more cells," said Fletcher, from the levers.

"Every cell is a pound on my back and a night of the forge."

That was the rest of it, and Emeric had been adding it up for weeks. The suits ran on flask cells, the stone cells with their round bodies and long necks that the forge pressed out of emptied stone one at a time, in the pattern store, at night, with nobody touching anything. Two hearts to a suit. A spare pair for every suit in the hold. And the forge had other work, a great deal of it, and always would: the node seats for the Drop House, the Walker's second form stone, the plates for hull four's lift bank, which Fletcher wanted by the autumn and was not going to get. Every night the forge spent on a spare cell for a knight's pack was a night it did not spend on something the barony was waiting for.

And the cells did not need replacing. That was the maddening part. A flask cell that had given its charge was not spent; it was only empty. Up here in the band, with its neck open to the thick air, it would fill again by itself in an hour. The problem was never the cells. It was that the suits spent the night on the ground, and the ground was thin, and there was nothing on the island to fill them from but the island's own thin air, which was like filling a bath from the steam off a cup.

"We don't need more cells," Emeric said. "We need to take the band down with us."

Ward looked at him.

"In a bottle," Emeric said. "A small one."

He did not know it would work. He said so to Bram the next morning, in Bram's shop, and Bram said that was all right, he had never known anything would work either, and what did the prince want him to build.

A pump, Emeric said. A good one. A piston pump of the kind that pushed air into a receiver, in stages, each stage squeezing what the last had squeezed, until the air in the receiver was at many times its own pressure. And at the end of the pump, instead of a receiver, a glass. A small glass, with a long neck, sitting in a jacket that could be made very cold.

He had been thinking about the flask in the iron room since the first winter: the way a flask opened into a shut room returned to the medium in three breaths and filled the room with it, as if the medium in the flask had been waiting, folded up small, to be let out. It had been folded up small. That was what the flask was. The Hod's racks drew band air through their open necks and the iron held what came in, thick, as thick as the band and no thicker. But if you could squeeze it past that, the way a pump squeezed air, and make it cold, the way the band was already cold, then in his old world there was only one thing that happened to anything that was a gas, if you squeezed it hard enough and made it cold enough.

It stopped being a gas.

He did not know whether the medium was a gas. Nobody did. Armand had spent his life not knowing what the medium was and had stopped expecting to find out. But it moved with the air, and filled a room, and thinned with height and thickened in the band, and leaked out through brick and not through glass, and all of that was what a gas did. It was worth a pump.

Bram built it in nine days: three iron cylinders on a frame, each smaller than the last, driven off one crank, with a cooling coil of copper between each stage and the next, and at the end a fitting of brass, very fine, that took the neck of a glass vial the length of a finger and a little thicker than a pencil. The glassworks blew the vials, two hundred of them, out of Orrin's new clear batch, the one that ran like water, with walls as thick as Orrin could blow them and still see through. Round the vial went a copper jacket, packed with ice stones from the ice house, so cold that the copper frosted white in the summer air of the shop and the frost smoked.

They ran it in the shop, on the ground, on a Thursday, with the belt off the line shaft.

Nothing happened.

The pump pumped. The air went in and was squeezed and came out of the bleed at the end hissing and cold, and the vial in its jacket frosted over on the outside and stayed empty on the inside, and after an hour Emeric put the sense on it and found in the glass nothing but what was in the shop: the ordinary thin medium of the ground, a little thicker than the air outside because there were three men and a pump in the room, and no more.

Bram stopped the belt.

"It's the ground," Emeric said. He had half expected it. "There's nothing to squeeze. It's like trying to fill a tank from the air in a room. You can pump all day. You'll never get more out than was in the room to start with, and there wasn't much."

"Then we pump in the band," said Bram.

"Then we pump in the band."

Fletcher fitted it to the K Discovery in a week, and complained the whole week, because it was a machine in his ship that he had not drawn.

He put it in the belly, forward of the boat door, on its own frame bolted to the ribs, with a belt off the ship's engine through a clutch, so that the engine could drive the pump or the screw but not both. And round the end of it, round the fitting and the jacket and the vial, he built a box: iron, riveted, the size of a bread oven, with two ports in its side for a man's hands in heavy gloves and a thick glass in its lid for his eyes, and a floor that was not a floor but a panel, held by four soft rivets, that would blow out downward into the night if anything in the box went wrong.

"What goes wrong?" said Ned, who had come down to look.

"I don't know yet," said Emeric. "That's why there's a panel."

They took her up on the first clear night after, with Bram and Emeric and Fletcher and Ned and nobody else, and no island to go to. It was a strange feeling, climbing into the band with nothing to do there but work. Fletcher held her in the thick air twenty miles out over the sea, south, where no ship ever went, and put the engine to the pump, and Bram knelt at the box with his hands in the gloves and his face at the glass, and Emeric knelt beside him with the sense on the vial.

The pump took the band's air in at the bow and squeezed it.

He felt it at once. The air coming into the first cylinder was thick with the medium, as thick as the air in the veins, and when the first stage squeezed it the medium squeezed with it, and it was thicker, and the second stage squeezed it again and it was thicker than he had ever felt the medium anywhere, thicker than the Drop House's bottle with a fresh flask poured, and the third stage squeezed it again, and it went through the brass fitting into the vial in its jacket of ice, and there it did something he had never felt anything do.

It slowed down.

The medium in the rest of the pump was moving, always, the way it moved everywhere, restless, like the air in a warm room. In the vial it stopped moving. It went heavy and still, and gathered, and the gathering was something he could feel as clearly as he could feel a stone: a place at the bottom of the glass where the medium had stopped being a thing that filled space and become a thing that lay in it.

"There," said Bram, very quietly, with his face at the glass.

It was a bead. A drop, no bigger than a tear, at the bottom of the vial, clear as water and not like water, because it did not catch the lamplight the way water caught it. The lamplight went into it and something else came out, a faint shiver in the clear, like heat over a road, only cold.

The drop grew. It grew very slowly while the pump beat, and the air that had carried the medium in went on through and out of the bleed, thin and spent, and the medium stayed behind in the glass. In a quarter of an hour there was a finger's width of it in the bottom of the vial. In an hour the vial was full to the neck.

Bram took a heat stone on a pair of tongs, through the other glove, and laid it on the vial's neck a finger above the liquid, and held it there while the glass went red and soft and slumped, and drew the tongs away, and the neck drew out into a thread and closed on itself and broke, and the vial stood in the jacket sealed, a small clear thing with a pointed tip where its neck had been, full of something that was not water.

Emeric had the sense on it, and did not take it off.

By everything he knew, it should not have stayed. A thing that had been a gas, squeezed until it ran and chilled until it lay still, did not stay a liquid when you took the squeeze away. It boiled. It went back to what it had been, as fast as it could, and if you had shut it in glass it went back to what it had been anyway and the glass went with it. He had seen what happened to a sealed bottle of the one and he had built the box with the panel for that reason.

The vial sat in its jacket and did nothing at all.

Bram lifted it out on the tongs, out of the ice, out of the box, into the ordinary air of the ship's belly, and held it up to the lamp, and it sat in the tongs, still, clear, shivering faintly, and did not boil and did not break. It stayed.

"It's not a gas," said Emeric.

Bram looked at him. "Is that bad?"

"No." He laughed, once, surprised out of him. "No. It's the best thing it could possibly be. It means I was wrong about what it is, and right about what it does."

They filled eleven that night, and on the twelfth it went wrong, and it was Bram's fault, and Bram said so before anybody else could.

He had wanted to know how much a vial would hold. Not how much it held when it was full to the neck, which they knew. How much it would take if you kept pumping, past the neck, with the liquid up in the narrow part and the pump still beating, because a man who built a thing wanted to know where it broke before the thing found out for him. So he let the twelfth run on. The liquid came up the neck. It came up to the brass fitting. The pump beat.

Emeric felt it before anything showed. The medium in the vial, which had been still, began to move again, and not the way it moved in the air. It moved the way it had moved in the forge, years ago, in the first stone they had tried to press into a cell before it was empty enough: a trembling, a pushing, as if something shut in a space too small for it had begun to look for the way out.

"Stop the pump," he said.

Ned threw the clutch. It was quick. It was not quick enough.

The vial vented. It did not burst. It did the thing an overfilled stone did: a sudden heat, a flash of it that Emeric felt on his face through the glass of the lid, and a push, a single hard shove of force outward from the vial in every direction at once, like a door slammed in a small room. The jacket jumped on its bracket. The box rang. And the floor of the box, the panel on its four soft rivets, went out downward into the dark with a bang like a musket, and the night air came up through the hole, roaring, three miles of it.

Bram took his hands out of the gloves.

He took them out slowly, and looked at them, front and back, the way he had looked at them in the forge the night the first stone vented and the sand and the lining and the flue took it, and his hands had been nowhere near. They had been in the gloves this time. The gloves were iron to the wrist and leather above, and the leather was scorched brown on the backs and smelled of it. His hands under them were red and whole.

"That was me," Bram said.

"It was the pump," said Emeric. "The pump doesn't know when to stop."

"It was me. I told it not to." Bram looked at the box, at the hole where its floor had been, at the jacket hanging crooked on its bracket and the glass of the twelfth vial in a glitter all over the inside. "Same as the forge. Too much in a thing that's got nowhere to put it. I knew that. I wanted to see where it was." He looked at the hole in the box for a while longer. "Now I know where it is."

Fletcher came down the ladder from the deckhouse at a speed Emeric had not known Fletcher could come down a ladder, and stood at the foot of it and looked at the box and the hole and the four of them, and said nothing whatever, and went back up the ladder. Later that night, in the band, on the way home, he came down again with a new panel and four soft rivets that he had apparently made in the time since he had fitted the first four, and put it in without a word, and went back up.

Bram put a stop on the pump in the morning. A small brass valve at the fitting, on a spring, that shut the line when the liquid in the vial came up to a line scratched round the glass a thumb below the neck. After that nobody's judgment was needed to know when a vial was full, and nobody's curiosity either, and it never vented again.

He tested them at the forge, the eleven, over two nights, in the pattern store with the door shut.

The test was the only one that mattered. He took a flask cell that had been flown empty on the island, one of Rylan's, dim as an old coal, and set it in a small iron cup with a lid, with its neck up. Bram had made the cup the day before: a heart's chamber, in little, with a port in its side. Into the port went a vial, in a sleeve of steel that Bram had turned on the lathe to fit it close, and at the bottom of the port was a spike, a short hard point of steel. When the sleeve was pushed home, the vial's sealed tip met the spike and broke off inside the port, inside the iron, where nothing could get out.

The liquid came out of the broken tip into the cup and went back to what it had been. It did it quickly, in about the time an opened flask took, three breaths, and the chamber was full of medium as thick as the band.

And the empty cell, sitting in it with its neck open, drank.

He felt it drink. It drank the way the cells drank in the veins of the ship, steadily, through the neck, until the chamber was thin again and the cell was full. He took it out and put it on Armand's scale and read it and it read what a new cell read the day it came off the forge. He did it again with another empty cell and another vial, and it read the same. And again. By the end of the second night he had done it eleven times, with the eleven, and every time it was the same: one vial, one cell, full.

He wrote it down in the back of his notebook, in the second column, where he wrote the things that were only for him.

One vial, one cell.

It was a private note. It did not stay private for long, because Ferra saw it upside down the next morning across the long-room table, as she saw everything, and wrote it in her book as a measure, and by the end of the week it was what everybody called it. A suit's charge was a pair of cells. A night's spare was a vial for each. A knight asked for so many vials. Nobody had decided it should be the rating. It was simply the only figure anybody had that was the same every time.

Ward flew the first vials on the island in the second week of the high summer, and Emeric watched him do the thing that had to be done before anybody else was allowed to.

The hearts on the suits were rebuilt for it: a port in the side of each, where Bram's little cup had had its port, with the spike at the bottom, and a clip on the thigh plate for three sleeves. A knight whose heart went thin put his hand to his thigh, drew a sleeve, pushed it into the port until it clicked, and felt the heart come full again in the time it took to breathe three times. The glass stayed in the port, broken, until the sleeve was drawn and thrown away.

Ward worked the flats until the fourth hour, and let his front heart run down on purpose, and pushed a sleeve home, and went on working. At the fifth hour he did it again. At dawn, on the line, he still had a sleeve on his thigh.

Then, on the flight home, in the band, with the island far behind and nobody below them but the sea, he did the other thing. He asked Fletcher to hold her, and went out of the boat door in the dark, and when he was clear of the ship he put the suit on Burn.

It roared. It always roared: the air stones at full flow, loud as the works' whistle, a long tearing sound that went out over the black water for miles. Ward went away from the ship in the dark like a thrown stone, and turned, and came back, and it was exactly the same burst it had always been. It was as fast and as loud and no faster and no louder. And then it began to thin, where it had always thinned, because the neck of a cell let out what it let out and no faster. Deep well. Narrow bucket. The vials had made the well deeper. They had done nothing to the bucket.

Ward came back in over the sill and stood dripping cold in the belly with his helm under his arm.

"Longer," he said. "Not harder."

"Longer," said Emeric. "Not harder."

"Good." Ward put the helm on its hook. "Harder was never the problem. Harder is how men get killed." He sat down on the bench. "Longer is how they get home."

Ferra came down to the pattern store at the end of the month, with the book, to count them.

There were a hundred and eight. Fletcher had taken the ship up nine nights in the month for nothing else, on nights Ferra chose, and the pump had run with Bram or Ned at the box and the stop on the line and a second jacket beside the first, and the vials had come down in a wooden case lined with felt, forty to a case, each one clear and still and faintly shivering, with the sealed point where its neck had been. They stood now in their cases on the long shelf in the pattern store, and would stand there, Emeric thought, for as long as anybody liked.

"They don't need sealing," Ferra said. She had taken one out of its felt and was holding it up to the window. "No lid. No pitch."

"The glass is the lid. It's all one piece."

"Then they don't go off." She turned it in the light. "Not in a month. Not in a season." She looked at him. "Not ever?"

"I don't know about ever. We've had them a month. Ask me in ten years." But he had put the sense on the first eleven every week since the night they were filled, and there was no change in any of them, not a hair, and he did not think there would be. "Nothing gets out of glass that's been melted shut. It's the same as the flask rule, only there's no seal to go wrong."

Ferra put the vial back in its felt, carefully, and looked along the shelf, at the cases.

"Then that's the first thing we've ever made that we can keep," she said. "Not a season. Keep."

"In a size too small to run anything but a man."

"I know." She had already worked it; he could see her working it still. "A flask pours into a room the size of a barn. One of these fills a cup." She closed the case. "The Drop House stays on the flasks. Under the island pitch. That's a season, and a season is enough for a room." She wrote in the book, and then wrote under it, and he read it upside down, the way she had read his: Vials: 108. Kept without seal. One vial, one cell. And, after a moment, in smaller letters: The first store that does not spend itself.

She shut the book.

Emeric stood with her in the pattern store with the shelf in front of them, a hundred and eight small clear glasses of the sky, and found himself thinking, not of the suits, and not of the island, but of the shut iron room where a flask had held its charge for the first time, and of how small a thing you could carry the band down to the ground in, and how many of them it would take to make a room of it.

He did not say so. It was not the year for it. He took one of the vials out of its felt and held it up to the light himself, and watched the faint cold shiver in it that was not light, and put it back.
