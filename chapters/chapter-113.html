Chapter 113 - The Pen

Saban had brought it down from the capital in January, inside his coat, wrapped in a square of oiled cloth and tied with a bootlace, and put it on Emeric's table the night he came in off the north road, after he had told the exchange about Sethe's three companies and before he went to bed.

"I went past the shop," he said. "Like you told me. Halfway down the street to the south road's posting house, green cloth in the window." He did not sit down. "The boy wanted to know who it was for. For the engraving, he said. They'll put a name on the band, for a Silver more." He looked at the parcel. "I said no name."

"Thank you."

"I didn't ask you who it was for," said Saban. He put his hat on. "I want that noted. I'd like it in somebody's book that I didn't ask." And he went to bed.

That had been the twentieth of January. It was the twentieth of April now, and the parcel was still in the inside pocket of Emeric's coat, on the other side from the notebook, where it had been every day for three months.

He had meant to give it to her the first week. Then the Order had been seated, and she had been made Chancellor in front of everyone with a clause she had not seen, and a pen in the same week would have looked like payment for it. Then the room, and the file, and Imani at the ward. Then the twin year closing, and the boxes, and every time he had seen her since she had been at a trestle with a column in front of her, and every time he had put his hand on the parcel in his coat he had seen himself put it down on the trestle beside the column, and her look at it, and put it with the other pens, and go on with the sum.

That was what he did not want. He had not been able to work out how to give her a thing that was not work, because he had never once in four years given her anything that was not.



The school roll was Imani's idea and Ferra's work and Sela's war.

Imani had said it at the ward, in February, with her feet on the second stool: have Ferra write down every child in Sela's room, and who brings them, and who fetches them, and where their fathers work. He had told the table on the Monday after. Ferra had written it down. And because schooling was the Chancellor's under the sheet Hale had read, it was Ferra who walked up to the old exchange that had been the school since the first autumn, in the middle of April, with a fresh book under her arm, to make the roll.

Sela met her at the door.

Emeric was there because he taught figures on Thursdays, and it was a Thursday, and he was standing at the back of the long room with a slate when Ferra came in. He saw Sela see the book. He saw her face do what it did when she saw his eyes, which it had stopped doing, mostly, in the last year, and now did again for somebody else.

"There's a roll," Sela said. "I keep it."

"I know," said Ferra. "I've read it. It's the names, and the days they come." She did not put the book down. "I need more than that."

"What more?"

"Who brings them. Who fetches them. Which way they walk. Who they go home to, and where that is, and what the people they go home to do all day." Ferra said it exactly as she would have said a list of columns. "And whether anybody's ever come to the door asking for one of them who wasn't the person who brings them."

The room had gone quiet. Forty children on the benches had stopped pretending to do their sums.

Sela looked at Ferra for some time.

"It's for Finn," she said.

"It's for the next Finn."

"You'll put my children in a book. In the keep. In that box of yours." Sela did not raise her voice. She never did, in the room; she said it was bad for them. "Who walks them home. Where they live. Every one of them. And the next man who wants a child to make a knight do something can come and read your book."

"He can't read my book," said Ferra. "Nobody can but me. And the next man who wants a child to make a knight do something won't need my book. He'll stand across the lane from your door for a week, the way Garrin did, and count them going in." She put the book down then, on the end of the nearest bench, and opened it at the first page, which was blank, and laid her pen across it. "This way somebody's counting them going in before he is."

Sela looked at the book.

"And if one of them doesn't come," said Ferra, "the morning he doesn't come, you'll know who to send to. Not on the fourth day. Not after you've let it go three days because his mother said he was at his aunt's." She did not soften it. She never softened a correction. "You let it go three days. With Finn. You said so to Ward."

The room was very quiet.

Emeric, at the back, did not move. He watched Sela's face. He had watched it on the day she told him to get out of her shop, and on the day she stood at the gate of the burial hollow and did not look at him, and on the day she let Finn back into his place on the bench beside Arthur without a word. He had never once seen it look as it did now, which was as though somebody had put a finger on exactly the place where it hurt, and pressed.

"Three days," Sela said.

"Yes."

Sela looked down at the book on the bench. Then she sat down beside it, on the end of the bench, among the children, who moved up to make room for her without being told.

"Arthur," she said. "Bring me the roll off my desk." She looked up at Ferra. "You'll do it with me. Not on your own. I'll not have you going round their mothers' doors in that coat with that book like the tithe man." She pulled the book toward her. "And you'll start with mine."

They did it all afternoon. Sela called the children up one at a time, and asked them, and Ferra wrote, and when a child did not know where its father worked Sela did, and when Sela did not know Ferra did, and by the time the light went there were forty-three children in the book, and their mothers and their fathers and their grandmothers, and which lane they walked down and which bridge they crossed, and Arthur, who was nearly fifteen and the last, had stood at the end of the bench and given his own name and his mother's and then said, with enormous dignity, that nobody walked him home, and Sela had said that was because nobody would have him, and the room had laughed.

Emeric taught figures to the back benches through the whole of it, and nobody learned anything.



Ferra came out of the school at dusk with the book under her arm and ink on two fingers, and he walked her back up to the keep.

The lane was dry for once. It was April, the first warm evening, and the town had its doors open; there were women sitting on steps, and somebody playing a pipe badly somewhere behind the north well, and the smell of the first bread of the night batch coming up from the bakehouse on the corner. The lamps were coming on along the wall, one after another, white, as Tess put them in down at the works, and below them the river was going from silver to grey.

"You were hard on her," he said.

"I was exact with her."

"Those are different?"

"You know they are." Ferra did not slow down. "You told her the same thing two years ago with your face. You've been telling her with your face ever since Tam. I told her with words, once, and now it's done, and she can be angry with me instead of you." She shifted the book on her arm. "She'll be angry with me for a month. Then she'll want the book to be good, because it's hers now. She'll be at my door every week to see it."

He looked at her.

"You did that on purpose," he said.

"I don't know what you mean."

"You let her take it off you. The book. You let her say you'd do it with her, and start with hers. You wanted her to."

Ferra did not answer that. She went on up the lane with the book under her arm and her eyes on the cobbles, and after a while he saw her mouth move at the corner, very slightly, and stop.

They came up through the inner ward and under the arch to the keep, and up the keep stair, and along the passage on the second floor to her door, where her name was, in Hale's plain black letters, with the candle on the sill across from it that somebody had kept lit every evening since February and nobody had ever admitted to.

She stopped at the door and turned, with the book under her arm, to say goodnight.

And she held out her hand.

She did it without thinking. He saw her do it without thinking: the hand came out, palm up, at the height of his chest, the way it came out across the trestle every evening when he stood in front of her with something, a column, a bill, a letter from the coast, a figure he wanted checked. She held out her hand for the paper. She did not even look at it. She was looking past him, down the passage, already thinking about the book under her arm, already doing the next thing.

He took the parcel out of his coat.

He did not untie it. He had thought he would, and then he did not, because she would want to do it herself. He put it in her hand. Not on the sill. Not on the book. In her hand, in the palm, and closed her fingers over it with his own, and let go.

She looked down.

It was oiled cloth tied with a bootlace, the length of her hand from the wrist to the end of the second finger, and light. She looked at it as though it were a letter in a hand she did not know.

"What's this?"

"It's not a column," said Emeric.

She looked at him. Then she put the school roll down on the floor of the passage at her feet, which he had never once seen her do with a book, and took the bootlace off the parcel and wound it round two fingers and put it in her pocket, and unfolded the oiled cloth.

The pen lay on it.

It was dark wood, some wood from the north, polished until it shone like water in the candle on the sill, all one piece from the end you held to the end that wrote, with a steel nib let into the end of it, and round it where the fingers went, a band of gold. Fine. Narrow. Plain. No name on it.

Ferra did not say anything.

She looked at it lying on the cloth in her palm. She did not pick it up. He watched her not pick it up for so long that he began to think he had done the wrong thing, that it was too much, that it was gold and she would say so, that she would ask what it cost and he would have to tell her and she would put it in a column after all.

"The stationer's," she said at last. "By the posting house. In the capital."

He had not known she knew it.

"I was in that street once," Ferra said. "The week we went up for Keller. I didn't go in. I didn't look in the window. I'd had their bills." She did not look up from the pen. "Wexler bought his paper there. Every quarter, for nine years, out of the barony's money, and every bill had their mark on it in red. A wheatsheaf." She turned her hand a little in the candlelight, and there it was, very small, scratched in fine where the gold met the wood: a wheatsheaf. "And one quarter, the year before the plague, there was a pen on the bill. A Gold Rand. For the baron's own use." She was quiet. "I added it to what he owed. I remember thinking a pen couldn't cost a Gold Rand. I remember thinking I'd like to see one that did."

"I didn't know."

"No. You wouldn't." She looked up then. "It's the same shop. It's the same pen, near enough."

"I walked past it," he said. "In the winter. After the rite. With Ward and two horses. It was in the window, on a little stand of its own. I didn't go in." He did not know how to say the rest of it, so he said it plainly. "It's the first thing I ever saw in that city that I wanted to give to anybody. I've had it in my coat since January. I didn't give it to you because every time I saw you, you were working, and I didn't want you to put it with the other pens."

Ferra looked at him.

She looked at him for a long breath in the passage, with the candle on the sill across from her door and her name on it and the school roll on the floor at her feet, and he could not read anything in her face at all, which happened so rarely that he did not know what to do with it.

Then she closed her hand on the pen.

Not hard. She closed her fingers round it in the cloth the way he had closed them a moment before, and held it, against her, at the front of her old coat, and did not say thank you.

"Goodnight," she said.

She picked up the school roll from the floor and opened her door and went in and shut it, and he stood in the passage and looked at her name on it, and then at the candle, and then went up the stair to his own room and sat at his table under the window without lighting anything and did not know whether that had gone well.



He found out on the Monday.

The Standing Order sat in the hall on the first Monday of every month, and in May the first Monday was the fourth, and Ferra brought the barony's book to it, as she brought it to every sitting, and opened it on the table in front of her, and took the minutes of the sitting in it in her own hand as Hale read out each matter.

She took them with the pen.

He saw it when she opened the book. Dark wood, gold band. She did not look at him. She dipped it and wrote the date, and the names of those present, and the first matter, which was the price of copper, and the second, which was the tank, and Bram's report on it, and her hand went across the page small and upright and even as it always went, and the pen in it was only a pen, doing a pen's work.

But before the date, at the top of the page, there was a line.

He read it upside down, across the table, as he had read her book upside down for four years. She knew he could. She had known since the first summer.

It was a ledger line. It had a date on the left, which was the twentieth of April. It had an entry in the middle, in her hand, that said: One pen. From the holder. Not the barony's. And in the right-hand column, where every line in every book she had ever kept had a figure in it, where the sum went, where the value went, the thing that every one of her columns existed to hold, there was nothing.

She had left it empty. The only column he had ever seen her leave empty on purpose, and she had ruled it, so that anybody who looked would see it had been left empty on purpose and not forgotten.

He looked at it. Then he looked up, and she was looking at him across the table, over the book, with the pen in her hand, and she held his eyes for exactly as long as it took Hale to read out the third matter, which was a dispute about a boundary ditch on Farris's land, and then she looked down and wrote it in.

Armand, at the fire end of the table, had seen all of it. Emeric saw him see it. The old man looked at the fire, and folded his hands on his blanket, and his face did nothing at all, which was the most eloquent thing it could have done.
