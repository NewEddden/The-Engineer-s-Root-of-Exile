Chapter 113 - The Second Pattern

He did nothing with the sum.

He sat with it in the dark until the lamp glass had gone cold, and then he lit the lamp again and did not turn to the back of the notebook. The king's city had been a morning away since the Chalk went under the bow. It would still be a morning away tomorrow, and at midwinter, and a figure like that did not spoil for being left. There was nothing he could do with it tonight that he would not have to undo in daylight, so he left it among the figures that did not belong anywhere, and turned forward to the pages that did.

The forge had a page. For five weeks it had run every night, sockets and sleeves and necks going down the river in crates, and on the night the last crate went it had stopped, and it had stood cold since behind its smoked glass in the pattern store. Under its heading there was the date it had stopped, and nothing else.

He looked at that for some time, and thought about the band.

Not about the ship. The ship had a roof now, and air under it a man could breathe, and up there she was a room. He thought about a man outside the room. He had stood in the band over the lake with nothing between him and the sky but a coat, and the cold had gone through the coat as if it were not there, and the air had come short in his chest, and he had come down on his knees on a hatch cover looking at the colour of Ward's mouth. A man in plate up there would have the same cold, and steel to hold it against him. A man in the harness Ward had flown since midsummer would have ten minutes of stones on his back and the rest of the way down to think about it.

Ward would go to Emberfell, and Osric with him, because a prince with one guard in a foreign hall had no guard at all. They would go two miles up in a hull that had flown a handful of times. If anything in her went, they would be the only people aboard who could leave her in the air, and the only thing they had to leave her in was a trial: stones bolted onto the outside of a knight's harness because the harness was there, and a felt box of cells riding high on a man's back like a sack of meal. It had flown. It had never been built to.

He wrote Ward's name under the forge's heading, and turned to a clean sheet, and began to draw.

Bram found him in the pattern store in the morning, at the hold bench, where he had carried the lamp some time after midnight. It was still burning in the daylight, and there were eleven sheets laid along the bench in a row.

He read them the way he read everything, from the first to the last and back to the first, standing, with his hands behind him so that he would not touch. The first sheet was a man seen from the front. The second was the same man from behind. The rest were the man taken apart, a piece to a sheet, and there was not a piece on any of them without something drawn inside it.

"Two," said Bram, at the second sheet.

"Two hearts. One at the breast, and one across the top of the back, between the shoulders." Emeric put a finger on each. "The cells in iron in the middle of each, a deciding stone at the core, a pane over the caps so a man can read what he's got. Either one flies the suit alone."

"So one can go."

"So one can go and he doesn't."

"Nine cells a heart?"

"Nine. Twice what Ward carries now."

Bram did the sum on his fingers against his leg, where he thought nobody could see. "Twenty minutes, flat out."

"He won't fly flat out. He'll glide, and push when he has to."

Bram looked at the front and the back side by side. He had stood in this room at the start of the summer with his hands full of Ward's harness, fitting it plate by plate, and he had heard what Ward said when it was on.

"Heavier on the back than the front," Bram said.

"He said I'd want to fix it."

"When it had flown."

"It's flown."

Bram grunted, which was agreement, and went on down the bench. He went slowly. At the fourth sheet he stopped and bent over it, and again at the sixth, and Emeric watched him look for something, and knew what he was looking for, and let him look.

"There's nothing here I can make," Bram said at last.

"No."

"Every plate's got a hole through it."

"Rods for the charge, the same as Ward's pack has, only inside the steel. Channels for warm air. A tube for water." Emeric turned the sixth sheet so that it faced him. "And the seats. The air stones sit at angles you couldn't hold a stone at with tongs, and the nodes sit where the plate's already curving. You'd make me a good plate, and I'd have to cut it."

"So the forge makes the plate."

"The forge makes all of it. Your foundry makes the steel, cast to the feed, and the forge makes the suit."

He had a rule about the forge, and Bram had watched him keep it all year: the forge did no work a hammer could do. There was one of it in the world and a thousand hammers. He waited to be reminded of it.

But there was no plain plate on these sheets. Every piece carried a rod or a seat or a channel, and the plate was the machine, and Bram could see that as well as he could. He looked through the smoked glass at the cold plate on its legs.

"You said it'd stand idle," Bram said.

"Mostly."

Bram grunted again. It was not the same grunt.

He went back up the bench to the second sheet and stood over the back heart for a long while, and then he said, without looking round, "The hearts. You'll write those in the room."

"Yes."

"I wrote the socket stone in the room. On my own." He said it as a fact, the way he would have said he had cast a pour. "It told the forge right. You checked it three times."

"I did."

"Show me a heart," said Bram.

He showed him that evening, after the works shut, in the room above Kaldo, with Bram come in through his own door.

He put the front heart in the air between them, the way he had once put a bearing there, and let it turn.

It did not look like a heart, in the room. It looked like what it was. The deciding stone sat at the centre, and round it, laid out in the air the way a printed page lay in the printer's stone, was every rule the stone would ever be asked to keep, one inside another. The two levers, and what every hand's width of each meant. How far to let a man sway before answering him, and how to answer once and wait. Glide, and Lighten, and Burn, and Heavy, and the notch that meant now. The air stones in the palms, and the rule that they would not fire round a hilt, because a man with a sword in his hand had given his hand to the sword. The nine cells in their iron and the order it drew on them. What to cut first when they ran low, which was his warmth, and what to keep to the very last, which was whatever held him up. The small stone outside all of it that watched whether all of it was still there. And under the rules, holding them up, the lattice: the shape they made when a stone had to keep all of them at once, in the time it takes a man to lose his footing, without stopping to think which came first.

Bram looked at it for a long time. He walked round it, which a man could do in the room, and bent to see under it, and straightened.

"I can see it," he said. "I can't see all of it at once."

"Nobody can. I couldn't, the first time."

"You wrote it."

"Half of it is the stone on Ward's breast. I've been at the rest since the lake."

Bram nodded slowly. He looked at the lattice a while longer, where the rules crossed, and Emeric saw him find the place where the wing was and follow it back to the stone that watched, and stop there.

"I'd want to see all of it at once," Bram said, "before I told a stone it. If it's wrong, a man comes down." He took his hands out of the air, where they had been hanging near the heart without touching it. "I'll do the sockets. And whatever's a shape. Not that."

Emeric had been ready to argue, a little, on the way up. There was nothing to argue with. Bram had asked, and looked, and drawn his own line in the right place.

So one writer was still true. It was true now of hearts, and of nothing else, and it was Bram who had decided it.

The frames went up on the third day.

Bram's men made them of ash, six of them, man-high, each with a crosspiece for the shoulders and a pole above for a helm, and set them in a row along the long wall of the pattern store, round the corner from the door so that nobody passing could see them. Nobody had told the men why six. Bram had told them six.

Emeric had stood in a yard once and looked at four empty suits on four frames, and understood that he had just done a new kind of thing. He stood in the pattern store and looked at six, and did not feel that he had. He felt that he was late.

Ferra came down with the forge's book under her arm, because she would rather see six frames than be told about them, and counted them, which she did not need to do.

"Ward," Emeric said. "Osric. Declan. Rylan." He went along the row. "Mine. And one for Rowena."

She opened the book on the hold bench and wrote, and he read it upside down. Ward's line, and Osric's, and Declan's. Rylan's, in exactly the same hand as the others: nobody had asked Rylan, and Rylan had not asked, and it made no difference to the book. His own, which she wrote without a word, and he could not tell whether that was trust or a question she was keeping for later.

Then she stopped, with the pen above the sixth line, and did not write in it.

"A queen's suit," Ferra said, "is decided at a table. Not in a forge book."

"I've no pattern for her yet."

"Then it'll keep until you have." She capped the pen. "Five. And the stone for five." She did not look anything up. She had had the figure before she came down the stair. "Six ounces a suit, as the harness had it. Thirty. Twelve come back off Ward's harness and Declan's when you strip them. Eighteen new." She looked at him. "At the Crown's two Platinum the pound, that is twenty-two Gold Rand and five Silver. In stone alone. Before a single heart."

She said it aloud, plainly, as a figure, the way she had said every figure that mattered to him for ten years: first, before anybody could start spending it.

He did not argue with it. The stone was Kaldo's own, out of its own hill, with the Crown's fifth paid on every ounce, and none of that made the figure any smaller. It only meant nobody would ever send them a bill for it.

The harnesses came apart the next day.

Ward's, and Declan's with it, whose nodes had come off the forge in the first weeks of the autumn. Bram and Emeric stripped them on the pattern store's floor, and every stone went back into the stock it had come out of. Vesk took the air stones onto the etching floor's count and looked at the workings a moment longer than counting needed, as he had when he issued them. Armand took the twelve ounces of gravity stone back into his chest, entered them as returned in the column where the summer's six had been written lent, issued eighteen under them, and asked nothing.

That left the harness itself.

It was Bram's steel, forged to each man years ago, with the capital's crystals asleep in their housings and the Crown's marks still on the breast, because the four had kept the Crown's marks when forty men went blank. With the nodes off, it was plain plate again, and a man getting a better suit had no more use for it than for last year's boots. Ward came in while both lay on the floor, and did not ask what they were doing there. He stood by the door with his arms folded, and Emeric understood that he was waiting to see whether they went into the furnace.

The rule in the front of the notebook was older than the forge. The four sets are the four men's.

They went back onto their own stands in the garrison, cleaned and oiled, with the capital's crystals in them exactly as they had come south. A new suit did not change whose the old one was.

Ward watched his carried out, and then went out after it, and said nothing to anybody.

Abloh came up from the soap house on the fifth morning with lye on his sleeves and a letter in his coat.

He had it out before he had sat down. It was from Fletcher, who had his six on the cabins, and said the prince's stern bed was the best joinery the yard had ever done and the prince was not to be told so, and that the six had asked what the slots in her frames were for and been told to mind their planes. Abloh read that part out, and laughed once, the way he had laughed on the beach the summer he went down with the grain boats.

"He says you'll have a job for me," Abloh said. "He didn't say what. He said I'd find it funny."

Emeric told him.

He told him about the cape first, because it was the part that was a sail. A cloak, to all appearances, with a hood, that covered the plate and the stone light. Down its two long edges, furled, battens of ash like the ones along the ship's sides, sprung at every joint so that they wanted to be straight and were held bent. Cords from the four corners to catches at the wrists and the heels. And a catch at the breast that held all of it shut, and let all of it go at once, and then a man in plate falling out of the sky would have a wing from his wrists to his heels.

Abloh took it the way he took a batch at the vats, without hurrying it.

"Not a bone at a time," he said at last.

"No."

"A bat doesn't open a wing one bone at a time." He was looking at the drawing, not at Emeric. "It opens it. Springs, then. Steel, from Bram. At the roots, where the battens join." He put a finger on the shoulder of the drawing. "And it'll have to come off. A cloak that can't come off, a man gets hung by it."

"Two catches, at the shoulders. Under the lions."

Abloh looked up. "Under the what?"

"You'll see."

"And a man that's taken his cloak off," Abloh said, "has taken his wing off."

"Yes."

"I'll want that said to them. By somebody they listen to. Not me." He went back to the drawing. "What's this at the back?"

That was the other half, and it was the half Emeric had been most careful with, because it was the one piece of the suit that had nothing to do with him.

Under the back heart, down the spine from the yoke to the waist, the forge was making a flat pack, closed along its edges with a lattice of seams. In it, folded, was a second wing that was not a wing: a canopy of the lightest cloth Abloh could cut, which would open above a man and let him down slowly if the cape could not carry him out of wherever he was. A ripcord at the left breast. A small spring to throw it out. No stone in it anywhere. Every other thing on the suit ran on stones, and every stone on the suit could die at once, and when they did, this was what would still work, because it did not know there had ever been any stones.

"Silk," Abloh said. "If Saban can find it. And if he can't, the finest linen on the coast, oiled." He sat back. "It'll have to throw clear of the cape. If it comes out into the cape, they'll wrap."

"That's what the bags are for."

Abloh looked at him for a moment, and then nodded, and took the drawing with him when he went.

On the ninth night the forge made the helm, and on the tenth the breastplate and the front heart's housing, and on the eleventh the shoulders.

It made them through the dark with nobody's hands on it, as it had made the sockets for the lower water: Bram's steel into the feed in billets cast to the cup, the plate up off its legs, the pattern going into it a piece at a time, and in the morning a thing on the sand that had not been there at dusk. It was black. The forge took each piece up to a blue heat in the last hour of the night and let it down again in oil, the way Bram blacked a blade, because it was going to fly over salt water and bright steel would not thank anybody for that.

Ward came in on the morning of the eleventh and found the pieces laid out on a cloth on the hold bench, and looked at them for a long time.

The breastplate was one piece. There was no rivet head anywhere on it, and no seam, and in the middle of it, round and shallow and a hand's breadth across, was the housing for the front heart, iron under steel, with a pane set in its face. The pane was dark. It was dark violet, the same as the small panes let into the plate at the shins and the calves and the back, over every place where a stone would sit. The glassworks had made them in one run, all one tint, on Emeric's asking.

"Why one colour?" Ward said, and then, before Emeric could answer: "So nobody can tell which one's lit."

"Yes."

Every kind of stone had its own light when it was working, and a man who knew the lights could read off a suit across a yard which nodes were live and which way the man inside was about to go. Behind one tint they were all the same light. Ward picked up the helm, with its two swept wings of steel above the brow, and turned it, and put it down without comment.

Then he picked up the left shoulder.

It was a lion's head. It sat over the place where the cape would catch, and it was there because the catch needed a housing, and because when Emeric had drawn the housing at three in the morning it had come out of the pencil with a mane. Its eyes were two chips of the same dark glass as the panes, with nothing behind them.

Ward held it in both hands and looked at it, and Emeric knew what he was going to say, because Ward had leaned on a rail in the yard with four blank suits on four frames in front of him, and had not moved, while Emeric told Wick that Kaldo's men would wear no crest, no device and no colour. That had been a comment too.

"That's a device," Ward said.

"It is."

"Forty men in good plate and not a device among them. So everybody who saw them drew the wrong conclusion." Ward turned the lion to the light. "That was the point."

"That was built for a broke barony with forty men in plate," Emeric said. "Nobody mistakes a man who flies for a mercenary."

Ward thought about that for a while. Then he set the lion down beside the helm, facing the door, and said, "Then it's a lion," and that was the end of it.

Osric came in the afternoon and found the collar.

It was fur, a band of dark pelt from the tanner's to sit round the neck of the suit above the gorget, and it did nothing at all. Emeric had drawn it in because the drawing had looked wrong without it. Osric took it off the cloth and held it up to his own neck and went to look at himself in the pane of the front heart, which was the only thing in the room that gave a reflection.

"I'm having this," Osric said.

"It's on all of them."

"I'm having it more than the others." He turned his head from side to side. "Ward. Look."

"It's a coat," said Ward, from the bench, without looking, "on a suit."

Imani came down on the twelfth night, because Ward's suit was off the forge and standing on its frame, whole, and she wanted to see what she would be taking a man out of.

She did not look at the lions. She looked at the gorget, and at the gap at the wrist, and at where a hand would go to reach a man's pulse if he could not take his own helm off, and she found the place and tried it, and it was not good enough, and she said so. By Osric's suit it was. Then she put her hand flat on the breastplate, and took it away, and put it back.

"It's cold."

"It'll be warm. There's a fire stone in the back heart's housing, facing up and out, and the air stones pull warm air round the padding." He showed her where the channels ran, under the lining. "Iron round it, and it faces away from the pack below, so it can't reach the cloth in there."

"And Emberfell?"

He had known she would ask, because Saban had said the word hot at the table in the long room and Imani had heard it.

"You can't make cold," he said. "Nobody can. You can only carry heat out of a place and put it somewhere else, and you pay for the carrying." He turned the suit on its frame so that she could see the back. "So it carries it. Cheapest is air: the fire stone off, and the air stones pulling outside air through the padding and out at the vents. It dries his sweat, and it's quiet. It won't beat the air outside, but in dry heat it feels well below it. Dearer, there's water in a tube against his skin, pushed round by a small stone to the plate at the back, where the vent air crosses it. Dearest, felt over that plate, kept wet from the tube, so the drying takes the heat with it. That costs water. It's for the worst of it."

Imani considered the suit.

"And when it fails?"

"It fails off. A dead system is a warm suit or a cold one. Never a burning one." He touched the front heart's pane. "And when the cells run low, the stone cuts warmth and cold before it cuts anything that keeps him up."

"Flight before comfort."

"Yes."

"Write that down somewhere I can read it," Imani said, "because the first man who cooks in one of these is mine," and went back up the stair.

Ward told Declan in the yard, after the morning's work, and Emeric watched it from the gallery and did not go down.

It was Ward's to tell. The garrison would be Declan's while they were gone, and garrisons were the Secretary of War's. So Emeric stood at the gallery rail with a sheet of the forge's schedule in his hand, not reading it, and watched Ward talk and Declan listen.

He saw the moment Declan understood. It came before Ward had finished. Declan's head came up a little, the way it did on the yard when he had seen the next move and did not like it, and then it went down again, and he stood still with his helmet under his arm and let Ward finish anyway, out of courtesy, because Declan was a man who let his seniors finish.

Armand would hold Kaldo with the sealed letter, as he had for the river and the coast, and a sealed letter in an old man's coat needed a knight on the wall who could be anywhere on it inside a minute. Declan could see who the knight was, and what he would be wearing, and that it meant he was not going.

He did not argue. He nodded, once, the way he took an order he did not care for, and Ward clapped him once on the shoulder and went in.

Declan came up the gallery stair a quarter of an hour later. He stood at the top of it and looked at Emeric, and looked at the sheet in Emeric's hand.

"Mine," he said. "Will it be off the forge before you go?"

"The twenty-sixth night."

Declan did the sum. It was a long way short of midwinter.

"Good," he said, and went back down, and on the yard below Emeric heard him start on the men louder than usual, and keep it up until noon.

The beads came in the nights between.

Some nights the pattern for the next piece was not ready, and on those nights, rather than let the forge stand cold, Bram fed it lamp stones.

It had been Emeric's idea and it was Bram's work. He wanted the four who would go ashore at Emberfell and speak for Kaldo, Ferra and Imani and Saban and himself, to look like what they were: envoys from a barony that could wear as ornament a thing other houses burned in their lamps. It was a small boast, the size a poor barony could make without anybody laughing, and he meant them to be looked at.

So the forge drank a lamp stone to nothing and pressed the empty stone into a bead, faceted, the size of a pea, and took the next. Bram had written the bead's shape into a writer's stone of his own, the third he had ever made, and he ran it himself. In the morning Orrin filled the night's beads, a dozen at a time in a tray, complaining that they were fiddly and that his fingers were not what they had been, and then holding one up to the window to watch it glow and saying nothing at all for nearly a minute. A goldsmith on the quay street set them in open barrels of engraved gold with dark caps at each end, so that the light showed, and sent up a bill for three Gold Rand and six Silver, nearly all of it gold.

There was no control stone in any of it. Emeric had thought about that for a long evening and left it alone. The forge could not shape a stone that held anything, so a control stone would have to go into the cup and be drunk to nothing first, and a stone bound to a man that ran dry ran dry through him. He had six of them in all the world and no spare, and he was not going to find out what that did on one of the six.

Ferra entered the bill, and every piece. A collar for herself. Cuffs for Imani. A chain for Saban. A ring and a chain for him. She entered them as the barony's property, worn on the barony's business, to be returned to the barony's strongbox at the end of it, so that nobody who wore one could ever be said to have been given anything.

She tried the collar on once, in the pattern store, to see that the goldsmith had it right.

It sat at the base of her throat, a row of small lights against the dark of her collar, and she stood very straight in it and looked down at the book instead of at anything else. Emeric looked at it for rather longer than checking the goldsmith's work needed. Then the forge's chamber rang softly behind its glass, the way it did when a piece came off the plate, and he turned to see what it had made, and when he turned back she had taken the collar off and was writing it into the book.

The watchdog was the part Emeric had got wrong the first time, on paper, and he was glad it had been on paper.

The first watchdog he drew was a small stone outside all the rest, on a cell of its own, that watched the deciding stone, and when the deciding stone went dark, it fired the cape. It was a good plan for everything but the one thing it was for. A dead zone would not take the deciding stone and spare the watchdog. It would take all of it at once, silently, the way the three he had made for the war had taken every working on their ground for as long as they lasted. A watchdog that had to wake up and do something would not wake up.

So he turned it over. The watchdog did nothing, as long as it could. It had a small air stone on its own cell, and the air stone kept a leather bellows full, and the full bellows held the breast catch shut against the springs. While the watchdog had power, and while the deciding stone still held, the air stone breathed and the cape stayed furled. If the deciding stone went dark, the watchdog stopped its own air. If its own cell ran dry, the air stopped. And if a dead zone took everything at once, the air stopped with everything else. Abloh stitched the bellows himself, and at Emeric's asking he left a leak in it the size of a needle, on purpose, so that it went slack in the time it took to count three once nothing was filling it. And then the springs won.

The last two needed no stone to decide anything. That was why it would work.

What happened after, he did not know. The cells rode in iron, as the ship's did, and iron had kept the ship's cells whole above the band while the sky outside drank everything it touched. If iron kept a dead zone out as well as it kept the sky out, a man who glided clear of the dead air might find his hearts still full on the far side of it, and his suit waiting for him. He thought it likely. He wrote it in the column for things he could not answer, because likely was not a thing to hang a man on, and the chute went in the pack regardless.

Ward found the hole in it on the day Abloh brought the first cape up to the pattern store, and he found it in about the time it took him to read the sheet.

"The war," he said.

Emeric waited.

"You put dead zones on a field full of men." Ward set the sheet down. "One of ours is standing in it with this on his back, fighting, and it opens. In the middle of a fight. A ten-foot wing on a man who's trying to keep his feet."

It was true, and it had not been on the sheet.

What went on the sheet that evening was a toggle: a plain piece of steel with two positions, behind the catch. Thrown one way, the catch was armed and the bellows held it. Thrown the other, nothing opened the cape but a man's own hand at the breast. The deciding stone threw it armed when the suit left the ground and safe when the man landed Heavy, and a toggle stays where it is put with no power at all, so it stayed armed in the air and safe on the ground whatever the stones were doing. A knight could throw it by hand either way. It would also keep a cape from bursting open on its frame at night when a watchdog cell ran down, which Emeric had not thought of until he wrote the rest.

Ward read it and said, "That'll do," and went back to his practice blade.

They took the bags down the river on the fourteenth day.

It was Emeric and Ward and Orrin and Abloh, and Bram at the steamboat's engine, and two sacks of sand under a tarpaulin in the waist, each an eighth of a ton, which was what a big man in full plate came to. Each was dressed in a set of old garrison leathers, with a cell on its back in an iron cup, a deciding stone, a watchdog, one of Abloh's capes and a packed chute, and no gravity stone at all, because a sandbag had nowhere to go but down.

Fletcher met them on the beach. He looked at the sacks, and at Abloh, and back at the sacks, and Emeric opened his mouth to ask the question he had come down the river to ask, which was whether Fletcher would answer for a man who had never been aboard her.

"He comes," Fletcher said, before he could. "I've known him since he sewed sails in the reeds for half what they were worth. He's kept every secret of mine since before you came south, and a few of his own that I'd not want told." He turned to Abloh. "She flies. You'll have guessed from the battens."

"I guessed from you," Abloh said. "You've not written me a letter that long in eight years."

Fletcher did not answer that. He went down the beach toward the skiff.

The six were on the cabins, and they watched her steam out through the gap in the bar and saw nothing else, because there was nothing else to see. A dozen miles out, with no sail in sight and the coast a grey line behind them, Ward put his hand on the lift lever, and Bram's lad put her legs down, and she climbed up the swell onto her feet and ran there a long way, lightening, until the blades came out of the sea one after another and the legs went up into her, and the screw raced and Bram's lad swung it up out of the sea, and the swell was no longer under her, and she was going up.

She held low, and slow. Ward kept her a hundred fathoms up, by the lead Fletcher let down over the side, going no faster than a man could walk, with the steamboat coming out under her through the grey water, and everybody who was not at the helm or the levers went out from under the roof onto the open deck aft, into a raw wind off the sea.

Abloh had stood at a port for the whole of the lift and said nothing. Now he came out onto the deck behind the others and looked along her side to where his own battens lay folded flush in their recesses, and Emeric saw him understand why they had been wanted flush.

"Right," Abloh said, to nobody, and went to the first bag.

It was made fast to a long line, and the line to a small empty cask, so that when it went into the sea the cask would float and the steamboat could haul it back instead of leaving the stones on the bottom. Orrin had filled its cell on the beach and seated the count stone, a small grey stone Emeric had written in the delve with one rule in it: when the toggle is thrown, count two, and cut the deciding stone's feed. Orrin had filled every cell on both bags and would have known if one was light.

Emeric opened the medium and held it on the bag. The deciding stone was awake. The watchdog was awake, a small bright place breathing steadily into its leather.

Ward threw its toggle to armed by hand as he and Abloh put the bag over, and Orrin threw the cask after it.

It fell. Emeric counted with the stone. At two the deciding stone went dark, and the watchdog felt it go and stopped its air, and the bellows went slack, and on the third count after that the springs won. The cape went out with a crack like a sail filling in a squall.

And the chute went out with it, up through the cape from the pack beneath, and the two of them met above the bag and went round each other like washing in a wind, and the bag came down through the tangle, turning, and struck the sea and went under. The cask bobbed where it had been.

Nobody at the rail said anything.

"It threw up," Abloh said. "Into the cape. I packed it to throw up." He did not sound angry. He sounded like a man who has found out which of two things it was. "It wants to throw down. Below the cape's edge. So it's out from under before it opens."

The steamboat hauled the first bag up by its cask. Abloh did not wait for it. He sat down on the deck in the wind with the second bag across his knees, opened its pack along its seams, and packed the chute again, differently, with his hands going fast and his mouth shut. It took him most of an hour. Nobody hurried him.

The second bag went over at the same height. At two the deciding stone went dark. Three counts after, the cape cracked open, and this time it filled, and the bag under it did not fall. It went out and away from the ship on a long, flat, sliding line over the grey water, lurching, a dead thing flying, and at the end of the line Abloh had built into the pack the chute went out below it, down and clear, and opened, pale against the sea, and the two of them came down together into the water, slowly, like a dropped kite.

The cask floated beside it. The steamboat turned toward it, trailing smoke, and Bram's arm went up from the engine hatch, once.

Abloh watched the chute settle on the water and collapse. Then he looked forward, at the roof of her where Fletcher stood at the helm, and down at the hundred fathoms of empty air between her keel and the sea.

"Next one's a man," he said.

"Next one's a bag in a man's suit," said Emeric. "Then a man."

Abloh nodded, and went below to find something to eat, and did not come up again until they were back inside the bar.

Osric's suit came off the forge on the sixteenth night.

It stood on its frame beside Ward's in the morning, black and whole, with its cape furled down its back like a sleeping bat, and Ferra came down to see it.

Emeric had said twelve nights for the first suit, and she had written twenty-four, because she had never once in ten years written down a figure of his as he said it. He had said four for the second, and she had written eight. The first had come off on the twelfth night and the second on the sixteenth, and she stood at the end of the row with the book open and looked at both of them for some time.

Then she wrote under her figures, in the same even hand, that they had been wrong, and the date, and the figures the forge had actually made.

He read it upside down, and said nothing, because anything he said would make it worse, and because it was the first time he had ever seen her do it and he wanted to remember exactly how it looked.

There were six frames in the row now, and two suits on them. Declan's would follow his own, and Rowena's pattern waited on a delve he had not yet asked Ward for, and Rylan's would come last, onto a frame that nobody had put a name on and that Ferra had entered anyway. All six by the thirty-eighth night, if the forge held, eleven nights before midwinter. One forge. One thing at a time. It was why there would be six of these, and not fifty, and he had stopped minding that sometime in the summer.

That night he did not go up to the long room. He went into the delve alone, and put the knight's pattern in the air in front of him, the one the forge had just made twice, and began, a rule at a time, to take its levers out.

It was the second pattern. It was his.
