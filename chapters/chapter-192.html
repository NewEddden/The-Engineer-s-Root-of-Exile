Chapter 192 - The Bottle That Sighed

The cattle were still in at the home farm, because the grass on the lake meadows was a green haze and no more, and Hollis would not put a beast on it until it would bear a hoof without tearing.

Emeric had come out with him to see the pump house on the irrigation line, because the pump house was going to be the model for the ones on the Milford benches and he wanted to stand in it again with Milford in his head. It was one of the first things he had ever built in Kaldo that ran on a stone, and it had run ever since, a squat stone hut at the head of the first division with a wheel and a rail and a bound stone behind a locked panel, and beside the rail a shelf of charged spares in a rack, in case. Hollis unlocked it for him. Emeric stood inside for a while with his hand on the rail and looked at nothing, and let the sense out a little way, the way he did in any room with stones in it, out of habit.

The bound stone, the spares, and the rail. They sat in the medium the way stones always sat, small dense knots in the thin weather of the room, each one a little brighter than the air round it.

He came out, and Hollis locked it, and they went next door into the byre to look at the calves, because Hollis wanted him to see the calves.

It was a long low building of the old sort, stone below and timber above, and there were forty head of cattle in it standing in their straw, steaming a little in the cold of the morning, and the smell of them and their breath filled it to the rafters. Emeric had not meant to leave the sense out. He simply had not drawn it back in when he came through the door.

The byre was thicker than the pump house.

He stood still, just inside the door. It was not much. It was not a thing anybody else would have felt, or that he would have felt himself if he had not been standing in the other room a moment before. But it was there. The air in the byre, the whole of it, was a little closer and fuller in the medium than the air next door, where a rack of charged stones sat on a shelf beside a rail. Not round any one beast. Everywhere. As though forty cattle standing in a building breathing were, together, a sort of stone themselves, a large dim one, spread out through the whole of the room.

He thought of the crowd in the capital, at the hearing, the density of a thousand people on benches like a weight on the sense. He had put that down to the number of them. He had not thought about what it was they were doing, being a thousand people, that made the medium lie thicker round them.

"The brindle," Hollis said. "She's the one I wanted you to see."

Emeric looked at the brindle calf, and said she was a fine calf, which she was, and filed the other thing where he filed things that he did not yet know what to do with, and went on down the byre with Hollis. It was a small thing. It was not the thing he was looking for. He had been looking for one thing since the night in the pattern store with his hand on the forge's brick, and it was not a byre.

He found it at the glassworks. He found it by not being there.

The glassworks was building a second furnace that spring, a larger one beside the old, for the quarters' windows, which the old furnace could no longer keep up with now that there were houses going up on the flat faster than panes could be blown for them. The walls of it Bram's masons had laid. The crown of it, the low arch of firebrick that would sit over the melt and take the heat of it day and night for years, was the hard part, because a furnace crown has to go up in one piece, every brick of it resting on every other, and the centring the masons had built to hold it while it set had cracked in the frost.

So Armand lent them Paskin.

Paskin was an earth mage, Tessaly Rook's pair on the wall's west side, and he had spent two summers lifting courses of dressed stone into line on the flat. He held the whole of the crown up, brick by brick, in the air over the furnace's walls, while the masons set it and wedged it and struck its joints, and it took him the morning, with the whole glassworks standing back against the walls in silence while he did it. When the last brick went in and the masons stood back and he let it go, the crown stayed where they had set it, as the wall on the flat stayed where the masons set it, because they had set it and not because he held it. Then he sat down on an upturned cullet box with his hands between his knees and did not get up.

Armand had come down to watch. He came down to watch most things now that he could no longer do them, and he sat on his own stool by the door with his stick across his knees. He looked at Paskin on his box for a while. Then he took a flask out of his coat.

He carried two now, always, in the deep pockets of the grey coat, the iron quarts with their black-pitched lids, the same as came round on the handcart at noon on the flat. He had stopped taking his behind the cart the summer before. He took them in front of everybody now, and Hobb wrote his name and the mark, and he had told Hobb that if he ever did not write it he would know. He got up off his stool, which took time, and crossed to Paskin, and put the flask in his hands.

"One," Armand said. "You're spent. I'll write it for Hobb."

Paskin turned the lid. He did it the way they all did, the way Emeric had watched forty mages do it at the handcart, quickly, as if it might get away, and the medium came out of the neck.

Emeric felt it go through the room. He had felt it a hundred times. The whole hot glasshouse flooded with it, thick as the band, for the space of a breath, so that the fire stones in the old furnace's hearth brightened and every lamp stone in the place came up a shade; and Paskin straightened on his box as though somebody had poured warm water down his back, and drank. And then it went on out, through the walls and the doors and the open louvres in the roof, thinning as it went, as it always went. Second breath, half of it gone. Third breath, the room was a room again, and the lamps sat back down, and Paskin was sitting up with his colour back and the empty flask in his hands, and that was that. Three breaths. It was always three breaths, in a room, and then it was the sky's.

Nobody paid it any mind. At the bench under the window, while the room was full, the gaffer's boy was finishing a row of the small dispensary bottles Imani ordered by the gross, the kind with a ground-glass stopper that fitted its own neck and no other, and he was pressing the stoppers home as they came off the annealing tray, one after another, the way he did at the end of every batch, with a little twist to seat them. He seated the last one in the breath the room was full. He did not know he had done anything. Nobody did.

Vesk found it the next day, across the yard.

The bottles went across to the packing shed in their straw, to be counted and crated for the ward, and Vesk kept the packing shed's book because he kept the books of anything in the glassworks that needed a careful man and a slow one. He was going down the row with a cloth, taking each stopper out and putting it back to see that it turned, because Imani had sent back a crate in the winter with three stoppers seized in their necks, and Imani did not send things back twice.

The last bottle in the row sighed.

That was the word he used, afterward, and it was the right one. He drew the stopper, and there was a sound out of the neck like a small breath let out, soft, and a movement of something past his fingers that was not air, and the two lamp stones on the shelf above the bench, which had been burning low in the grey afternoon, flickered and came up bright for the space of a heartbeat and went down again.

Vesk stood with the stopper in one hand and the bottle in the other for a long time.

He had been to the band. He had sat on the K Discovery's deck with his back to the bulkhead and his eyes shut, breathing it, and he had asked to go again so that he would know whether it was the same the second time, and he had written down what he felt beside what Emeric felt. He knew what had just come out of the neck of the bottle. It was a very little of it. It was a bottle's worth. But he knew what it was.

He put the stopper back, carefully, and wrapped the bottle in its straw, and did not put it in the crate. He carried it across the yard and up to the keep in both hands, the way a man carries a thing he is afraid to jog, and found Emeric in the long room, and set it on the table in front of him.

"This held the band," Vesk said. "Overnight. In a shed."

Emeric did not believe it. He would say so afterward, at the table, plainly, because it was true and because it was the part worth saying.

He had worked it out in the pattern store with his hand on the brick. The medium went through brick like water through a sieve. It went out of a room in three breaths, through the walls and the doors and the roof. It went through everything except iron. He had known iron held it since the cells came down through the top of the band whole because they rode in iron, and he had built the ship's rails in iron sleeves on it, and the flasks, and every housing that had to keep the sky out or keep the band in. Iron held. Everything else leaked. He had never once tested the everything else. He had never needed to. He had an answer that worked, and he had stopped asking the question the day the answer worked.

He took the stopper out of the bottle himself, there at the table, with Vesk watching. There was nothing. The little that had been in it had gone out when Vesk opened it in the shed, and the bottle was only a bottle, and the room was only a room.

He put the stopper back.

"Show me again," he said. "Properly. And get Armand."

Armand laid out the row in the packing shed the next morning, on the long bench under the window, and would not let anybody help him do it, and took most of the morning.

He did it the way he did everything that was going into the box: slowly, exactly, and with a control at each end. Six vessels in a row, each big enough to take a flask inside it with room to spare, each with a lid that fitted. An iron box, riveted, with an iron lid on a gasket, off the forge's shelf. A glass jar, the widest the glassworks had ever blown, with a ground-glass lid that Vesk had spent the evening grinding to its rim with fine sand until it would hold water upside down. A crock from the potter on the island, glazed inside and out, with a glazed lid. Its twin, the same crock from the same kiln, fired in the same batch, left unglazed, the red clay bare. A little cask, oak, from the cooper, tight enough to hold small beer. And a box of firebrick, laid dry and mortared, with a brick lid, the same brick as the forge's chamber.

At each end of the row, on its own, a lamp stone of the common cut, charged that morning by Orrin and weighed on Ferra's grain balance: one to put into every vessel in turn when it was opened, to read it, and one never to go into anything at all, so that whatever the first one did could be set against a stone that had done nothing.

"One flask to each," Armand said. "Opened inside, lid on at once. Tomorrow, the same hour, opened in the same order, the reading stone held in the mouth for a count of ten. Bram counts. I read. The prince feels, and says nothing till I've written, so I'm not told what I felt." He looked at Emeric. "And Vesk writes, because Vesk found it, and it's his."

Vesk went red and did not say anything, and took the book.

They did it. It took an hour, because Armand's hands were slow and he would not let anybody else turn a lid. A flask set down inside each vessel on the floor of it, the lid given a turn, and the vessel's own lid clapped down on top in the same movement, before the three breaths were done. Six times. The shed filled a little each time round the edges of a lid going down, and emptied again through its walls. Then the six vessels sat in their row on the bench under the window in the spring light, closed, and looked like nothing: a box, a jar, two pots, a cask, a pile of bricks.

Emeric put his hand on each of them before he left. Through the iron he could feel it, faintly, as he always could through iron, like a banked fire through a stove door. Through the glass he could feel it the same way, no stronger and no weaker, and he stood with his palm on the cold curve of the jar for longer than he meant to. The glazed crock he could feel. The unglazed crock he could feel the way he had felt the forge's brick: going, everywhere, slowly. The cask the same. The brick the same.

He did not say anything. He had been told not to.

They opened them at the same hour the next day.

The iron box first. Bram lifted the lid off its gasket, and there was the sigh, the same as Vesk's bottle but longer, deeper, a breath let out by something the size of a box, and Armand held the reading stone in the mouth of it and it came up bright, white-bright, brighter than Orrin had left it, and held, and Bram counted ten, and it was still bright at ten. Armand took it out and put it on Ferra's balance and wrote nothing and gave it to Vesk to write.

The glass jar. Vesk lifted the lid himself, because he had ground it. The same sigh. The stone the same, bright, and holding at ten.

The glazed crock. The sigh was there, and the stone came up, and held, not quite so bright as the other two, a little less by Armand's eye and by the balance, but holding.

The bare crock. No sigh. The stone flickered in its mouth, once, and was a lamp stone in a pot.

The cask. Nothing.

The brick. Nothing at all.

Armand put the reading stone down on the bench beside the control, which had sat at the end of the row all day and all night doing nothing, as it had been told. He looked at the six vessels for a long time. Then he picked up his pen and wrote in his own hand under Vesk's lines, in the small cramped hand that got worse every winter, and Emeric read it upside down.

Fused holds. Porous leaks.

"Iron's fused," Armand said. "Glass is fused. A glaze is a skin of glass on a pot, melted on, and it holds as long as it's whole, and a little less, I think, because nobody glazes a pot perfectly." He tapped the bare crock with his pen. "Fired clay is grains stuck together. There's a way between every grain. Wood's a bundle of straws. Brick's clay." He put the pen down. "The medium goes between things. It doesn't go through them. If there's a way between, it finds it, and if there isn't, it stays. That's all this is." He looked at Emeric. "Your forge was never going to hold it. It's brick. It was holding it the way a sieve holds water, for as long as you were pouring."

"I know," said Emeric. "I know that now."

Ferra came down at noon, because Vesk's book had gone up to her and she wanted to see the row for herself before she wrote anything in hers. She walked along it with her hands behind her back, box and jar and crocks and cask and bricks, and stopped at the jar.

Armand was sitting on a stool at the bench's end with his stick across his knees, and he had been quiet for a while, and when he spoke it was to Emeric and not to her.

"The ship," he said. "The glass stone. The one you hung in her vein, the autumn you put the roof on her, behind a pane in the vein's wall, so the man at the helm could see it." He turned his stick a quarter turn on the floor. "I've read that log twenty times. That vein was full of the band, pushed through her at a gallop, and the room under the roof was thinner, and the pane sat between them the whole flight. And the pane never let the band through into the room. I thought at the time it was because the pane was small. It wasn't small enough to matter. Why didn't it leak?"

Emeric looked at the glass jar.

"Because it couldn't," he said. "It was a window, not a door. The stone hung in the vein, in the vein's own air. The glass never had to let anything through. It only had to let us see." He was quiet for a moment. "I had it in front of me on every flight for a year. I watched that stone glow through that pane every time she came into the band, and I never once asked why the band stayed on its own side of the glass. I'd decided iron was the answer. I stopped looking at anything that wasn't iron."

Nobody said anything to that. It was not a thing that needed an answer. Ferra wrote something, short, and he did not read it.

Then she put her hand flat on the glass jar, the way Armand put his on stones, though she could feel nothing through it and never would.

"You can see into this," she said.

"Yes."

"Into the iron you can't. You have to open it or feel it, and I can't feel it." She looked at the jar. "If there were a stone in here, a reading stone, and it glowed brighter the fuller the jar was, I could stand in the door and read it. Anybody could. A clerk could read it. A boy could. You wouldn't have to be you, or Armand, or Vesk, to know how full a thing was." She took her hand off the glass. "That's a gauge. That's a gauge a clerk can write down."

Emeric looked at her, and then at the jar, and then at the iron box beside it with its lid off, dark inside, that nobody could see into without leaning over it with a lamp.

"Iron was the only answer I ever thought of," he said. "In all that time, it was the only one."

"It was a good answer," said Ferra. "It was right every time you tried it."

"That's the worst kind."

"I know," she said. "You've said." And went back up to her book.

He said the rest at the table that evening, once, because it was the kind of thing that needed saying once and then being true.

"Glass holds as well as iron," he said. "Better, for some things, because you can see in. But glass breaks. A man drops a flask of iron on the quay and he picks it up. A man drops a jar of glass on the quay and he's standing in the band for three breaths and then he isn't." He put his hands flat on the table. "So glass where it stands still. On benches. In walls. Fixed in its place, where it won't be knocked and where somebody needs to see what's in it. And iron for anything that's carried. Anything that goes up and comes down, or goes in a hold, or in a man's pocket. Iron."

Ferra wrote it.

Bram, at the foot of the table, had said nothing all evening. He had his big hands on the table and he was looking at them, and Emeric knew what he was thinking about, because he was thinking about the same thing. A room that held the band, on the ground. A room that was not brick.

"Iron," Bram said, "for what's carried."

"Yes."

"And for what isn't carried, but mustn't break." Bram did not look up. "What mustn't break, whatever knocks it. What's got a thing inside it moving." He turned one hand over on the table, palm up, and looked at the palm. "That's iron too. Glass for looking in. Iron for the box."

Emeric looked at him.

"I've seen you draw a ring," Bram said. "In the pattern store. You left it on the bench." He closed the hand. "You'll want it in iron."

The bottle that sighed went into the box that night, in its straw, with a label in Vesk's hand tied round its neck by a thread. Ferra entered it: one dispensary bottle, glass, ground stopper, from the glassworks' batch of that week, stoppered by chance in a flood, opened next day across the yard, found full. By V. And the date.

Emeric went down to the pattern store afterward and stood at the long bench under the high window, where the sheet with the ring on it still lay under its weights where he had left it.

He took up the pencil. Round the outside of the ring, not touching it, a finger's width off, he drew a second line, the same shape, all the way round. A box. Thick-walled, and closed. Then he sat and looked at it, and then he wrote one word in the margin, small, beside the box, and it was the first word that had gone on the sheet.

Iron.
