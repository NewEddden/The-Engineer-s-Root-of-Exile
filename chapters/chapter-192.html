Chapter 192 - Burning Water

Ferra was at the dining room table at dawn with the book open, as he had known she would be, and a cup of something gone cold beside it, and her hair up, and no sign at all on her that she had slept.

He sat down across from her.

She did not look up. She turned a page, and ran her finger down it, and wrote a figure, and blotted it. Out on the balcony the light was coming up grey over the inlet and the ships' lamps were going out one by one on the water, and down on the quay a man was shouting at a cart.

"You were right," said Emeric.

Ferra's pen stopped.

"About the grain," he said. "In October. You said she had a coast, and southern grain was cheaper at her quay, and I'd be offering bread to a harbor. You wrote it down. I overruled you." He looked at the book. "You were right. I was wrong."

She sat with the pen over the page for a while.

Then she put the pen down, carefully, beside the book, the way she always put it down, and looked at him across the table; and her face was perfectly composed, and very pink at the tops of her ears.

"Say it again," she said, "when Saban's in the room."

He looked at her.

"He was at the long table in October when you overruled me. He heard you do it." She folded her hands on the book. "He'll want to have heard this too. It's his trade. He'll be asked, on the coast, whether the Holder of Kaldo listens to his chancellor, and I'd like him to be able to say yes and have been there." She did not look away. "And I'd like to hear it twice."

Saban was in the kitchen. Emeric could hear him through the door, talking to the harbor house's cook about fish. He got up and opened the door and asked him to come in for a moment, and Saban came in with a heel of bread in one hand and a cup in the other and the look of a man who has been woken by gulls and does not hold it against them.

"Ferra was right about the grain," said Emeric. "In October. She said so at the long table and I overruled her. I was wrong."

Saban looked at him. Then at Ferra. Then at the bread in his hand, as if it might help.

"I know," he said. "I was there."

"I know you were."

"I've been waiting since October to hear you say it." Saban bit the bread. He chewed for a while. "So's half the counting room. Hale had money on it." He swallowed. "Not on whether. On when. He said by midwinter." He looked at the window, where the sun was getting up over the inlet. "It's three days after midwinter. Hale loses." He went back toward the kitchen; at the door he turned. "I'll tell the coast," he said. "When they ask. I'll tell them I heard it at dawn, at a table in Emberfell, with the bread still warm." And went out.

Ferra picked up her pen.

She turned back in the book. Back past the voyage and the manifest and the months, to October, to the page he knew without seeing it; and under the line in her square careful hand, Grain to Emberfell, at the holder's wish. Said so. Overruled. she wrote, in the same hand, smaller:

Admitted. Emberfell. Before Saban.

And the date. And she ruled a line under all of it, straight across the page, and closed it.

"There," she said. She turned to a clean page at the front. "Now. The clerk."



They drafted it at the dining room table in the two hours before Rowena's grey knight came for them, from her line, with the cold cup at Ferra's elbow and the bread Saban had brought them going stale between them.

Ferra wrote. He talked. Then he wrote and she talked, because she had thought about it all night and he had only thought about it since midnight, and it showed.

The clerk first. One clerk, in the standing room, sworn and paid out of the duchy's purse, who could put the Sovereign's name to a thing when the Sovereign was not in the room, under a seal of his own that said so, for a list of things the Sovereign wrote down; and the things went through, and the clerk answered for them to the table, and the table answered to her. Then the table, sat. Not once in twenty years; every month, on a fixed day, with a book, the eight chairs filled by whoever held the eight offices, so that the day the stone on the wall was needed there would be eight men in Emberfell who had sat at that table a hundred times and knew how. Then a school, a small one, under the dome, to make the next clerk, and the clerk after; reading and figures and the law on the stone.

"Printing," said Emeric.

Ferra looked up.

"The law on the stone. Printed. A sheet of it on every hall door and every quay and every chapel in the duchy, so every man in Emberfell can read it who can read, and hear it read who can't. Not one stone on one wall under the dome where nobody goes." He tapped the page. "If they all know what it says, it's harder to put a knife in the arena. A man with a knife has to explain himself to people who've read the sheet."

"A press," said Ferra. "Ours. Here."

"A small one. And a man to run it. And type."

She wrote it. "Standards," she said. "Like the market hall. A yard bar and a set of weights on the quay wall in an iron case, cast from the forge's masters, so that every boat that comes in can test the harbor's measures and the harbor can test theirs." She wrote. "They take dues by weight. Saban says the fishing families think the weights are light." She looked up. "He's right. They are. I looked at the harbor book. The harbor-reeve's been short-weighting the fish for thirty years and doesn't know it, because his father did."

"And drains."

"And drains. Imani will want to see their wells before she says what. She'll tell you tonight." Ferra turned the page. "All of it in her name. Not Kaldo's. Not yours. The press is the Sovereign's press, the school is the Sovereign's school, the clerk signs for the Sovereign. So that in ten years none of it needs you, or us, or anybody from Randillia, and nobody on that quay can say it's Harrold's son's." She looked at him. "That's the point. If it needs you, it's grain again."

"Yes."

"And for us," said Ferra. She wrote a heading. "Sulphur. By the boatload. From her pits." She did not say what for, and neither did he; they both knew, and it was not a thing to write in a book at a table in a foreign harbor. "Something for the forge's glass, and later the steel. I don't know what. You'll know when you see the works."

"A throat," said Emeric. "If they have what I think they have."

"A throat," wrote Ferra, and looked at the word, and did not ask. "And a lane. Our boats down the coast to her quay, her boats up to ours. Both ways, with a factor at each end who knows the dues." She paused, with the pen over the page. "Saban."

"Saban will want it."

"Saban will want it more than he's wanted anything since the post." She wrote his name and underlined it. Then she sat back, and looked at the page, and at him across the table; and she looked tired, suddenly, and young, and very pleased, and not at all as if she meant him to see any of it.

"It's good," she said. "It's better than the grain."

"It's yours."

"It's ours," said Ferra. "I had the line. You had the press." She closed the book. "Don't tell her the line was mine. Tell her it was Kaldo's." She stood up. "She'll know anyway. She knew yesterday. She looked straight at me down that table, when she said somebody at your table might have an opinion." She put the book under her arm. "I'm going to wash my face. The grey knight will be on the stair in ten minutes and I've been awake since the bath."



Rowena took them through the works in the morning.

The works were on the mountain's flank above the city to the east, a mile up a road cut in terraces, and she walked it, in a plain coat with the long sword at her hip, with the grey knight beside her and the master of the works, a thin anxious man with burn scars on both hands, half a step behind; and the Kaldo party behind them, Emeric and Ward and Ferra and Saban and Imani, and Fletcher, who had asked to come because he had heard there was a boatyard at the bottom of it and would have walked up a volcano to see a boatyard.

The road smelled of eggs. The higher they went the more it smelled, until the smell was not under the air but was the air, and Saban had his handkerchief over his nose. The ground at the roadside was yellow in places, crusted, and steaming, and the terraces above the road had no vines on them; nothing grew there. Men worked along them with long iron rakes and baskets, raking the yellow crust off the rock where it came up out of cracks in the mountain, in wet cloths tied over their mouths, and carried it down to a long shed by the road in the baskets on their backs.

"Sulphur," said Rowena. "The mountain breathes it. Every crack. It grows on the rock like frost." She looked at the men. "We burn it to clean the wine casks and sell what's left to the dyers and the doctors." She looked at Emeric. "It's not worth much. There's a mountain of it."

"It's worth a great deal," said Emeric, "to somebody who needs a mountain of it."

She looked at him a while longer, and went on up the road.

The works were at the top of it, against the mountain's side, where a long cliff of black rock went up out of the terraces; and the first thing Emeric saw was that there was no smoke.

No chimneys. No woodpiles. No coal heaps, no charcoal carts, nothing burning anywhere. There were furnaces. He could see them from the road, a row of them along the foot of the cliff, glass furnaces and kilns and a lime oven, their mouths glowing orange in the shade of the rock; and the heat came off them across the yard so that the air shook. But nothing was being fed into them. The men at the glass furnaces worked their pipes and their marvers and their gathers the way Ivo's men did at Kaldo, and nobody stood behind them with a shovel.

"The ground," said Rowena.

The furnaces were built over the cliff's foot, where the mountain came closest to the air. There were cracks in the black rock there, like the cracks the sulphur grew in on the terraces, but bigger, and deeper, and what came up out of them was heat. Not steam; heat, dry and steady and endless, out of the mountain's own body, and the furnaces had been built over the cracks in the time of her grandfather's grandfather, with flues to take it up into their chambers, and dampers to turn it down; and they had never gone out. Not once. Not in two hundred years.

Emeric stood in the yard and looked at the glass furnace nearest him, at the orange throat of it and the flue going down from its floor into the black rock, and put his hand flat on the rock beside the flue, and let his sense go in.

He knew she would feel it. He did it anyway; he could not have stopped himself. He went down into the rock, past the flue, past the cracks, down, following the heat, and it went down further than his sense would follow, and got hotter all the way, and did not stop. Under the works, under the cliff, under the whole mountain, there was heat enough to melt every grain of sand on every beach in Randillia, and it had been there for longer than there had been people, and it would be there after.

A furnace that never needed feeding. A heat that did not burn anything to make it. Glass, at Kaldo, cost Ivo a cart of wood a melt. Steel cost the converter a mountain of coke. Here, a man had only to build over the right crack.

He took his hand off the rock.

Rowena was watching him. She did not say anything about it; she only looked at him, and at his hand, and at the rock, and then at the master of the works, who was explaining to Ferra how the dampers were set and had not noticed anything at all.

"A throat," said Emeric, to her. "One of those. For Kaldo. For our glass, first."

"And then?"

"And then something harder than glass."

"You don't say much," said Rowena, "when you're asking for something big." She turned and walked on along the furnaces. "Amara was the same. You'd ask her what she wanted for her birthday and she'd say a ribbon, and you'd find out in March she'd meant a horse."



The boatyard was at the bottom of the mountain again, on the harbor's north side, beyond the fish quay, and it was the smell he noticed first there too.

Not eggs. Something he had not smelled in seven years, and had not expected to smell again in this world, and knew the instant it reached him, the way a man knows his mother's voice across a market.

He stopped in the yard gate.

The yard was the usual yard: slips going down into the water with hulls on them in every stage, frames like the ribs of a whale, planked hulls, a coaster nearly done with her masts going in; sawpits; a steambox for bending planks; and at the water's edge a long low shed with its doors open and a fire in it. Not a fire. Men were caulking a hull on the nearest slip, driving oakum into her seams with irons and mallets and paying it over with pitch from a kettle; and the kettle sat on a ring of iron over a flame, and the flame came out of a copper bowl with nothing in it but a dark liquid and a strip of cloth for a wick; and it burned hot and yellow, with less smoke than he would have believed of it, and smelled of it.

Fletcher had gone straight to the coaster. Ward had stopped beside Emeric in the gate, because Emeric had stopped. Ferra came up on his other side and looked at his face, and then at the kettle, and then at his face again.

"What is it?" she said.

He did not answer. He went across the yard to the slip and the kettle and the copper bowl under it, and crouched down beside it, and looked at the flame, and at the liquid in the bowl. Dark brown, nearly black, and thin, thinner than lamp oil, and it moved in the bowl like water when the man at the kettle knocked it with his boot.

"Burning water," said Rowena, behind him.

He looked up at her.

"From Duvrenne. They have pools of it on their beaches, beyond the peninsula, they say, that come up out of the sand by themselves. Black, and it stinks. The fishermen up there skim it off the pools with buckets and sell it in casks to anybody who'll carry it." She looked at the flame. "My shipwrights swear by it. It's hotter than wood under a pitch kettle and cleaner than charcoal and you can carry a summer's worth in a barrel. We put it in the street lamps on the lower terraces where the light stones keep getting stolen." She looked at him crouched there. "It's not worth much either. They practically give it away. The Duvrenne fishermen think it's a curse on their beaches."

He put out his hand and dipped one finger into the bowl, past the wick, and took it out again, and rubbed the oil between his finger and his thumb, and smelled it.

Seven years.

Light. Sweet under the stink, sulphurous, thin. A light crude, not a heavy one; the kind that came up out of the ground nearly ready to use, the kind Earth's first refiners had boiled in iron pots in the open and drawn off the lamp oil and the grease and the spirit that would run an engine. He knelt in a boatyard in Emberfell with oil on his fingers and saw lamps, and grease for every axle and bearing Kaldo turned, and the converter running hotter, and the forge in a bigger room; and, very far off, past all of it, a thing on four wheels on a road with nobody pushing it.

He stood up. His knee told him about the clay.

"It's worth a great deal," he said. "To me."

Rowena looked at him for a long moment.

"Is it," she said.

That was all she said about it. She turned to the master shipwright, who had come out of the shed wiping his hands, and asked him about the coaster, and Fletcher came back across the yard to tell her what he thought of the coaster's sheer, which was a great deal, and not all of it kind, and she listened to him as if he were the only man in the yard.

When they came down to the quay in the afternoon, there were two casks on it beside the K Discovery's gangway, iron-bound, tarred, with Duvrenne's mark burned into their heads and Rowena's seal in wax on their bungs. Cade stood beside them, looking at them, whistling.

"Came an hour ago," he said. "On a cart. Man said they were for the ship. From the Sovereign. No charge." He looked at the casks. "No note either."

"She didn't need a note," said Ferra.



Dinner that night was smaller, in a long room off the hall with one table, and louder.

Rowena's knights ate with them, a dozen of them, out of their plate and in coats, and the Kaldo party, and the grey knight at the foot of the table, and the treasurer, who had decided overnight that he liked Saban and sat next to him and talked about the price of glass. Rowena at the head. She was not cold tonight. She was not warm either; she was a Sovereign at her own table among her own people, and she talked to the knight on her left about a horse and to Ward on her right about Harrold's yard twenty years ago, in which, it emerged, Ward had once fallen off a wall in front of the whole court and claimed afterward to have meant to.

"You hadn't meant to," said Rowena.

"No," said Ward.

"You landed on the provost."

"He was in the way."

"He was the provost," said Rowena. "It was his wall."

The knights laughed. One of them, a young one with a split lip from the yard, asked down the table whether it was true that in Kaldo they let the girls into the yard; he had heard it from a coaster's mate who had heard it from a carter; and Ward said it was true, there were two in his school that spring, and the young knight said, with his mouth full, that he had not thought anybody north of the bay did that any more, since the old stories; and Imani, across the table, asked him what old stories.

He did not know. Nobody at the table knew, exactly; there had been a woman with a spear, a long time ago, in the founding of the duchy, and the dome's painted ceiling had her on it somewhere, faded, and the fishing families still said her name when they cast off. That was all.

"There are a lot of stories like that," said Emeric.

He had had two cups of the black wine. He had drunk them, tonight, which he did not often do; and the room was warm and loud, and Ferra was beside him with her book closed for once under her hand, and Imani across from him, laughing at something; and he started to tell them before he had decided whether to.

A goddess born out of her father's head in full armor, grey-eyed, with an owl on her shoulder, who was wisdom and war at once and could not see why anybody thought those were different things. A queen of crows who stood over battlefields and washed the armor of the men who were going to die in the ford before the battle, so that they would see her and know. Women on horses who rode down out of the sky over every battlefield and chose which of the dead to take, and took the best, and left the rest. A goddess with ten arms and a weapon in every hand, riding a lion, sent by all the other gods together because none of them could kill the thing she was sent to kill, and she could.

The knights were listening. The young one with the split lip had stopped eating. Fletcher, at the end, had his chin on his fist. Rowena sat at the head of the table with her cup in her hand and did not drink from it.

"And another one," said Emeric, "who took half the dead from every battle for her own hall, and the king of the gods had to make do with the other half." He drank. "Nobody ever argued with her about it."

The young knight laughed. So did Ward, which was rare.

"What language," said Rowena, "are those names in?"

He had not said the names. He realized it as she asked. He had told every one of them without a name, out of some habit he had not known he had; and she had heard him not say them, and asked.

"An old one," he said.

"Not one of ours."

"No."

She looked at him down the table.

"You talk about gods," said Rowena, "the way a sailor talks about weather." She turned the cup in her hand. "As if they were simply there, all of them, and you'd dressed for them and would be glad when they'd blown over." She did not say it unkindly. She said it as she had said the cup's level, at the first dinner: a fact, put down on the table where he could see it. "I've sat at Harrold's table with three cardinals. None of them talked about God as easily as you talk about a woman with ten arms."

Nobody said anything. The young knight looked from her to Emeric and back.

Emeric opened the door to Ward without thinking.

The width of a hair, across the corner of the table, to Ward's note at Rowena's right hand: a reflex, the thing he did at a table in the capital or a hall in the north when he needed his knight to know something without the room knowing it. He did not even know what he was going to say down it. Something about the cardinals.

Rowena put her cup down.

"Stop that," she said.

The table went quiet.

"Whatever it is." She was looking at him, and then, briefly, at Ward, and back. "Whatever you do, the two of you. There's a draft in this room, between you and your knight, like a door open in the next room. I can't hear a word of it and I don't want to." She picked her cup up again. "Stop whispering to your knight at my table, Emeric. It's rude in any language. Including the old one."

He shut the door.

He shut it so fast that Ward, at her right hand, blinked; and the knights round the table looked at each other, and at him, and at their Sovereign, with the faces of men who have heard their lady say a thing they do not understand and will not ask about; and the treasurer went on telling Saban about the price of glass, a little louder.

Rowena drank. She looked at him over the cup for a long moment.

And then she smiled.

It was the first time he had seen her do it. It came up slowly at the side of her face that had no scar, and pulled, at the corner, at the other side, where the scar was; and Imani across the table saw it pull, and he saw her see it.

"Go to bed, Emmy," said Rowena.

He sat very still.

Nobody had called him that since he was twelve. He had forgotten, until she said it, that he had ever been called it at all. Once, at court, the season he was twelve, in a passage, after a dinner where he had fallen asleep in his chair, this woman had stood over him and said it, Go to bed, Emmy; and then, as if to herself, that it had been his mother's word for him, and nobody else's. He had no memory of his mother saying it. He had no memory of his mother before the carriage at all. He had only this, in a passage, from a queen who was not his mother, borrowed, and he had kept it without knowing he had kept it for seventeen years.

"Your knight's laughing at you," said Rowena. "Go to bed. You fight me again in the morning, and this time you can bring whatever you've got." She turned to the knight on her left and asked him about his horse.

He went to bed.

Ferra came down the stair with him, and Imani, and neither of them said anything until the harbor house door; and at the door Ferra stopped and looked up at him in the lamplight, with the book under her arm.

"Emmy," she said.

"Don't."

"I'm not going to." She went past him into the house. "I'm writing it down," she said, from the stair. "That's different."
