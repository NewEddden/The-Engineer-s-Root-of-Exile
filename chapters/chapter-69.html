Chapter 69 - Empty Chairs

He inherited two administrations that autumn, and both of them had been built by men who were now dead or condemned, and both of them went on working exactly as before, because that is what an administration does when nobody tells it to stop.

Wayford's was a junction that skimmed its own tolls. It was not subtle. Every cart through the crossing paid a crown rate and a baron's rate, and somewhere between the gate and the counting house a third rate had grown up that belonged to neither, and it had been growing for twenty years under a factor who had grown fat on it and a baron who had known and taken his share. Grayrock's was worse and duller. Forty years of Renner and Renner's father taking the veins as a baron's due, run by clerks who had never once been asked to account for anything, because there had never been anybody above the baron to ask.

Both of them had to be emptied and filled again. He had done it once already, in Kaldo, in his first year, on instinct, by liking some men and not liking others, and not all of it had held. He did not do it that way now.

It was the best method he had used in seven years, and very little of it was his.

Ferra read the ledgers. Every line of every seized book in both baronies, and she was looking for one thing only, which was the lines that reconciled. A man who had kept honest books inside a dishonest house was a man who either had not been worth corrupting or had been too careful to be corrupted, and either was worth having. Ward went the other way. Ward had a quiet channel into every garrison and gatehouse and alehouse in the south, and he was looking for the men nobody had anything on. No debts that had been bought. No sisters in the wrong household. No friends who had asked for favours.

Where the two lists met, the man got the post.

It produced almost nobody Emeric would have picked. He said so, at the table, looking at the names. A clerk with a stammer for the Wayford gate. A widow's son for the Grayrock tally house who had been a copyist for eleven years and had never once looked up from his desk. A retired sergeant for the crossing guard who had been passed over for promotion three times and was, Ward said, the most honest man he had ever met and also the most unpleasant.

"I'd not have chosen one of them," Emeric said.

"No," said Ferra.

"I'd have chosen men I liked."

"You'd have chosen men who were good in a room," Ward said. "Sethe's factor was very good in a room."

He did it their way. He signed every name on the list. It was seven years of learning arriving as a shrug, and he noticed that, and was privately rather pleased with himself for it, which was the wrong thing to be pleased about.

The man for Wayford took Ferra a week.

She came into the long room with a single ledger under her arm and laid it open in front of him. It was one of Sethe's own books, from the counting house at the junction, and it was in three hands. Two of them were sloppy in the particular way of men who know nobody will ever check. The third was small and even and exact, and every line it had written reconciled to the coin, and the lines on either side of it did not.

"His name's Pennock," Ferra said. "He was the most junior man in the house. He did the carrier tallies, the small ones, the ones nobody bothered to skim because there wasn't enough in them. He's been doing them correctly for four years in a house where nobody else did anything correctly, and as far as I can tell nobody ever noticed, including Sethe."

"Is he honest or is he frightened?"

"He's careful." She closed the book. "Too far down to be worth buying, and too careful to be any use to a man like Sethe. I don't know which of those came first. I'm not sure it matters."

He rode to Wayford in the first week of autumn to see the man for himself. Pennock was thin and neat and very quiet, and he answered every question put to him in as few words as the question needed and not one more, and at the end of an hour Emeric could not have said a single thing about him except that he had never once been wrong. He gave him the counting house. He gave him the tolls, all three rates of them, collapsed back into two. He gave him a room with a lock and a clerk of his own and the authority, in writing, under Emeric's seal, to administer the junction in the name of the Baron of Kaldo.

Then he fixed the post, and he fixed it thoroughly, because it was the thing he knew best.

He had spent two years learning exactly how a post is broken. He knew every place a letter could be lifted between a bag and a door, and he knew that the crossing at Wayford had been the hinge of it, because every road in the south ran through the junction and so every letter in the south did too. He put in paired lodgment at the gate, two clerks who did not know each other signing every bag in and out. He put in a standing audit, a count of letters in against letters out, every week, sent by one road and checked by another. He sent the skimming factor to the Crown's court with his own books under his arm and a man either side of him. He walked the whole route from the gate to the post house twice, with Pennock, and changed three things, and signed the orders on the spot.

At the end of the fourth day he stood in the counting-house door and looked at it, and it was good. The post would work. It would work if Pennock died and it would work if Emeric died, and nobody would ever again burn three years of a man's letters in a room behind a toll gate.

He considered it handled.

The square below the counting house was nearly empty in the late afternoon, though it was a market day, and the few people crossing it went round the edges and did not look up at the door. He saw that. He put it down to the season, and to the sentence at midsummer, a thing a town would take time to get over. He thought that a town recovers from a thing like that by degrees, and that the best thing he could do for it was give it a post that worked and a junction that did not steal, and go home.

A cloth merchant came to the counting house on the last morning with a complaint about a toll, and Pennock heard him out and ruled against him, correctly, citing the rate. The merchant listened to the ruling. Then he turned to Emeric, who had been standing at the back of the room saying nothing, and asked him whether the ruling was his.

"It's the rate," Emeric said. "Pennock's right."

The merchant looked at Pennock, and back at Emeric, and nodded, and went out. It did not strike Emeric as anything. It was a man checking an answer with whoever was most senior in the room, which men did everywhere.

He rode home the next morning, and the post worked, and it went on working.

Grayrock he did not go to. He did not need to, because Bryda was already in the hall.

He wrote to her and she wrote back, and between the letters and Ferra's reads of the tally-house books and Ward's channel into the mine captains, the clean- out ran through her. She did it well. He could see from her letters that she did it well, because they were short and exact and every one of them had a list in it of what had been done since the last. Six clerks gone. Two new. The tally-house keys changed and the old ones melted, at her order, in front of the men who had held them.

He could also see what it cost her, because she did not say.

The men she was removing had been appointed by her husband. Several of them had sat at her table for twenty years. She had fed their children and sent broth to their wives in childbed and stood at their fathers' gravesides, and now she was reading their names to Ward's man off a list that Ferra had drawn up in Kaldo, and dismissing them from posts they had held since before her daughter was born. None of that was in the letters. What was in the letters was the number of men and the dates. He read them twice, each one, and understood what they did not say, which was more than he would have understood four years earlier.

There was a question sitting in the middle of it that nobody asked.

When he had first gone up to Grayrock with Renner's body in a cart, he had made a contract for the ore: grain up the river, ore down it, exclusive, at a fixed rate. He had made it with the holder of Grayrock, whoever that was. Now the holder of Grayrock was him. The contract was a contract with himself, and so was every tally Bryda kept and every order she gave, and somebody was going to have to decide what exactly she was administering, and for whom, and under what instrument, and on whose authority she dismissed a man who had sat at her table for twenty years.

He did not decide it. Neither did she. Ferra noted it in the margin of the Grayrock book with a small cross, the way she noted things she expected to come back, and the question sat there in the open, where it belonged.

Ferra was running four baronies' books from a table in the long room that had been built for one, and she did not complain, and she did not ask for help. He noticed about six weeks late.

He noticed because he came down at the first bell to find her already there, which was not unusual, and found the candle beside her burned down to the stub, which was. She had been there all night. There were ledgers on the table, on the chairs, on the floor in stacks by barony, and her hand was stained to the second knuckle.

"What do you need?"

She did not look up. "Nothing."

"What do you need?"

She set the pen down. He watched her decide whether to argue, and then decide that she was too tired to do it well.

"Three names," she said. "And a room."

She gave him the names. A girl from the school who had come top in every sum for three years. A clerk from the Grayrock tally house who had kept the only honest book in it. And a man from Milford whose name Emeric had never heard, who kept Paxton's factor's accounts and whom Ferra had apparently been watching for two years through the cattle invoices. She wanted the room at the end of the passage, the one with the window that faced south.

He gave her all of it inside the hour. He sent for the girl and wrote to Grayrock and to Paxton, and had the room at the end of the passage cleared of the old harness that had been rotting in it since Wexler's time, and came back and told her it was done.

She looked at him for a long moment, and he saw that she had been thrown, and that she did not like it.

"I had an argument ready," she said.

"I'm sure it was a good one."

"It was very good." She picked the pen up again. "It had a figure in it for every hour I'd lost since midsummer."

"You can still give it to me."

"No," said Ferra. "It's no use to me now." And she went back to the column, and did not look up again until the room was cleared, and then went and stood in it for a while by the south window, by herself, before she moved a single book.

Vantic came to see him at the end of the autumn, as she was required to, and said the only thing she was professionally required to say.

"The security has quadrupled," she said. "Ducatis's position is materially improved. I will report it as such."

"Thank you."

"I'm not doing you a kindness, Your Highness. It's a fact." She turned a page in her small book. Then she asked, without any change in her voice, whether the covenant would be revisited, now that the security it was written against had changed so much.

"I've asked," Emeric said.

"I know," said Vantic. "I forwarded it."

He looked at her. She was writing something, and did not look up.

It was, he realised afterward, the first time the two of them had been on the same side of a question since she had come up the road with the strongbox. Neither of them remarked on it. She finished her line and closed the book and went back to her room, and the covenant was not revisited that autumn.

He went to see Petram in the first week of winter, as he did every month.

Petram was still in Kaldo and still serving, and still silent, and had now been silent for four years. Emeric sat with him for the half hour the visit allowed and talked, as he always did, about ordinary things: the harvest, the new clerks, the weather on the Wayford road. Petram listened, as he always did, and said nothing, as he had said nothing since the week in the yard when his cousin Corliss had been named a thief in front of forty men. Emeric had never put those two things side by side. He did not put them side by side now. He finished the half hour, and said he would come again next month, and went.

At the end of the year he sat down with the notebook.

Four baronies. Two administrations emptied and filled with people he would not have chosen, who were doing better than the people he would have chosen. A post that worked from the gate at Wayford to his own door. A widow doing the hardest work in the south in a hall that was not hers, under an arrangement nobody had named. A Chancellor, though nobody called her that yet, with three clerks and a room with a south window.

And the notebook. He opened it at the back, where the covenant clauses were numbered down the margin, and beside them the second column, the one he had started three years before listing what every frozen project would need on the day the freeze lifted. He had meant it as a comfort. He added three lines to it that night, for Wayford and Grayrock and Milford, and when he had finished he saw that the second column was now longer than the first.

Everything was fixed. He sat back and looked at the notebook and felt, very clearly, that one thing was not, and could not for the life of him have said which.
