Chapter 69 - Vantic

The river had been dropping since the end of July.

Nobody noticed it at first but Bram, because Bram, or one of his boys, sat with the machine every night on Harl's stool between the two buckets, and the machine told him. Its note came down. Not much. A tone, perhaps, from the hum it had kept since February, and then, in the second week of August, a little more. The wheel was turning slower. There was less river to turn it with: the summer had been dry after the spring, and every week the water stood a little lower on the mill's race wall, and the green line where it had been in June showed a hand above it, and then two.

The lamps told everybody else. They came on at dusk along the wall as they had come on every night since March, and they were yellower. Then, toward the end of August, they began to stutter at about midnight, flaring and sinking, the whole wall flickering together, and on the last night of the month the whole wall went down to orange for most of an hour while the wheel labored in the low water, and came back, and went down again.

Tess heard about it before Emeric did. She heard about everything before him now. She lay on her side by the far wall of the ward with the bench book open on the blanket by her hand, because there were blankets again, and Bram came up the hill every morning on his way home from the powerhouse and told her how it had run, and she wrote it down. Under the column for the lamps she had begun another, in the second week of August, without being asked, for the river: the height of it on the race wall, every morning, in thumbs. She had Bram measure it with his.

She showed Emeric the two columns side by side on the first morning of September. She did not say anything. She did not need to. The numbers went down together, line by line.

"It'll come back with the rain," he said.

"In October." Tess looked at the page. "And then it'll go again next August. And the one after." She turned her head on the pillow to look at him. "The river's the river. You can't make it bigger."

He could not. He could see the answer to it as clearly as he could see the page: a fire that did not care how much water was in the river, a boiler and a cylinder and a beam, turning the shaft at Harl's mill all night at the same speed in August as in March. He had drawn it in his head a hundred times. It would need iron Kaldo did not have, and copper, and a year, and it would be the newest of new works.

The share was a fifth. Of a year that had had a war in it.

"Not this year," he said.

Tess wrote the date at the top of the next line, and the river's height under it, and closed the book.

That night Ward went round the wall and chose. Two lamps would burn on the low nights, he said, the one over the South Gate and the one at the river tower, and the others would be put out of the line with the copper strip Tess had made for it, so that the slow machine had two arcs to drive instead of eight. The rest of the wall went dark again, for the first time since the barges. People in the lanes under it came out of their doors and looked up at the dark hoods and went in again, and did not say anything, and at the north well the next morning nobody mentioned it to him, which was how he knew everybody was talking about it.



The House's rider had come back on the eighth day after he left, with the same grey horse and a letter in the same even hand, which said that the House accepted the barony's amendment to the security as written, and would send the money, and the factor with it, before the equinox.

The House's factor came in at the South Gate on the fourteenth of September, a little after noon, in a closed carriage.

It was black and plain and well made, with the House's mark small on the door, and it came behind two riders in good leather who had the look of men who were paid to stay awake, and it stopped in the gate yard. Ward was at the gate. He looked at the riders, and they looked at him, and some understanding passed among the three of them that nobody else in the yard was meant to follow.

The carriage door opened from inside before anybody could reach it.

A woman got down. She was in a dark traveling coat, plain, buttoned to the throat, and gloves, grey, that fitted as if they had been made on her hands, and she had a leather case of books under one arm, and she stood in the gate yard of Kaldo, in the dust and the smell of the burned quarter that had never quite gone out of the town, and looked at it.

It was the stillness he noticed. She did not look round the yard as people did who had arrived somewhere new, a little here and a little there. She looked at it once, all of it, from where she stood, the way a man takes a reading off an instrument, and then she was finished looking, and she turned to him.

"Vantic," she said. "For the House."

He knew enough about the great houses to know what that was. A chair: the name the House gave whoever sat with one of its debts, so that the debt could be talked about without anybody having to know who was sitting with it. She said it as if it were her name, and it would be, here, for as long as Kaldo owed the House anything.

"Emeric," he said.

"I know." She held out a gloved hand, and he took it. "I have your file."



The strongbox came down off the carriage roof on a rope, iron-bound, with two locks, and went across the yard on the shoulders of the riders and two of the garrison, and down the hill, because the barony's money was counted at the exchange and nowhere else, and Ferra would not count it at the keep.

Vantic walked down the hill behind it. She did not ask why. She looked at the lanes as she went, and at the people in the doors, and at the dark hoods on the wall above the lower ward, and once at the black smear across the river where the dock quarter had been, and did not ask about any of it.

Ferra was at the trestle in the back room of the exchange, with her book open, and a second book beside it with nothing in it yet, and two pens.

The two women looked at each other across the trestle, and Emeric, who was standing in the door, saw something go between them, a kind of measuring, like two smiths each picking up the other's hammer to feel the balance. Then Vantic took off her gloves, finger by finger, and laid them side by side on the corner of the trestle, and sat down opposite Ferra, and opened her own book from the case to a clean page, and laid out her own pen and a stoppered ink bottle.

They counted the strongbox, each of them in her own column.

They did it in silence, both of them, in separate columns, Ferra in her book and Vantic in hers. The riders lifted the coin out in its bags and the bags were opened one at a time and the coin was counted out onto the trestle in piles of ten, and each woman wrote each pile as it was made, and neither looked at the other's page. It took the rest of the afternoon. The light went across the floor of the back room and up the wall. When the last pile had been written they each drew a line under their column and added it, Ferra without moving her lips, Vantic with her pen moving down the column very fast and never stopping.

Then Ferra turned her book round so that Vantic could read it, and Vantic turned hers.

They agreed. To the copper, the first time.

Neither of them looked pleased. Ferra looked at Vantic's total and then at her own, and then at Vantic, with her eyes narrowed very slightly, as she looked at a column that had come out too neatly. Vantic looked at Ferra's total and did not move at all.

"Who taught you to keep a book like this?" Vantic said.

"Who taught you?" said Ferra.

Neither of them answered. Then Vantic wrote something at the foot of her page, small, and Ferra watched her write it, and wrote something at the foot of hers.

Emeric was watching Vantic's hands. They were long and quiet, and without the gloves they looked younger than her face, and they did not do anything they did not need to do. They did not drum on the trestle. They did not touch her hair. When she was not writing, they lay on the book, one on the other, and were still.

She looked up and saw him looking.

She did not say anything. She looked at him for exactly as long as it took to see what he was looking at, and then she looked down at her book again, and wrote something else at the foot of the page, a single line, and blotted it, and turned the page.



Hale had made the east tower ready. The rooms at the top of the keep had stood empty since Armand went downstairs, and they had been aired and swept and the bed made up, and there was a table under the window with a lamp on it, and a chair, and a shelf for the case of books. Armand had asked to be carried up the stair to see them before she came, and had been refused, and had sent up instead a jar of the spirit and a note in his cramped hand that Hale delivered without reading.

She stood in the door of the rooms and looked at them once, all of it, as she had looked at the gate yard. Then she set her case on the shelf.

Emeric had come up the stair with her, because it was his house, and because there were things the terms said she had the right to see, and he would rather show them to her than have her find them.

"The books are at the exchange," he said. "All of them. The clerk keeps them. You'll want to start there."

"I'll start everywhere." She was taking off her gloves again. "It's in the terms."

"It's in the terms."

She laid the gloves on the table under the window, side by side, finger to finger. Then she looked at him.

"Your file says I drink," he said.

He had not meant to say it. It had been in his head since the gate yard, since I have your file, and it came out on its own, and he heard how it sounded.

Vantic considered him.

"My file says a number of things," she said.

"Is it accurate?"

"It is accurately copied."

She said it without any expression at all.



On the second morning Ferra gave her the books.

She did not take them up to the keep. She sent word that they were at the exchange, at the trestle in the back room, and that the clerk would be there from the first bell, and Vantic came down the hill at the first bell and sat down opposite her, and they began.

Emeric came in at noon and found them on the households' pay. Vantic had her finger on the line.

"This is carried without security," she said. "A wage for children's hours, paid all summer while the school was shut. There's nothing behind it."

"It's carried," said Ferra.

"I'm not asking you to stop it. I'm telling you what it is." Vantic wrote in her own book. "An operating cost, elevated, carried without security, at the barony's choice." She looked at what she had written. "That's what the House will read."

"Then the House will read it right," Ferra said.

Vantic looked up at her, and then went on to the next line. Emeric stood in the door and listened to the two of them go down the barony's book, line by line, for an hour, and did not say anything, because neither of them needed him to, and because it was the first time in two years he had heard anybody read Ferra's book back to her and get every line of it right.



She walked the town alone for the next four days, with a notebook.

He saw her in pieces of it, from a distance, as he went about his own work. At the school, which had opened again on the first of September with Sela at the front of the long room and the dock quarter's twenty-two on the front benches: Vantic stood at the back for a whole morning and wrote, and Sela, who had seen her come in, did not look at her once. At bay three, where Bram was drawing bar under the hammer for the irons of the mill's new roof: she asked him a question about the reject rate, and Bram answered it, and then she asked him a second question, and Bram put the hammer down and looked at her properly for the first time. At the powerhouse, at dusk, where Bram sat on Harl's stool: she stood in the door and watched the needle by the machine while the wheel labored in the low river, and wrote, and went away.

On the fourth day she went into the soap shop on the north side of the square, where Sela kept the counter in the afternoons when the school was out, and bought a bar of the plain soap.

Emeric was across the square at the well, and heard her. "Two coppers," Sela said, loud enough for the square: the posted price, to the House as to anybody. She counted the change out of the drawer, a copper at a time, into Vantic's gloved palm, slowly, every coin, while the woman behind waited.

"Next," said Sela.

Vantic closed her glove on the change, and inclined her head to Sela, very slightly, as one clerk might to another, and went out.



That night the river was down another finger on the race wall, and Ward put out the river-tower lamp too, and left the gate.

Emeric went up onto the wall above the east bridge, where the first lamp hung cold in its hood, and stood at the parapet in his shrunken coat.

Below him the river went past in the dark, low and slow, showing its stones at the edges. Across it, where the dock quarter had been, there were a few lights now, in the six houses on the black ground, and more beyond them where the net-menders had raised walls that week without asking anybody. Down the bank, the powerhouse, and the roofless mill, and the shaft turning in the dark, slowly, too slowly. Above him, over his shoulder, the keep, and the east tower at the top of it, with one window lit.

Now there was a lamp in his own tower, and a woman under it with every book in the barony open to her, writing down what she read for a house in the capital that had priced the plague, and the war, and him. She would read it well. He had watched her count. That was the trouble.

Over his head the lamp in its hood stayed dark. Down at the South Gate, the one lamp Ward had left burning flickered, steadied, and burned on, yellow, over the empty road.
