Chapter 266 - Seven in a Pod

The first wall Kaldo ever printed went up on the fourth of April, a Monday, on the cleared ground at the bottom of ward 3, and it came out looking like a pot.

Emeric saw it from the top of the lane at a quarter to eight with a roll of bread in one hand and Ferra's quarter-day list in the other, and stopped walking. He had meant to come down at nine. Bram had said nine. Bram had said, at the yard gate on Saturday with his hands in his apron, that the first course would go down at nine and not before, because the pump wanted an hour to settle and the mix wanted an hour to stand and he wanted an hour to stand and look at both of them; and Emeric had believed him, which he ought to have known better than to do by now.

The gantry stood across the bottom of the ground on its four legs like a bridge somebody had built in the wrong place. Under it, between the rails, on the long strip of pegged and rolled clay where a terrace of four houses was going to stand by the autumn, there was a wall. It was knee-high and twenty feet long and it turned a corner at each end. It was grey. It was made of rope.

That was how it looked from the lane: as though somebody had laid a fat grey rope along the ground and then laid another on top of it, and another, round and round the plan of a room, the way a child at the school's clay table makes a pot by coiling a snake of clay on itself and pinching it up. Every layer stood a finger's width proud of the one below it. The morning light came along the ground from the east and lay in every groove, so that the whole wall was striped, dark and pale, dark and pale, from the clay to the top course.

Down at the far end of the rails the nozzle was still moving. It came along the top of the wall at a slow walk, hanging from its arm under the gantry, with the hose sagging behind it to the pump cart, and laid down another rope as it went, grey and wet and round; and when it reached the corner it slowed, and turned, and the rope went round the corner a little fatter than it went along the straight, and sagged there, and stayed.

Emeric ate the rest of the bread on the way down.

There were more people at the bottom of the ground than there should have been at a quarter to eight on a Monday. The printer's crew were there, which was right: the four men on the pump cart, the man at the hoist winch, the two on the mixer, the boy on the water cart. Bram was there, under the gantry, walking backward along the wall a pace ahead of the nozzle with his eyes on the bead and his hands behind him. Tess was in the door of the tracer shed at the head of the rails, her chair turned side-on to the doorway so she could see the table and the wall both, with her right hand on the tracer's stylus and her left on the lever that set the gantry's pace. Osric was standing behind her chair in his shirtsleeves, not pushing it, with his bandaged wrist held up out of the way. The masons were there, which Emeric had not expected.

There were eight of them, along the edge of the ground by the old cart track, in their leather aprons with their hammers in their belts, as though they had been on their way to work somewhere and had stopped. They were not on their way to work. Ward 3's houses were the printer's, and the masons knew it, and they had been told in February, in the hall, by Ferra, what Kaldo would pay a mason for laying a printed house's openings and lintels and chimneys and what it would pay him for teaching apprentices to do it, and what it would pay him for a summer on the new walls at the falls if he would sooner have that; and the money had been more than any of them had ever been offered for anything, and most of them had taken it, and one or two had not. They stood along the cart track now with their arms folded and watched the nozzle come down the wall, and when it went round the corner and the bead sagged, one of them, an old man with a beard like a hearth brush, laughed out loud.

Bram did not look round.

Emeric came and stood by the rail at the foot of the gantry's nearest leg, out of the way.

The nozzle reached the end of its course. The man at the winch let the arm up a hand's breadth and Tess's left hand moved the lever back to stop, and the gantry halted on its wheels with a small iron sound, and the pump cart's men shut their valve, and for the first time since he had come down the lane it was quiet enough to hear the river.

Bram walked back along the wall from one end to the other, slowly, looking at it. Then he walked along the other face. Then he put his hand flat on the top course, very lightly, and took it away and looked at the grey on his palm.

"It's standing," said Emeric.

"It's standing," said Bram.

"It's straight."

"It's straight." Bram wiped his palm down his apron. "It's straight and it's level and it's the thickness on the drawing to a sixteenth, and it's standing, and it's the ugliest thing I've built since I was twelve." He looked along it again. "My father would have taken a stick to me for this. He'd have been right."

"It's a test wall."

"It's a test wall that eight masons are looking at."

"Seven," said Tess, from the shed. "One's gone for his breakfast."

"Seven, then."

Emeric looked at it. It was what he had expected, near enough: the bare bead, laid without anything touching it but the air, so that it set in the shape the nozzle gave it and nothing else. He had seen pictures of walls like it on the other side of everything, printed houses in deserts and car parks, ridged like corduroy, and had thought them handsome in a raw way, and had known even then that nobody who had ever had to plaster a room would think so. A ridged wall shed water down its grooves into its own joints. A ridged wall held dust and soot and damp and spiders. A ridged wall could not take paper and would not take paint without three coats, and the first thing anyone who lived behind it would do was hire a plasterer, at which point you had saved a mason's wages and paid a plasterer's.

It was meant to be ugly. That was the order they had agreed, in Ferra's room, in January: the bare machine first, with nothing on it but iron and the pump and the tracer and the crew, and its stones pulled out and laid on the bench in the yard, so that everyone could see what Kaldo's printer did when everything that could fail had failed. Then the magic on top, one layer at a time, and each layer had to beat the bare machine at something or it came off again. It was the rule. He had written it, and Bram had copied it in the hand that could be read from the lane, and pinned it on the yard gate beside the Works Rule.

It was still ugly.

"The trowels," said Emeric.

"The trowels go on the stones." Bram nodded up at the nozzle, where the two steel blades hung folded up on either side of the mouth, as they had hung all winter under the tarpaulin. "Stones are on the bench. That's the rule. Your rule. We print it bare and we look at it bare and then we put the stones in."

"I know whose rule it is."

"Then don't stand there and say 'the trowels' at me as though I'd forgotten I'd got them."

The old mason with the hearth-brush beard had come along the cart track while they were talking, and was standing at the corner of the wall with his hands behind his back, looking down at the sag where the bead had gone round. He did not touch it. He looked at it the way a man looks at a horse that somebody else has paid too much for.

"Who's it for?" he said.

"Ward 3," said Bram. "Terrace of four."

"Not that." The mason lifted his chin at the wall. "That. Who's going to live in that?"

"Nobody lives in a test wall."

"Somebody'll live in the next one."

Bram looked at him. Emeric knew the look; he had been on the end of it in Bram's shop in the first year, over a drawing of a lathe bed that had been very nearly right. It meant that Bram had heard what had been said, and agreed with it, and was not going to say so to the man who had said it.

"Hob's coming down at nine with the first plan," said Bram instead. "The proper plan. Kitchen and parlour and the stair. You can stand and look at that one too, if you like. Ferra pays you from the half hour."

"I know what she pays." The mason looked at the wall a moment longer, and then he did something Emeric had not seen a mason do before: he bent down, slowly, with a hand on his knee, and put his thumb against the side of the bottom course and pushed. It did not give. He pushed harder. It did not give at all. He straightened up, and looked at his thumb, and went back along the cart track to the others without saying anything else.

"That's Gale," said Tess, when he had gone. "He laid the north well's apron. Twenty years ago. It's still the best bit of stonework in the lower town."

"I know who he is," said Bram.

"I'm only saying."

"I know what you're only saying." Bram turned round. "Go on, then."

"Go on what?"

"You've been sitting in that door with a face on you since the first course. Say it."

Tess took her hand off the stylus. She looked at the wall, and then up at the nozzle with its folded blades, and then at Bram, and she had the look Emeric had seen on her at fourteen, at the old dynamo's commutator, when she had been about to ask a question that would make her master go red.

"Why doesn't anybody just wipe it?"

Bram opened his mouth.

"Not the trowels," said Tess. "Not the stones. I know they're for the stones. I mean wipe it. Like a cake. You do a cake, you put the icing on, it goes all over, you take a knife and run it round. You don't put the knife on a stone to do it. You hold the knife." She pointed at the folded blades. "Those are knives. Put them down on springs and let the arm drag them. It doesn't need to be even. It needs to not look like Gale's laughing at it."

"Springs drag," said Bram. "Springs catch on the corners and tear the bead."

"Then go slower on the corners. It already goes slower on the corners. Look, it sagged." She pointed. "It's going slow there anyway. Make it go slower and lift the blades a hair and let the spring take it round."

"And the press is uneven the length of the wall, because a spring's harder when it's pushed further, and so the wall's smooth in the middle and rough where the bead's thin."

"Then it's smooth in the middle," said Tess. "That's better than it is now."

Nobody said anything. On the pump cart one of the men had taken his cap off and was scratching his head under it and watching Bram.

Emeric did not say anything either. He knew what she was describing. She was describing the first thing anybody on Earth had tried: blades riding the nozzle on springs, pressing the bead flat as it came out. It worked. It worked badly. It dragged at corners and tore and needed a man with a float to follow the machine and fix what it had torn, and somebody had put a motor on the blades in the end, and somebody else had given up and plastered the walls the old way. He had meant to go straight to the stones, because the stones pressed evenly at any speed and any angle and did not care how hard they were pushed; it was the whole reason the blades were mounted where they were. He had drawn the stone mount first and the blades round it.

He had not drawn the spring mount at all. It had not occurred to him to draw the worse thing first.

It was the rule. His rule. Each magic layer had to beat the bare machine at something, or it came off; and he had been about to bolt on a magic layer that beat a bare machine that had never been allowed to try.

"There's a pair of carriage springs in the yard," said Osric, behind the chair. "Off the old ash wagon. They're about right. I could have them here in a quarter of an hour."

Bram looked at the blades. Then at the wall. Then at the masons along the cart track, who had stopped talking among themselves.

"Half an hour," he said. "And bring the big brace. And somebody tell Hob not to come at nine, tell him to come at noon, I'm not printing his kitchen till I've printed something I'd let a dog sleep behind."

Osric went. Bram turned on Emeric.

"Did you know?"

"Know what?"

"That it'd work on springs."

Emeric thought about how to answer that, and decided the only answer was the true one.

"I knew it would work badly on springs," he said. "I didn't think to try it."

"Hm," said Bram, and turned away to shout at the mixer.

Tess had her hand back on the stylus. She did not look at Emeric, and she did not smile, and the back of her neck had gone pink.

He went to the hall at nine with Ferra's list and did the morning's work, and at a quarter past one he came back down the lane with his coat over his arm because the day had come up warm, and the second test wall was standing beside the first.

It was not smooth. From the lane it looked smooth, which was not the same thing; close to, he could see where the springs had pressed hard and soft, the faint waves along the face where the bead had been thinner and the blade had ridden harder on it, and at each corner a short stretch of torn grey where the blades had caught and dragged, and a man in a mason's apron down on his knees in the clay going over the tears with a steel float. The man was Gale. He was being paid from the half hour.

But it was a wall. You could have papered it.

Bram had the stones out of their box on the bench under the gantry, four flat grey gravity stones the size of a hand, each in its brass mount, and he and Osric were bolting the mounts into the frame behind the blades that had been drawn for them in January, with Tess telling them from the shed which way round each one went, and by half past two the third wall was going down beside the second. Emeric stood at the rail and watched the nozzle come along the course with its blades down on either side, and felt the four stones take the load behind them, small and steady, with a faint violet in the brass that nobody in the sunlight would see unless they knew to look. The blades did not ride. They pressed. They pressed the bead the same at the start of the course and the middle and the end, and slowed a hair on the corner when the arm slowed and pressed just as hard going round it, and the wall came out behind them the way wet plaster comes off a good plasterer's float, flat and close and dull, with nothing in it to hold a shadow.

Gale had stood up off his knees to watch. He watched the whole course. When it was done he walked along the new wall, and put his thumb against it, and pushed, and looked at his thumb.

"Now that," he said, "a man could live behind."

Bram, who had been holding his breath, let it out.

The fourth wall that day was Hob's kitchen, at the gantry's full pace, with its stones in and its blades down and Tess's stylus going round the kitchen on the drawing table at a twentieth of the size, and it was standing at sundown. In the shed at the head of the rails, on the drawing board's edge, somebody had pinned a scrap of paper in Osric's hand. It said THE SMOOTHING HAND, and under it, smaller: Tess's. Bram saw it at six and left it there.



The letter from the king came on the Wednesday, inside a bale of book cloth.

It came the way they always came: on the Tuesday down train to the capital mooring and the Hod's Wednesday run home, in the ordinary mail sacks, inside something else. This time it was a bale of red buckram for the Almanac's next printing, consigned to Kaldo's press by a cloth merchant's clerk in the capital who had never, so far as Emeric knew, heard of him, and in the middle of the bale between the fourth and fifth fold there was a sheet of paper that was not buckram. Ward found it. Ward always found it. He went down to the press on the Wednesday afternoon with a knife in his pocket, and came back up to the keep with the bale under his arm, and took it into his own room, and shut the door.

He did not come out for supper.

At nine Emeric went and knocked. Ward said, "Come," and he went in.

Ward was at his table by the window, in his shirt, with the cipher sheet flat in front of him under a weight and his own clean copy beside it in pencil, and the bale of buckram on the bed, slit open and folded back on itself like a gutted fish. He had the lamp turned low. He did not look round.

"Sit down," he said.

Emeric sat on the end of the bed, beside the buckram.

Ward read it to him.

He did not read it in the king's voice, which Emeric had not heard in eleven years and still could not hear without his shoulders coming up a little, as if a door had opened behind him. He read it in his own, flat and even, the way he read the drill sheets to the class at six; and he read the cipher's dates and the formulae the cipher wrapped round everything, the greetings to the merchant's family and the weather in the capital and the price of buckram, all of it, without leaving anything out, because that was how the cipher was read and because he was Ward. It took a long time to get to the king.

When it got there it did not take long at all.

The king had the report. He had read it three times. He believed it.

He believed it, Ward read, because of who it had come from: from his son, who did not lie to him, though there were things his son had never told him; from Armand, who had been at the top of the tower before the king was born and who did not write a sum down unless it would stand; and from the man who had guarded the boy's mother and now guarded the boy, and who had stood on that floor himself. There was no one in his kingdom whose word he would put over those three together. He would not insult them by asking for proofs. He had their proofs. He had them in a hand he had known for most of his life.

He could not stop it.

Ward read that line, and stopped, and read it again, as it was written.

Then he read why. The Isle, first: Duvrenne's ships off the Isle every month this last year and its envoy at court every week, and a crown at odds with its neighbour could not be at odds with its own altar in the same season and keep either. The Light, second: the rite was the Light's choosing, read from every pulpit in the kingdom every winter for longer than there had been kings, and a king who closed the tower's floor by his own word would be a king telling every parish priest from the capital to the sea that the Light had been wrong; and the priests would not say that he was right. They would say what the Church told them to say. The road, third, and last, and longest: the slow road in Kaldo was paid for by a thing that existed in Kaldo and nowhere else. The king did not know what the thing was. He did not ask. But he could read a report, and he could see that every candidate who had stood clean in the yard had eaten Kaldo's food under Kaldo's healer and been drained into Kaldo's stones, and he had nothing to put in its place. He could close the floor tomorrow, and every boy who would have gone to it would have no road at all, and his lords would have no knights, and his kingdom would have no knights, with Duvrenne's ships off the Isle.

"Bring me a road I can give them," Ward read. "A road that runs outside your gate. Until then I will do what I can, and it will not be much, and it will have to do."

He put the copy down.

"There's more," he said. "There's what he can do."

What he could do was the roll.

The tower kept a roll of every candidate for every winter, their names and ages and who had sent them, and the crown's portion of it came from the crown's own service: soldiers who had served their years and been found strong enough, and put forward, as Rylan had been put forward, by their captains. The king could not touch the lords' sons. The lords sent their own, and the tower took them, and that was between the lords and the tower and the Light. But a crown soldier was a crown soldier until the day he went up the tower stair, and a king could post his soldiers anywhere in his kingdom he liked, and nobody had any right to ask him why.

From the coming season, the king's soldiers who had been put forward for the floor would be put forward and then posted. South. To the garrison of the Duke of Kaldo, for training, under the command of his captain, by name, for as long as the captain thought fit. Their names would come by the same road as this letter, a season ahead.

And Kaldo's own four baronies, the king added, in a line at the end that Ward read in the same flat voice as everything else, had always been free to send nobody, and he gathered they had stopped. He had noticed. He had not said anything to anyone, and would not.

Ward folded the copy in half, and in half again.

"He'll pay for that," said Emeric.

"He knows."

"The Church counts the roll. They'll see the crown's men thinning off it."

"He knows that too." Ward put the folded copy in his shirt. "He's known it all his life. He knew it when he sent me south with you. He's paying for that one still." He looked at the cipher sheet under its weight. "He's posting men to me by name. Under his seal. To a duke the whole court thinks he exiled. Every one of those orders crosses a clerk's desk, and every clerk at court can read."

They sat there.

"I didn't know what he'd say," said Ward. "When I wrote. I didn't know if he'd answer at all."

"You knew he'd answer."

"I knew he'd answer. I didn't know if it'd be this." He put his hand flat on the sheet, on the cipher, as if it might go somewhere. "He says I stood on the floor. Nobody ever told him that. I never told him that."

"Armand?"

"Armand would not." Ward took his hand away. "He'd have had the roll looked up. My name. My year. All those years ago, in a box in the tower. He'd have had a man go down and find it, just to know." He looked at the window, at the dark ward with the screens drawn round the glass room in the middle of it, grey, and the faint violet line along their tops where the light got over. "He's a king. He's never once in his life had to do a thing himself. He had a man go and find it."

Emeric did not say anything.

"Bring me a road I can give them," said Ward, after a while, to the window.

"We'll bring it."

"Not this year."

"No."

"Not next year."

"I don't know," said Emeric. "No. Probably not."

"Then there's a floor this winter," said Ward, "and the winter after," and he got up from the table and took the lamp and turned it up, as if he had remembered that he could; and Emeric saw his face properly for the first time since he had come in, and looked away from it, at the buckram.



Ferra did the sum on the Thursday morning, and it took her four hours.

She did it because Emeric asked her to, at breakfast, over the bread, with Imani at the end of the table reading her ward's night sheet and not listening; and he asked it badly, the way he asked for things he did not want, all at once and without a reason. He wanted to know how many died on the floor in a winter. Not over the years. In one season, from first snow to the Ascent. A number he could hold.

Ferra put her cup down.

"Why?"

"Because he said 'not much' and 'it'll have to do,' and I want to know what 'not much' is out of."

"Your father."

"Ward."

She looked at him for a while longer, over the bread. Then she said, "Armand's books," and finished her tea and went.

Armand's books were in Ottoline's room in the records hall, in the box they had come up out of his cellar in, twenty winters of the old man's small square hand, every man who had lain on the floor in his rotation and his age and his size and how long he had lasted and what had been done to him and when. Ottoline had copied the lot onto her cards two summers since, for a different sum. Ferra did not use the cards. She sat down at Ottoline's long table under the window with the books themselves, in order, and a ruler, and her own book open at a clean page, and Ottoline brought her a glass and a second pencil and did not ask what she was doing, and went back to her own work at the other end of the table, and listened to Ferra turn pages for four hours.

Emeric came at half past twelve because she had said one. She was not finished. He sat on the window seat and waited, and watched her work.

He had watched her work for ten years and still could not have said exactly what she did. She did not do sums the way he did them, down the column and carry the one. She looked at a page of Armand's figures, a night's work, names and hours, and her pencil moved twice in her own book, small, and she turned the page; and it went on like that, page after page, steady as a loom, and what came out of the end of it was always right. He had checked her four times in the first year and found her right four times, and had been ashamed of himself, and had stopped.

At ten past one she wrote a line under everything, and a number under the line, and sat back.

"Nights," she said. "First, because nobody ever tells you how many nights a season is. It depends on the snow. Armand's twenty winters run from fifty-one nights, the year the snow came in December, to seventy-eight, the year it came in October. I've taken the middle ten and thrown away the ends. Sixty-two."

"Sixty-two."

"Men on the floor a night. Fewer at the start of a season, when the lords are still deciding, and more at the end, when they've decided. Armand's rows run eleven at the fewest to twenty-six at the most." She touched a column. "Nineteen. That's his whole rotation's middle, and it's the night he took you down, Ward says, so it's a fair night."

"And how many."

"Out of every ten who lie down." She did not look at the page. She knew it. "Armand's twenty winters, the men he wrote down, all of them. Four. A little over. Four and a quarter, if you want it to the quarter, and I do."

Four in ten. He had known it was about that. Ward had said it at the fire, years ago; Armand had said it in the cellar. He had known it the way you know a thing other people have told you.

"So it's sixty-two nights at nineteen," said Ferra, "less the nights the floor's shut for the high days, and less the ones Armand marks as a short night because the mages were spent. That's eleven hundred and sixty-odd men on the floor in an ordinary season." She put the pencil on the line. "And four and a quarter in ten of those."

He waited.

"Four hundred and ninety-four," said Ferra. "In an ordinary winter. In the tower. That's the floor. It's not the ones who are carried out alive and die in their lodgings by the spring, which Armand only sometimes heard about, and wrote in the margin when he did, and that's another twenty or so in a season he heard about, and nobody knows how many he didn't." She looked at the number. "I'll not put a number on what nobody knows. Call it five hundred. I'll write four hundred and ninety-four."

At the other end of the table, Ottoline's pencil had stopped.

Emeric looked at the window.

He had thought, on the way across the ward, that it would be three hundred. He did not know why he had thought so. It had seemed like a large enough number. He had carried three hundred across the ward in his chest like a stone, getting used to the weight of it; and it was not three hundred.

"The crown's men," he said. "How many of those are the king's soldiers. Out of the four hundred and ninety-four."

Ferra turned back through Armand's books. She did not need to; he could see she knew it already, and was only giving him the time.

"Armand writes who sent them," she said. "Lord's son, lord's man, crown. A little under one in four was crown. Say a hundred and fifteen of the dead, in a winter."

"So the king's taking a hundred and fifteen men off the floor."

"A hundred and fifteen men a winter will come south instead of dying." Ferra closed the book. "Three hundred and seventy-nine of them still won't."

She tore the page out.

He had never seen her tear a page out of anything. She tore it carefully, along the spine, with the ruler laid down the fold, so that the edge came clean, and she folded it once, and held it out to him.

"I'll have it again in the book by tonight," she said, "in my own copy. That's yours."

He took it.

"Don't put it in a drawer," said Ferra.

He did not put it in a drawer. He put it on the wall of the delve room that evening, in the castle's old wine store under the hall, where the six went down at night to the long table and the chairs and the dark; on the plain plaster beside the door at the height of a man's eyes, with four of Ottoline's brass drawing pins, one at each corner, so that anybody who went into the room to go under would have to walk past it, and anybody who came out of it would see it last. It said, in Ferra's square hand, 494, and under it, smaller, one winter, and under that, smaller still, ordinary.

Ward came down at ten with Armand on his arm, as he did on Thursdays, to go under. He saw it in the door. He stopped there, with the old man on his arm, and read it, and Armand read it beside him, and neither of them said anything.

Then Ward settled Armand into his chair at the table's end and sat down in his own, and put his hands flat on the wood, and they went under; and when they came up at a quarter to one and went out, Ward stopped at the door again and read it again, the same way.

He stopped every time after that. Nobody ever asked him to.



The base was nine miles out on the Milford road, where Rylan had asked for it in the spring of the year before, on the long rise between the four baronies' corners that Emeric had walked with him and marked with a line of ash stakes in the frost: the knights' ground. On the Friday he rode out to see it with Rylan and Imani, because Imani wanted to see the barracks' wash-house before anyone slept in it, and because Rylan had asked him to, twice, and then not asked a third time, which with Rylan meant it mattered.

It had gone up over the winter. He had known that. He had signed for it. He had read Bram's weekly lines about the footings and the roofs in Ferra's book and initialled them and had not once ridden out to look, because there had been the Isle in the autumn and the ward through December and the stone in his chest all winter, the thing nobody talked about at the table, the pod on the tree; and it was a shock to come over the rise from the Milford road at ten in the morning and see a place.

It was not a castle. He had told Rylan that it must not look like one, and Rylan had said, quite reasonably, what did he think it should look like, then, and he had said, a school. So it looked like a school. Long low buildings of Kaldo brick round three sides of a yard as big as the castle ward, with slate roofs and tall windows and a clock over the gate that Ottoline's shop had made and somebody had already set wrong; a kitchen and a wash-house and a store on the fourth side; and beyond the long buildings, on the slope behind, a running track pegged out in white posts round a field, and a long timber frame with ropes and ladders and a wall in it that Emeric did not remember drawing. Past the field, on its own, a great plain brick shell with no windows in it at all and no roof yet, only its walls, and scaffold poles standing up out of them like a cut stubble.

"The hall," said Rylan, when he saw Emeric looking. "Bram says June."

"Bram said May."

"Bram said May in October. In January he said May, if the frost broke. In March he said June." Rylan put his heels to his horse. "It's June."

There were people in the yard. Not many; the class was due off the terraces on Monday, and this was the last week of the long rooms standing empty with the smell of whitewash in them and the stoves cold. But there were men on the roofs still, slating the kitchen, and a cart of beds at the store door being handed in a frame at a time, and at the side of the gate, in a Kaldo coat that fitted him, a boy of seventeen or so leaning on a broom.

Emeric knew him before he saw his face, by the way he held his left hand: curled a little against his belt, as if it were cold. It was not cold. It had not stopped shaking since the summer before last. The boy had the lines on his forearm to go with it, under his sleeve, one ring of silver welt over the wrist that should not have been there and would not now go away; and he had been one of four boys who ate in a cellar in the summer of the tenth year because somebody had told them it would make them knights, and he was the one who had lived.

He looked at the horses, and at Rylan, and at Imani, and at Emeric last, and went very still, the way a dog does in a yard it has been beaten in.

Then he went on sweeping.

"Joss," said Rylan, riding past him, not stopping. "That's the third time you've done that bit."

"Yes, sir."

"Do the bit by the kitchen. They've been dropping slates."

"Yes, sir."

He went off with the broom. Imani's head had turned to follow him across the yard. She did not say anything.

"He came with the beds," said Rylan, when they had dismounted in the yard. "On Wednesday. Ward sent him. He's been here three days. He sweeps. He sleeps in the store with the beds. He eats with the slaters." He looked at the long buildings. "He watched the cart come in with the drill frames on it on Wednesday night and stood there till it was dark."

"He's on the count," said Emeric. "Ward put him on the count in March."

"I know he's on the count. He's on the count for the broom. He's not on the count for anything else." Rylan took off his gloves. "He's never going to be on the count for anything else, with that arm. He knows it. Every time he looks at the frames, he knows it."

Imani was still looking across the yard. The boy had got to the kitchen wall and was sweeping slate dust into a heap with great care.

"That's not certain," she said.

Rylan looked at her.

"It's not certain," she said again. "I don't know. Nobody knows. Nobody's ever had one on the floor that came at it from his side, with the lines already on. Armand's books don't have one. He wouldn't have lasted the first night." She was frowning. "But I know what the shaking is. It's not the tracery. The tracery's done; it's healed, it's a scar. The shaking's the nerve in his forearm that it ran over on the way in, and a nerve can come back, sometimes, slowly, if it's fed, and kept, and used. I've seen one come back. In a man from a mill, years ago, with nothing in it at all but time." She looked at Emeric. "And there's nothing in Kaldo but mana in the food."

"You want to put him on the Proving," said Emeric.

"I don't want anything. It's not a question I can answer standing in a yard." She turned back to the horses. "I want him on my list. I want him in the tank. I want to see what his arm looks like inside." She took her bag off the saddle. "Then I'll tell you what I want."

Rylan watched her go toward the wash-house with her bag. Then he looked across the yard at the boy, who had finished the heap and was looking round for something to put it in.

"Ward will hate that," he said.

"Ward hates the Proving."

"Ward hates the Proving because it's harm and it's going to be done whatever he hates." Rylan put his gloves in his belt. "That boy he won't hate. That boy he gave a broom." He started toward the long buildings. "Come and see the rooms. Bram's put a window in every one. Every one. I told him knights don't need windows."

"What did he say?"

"He said I'd been a knight for twenty years and I could see out of one, couldn't I."

The rooms were good. They were long and white and plain, twenty beds to a room in two lines under the tall windows, with a box at each bed's foot and a peg above it and a stove at each end; and the class would be in them on Monday, off the terraces in the town where they had been packed into rented rooms over shops since October four and five to a bed, and nobody had said a word about it, because nobody in the class had ever slept in a room with fewer than four in it in their lives. Rylan walked Emeric down every room. He showed him the boot racks and the lamp brackets and the speaking tube Ottoline had run from the gate to the officers' room and the bell on it, and the drying room behind the wash-house with its stone floor and its racks and the fire stone set in the wall to warm it, and beside the fire stone, on an iron shelf, a brazier and a coal scuttle, full, because the drying room's stone was the drying room's stone and Kaldo did not let a room go cold because a stone had.

At the end of the last room Rylan stopped by a window that looked out over the track and the field and the ropes and the brick shell of the hall beyond, roofless, with its scaffold poles.

"Years," he said.

Emeric waited.

"I came south to Kaldo in a wagon," said Rylan, "with a wife and two children and a hand's worth of coins and a crest I didn't wear, to guard a wall that had no men on it, for a duke nobody in the capital thought would last the winter." He looked out of the window. "I've got a son who'll be a knight of Kaldo in two years if Ward says so, without ever having lain on the floor. I've got a class who don't know what the floor was. And on Monday I've got a base." He put his hand on the window frame, flat, the way Ward had put his on the cipher sheet. "The king's men will come here. In the autumn. Ward told me last night. A hundred, maybe more. Soldiers who were going to the floor, and won't now. Here." He took his hand away. "I don't know what to say about it."

"You don't have to say anything."

"No," said Rylan. "But I wanted you to see it before they came. Before it was a place men were in." He turned from the window. "I wanted somebody to see it empty."



The pod was ripe on the Saturday.

Imani saw it first, at six, from the pass-box, through the inner door's glass, with her scrubber mask on and her ward coat over her nightgown and her feet bare on the iron floor because she had heard the tree in her sleep, she said afterward, and come down without her boots. Nobody believed her about hearing it. It did not matter. She came up the stair to Emeric's room at ten past six and opened the door without knocking and said "It's lit," and went back down again, and he was out of bed and into his trousers before he understood what she had said.

He had been waiting for a year. He had been waiting, if he was honest, for longer than that: since the night on the Isle when he had first held Vey's drawings in the lantern's light and seen the tree and the hanging pods and the figures under them, and the one figure with its hand to its mouth. A year since the seed went into the ground in the glass room at ten on an April morning with the screens drawn back. Eight months since the first pod showed, a small grey nub on a long thin vine under the tree's lowest tier, no bigger than a thumb. He had watched it every day since. Everyone had. Bram had a mark on the pass-box's inner door, a scratch in the paint at the height the pod's tip hung, and had made a new scratch every Sunday, and there were thirty-one of them, going down the door in a ladder, and the last six had been very close together.

He went down the stair two at a time.

The glass room stood in the middle of the castle ward with the screens round it and the dawn coming grey over the keep. He went round the screens to the pass-box, where Imani was, and Ferra behind her in her dressing gown with her hair down her back and her book under her arm, because Ferra slept with one ear on the stair; and he looked through the inner door's glass past Imani's shoulder at the tree.

It was not tall yet. It came up to perhaps three times his height, and its canopy had only the two lowest tiers, wide and flat, dense with violet leaves that glowed faintly from inside like the sky glows over a town at night; blue-white light ran along the branches under them, very faint, in the grey of the morning. Its trunk was a long braid of pale grey, twisted, as thick now as his waist. Three long thin vines hung down from under the lowest tier. Two of them ended in pale grey nubs the size of his fist, shut, unlit.

The third had a pod on it, a long pinecone of overlapping pale plates the length of his forearm, hanging at about twice the height of a man.

And it was gold.

The light came from inside it. Warm, yellow, steady, a lamp's light through horn: the whole shell lit, every plate of it, and through the plates, dark, packed close, the shapes of the seeds. He counted them. He could not help counting them; it was the first thing his eyes did. Seven. One fewer than Vey had drawn. A long pod, and lean, the seeds sitting a little loose in it, with a space at the bottom of the cone where an eighth ought to have been and was not.

"Seven," said Ferra, behind him.

"The seed's own charge," said Emeric. "The first pod's only got what the seed brought with it. The second'll have what the tree's taken in since."

"You don't know that."

"No."

"Then I'll write seven and not why." She opened her book.

Imani had taken her hand off the inner door. She turned round. Over the scrubber mask her eyes were very wide and very dark.

"It's beautiful," she said, and sounded angry about it.



They met in Ferra's room at eight: the six, and Imani, and Ferra with her book. There was nothing to decide that had not been decided. They had decided it in February, at this table, over three nights, with Vey's drawings spread out between the cups and Imani's list of objections on top of them, and they had decided it again in March, and Imani had made them decide it a third time on the first of April, because she said that nothing that had been decided twice in a warm room in winter was decided until it had been decided in the spring by people who were going to have to do it. So they decided it again, in Ferra's room, on the morning the pod was lit; and it took an hour, and came out the same.

Emeric would eat the first one.

He had said so in February, and nobody had argued with it in February except Imani, and she had not argued with the who, only the how. It was his rule, and it was older than Kaldo; it was a rule he had brought with him from the other side of everything, from a life he did not talk about, out of a profession where you tested a thing on yourself first if you could and on nobody else until you had. It was his tree. It was his mother's seed. Nobody in the world knew what eating one did except what a dead man had drawn a thousand years ago on the walls of a drowned cave, and Vey's drawings showed figures lying down under the tree afterward. Lying down, not standing up. Not one of them standing up.

Whatever it did, it would not be quick.

Imani's terms were on the table, on a sheet in her hospital hand, and she read them out again, as she had read them in February.

She would sit with him. Not in the dome; nobody walked into a charged dome, not even her, not even in a mask, not for longer than the length of a visit. She would sit at the pass-box. She would have the box's inner door open and the outer one shut, and a mask on, and she would take her turns there in shifts of an hour, no longer, and Bettany or one of the ward's other women would take the hour between, and she would sleep in the ward. She would have his pulse and his breath and his heat every hour, written down, through the box, by whatever means she had: her hand through the door, or the stethoscope's long tube, or her eyes. And she would decide when it was over. Not him. Him least of anybody. If she said it was over, he came out of the dome, whatever state he was in, on a board between two men in masks if he had to; and whatever the stone was doing to him it would go on doing out here, in thin air, or it would stop, and either way she would have him where she could put her hands on him.

"And if it stops out here," said Bram, "and he's half done?"

"Then he's half done and alive." Imani did not look up from the sheet. "I can work with half done and alive. I can't work with the other thing."

Nobody said anything to that.

Ward would cuff him.

That was Emeric's own term, and it was the one Ward had argued with, in February, for most of a night. Vey's figures lay still. But Emeric's mana did not lie still; it never had. He had known since he was a child at the palace, before he knew anything, that when he was ill or frightened or asleep and dreaming, it went outward. Things moved. Water in a basin by his bed had frozen once, in a fever, when he was six. He did not remember it. Armand did. And whatever the stone did, if it did anything like what Vey's drawings said it did, it was going to come in through his mana and change it, and while it changed it the mana was going to push outward against everything, against the glass, against the air, against the tree. A half-made glyph near a pane of Ivo's glass could crack it. A cracked pane in a charged dome would let the dome's air into the ward in the time it took to say it, and the dome's whole year of air would be in the ward with the morning's cooks crossing it.

So, the cuffs. Stone cuffs, the same as Kaldo's cells used: flat stones in iron bands that sat against the skin and drank any mana that tried to leave the hand as a cast, and left anything a man used inside his own body alone, because that never left him. They would not touch what the stone did, if the stone worked inside him, as Vey's drawings said it did. They would stop anything going out.

"It's two sets," said Ward. "Wrists and ankles. You'll have them on for a week. You'll not be able to light a lamp."

"I know."

"You'll not be able to lift a cup if it's out of your reach. You'll not be able to put a fire out if your bed catches. If the dome goes wrong you'll lie there with your hands in iron and watch it."

"I know," said Emeric. "That's why it's you."

Ward looked at him for a long time, across the table, with the drawings between them.

Then he said, "Boards," and looked at Bram.

Bram had made the boards. A bed of planks on short trestles, by the trunk, on the side away from the glass, as far from every pane as the dome allowed, with a straw mattress Imani had stuffed herself and a blanket, and a bucket, and a jug, and a rope from the trestle's foot to a ring in the pass-box's frame so that two men in masks could drag the whole thing to the door in under a minute if Imani said so. Bram had dragged it to the door eleven times on Thursday with Osric, timing it on Ottoline's watch, until Ottoline said they were making her watch dizzy.

And the dome itself would be held at his line.

That was Armand's. He sat at the end of the table in his fur with his eyes half shut and spoke without opening them. The glass room had been fed for a year at the tree's line, which was higher than any living man's, Emeric's included, because a seed needed what it needed. A man lying in it for a week at the tree's line would be a man lying in a bath that was a little too hot. The tree would not die at a man's line. It would slow. It would rest. It would be, Armand said, as near as he could tell from twenty years at the top of a tower and four days with Vey's drawings, rather like a cat whose fire has been banked; unhappy, and alive. They would bring the room down to Emeric's own line by letting it bleed through the pass-box all day, and hold it there, a cartridge at a time through the collar, for as long as it took; and Bram would count the cartridges.

"Every one," said Armand. "Out loud. I want to hear them."

"Why out loud?" said Bram.

"Because a man who counts out loud doesn't fall asleep," said Armand, and opened his eyes, "and I'm an old man, and I may."

Then the others.

There were seven stones in the pod. One for Emeric. That left six.

Nobody else would eat one. Not this season. That was Imani's too, the last line on her sheet, and she had underlined it twice: no second eater until there was a season of records on the first, at the least. A whole season. Not a week, not a month. She did not know what the stone did in a week, and she did not know what it did in a month, and she would not let anybody find out on their own body because they could not wait.

"Then the six go in Ferra's box," said Emeric. "Under her key."

"Not seven," said Ferra.

"Six."

"There's seven in the pod. You're eating one. That's six." She did not look up from her book. "You said 'the six go in Ferra's box' as though you'd got a seventh for somewhere else."

They were all looking at him.

"One for my father," he said. "Set aside. Not given. Set aside."

The table was quiet.

"He's not a delver," said Bram, after a while.

"No."

"He doesn't know what it is."

"No."

"Then what's it for?"

Emeric did not have an answer to that he could say at a table. He had thought about it for three nights, since the buckram. He had thought about a king who had sent a man down to a tower's cellar to find a name on a roll thirty years old, just to know; who could not do a single thing himself; who had written bring me a road I can give them in a cipher he had kept for thirty years to talk to the one man in his service he trusted. He had not thought about what it was for. He had only thought that he would not put all seven in a box and turn a key on them, and leave none in the world with his father's name on it.

"I don't know," he said. "I don't know what it's for yet. That's why it's set aside."

Ferra wrote something in her book. Short.

"Six in the box," she said. "One beside it with nothing on it." She closed the book. "If anybody asks me what the seventh is for I'll say I don't know, and that'll be true, and I'll enjoy it."



They let the pod fall at four.

That was in the drawings too: the ripe pod let go of its vine and dropped, and when it hit the ground it opened, and what came out of it when it opened was not only the seeds. It was a breath of mana, hard and short, like the wind off a stone that has been overloaded; and in Vey's drawings there were two trees, side by side, and the breath went from the one to the other. It was how they took. Emeric had read that for a year and understood it by halves, the way he understood a lot of Vey. He thought it was pollen. He thought the breath carried the tree's own instructions across to its partner and set something going there. It did not matter yet. There was one tree, and nothing for the breath to go to, and nobody wanted it loose in a room with the floor full of glass.

So Bram had built a basket.

It hung under the pod on four thin chains from the tree's lowest tier, and it was lined in lead, and it was the size of a baby's bath: a basket to catch a thing as it fell and keep its breath in when it opened. Bram had hung it there in March from a ladder, in a mask, in nine minutes, which was as long as Imani would let him be inside, and had come out through the pass-box red and swearing and sat in the snow in the ward for a quarter of an hour with his head between his knees, and had said it was worth it.

At four the pod let go.

Emeric was inside. He had gone in at half past three through the pass-box with the cuffs not yet on, and a mask on, which was ridiculous, because he did not need it and everybody knew it; Imani had made him wear it anyway, for the first half hour, so that she could see whether he did. He took it off at a quarter to four and she let him. The dome's air was thick and quiet and smelled of wet earth and green leaves and something under both that was not a smell at all, that his mana sense took for one because it did not have another word for it. The tree hummed. He could feel it with his feet on the path; he had felt it every time he came in. Not a sound. A note, slow, like the lowest string of a bass held down and bowed, felt in the chest and the soles and nowhere else.

He stood under the pod and looked up at it.

The gold light came down through the plates onto his face. He could see the seeds. He could feel them. Seven small hard silences in a living thing, each one as quiet and dense and complete as a closed door, exactly like the stones in the pouch he had carried through the gates of the capital at twenty-two with his name struck off the rolls behind him, and exactly unlike them, because those were dead and these were not yet.

At four the vine let go of the pod, as softly as a hand opens. The pod dropped a body's length into Bram's lead-lined basket and the basket swung on its chains, and the pod split along its plates, and the breath came out of it.

It was not as hard as he had expected. It came out of the basket like the warmth comes off a loaf when it is broken, all at once and soft, and went upward through the canopy and into the dome's air and was gone into it, and the tree's leaves all lifted, every leaf on both tiers at once, as if a wind had come up underneath them; and then lay down again. The note in the floor changed. He would have said it went up a tone. It did not go back down.

Outside, through the glass, he saw Imani's hand go flat on the pass-box's inner door.

He reached into the basket with both hands and took the seeds out.

They were cooling already. That was in the drawings too. The gold went out of each one as it came out of the shell, slowly, from the outside in, like an ember going dark, and in his palm it was grey, and unlit, and smooth, a long smooth egg of stone, heavier than it looked, the size of the top joint of his thumb. Seven. He laid six of them in the felt-lined box Ferra had given him, one by one, in its six hollows; the seventh in a separate box of plain wood with nothing written on it. He shut both boxes and took them to the pass-box and set them on the floor of the box and shut the inner door on them, and Imani opened the outer door and took them out and gave them to Ferra, and Ferra took them away without a word.

He went back to the boards.

Ward came in at a quarter past four.

He came in through the pass-box in his mask with the cuffs on his belt, and he came in fast, because Imani had given him five minutes and he meant to use four. He did not look at the tree. He knelt by the boards and Emeric lay down on them and held out his wrists, and Ward put the first band on his right wrist and closed it, and Emeric felt the flat stone settle against the inside of the joint and begin to drink.

It did not hurt. It was a pull, steady and cold, like a hand on a door that you were not leaning on; there was nothing to take, because he was not casting. If he had reached for a glyph it would have gone into the stone like water into sand. Ward closed the second band on his left wrist. Then the ankles, one and then the other, over his socks, because Imani had said the skin there would rub raw in a week otherwise and she would not have him bleeding into her straw. Ward did not say anything while he did it. His hands were quick and quite steady, and the mask made his breathing loud.

When the last band was closed Ward sat back on his heels.

"Tight?"

"No."

"Tell Imani if they're tight."

"I will."

Ward looked at him. Then he looked, for the first time, up at the tree; at the canopy, glowing; at the empty vine above the basket, swinging a little still.

"Your mother would have liked to see that," he said.

Then he got up and went out through the pass-box, and did not look back.

Emeric lay on the boards for a while and looked at the tree.

There was nobody in the dome. He could feel them outside it, all of them, round the pass-box and along the screens, every note he knew best in the world standing in the castle ward in the grey April afternoon pretending to be busy: Bram's, at the collar, with the rack of cartridges; Armand's, faint and frail, in his chair under a rug in the lee of the screens; Ward's, by the pass-box, very still; Ferra's, going away across the ward with two boxes, and stopping at the keep's door, and coming back. Imani's, at the inner door's glass, with her hand flat on it. And further off, all round, the town: the whole town, going about its Saturday, not knowing that anything was happening at all.

He sat up on the boards and took the stone out of his pocket.

He had kept it back. He had not said he would. Ferra had seen him do it, he thought; she had seen everything, always. But she had said nothing. It lay in his palm, grey, smooth, cool, heavy, a long small egg of stone, the most ordinary-looking thing in the dome; and he held it up between his thumb and finger against the gold light that was still faintly in the canopy, and could not see through it at all.

In Vey's drawing the one figure had its hand to its mouth. He had looked at that figure for a year. You could not tell from the drawing whether the figure was afraid.

He looked at the inner door. Imani was there, behind the glass, in the mask, with her hand flat on it. Over the mask her eyes had not moved off him since Ward went out.

He lifted his cuffed hand, a little, to her, the stone in it.

She did not lift hers. She did not nod. She stood there with her hand flat on the glass and looked at him; and after a moment she took her hand off the door and put it in the pocket of her coat, where her watch was, and he saw her take the watch out and open it, and look at it, and look back at him.

He put the stone in his mouth and swallowed it.

It went down hard and cold and too big, like swallowing a pebble out of a river, and he felt it go all the way down and stop. He drank from the jug. He lay back on the boards and pulled the blanket up and put his cuffed hands on his chest, and looked at the tree, and waited.

Outside, through the glass, he saw Imani write something on her sheet, and look at her watch again.

Bram's voice came through the collar, very faint, the first of them.

"One."

For half an hour nothing happened at all.

Then something did; and it was not in his stomach, where the stone was, or in his head, or anywhere he would have looked for it. It was in his hands. It started in the small bones at the base of his fingers, where his mana had always been loudest, the place it went first when he reached for a cast; a slow warmth, rising, as if he had held his hands out to a fire. He looked at them. They looked the same. The warmth went on rising. It went past warm, and into his wrists, and the cuffs woke on his wrists, both at once, as something tried to leave him and could not, and drank.

It was not him casting. He was not casting anything. It was coming out of him on its own.

He lay still and breathed and watched the light in the canopy. It was going violet again, now the gold was gone. The note in the floor had come up another tone.

Through the glass Imani had her watch in one hand and her pencil in the other. Thirty-one minutes, he thought. She'll write thirty-one minutes.

The warmth reached his elbows, and kept on.
