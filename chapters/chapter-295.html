Chapter 295 - Beside the Tree

By nine on the morning of the twenty-sixth they had stopped trying to decide whether to be angry with him and started building round him instead, which was what Kaldo did with most things.

The great dome stood over all of it, its iron ribs going up from the colonnade to the crown ring, enamelled dark on every inside face, most of their glass in and the last of it waiting in crates under tarpaulins along the colonnade's foot, where the glaziers had left it when the frost came. It would not hold its own air until the spring. So under it, at its heart, the old glass room still stood on its steel ring, the fifty-foot dome of triangles that the town called the winter garden, sealed and full, with its pass-box and its collar: a glass room inside a glass house, and the tree inside both, its third tier close now under the triangles at the crown.

And under the tree, on Bram's boards by the trunk, on the north side, with his head to the roots, Armand lay on his back with his cuffed hands on the blanket and Ward's key still standing in the lock of the right one, because Ward had said to leave it.



Imani wanted the room brought down. Not out; she knew now what out did. Down. She said so at the pass-box at half past eight, with her ward coat buttoned wrong, and she said it the way she said a thing she had already argued with herself about and lost.

"To his own line," she said. "Not under it. I've got it on his sheet. I'd bleed it through the box all day, slowly, until it sits on him no harder than the Band's foot ever did, and the tearing goes slower, and his heart gets a chance to keep up." She did not look round. "I've been holding that heart together with foxglove and a balance for three years. I don't want a full room tearing roads through it."

Emeric was on the crate by the collar where Bram had sat through his own week. He could feel, without reaching for it, what the room was doing. The whole dome leaned toward the north side of the trunk the way water leans toward a drain, and where it reached the old man there was the knot, not still, and round it a door standing open wider than Emeric had ever felt a door stand in anybody but himself.

"It'd still tear," he said. "Slower."

"I know."

"And mend slower."

"I know that too." She took her hand off the glass. "A thin room does it by halves. He said so himself, at that table, about you. I wrote it down." She looked through the glass at the boards. "And half a mend's no use to a heart. A heart that's half mended is a heart that's still failing." She put the mask up over her mouth and nose and said to Bram, through it, "Keep it full. The tree's line. If he drinks it down, put it back." And then, lower, to nobody: "He knew I'd stand here and want to thin it, and not. He timed that too."

Bram went to the valve.

It was in the pass-box's outer room, under the gauge, the brass wheel on the pipe that came in under the floor from the plant's reservoir; and it had stood wide open since a quarter past eleven on Long Table night, with the pipe singing its thin note in the iron, because the old man had opened it with both hands. Ferra had the night's fall in the feed gauge book already, in her own hand under his pencil: forty-one cartridges' worth since a quarter past eleven, out of a room that drank one a day to hold itself for the tree.

"Forty-one," said Bram, reading it over her shoulder. "Out of a day's one."

"Out of a day's one." Ferra did not close the book. "You took forty-three in your first nine hours," she said to Emeric. "He's had forty-one in six, with the wheel wide. I can't tell yet what's him and what's the pipe." She looked at the needle. "I'll know by tomorrow's fall."

Bram eased the wheel back by a quarter turn and watched the needle, and opened it again a little, and stood with his hand on it for most of an hour, until the needle sat a hair above Ferra's red line and stayed. Then he took a stick of chalk from his apron and marked the wheel's rim where it stood, and wrote beside the mark, on the iron, in the hand that could be read from the lane: FULL. DON'T.



Imani went in at ten for her first proper look, with Ward behind her in his mask, because the old man had slid a little on the straw since dawn and she wanted him straight. They lifted him a hand's height between them, Ward at the shoulders and Imani at the hips, as if he were a full glass, and set him back with his head hard to the roots; and she put the blanket over him, and her fingers on his neck, and looked at his arms above the cuffs.

There was a line on the left forearm. Silver, fine, a hand's width above the wrist, branching. They all saw it from the box, dulling while they watched, and going, and another coming up a little higher.

Imani came out on the fourth minute and wrote standing, against the box's iron wall.

"Sixty-two," she said. "In step. Still."

"Is that the stone?" said Bram.

"I don't know what it is. I know it's sixty-two and in step. I'll write that I don't know."

Ward had come out behind her. He stood looking back through the glass at the key in the lock on the old man's wrist, and put his hand inside his coat to the place where the cord had been, and took it away again.

"I'll change them," he said. "When they fill. With that."

After a while he went and stood by the pass-box's outer door, which was where he stood for most of the next six weeks when he was not anywhere else.



Bram had not been there at dawn when Ferra read the note out, and he wanted it read again in front of the tree, because he had had it from Ward in four words on the stair and did not trust Ward with a page.

So Ferra read it at noon from the back of the feed gauge book, in her reading voice, every word: the old man's pencil, small and square and cramped. The night's fall. The middle stone of three. The cuffs and the key. Imani's no, and that she had been right to say it. And then the part for if it went the other way: that they were to bury him beside the tree, and build a house of windows over him when the duchy could afford the glass, and tell the tower his heart had failed.

Ferra stopped.

Nobody looked up. Everybody was trying very hard not to look up. Over the ward and the glass room and the tree and the old man on Bram's boards stood the great dome, ribs and colonnade and most of its glass, with the last of the panes in their crates along its foot; and Ferra had costed every one of those panes, and had the figure in her head, and Emeric watched it go through her face.

"Well," said Bram, at last. "He's got two."

Tess, who had come up from the works at eleven and not said a word since, made a sound into her glove and turned her chair round to face the other way.

"He says it was written in October," said Ward. "Before the glazing."

"The glass is bought," said Ferra, and closed the book. "I'll write that we've followed the note. Beside the tree, yes. A house of windows, yes, at a cost I'd rather not read out." She looked through the glass at the cuffed hands on the blanket. "Nobody's putting earth over him. That's the one line we're not doing. He can be cross about it when he wakes."

"If," said Bram.

"When," said Ferra, "is the word I'm writing."



Imani's terms went up on the pass-box's outer door that afternoon, on a card in her hospital hand, pinned under the old ONE AT A TIME.

The first hours were the stone's, and she could not stop them. After them she would sit with him every night at the box, nine to six; Bettany would sit the days; Ward would sit wherever he liked. Every hour, whoever was sitting went in, masked, for no longer than they needed, and looked at his arms above the cuffs and the sides of his neck, and his pulse and breath and heat went on the sheet, and so did the lines. Lines that came and went were the work: the room tore, and the stone mended behind. A line that stayed was a line where the mending had stopped. If one stayed an hour by the watch on the box's shelf, he came out.

Coming out was underlined, and she had written under it what it meant, because the card was for whoever came after her. Not a rope and a drag to the door, whatever she had said to him at dawn; she knew better now, and so did he. The room. The collar shut, and the dome bled down through the box into the ward, slowly, over a day, to his own line and under it, so that the room stopped tearing him; and whatever the stone was doing, it would go on doing in thin air, or stop.

And last, smaller, added after: Not the Hod. Not the Band. Not with that heart, at that height, in this cold. Ned had asked her that morning, and so had Linnet Shaw, who had stood at the glass an hour with her hands in her sleeves; and Imani was not going to be asked a third time.

"Bleeding it down," said Emeric. "In the middle of a tear. With that heart."

"It's the worst thing on the card. It's there because the other thing's worse: if the mending stops and the room keeps tearing, he bleeds inside where I can't reach." She pinned the card's corner down again, though it had not moved. "It won't come to that. It didn't with you. It's written so nobody has to decide anything at three in the morning. They read it. They do it."



The Hod went up at nine that morning for the first of the winter's charge runs, because Ward had said so at dawn, and came home after dark.

The rest was Ferra's sum, done at supper on the back of a feed slip with Ned across the table eating Maren's leftover goose with his fingers. The plant's reservoir fed the forge and the racks and the tree's one a day, and now an old man at four a day if he settled where Emeric had settled, and far more until he did. For how long, Ferra wrote "weeks" with nothing in front of it.

"Every second night," she said. "To the Band and back with every empty canister on the dock. All winter, until I say."

"It's forty below up there in January," said Ned. "Cade's had his moustache freeze to a valve." He wiped his fingers. "I'll want the three."

"You've got the three," said Ward.

So it was Arthur and Mira and Bren, who had sat the Band's foot with the scrubber shut longer than any crew in Kaldo, and who would fly every second night for two months, Ward said, without once asking whether the old man was worth it. They went out from the secret dock on the twenty-eighth at eleven at night with the empties racked behind their iron and Mira's breath standing up in the quay lamp like smoke. Bren had his new harness clipped to the hull before he was through the hatch. Arthur stopped at the top of the ladder and looked back toward the town, where the dome's glow showed faintly on the underside of the cloud.

"How long, Highness?"

"I don't know."

Arthur nodded as if that were an answer and went down into the hold.

The Hod came home at five with every canister full and Ned's eyebrows white. And the night's fall in the feed gauge book, which Ferra read first every morning as she always had, came down: fourteen the second day, eight the third, five the fourth, four the fifth, and four the sixth.

"Four," said Ferra. "Against one."

"He's settled."

"He's settled at four times the tree. The tree's never once complained."



She put the note away on the night of the twenty-seventh, in the study, while he watched.

She cut the page out of the feed gauge book along the spine with the ruler laid down the fold, and copied the readings from its back onto the next clean page so the book had no gap. Then she folded the note once and opened the drawer in the study table. The tooth in its sailcloth, smaller than it had been in the summer. The scale, which was nothing. The slip that said drained. The Hollow Night proof with his struck line. She laid the note on top of them, square.

"It's getting to be a drawer," she said.

"It was always a drawer."

"It was a drawer with a tooth in it. Now it's a drawer of things you can't explain." She did not shut it. "Is it going to work?"

He had known she would ask, and that it would be Ferra, and here, and that he would want very much to give her an answer.

"I don't know," he said.

She waited.

"I know what the room does. I know what the stone did to me. He's bonded his, and I hadn't bonded mine, and I don't know what that changes; that's why he did it." He looked at the drawer. "I don't know what a full room does to a heart that old. It'll tear his heart's channels the way it tore mine, and mine was thirty-three and his has been failing since before he came south. I don't know if a thing that old can be torn and mended, or only torn." He stopped. "I keep trying to think of the number. The chance. There isn't one. There's one man who's done it, and it was me, and I'm no use to him."

Ferra shut the drawer and turned the key and put it in her pocket.

"Then I'll write that," she said. "That you don't know." She put her hand flat on the drawer's front. "Under his note. I want it written down somewhere that somebody in this castle didn't pretend."



The nights at the pass-box were long and very cold and, for the first few, almost pleasant.

He had not expected that. He sat the first night in dread, and the second; and then the third night came and the old man was still on the boards with the lines coming and going on his arms in their slow silver, and Imani's sheet in its pigeonhole by the door was filled in every hour in her plain hand, pulse, breath, heat, lines, and every line said came and went, came and went; and some time after midnight he found he had stopped waiting for something to happen and was only watching.

Imani sat on the stool in the box with the inner door open and the outer one shut, her mask round her neck and the sheet on her knee. Ferra sat on the crate by the collar in Emeric's old coat over her own, with her book and her feet on a brick Bram had warmed in the forge and wrapped in sacking, which he had brought up at ten and set down without a word and gone home. Emeric sat on the paving between them with his back to the box's iron wall, which was faintly warm from the dome's side, the way a wall is warm from a room with a fire in it.

Through the box's window, when he turned, he could see the tree, lit low for the night: the violet in the canopy down to its sunset glow, the blue going faintly along the grooves of the braided trunk. The old man lay with his head an arm's length from it, his white hair spread on the straw and his face turned a little toward the trunk, as if somebody had said something from that side and he had turned to hear it.

"He looks pleased," said Ferra.

"He's asleep," said Imani.

"Asleep and pleased. Look at his mouth."

Imani looked. "That's the corner of his mouth. That's what it does when he's won an argument. It's been like that since dawn." She wrote something. "I'm not writing it down."

"You've just written something."

"His heat."

The watch on the shelf came round to the hour. She put her mask up and went in, and they watched through the glass: the four steps along the path, the hand on his throat, the blanket turned back, his left arm lifted gently by the cuff and turned to the lamp and laid down; the right; the neck, one side and then the other, her head bent close and her fingers moving the white hair aside. A line came up under his jaw while she was looking. She watched it until it went. Then she stood over him a breath longer than she needed to, and came out.

"Came and went," she said, and wrote it, and pulled the mask off. She was flushed from the room, high on the cheekbones.

"You're red."

"I've been in a charged room two minutes, eight times tonight, and I'm not a tree." She leaned her head back against the iron. "Ask me again at six."

He put the back of his hand against her cheek where it was hot. She let him, and then turned her face a little into his hand and shut her eyes, the pencil still in her fingers, until Ferra said from the crate, without looking up, "If you two fall asleep I'm not taking the next hour. I can't read her writing."

"Everybody can read my writing," said Imani, eyes shut. "I write so anybody can."

"I can read it. I don't want to go in there and look at his neck."

"Then don't fall asleep either."

Ferra turned a page. After a while she put her stockinged foot out from under the coat, off the warm brick, and set it against Emeric's leg on the paving, and left it there.

There were three more nights like it, near enough. On the fourth, at four in the morning, Ward changed the cuffs, because the left wrist's stone had gone the slate grey of a full one and he had been watching it since two. He was inside the four minutes Imani gave him, and came out with the four full cuffs in his arms and put them down on the paving.

"He's lighter," he said. "To lift."

"He's eighty-seven."

"He's lighter than he was a week ago. I carried him up the keep stair in November. I know what he weighs."

Imani wrote it down.



On the night of the thirtieth, at eight minutes past two, a line stayed.

Emeric saw Imani see it. She was in the dome on her hour, bent over the old man's left arm with the cuff turned to the lamp, and she stopped moving: not as people stop when they are startled, but as she stopped on the ward when she had found the thing she had been looking for and did not want to have found it.

She stayed past her two minutes, and past three. When she came out she did not sit down.

"It's dark," she said. "Inside the left forearm, a hand above the cuff. It came up silver the way they all do, and it didn't go. It went dark. Like a line drawn on him in plum ink with a fine pen." She looked at the watch. "I watched it four minutes. It's still there."

She went back at the half hour, and at a quarter to three, and at three. At three she came out and wrote, and sat down and looked at what she had written.

"Two. The first one's still there. And another on the right, at the elbow, since a quarter to."

At eight minutes past three by the watch on the shelf, the first line had stayed an hour.

Nobody said it. Ferra was looking at the card on the outer door, and Emeric was looking at it too, and Imani was not, because she had written it. If a line stays an hour, he comes out. The collar shut, the room bled down over a day, with the stone in the middle of whatever it was doing to his heart. Written. Read. Done.

"Bettany," said Imani.

Bettany sat up on the bench by the screen post under two coats.

"Get Ward. And Bram. Tell Bram to come to the collar and put his hand on the wheel and not turn it. Tell him not to turn it. Say it twice."

Bettany went.

"Imani," said Emeric.

"I know what it says. I wrote it." She was turning the stool to face the inner door. "I'm going to look first. Properly. And if I don't like what I see I'll do what it says, and if I do like it I'll probably do what it says anyway, because I wrote it when I could think." She shut the box's outer door. "Sit down, both of you. Don't talk to me."

She sat with her hands folded on her knee and her eyes shut, the room's air coming round the edges of the inner door onto her face, and went in.



She was gone forty minutes.

He could feel her do it, her sense going out of her thinner than breath and into the old man. He kept his own drawn tight and did not follow. Ward came and stood at the door. Bram came in his coat over his nightshirt and put his hand on the wheel beside the chalk and did not turn it. Nobody spoke. The tree hummed in the paving.

At a quarter to four she opened her eyes, and wrote for a long while, small, and turned back to the twenty-sixth and read, and turned forward and wrote more.

"It's blood," she said.

Ferra let her breath out.

"The mending hasn't stopped. I followed the left one in. The tear's under it, the way the room tears all of them; and the stone's thread is in it, running along it, closing it. I watched it close a finger's length. Behind it the wall's whole." She was looking at the sheet as if it might change. "But the tear's bleeding into the flesh round it. They all bleed a little when the room goes through. I saw it in you, and in him on the first day, and it stops; it stops the way a cut stops. These don't. The thread closes the wall behind and the blood keeps coming out in front of it and lies there under the skin along the whole tear. That's the dark. It's not a line that's stopped. It's a line that's bled."

"Why," said Ward, from the door.

"I don't know yet." She turned back a page. "Bettany swabbed his mouth at noon yesterday with the wet cloth, and his gum bled, and went on bleeding for half an hour, and she wrote it because I tell them to write everything. I read it at six and thought, he's old, his gums are old." She was very quiet. "And the left cuff, where it rubbed. Ward wrapped it in lint on the twenty-ninth. At midnight the lint was wet. A rub. Not a cut. Still wet."

She looked up. Not at Ward or Ferra. At Emeric.

"The willow," she said.

He had known four words before she said it. He had known at the gum. It had come up in him the way the answer to a sum comes up, all at once and cold, sitting on the paving with her voice saying half an hour; and he had said nothing, because it was hers and she was getting there, and because he had wanted, for one more breath, for it to be something else.

"Aspirin," he said.

"I've been giving it him. The tablet crushed in water off the spoon, with the foxglove, at seven every night since he went under, the way he's had it every night since November, because it was on his card and nobody took it off." She put the pencil down. "Since you said it would stop his heart's blood clotting."

"It does. It was right. It's what it's for." He heard his own voice, quite level. "It stops the blood clotting in his heart. It stops it clotting anywhere. It doesn't know where it is." He looked through the glass at the boards. "And the stone's tearing him open from the inside a hundred times an hour, and every tear has to close, and nothing's closing them ahead of the thread, because I had you give him a thing that keeps blood from closing."

"So you stop it," said Ferra. "Now. And it stops bleeding."

"I stop it now," said Imani, "and it goes on bleeding." She looked at Emeric. "Doesn't it."

"Yes."

"For how long." And then, before he could answer, she shut her eyes. "No. I know how long. I wrote it on his card. In November. You said it and I wrote it. Bleeds, bruises. A week to clear." She opened them. "Say it anyway."

He had the number. He had it the way he had every page he had ever read: one dry sentence in a pharmacology text on the other side of everything, read once at a kitchen table in a life nobody in this ward would ever hear of. It did not thin the blood the way water thins milk. It spoiled the small part of the blood that gathered at a cut and made the first seal, and every grain of that it touched it spoiled for good, for the whole of that grain's life. The body did not mend them. It made new ones, a little every day. And until enough of the new were made, the old man would go on bleeding along every tear the room made, and the room was not going to stop making them.

"A week," he said. "About a week. A little better every day from tomorrow. All of it his own again by a week, near enough."

"I had it on the card," said Imani, "and I didn't read it."

"You read it every night for a month. It was right every night for a month."

Imani looked at the card on the outer door. Then she got up and unpinned it, and held it against the box's iron wall, and wrote under the underlined words in the same plain upright hand, slowly, so that anybody could read it.

A line that stays dark is not always a line that has stopped. Look before you bleed the room. If the thread is working under the dark, the mending has not stopped and the dark is blood. Keep the room full. I. 31 Dec, yr 12, 4 in the morning.

She pinned it back up.

"If I'm wrong," she said, to the card, "the room goes on tearing him and nothing mends it, and he bleeds out under that tree in a fortnight, and I'll have done it with my eyes open." She turned round. "And if I bleed it down, the tearing stops halfway through his heart, and he dies tomorrow in thin air, and I'll have done that with my eyes open too, because my own card told me to." She looked at Bram. "Leave it full. Go to bed."

Bram took his hand off the wheel. He did not go to bed. He sat down on the crate by the collar in his nightshirt and looked at the chalk on the wheel's rim until it was light.



He went into the dome at five, without a mask, when Imani had done the hour and written came, stayed, and under it, thread working, and sat with her head against the iron and her eyes shut.

He knelt on the roots by the old man's head in the low violet light and looked at the arms. Dark in three places now: one above the left wrist, one at the right elbow, one coming up under the jaw. Fine, branching, plum-coloured under the old skin, like the veins in a leaf held up to a lamp. Between them the silver ones came and went as they always had.

He had not been on anything. That kept coming back. In his own week he had been well and young and on nothing at all. Nobody had ever lain under a stone on a blood thinner, because there had been no blood thinner in this world until he drew the willow's acid on a sheet of paper in November for Bram's acid works. It had helped. Every book he had ever read would have said to give it; He had even told her it would make the old man bleed longer. And he had been a little pleased with himself when Imani held the first white tablet off Ottoline's die up to the lamp and said, that's my bark? None of those books had known about the stone, because none of them had been written anywhere a stone was. And in November there had been no stone in the old man, and nobody, the old man least of all, had told him there was going to be.

All of that was true. It did not help.

The corner of the old man's mouth was the same.

"I'm sorry," said Emeric, very low, to nobody who could hear him.

When he came out Imani's eyes were open, and she had heard; he did not know how. She held her hand out from the stool and pulled him down onto the paving beside her, and kept his hand on her knee on top of the sheet while she wrote the next hour with the other.

"Nobody's fault," she said, writing.

"I know."

"You don't know. You've got a face on." She did not look up. "It was right. It's right for a man with that heart, and it'll be right tomorrow for any man in my ward with that heart, and I'd have given it whether you'd told me or not, if I'd known it existed." She finished the line. "And nobody who ever gave it had a stone. There's no book that has both. There's only my sheet." She turned it to the next hour. "So I'm writing the book. You can help. You can't sit in the corner of it being sorry. There isn't room."



It was a long week.

Imani struck the aspirin off his card on the morning of the thirty-first, in ink, with her initial and the hour, and wrote NOT FOR THE DOME on the jar in the dispensary and then locked the jar in a drawer anyway. The foxglove stayed.

The dark did not go. For three days it got worse, as Emeric had known it would and had not said: a new line on the right forearm on the first of January, another up the left side of the neck from the collarbone toward the ear; and the first ones spreading a little at their edges, plum going to brown. Every hour Imani went in. Every hour she sat on the stool with her eyes shut and looked at every dark line there was, and came out and wrote the same two words under it, thread working, and her initial, and the hour.

The Hod flew on the first, and the third, and the fifth. Bram went home to sleep because Imani made him, and came back. Ward brought a camp bed from the garrison store and slept on it in the afternoons against the screen post in his coat and boots, with his hands folded on his chest the way the old man's were, and nobody pointed it out to him.

And the town went on. That was what Emeric remembered of that week afterward. The glaziers came back for a day when the frost broke, and looked down through their own new glass at the man on the boards, and then were careful to look at their putty. Sela's school came back from its midwinter week on the fourth with every child in it knowing the old master was ill and lying in the garden, because Sela said a school that was not told things made them up; and the youngest class sent up a drawing of the tree with the old man under it in a bed with a red blanket, smiling, and a house of glass over everything in yellow chalk with every pane outlined. Ferra received it at the gate with perfect gravity and took it to the study and did not put it in the drawer.



The worst night was the fourth.

It did not look like the worst night. A new dark line came up at ten, beside the long one on the neck. At half past eleven his pulse went to seventy-four and out of step for the first time since the twenty-sixth, and stayed out for a quarter of an hour while Imani leaned in through the inner door with two fingers on his throat and counted against her watch with her lips moving; then it came back to sixty-one, in step. That was all. She wrote it down.

But it was the fourth of seven. By Emeric's number, half the spoiled blood ought to have been made new by now, and the lines ought at least to have started to slow; and they had not. Everybody at the box knew it, and nobody said it. At midnight Bettany came to take the hour so that Imani could sleep, as Imani had promised Ferra she would, and Imani got up off the stool and stood in the box's door with her hand on the frame and did not go.

"The long room," said Ferra. "It's warm. You can see the dome from the end windows. Bettany can ring the box's bell, and it's thirty-eight steps." She had her book under her arm. "I've timed it. Come and not sleep somewhere warm."

So they went up, the three of them, at ten past midnight on the fifth of January, and did not light the lamps. Imani did not sit. She stood at the middle window at the room's end with her arms folded and her forehead almost on the cold pane, and looked down.

From up there you could see all of it at once. The inner ward in its frost; the great dome's ribs going up black against the town's glow, its glass giving the stars back, with a few dark gaps at the crown where the last panes were not in; inside them the glass room lit violet from within, every triangle of its skin showing; and through the triangles, small, on the north side of the trunk, the boards and the pale shape on them, and Bettany's lamp at the box.

Emeric stood beside her. After a while Ferra stood on her other side.

"I wrote it," said Imani. "The card. With my own hand, so nobody had to decide at three in the morning. And at three in the morning I decided. I read it and said, no, not this time, I've looked." She did not take her forehead from the glass. "My mother used to say the rules are for the nights you're too tired to think, and the night you think you're cleverer than the rule is the night you kill somebody. I'm cleverer than the rule every hour. I go in and the threads are working, and I come out and write thread working, and I'm right, every hour, and every hour there's more dark on him."

"It'll slow," said Emeric. "It should have started."

"Should."

"Should. I don't know why it hasn't." He did not lie to her; with her it was not even possible. "Old men make new blood slower. Maybe a week's longer than a week, in him. I gave you a number out of a book written for men who weren't eighty-seven and weren't lying under a tree."

"You gave me the only number there is." She turned her head against the glass and looked at him. Her eyes were very tired and quite dry. "Keep saying it. Every time I come out. I'd like somebody to say it to me who isn't me."

"A week," he said.

Ferra put her hand flat on Imani's back between the shoulder blades, the way she put it on a ledger to keep a page from turning.

At one Bettany's lamp went into the dome and came out, and nothing rang. At two, the same. Some time after two Imani's knees went, as knees do at the end of twenty hours, and Emeric caught her under the arms before she had gone an inch. "I'm fine," she said, and he said "I know," and sat down on the floor under the window with his back to the wall and pulled her down with him, and she let him. Ferra brought the rug off the old man's chair at the table's head and put it over the two of them and sat down on his other side with her shoulder against his and her book closed in her lap, for once. Imani's head went onto his shoulder. Her hair had come down on one side out of its strip of cloth and lay over his arm and smelled of the dome, wet earth and green leaves and the thing under both that was not a smell. Neither of them slept. He could feel them both not sleeping, through his arm and his shoulder: the small held stillness of two people listening for a bell.

At three Ferra said, without lifting her head from the wall, "Forty with your shoes off. I've got my shoes off. I've counted them nine times."

Imani's hand found his on top of the rug, and then found Ferra's, and pulled it across his knee and put it with his, and held both.

The bell did not ring.

At five, when the window had gone grey and the glass room below had begun to pale the way a lamp pales when the day comes up round it, Imani got up and put the rug back on the old man's chair and smoothed it, and went down the forty steps; and they went after her and stood at the box's outer door while she went in on the hour and bent over his left arm.

She was in a long time. Long enough that Ferra put her hand on Emeric's sleeve.

When she came out she sat on the stool and did not write.

"The first one," she said. "From the thirtieth. It's brown at its edges. Going yellow. The way a bruise goes on the seventh day." She looked at the pencil. "And a line came up just past it, silver, and I watched it the whole four minutes." She wrote. "It went."

She wrote: came and went. Then she wrote it again underneath, the same two words, as if the first time might not have taken, with her initial and the hour; and put the pencil down, and put both hands over her face, and sat on the stool in the open box with her shoulders shaking and no sound at all.

Bettany, on the bench under her two coats, looked very hard at the ribs of the great dome.



It took the rest of the week, as he had said, and a little longer, as he had been afraid. The new lines came up silver and went, one in four, then one in three, then all of them by the ninth; and the dark ones turned brown and yellow and the faint green of a bruise on an apple, and sank, and were gone by the middle of the month. On the twelfth Bettany's cloth nicked his gum again, and it bled the length of a breath and stopped; and Imani, reading the night sheet at six, read that line twice and went out into the ward and stood in the frost a while with her back to everybody.

By the eighteenth the night's fall said two, and stayed at two. "Either there's less of him left to tear or more of him mended," said Ferra. "I've written both. I'll cross one out when I know which."

Two a day meant every third night for the Hod, not every second; Ned asked to have that in writing, and put Ferra's slip in his hat. That night Arthur came up to the glass instead, Bettany said, and stood an hour, and then asked her whether it was all right to say something to a man who could not hear; and she said she supposed so, and he went into the pass-box with both doors shut, so that nobody could hear him either, and said it through the inner door's glass, and came out.



On the twenty-fourth Imani thought he was dying.

She wrote it on the sheet afterward, so it was there for anybody. On her two o'clock hour she turned the lamp on his face and found it wrong. Not grey; she had been looking at grey on old faces for twenty years. His skin had lost its shine. A faint dull cloud lay over it, the way a window looks when somebody has breathed on it in a cold room and the breath has not quite dried: the face, the throat, the backs of the hands on the blanket. By the lamp she could not be sure. By the tree's light alone, she was. And his eyelids had gone grey at their rims, like a plum's skin before it is wiped.

Her note said: I thought it was the end. I went for his pulse to see how long. Sixty, in step, strong. Breath twelve, deep. Heat good. Lines coming and going on both arms, two in the hour, fewer than any night since the 26th. Hollow view, twenty minutes, all of him I could reach. Threads still working. Nothing stopped, nothing failing that I could find. I do not know what this is. It is not dying. It looks like dying. I have written that it looks like dying so whoever sees it next is not frightened, and that it is not, so whoever sees it next does not bleed the room.

She showed Emeric at six with her finger under the line, and he went in and looked. Very faint, and there: the old man's face filmed over in the violet light like a face seen through thin ice, the lids grey at the rims. He had seen it before. Then he had it: a glass tank in a classroom on the other side of everything, and a thing in it that had gone dull and milky-eyed and lain under its rock a week and would not eat, and a teacher telling the class not to worry, it was getting ready.

He came out and sat down on the paving by her stool.

"It's not dying," he said.

"I wrote that it's not dying. What is it?"

"I think it's the skin. The old one. Coming off."

She looked at him, and then at the old man through the glass; and then wrote a line under the night's last line and turned the sheet so he could read it. E. thinks, the skin. Coming off. Watch the hands.



It came off on the twenty-eighth.

It began at the face, where the cloud had been thickest: at the corner of the mouth, the corner that did what it did when he had won an argument. Bettany saw it on her noon hour and fetched Imani from her bed without asking whether she was allowed to. A fine edge, lifting, dry and pale and thin as onion skin, curled back from the lip like the corner of a page that has been damp and dried; and under it, where it had come away, something pink.

Imani went in with forceps and a bowl of warm water and a soft cloth, hour after hour. It came away like the peel off a sunburnt back, except that it came in sheets. It lifted along the jaw and the cheek and the forehead a piece at a time, thin and dry and so clear the tree's light showed through it, and she lifted it with the forceps and the damp cloth the way she would lift an old dressing off a burn that had healed under it: slowly, wet ahead of it, never pulling. There was no blood. There was no smell. Nothing raw underneath. The new skin came up pink as a child's after a bath, and by the next hour had begun to settle toward his own colour.

It went on three days. The face and neck the first. The hands the second, the backs and palms peeling off in two great clear gloves and a dozen smaller pieces, with Ward kneeling on the far side of the boards in his mask holding each cuffed hand up to the lamp for her, perfectly still, not breathing much. The scalp and arms the third, under the white hair. Imani laid every piece in a tray of damp lint on the box's shelf and wrote each one on the sheet, where from, what size, the hour; and by the thirtieth the tray held more than Emeric would have believed one old man had worn.

On the thirty-first she kept one.

Ferra had come down at nine with the night's fall, which said two. She watched Imani take a piece out of the lint and hold it to the lamp: the back of a left hand, most of it, knuckles to wrist, all in one, clear as a window, and in it still, small and brown and scattered like spilt tea, the age spots, every one. Imani laid it flat between two squares of glass, slid them into a squat jar of Ivo's with a ground stopper, pressed the stopper home, and sealed it round with wax from the shelf's candle.

"Do you mean to label it," said Ferra.

"Everything in my cabinet's labelled."

"With what."

Imani wrote on a gummed slip and stuck it to the jar's shoulder and held it up. In the hospital's plain hand: A. Back of left hand. 31 Jan, yr 13. The old skin.

"He'll want it back," said Ferra.

"He can't have it." Imani put the jar in her coat pocket. "It's a record. It's mine. He's had it on him eighty-seven years. He can manage without it."

Emeric, on the crate by the collar, laughed out loud for the first time since the twenty-sixth, and could not stop for a while; and Ferra looked at him as if he had made a sound she had been waiting some time to hear, and wrote something in her book that she did not show either of them.



He went in at midnight on the last night of January, when Imani had done her hour and sat with her head on the iron, and Ferra had gone up to bed because Imani had told her to in her ward voice; and he knelt on the roots by the old man's head as he had knelt at five on the last morning of the old year, and looked.

The cloud was gone. It was the same face, and it was not. The long nose, the hollow temples, the corner of the mouth, all the same; but the skin over them new and smooth and faintly shining in the tree's light, with colour in it. The age spots were gone from the hands on the blanket. The knuckles were still an old man's, swollen with sixty years of the pen, and the skin over them was a young man's. On the left forearm above the cuff a silver line came up, fine as a hair under wet paper, and dulled, and went.

His hair had grown past his collar, white, and the beard was coming in thick and white along the new skin of his jaw. But at the temple nearest the lamp, when Emeric bent close, there was a finger's width at the roots that was not white.

He did not touch it.

He knelt there with his sense drawn in as close and quiet as he could hold it, and felt the old man's door, still open, wider than on the first morning; and the stone, that was not a knot any longer, nor anywhere in particular, but all through him, in every road the room had cut and the thread had laid again behind it, like a map opened out on a table and smoothed flat and being read a line at a time by somebody who knew where every road on it went. And under it, steady, the heart. He could not feel it the way Imani could. He could only feel that it was going, and did not stumble.

He did not know what the old man would be when he woke, or when. He did not know whether it had worked, or what working would look like. He knew it was not dying; Imani had written that down. He knew the lines came and went.

He went back along the path to the box and sat down on the paving beside her stool with his back to the iron. She put her hand on his head without opening her eyes, and left it there; and they sat the rest of the night like that, and at every hour she got up, and went in, and looked, and came out, and wrote it down.
