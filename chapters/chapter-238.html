Then the glass ran out, and they went up the last ten feet, and put their heads up through the water into the heart.

He did not take his helmet off. Neither of them did. They had said on the jetty that they would not, whatever the flame and the bird said, and they had no flame here and no bird, and they did not need either. The air in the heart was air nobody should breathe. He knew that without the sense. He could see it.

And then the lamps began to brighten.

He had not told them to. His chest lamp, cold green behind its lens at his breastbone, and the one at his wrist, and the one at his belt; and Ferra's, all three; they had come up out of the water at the brightness they always had, a small cold glow a man could read a slate by. And standing on the step with the water running off him he watched them get brighter. Slowly, and then not slowly. Green, and then paler, and then white, the way a coal goes white when you blow on it, until they were throwing light across the floor and up the walls and into the dark overhead, a great deal further than a lamp stone had any right to throw it, and he had to put his hand over the one at his chest and look aside.

They were drinking. The little light stones in their iron cups were sitting in air thicker than the band, and drinking it the way the stones in the ship's keel drank the band, the way the tree in the glass bowl drank at the line; and burning what they drank. He had never seen a light stone do it. Nobody had ever held a light stone in air like this.

He told his own to go down, and they went down, a little, sulkily, like a fire damped. Ferra's he could not tell. She held her wrist lamp up over her head instead, and let it blaze, and by its light and his they saw where they were.

They climbed out of the water onto the steps and stood.

It was a room. A great room, round, cut out of the rock, bigger than the great hall; bigger, he thought, than any room he had ever stood in, in either of his lives. Its walls went up round them and curved in overhead into a dome. A dome of rock. And the rock of the dome was pale. He could see it in the white of the lamps, far up: that the walls were the dark stone of the ridge's heart, but that high up, where they curved in to meet, the stone changed, went pale, glassy, broken into long splinters and ridges like the bones of something enormous. The stone of the outcrops. The glass stone Kesrath cut off the ridge a splinter at a time and sold in every port on the northern coasts. It was the roof. The whole top of the dome was the underside of the outcrops, the pale stone, from inside.

In the middle of the room was the tree.

He had known it would be. He had known since the pods on the wall. He had known, if he was honest, since the tree in the glass bowl in the middle of his house began to glow along its veins at night. It did not help. He stood on the top step with the water running off his suit and looked at it, and Ferra beside him looked at it, and neither of them said anything, in the air or in his head.

It filled the room. Its trunk came up out of the floor in the middle of the round chamber as wide as a house, and went up, and up, grey, pale grey, into the lamps' white glare and past it, and spread out into a crown that reached nearly to the walls on every side and nearly to the pale roof; so that the whole of the great room was the inside of the tree's crown, and they were standing under it the way you stand under an oak in a field. Its bark was the stone's lattice. He could see it from where he stood: the fine net of crossing lines, all over the grey, the net on the broken face of his mother's stone, on the stem of the thing in the glass bowl, on the painted trunk in the hall upstairs; but here it was on a trunk as wide as a house, and each line of it was as thick as his finger.

And the trunk had faces.

He went down the steps toward it, across the floor, slowly, and it came clearer as he went. It was not round. It came up out of the floor in flat faces, six of them, each as wide as a door, meeting at clean edges, as if it had been cut; as if a round tree had been set in a six-sided mould and grown to fill it. It had not been cut. There were no tool marks anywhere. It had grown that way. Six faces, six edges, going up out of the floor into the crown.

He thought of Armand's drawing. The long room, the autumn the seed came home, the week after the inventory: a cutting in the north of Solreth, drawn from below, layers of rock like the leaves of a book, and in one of the layers a great many columns of stone standing up side by side like the pipes of an organ, and every top of them six-sided. The rock does it, Armand had said. It cracks as it cools. And something at the back of Emeric's head had turned over once, looking at those tops, and lain down again, and he had let it lie.

It turned over now, and did not lie down.

He thought it. He did not say it. He did not say anything.

The tree was dead.

He knew it before he touched it. He knew it the way a man knows a house is empty before he has called out. There were no leaves in the crown. The branches went out overhead grey and bare in the lamps' glare, and on the floor under them, all over the floor, drifted against the foot of the trunk and against the walls, dry, there were leaves. Long narrow dark leaves, pointed, with a fine pale vein down the middle, curled and brittle, lying in drifts ankle deep. He had twelve like them growing on the thing in the glass bowl at home, and three burned ones.

He put his gloved hand flat on the trunk. It was cold. It was hard, like old wood, and dry, and it did not give. But it had not rotted. It had not cracked. The bark was whole under his palm, the lattice sharp. Whatever had killed it had not killed it long ago. Not a thousand years. Not a hundred. He did not know how long a thing like this took to die, and he did not know how long it took to rot, in air like this, in a room like this. But the leaves on the floor were still whole. A leaf does not stay whole on a floor for a hundred years.

Dead, Ferra said, in his head. One word. Faint, rough, like a voice through a wall.

Not long, he said back, and did not know if she heard.

There were rooms in the walls. Cut into the rock of the chamber's sides, round the whole circle of it at the floor's level, a ring of tall narrow doorways like the ones in the city above; and behind them, low rooms, deep, with bins cut in their floors and walls. Stone bins, square, rows of them, the size of a manger, each with a lip.

He went into the first. Ferra went into the next. They went round the ring of them, one after another, with their lamps, and in every bin in every room there was the same thing.

Husks.

Grey. Split, each of them, into two halves, lying cupped and empty in the bottom of the bins like the half-shells of walnuts, like the two grey halves still held out on either side of the stem in the glass bowl at home. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Every one split, and every one empty.

And swept. That was the thing he stood and looked at, in the fourth room, with his lamp held over the bin. The husks were in the bins, and only in the bins. The floors of the rooms were bare. Clean. Swept, the way the rooms upstairs were swept, the way a house is swept by people who are leaving it on purpose and mean to leave it clean. Not a husk on the floor. Not a seed.

Not one seed. He looked. He went through the bins in the first room with his hands, carefully, through the dry grey shells, down to the stone at the bottom of each, and he knew the weight of a seed, a live one, a dead one, he had held enough of both; and there was nothing in any of them but husks.

Nobody leaves a harvest like that. Nobody sweeps a granary clean and leaves it full of husks and takes the grain unless they are taking it somewhere.

They took the seeds with them.

Took them, said Ferra, from the doorway of the room, faint. She had come to find him. She had a husk in her gloved hand, a whole one, two halves still together at the hinge, with the lattice fine and sharp on it. All of them.

The walls of the chamber were painted, between the doorways, all the way round. The same hand, or the same school of hands, as the great hall above; the same faded reds and ochres and the blue that had been a summer noon. But here there was no crowd and no beast. Here there was only the tree, over and over, panel after panel round the circle between the doors, and the tall ones and the broad ones round it, tending it, through the year: budding, and in leaf, and in pod, and the pods splitting, and the seeds being gathered, and carried, and put into the bins, and the husks. Panel after panel. The same tree. The same people. Round and round the room, through the seasons, again and again, as if the painter had wanted to show that it went round forever.

He went round with his lamp. He did not know why. He could see well enough; Ferra's wrist lamp was still blazing white over her head, and his own, damped, showed a panel at a time. But he went round, panel by panel, and Ferra went with him, and they came, at last, to the panel beside the steps where they had come up out of the water.

It was not painted.

It was drawn. The same tree, the same people round it, the same shapes; drawn on the plaster in thin dark lines, in outline, carefully, every figure, every branch, every pod, the way a painter draws a thing out on a wall before he fills it with colour. And then not filled. The outlines stood on the bare pale plaster with nothing inside them. A tree with no grey in it. Tall ones with no colour on their faces. A broad one at the roots with his hand raised, holding a tool that was only a line.

Somebody had drawn this, and stopped, and put down his brush, and not come back.

Stopped, said Ferra. Why.

He did not answer. He was looking up.

He had not looked at the roof properly, coming in. He had looked at the tree. He looked now, standing by the unfinished panel with his head back; and in the white of Ferra's lamp, held up as high as her arm would go, he saw it, going across the pale glassy stone of the dome from one side almost to the other, high up, over the tree's bare crown.

A crack.

A black line across the pale. Ragged. Wide, in the middle, as wide as a man was long, and narrowing at both ends to nothing. Not old. The edges of it were sharp. The pale stone round it was not weathered or stained the way the rest of the roof was, but raw, bright, the way the splinters had been in Zale's pouch on the long-room table: broken off something, not long ago.

And under it, on the floor of the chamber, on the far side of the tree, a slope of rubble. Pale stone, broken, in a fan, spilled down across the drifts of dead leaves and over the edge of the ring of doorways and into one of them.

He went round the tree to it. Ferra came with him. He crouched at the foot of the fan with his lamp.

It was the glass stone. The outcrop stone, the same stone as the roof, in broken lumps and splinters. And in among it, lying on the rubble, the thing he had half known he would find since he stood on the lowest bench's spoil in the winter and felt the space under his feet: a pick head. Iron, rusted, with the stub of its haft still in the eye, broken off short. He turned it over with his glove. On the stub of the haft, burned into the wood, small, was a mark. A tower, and a line under it.

The mark on the barrel heads in the hut on the ridge. The mark of whoever paid for the Kesrath boats.

He crouched there with it in his hand.

They had been cutting the ridge for twenty years. Down, bench by bench, taking the glass stone off it a splinter at a time. And the glass stone was the roof. They had been cutting the roof of this room, from above, without knowing it was a roof, for twenty years, going down; and one day, on the lowest bench, a man had put his pick into the stone, and it had gone through.

And the room had breathed out.

He could see it. He did not want to and he could see it. All of this: the medium packed into this room under its pale roof, thicker than the band, held there for longer than anyone could count, the way a flask holds it under iron, the way the dome held it under glass. And a pick through the roof. And the whole of it coming up through the hole at once, into the air, into the faces of the men on the lowest bench, the way the dome's charge had come out through the crack in the far panel on the night of the quarter day, burning; three leaves on the tree at home had gone bronze at their edges in a single hour at a fraction of this. Men on the bench. Their lanterns. The thing that came up out of the stone at them. Men who went out and did not come back. A place nobody would go down to after dark. If I came up the benches again I'd go into the hole with the others.

Ashendeep. A name given to a place by people who had worked in it, and burned in it, and gone down into it.

The sacrifice ground.

And then the room had gone on breathing out. Slowly, after the first rush, for years, up through the crack, the way the dome would have gone down into ordinary air in a night if the far panel had not been closed. And the tree in the middle of it, that needed air like the band held round it, thick, to live, had sat in the thinning air under its cracked roof, and drunk what was left, and starved. Above the crack, he supposed, was the lowest bench, and on the lowest bench the spoil that Kesrath threw down from the benches above and never came down to clear; they had choked the hole without knowing it was a hole, and it had leaked slower for that. Not slow enough.

Not long ago. Not a thousand years. Twenty years. Less. The leaves on the floor were still whole.

Bram had said it at Grayrock, of the old workings: that there are two airs in a deep place, and the one that sits on the floor is the one that kills you. And Emeric had said, or near enough, in the dome's first summer, standing at the gauge with three burned leaves on the tree behind him, that a thing that needed the band needed it held; that the dome was not a greenhouse but a flask; that a pocket was only a pocket for as long as its skin was whole.

They had both been right. It was not a comfort.

The crack, said Ferra. Very faint now. Almost gone. She was standing beside him looking up at it, and then down at the pick head in his hand, and he saw her see the mark on the haft, and see it. Them.

He nodded. He did not try to say anything back.

They took what they could carry and nothing they could not.

A husk, whole, the two halves still hinged, the lattice sharp on it: Ferra's, from the bin, wrapped in a square of oilcloth and put in the bag at her hip. A piece of the tree: he cut it with his knife from the lowest face of the trunk, at the foot, where it met the floor, a sliver the length of his hand, and it cut like old hard wood, cleanly, with a smell under it that came even through the helmet's seal, faint, green, and gone. No seeds. There were no seeds. He had gone through every bin in four rooms with his hands and he knew there were none, and he did not go through the rest, because he knew.

And Ferra's bottle.

She had brought it without telling him. He did not know until she took it out of the bag at her hip: a bottle of the glassworks' clear glass, small, the size of a man's hand, stoppered with a plug of black horn in a collar of rubber, the way Bram stoppered the vials. She held it up in her lamp's glare where he could see it, and he understood.

Glass holds. She had written that in the book the day the flask held in the iron room and the tear held in the glassworks, and she had written it again under the dome's panes. Glass holds the medium the way iron does. A bottle of glass, opened in this room and closed in it, would hold what was in this room. The old air. Whatever was left of it, after twenty years of breathing out through a crack. A sample. A reading Armand could put beside his dome readings and his band readings, with a number on it.

She drew the stopper. She held the open bottle up in the middle of the chamber, under the tree, at arm's length, for a slow count; he watched her lips move inside her glass, counting. Then she put the stopper back, hard, with her thumb, and turned it, and it seated, and she held the bottle up to her lamp and looked at it. There was nothing in it that anybody could see. It was a small glass bottle full of air.

Her wrist lamp, a hand from it, was still burning white.

Home, she said, in his head. It was the last clear word he had from her in that room.

They went down the steps into the water with the pick head and the sliver and the husk and the bottle, and the weight of the medium leaning on them all the way down, and eased, as the water closed over them, a little; and more at the foot of the stair; and at the bottom of it, in the level passage, it was only a pressure behind him, fading, like the sound of a crowd in a hall you have left.

Emeric. Ferra's voice, clear again. All of it. Emeric, can you hear me.

I can hear you.

Good. A long pause, on the line, in the black, going home. I didn't like not hearing you.

No.

I'm writing that down, she said. When we're up.

They went back along the level a hundred and forty paces, past the empty clip where the stage bottle had hung, and up the shaft on the knots, never faster than the smallest bubble, and stopped at ten feet, and turned the glass, and waited it out, all of it, and then half of it again, as he had for Zale. Above them, as they waited, the line in his hand gave a long slow draw and a long slow give: we have you. Not Wes's hand, this time, and not Sir Gavin's. He knew the hand. It had been at the other end of a line in the dark for most of the night, on the rim, waiting, with lye in its throat.

He answered it. One. All well.

They came up into the cold room at the shaft's head. Zale was on the rim, on his knees, with the line in both hands and his helmet in his lap, and he did not say anything, because it would have hurt, and because there was nothing to say. He looked at his brother come up out of the water. And then at Ferra. And then at what was in Emeric's hand: the pick head, rusted, with the stub of its haft, and the tower and the line under it burned into the wood.

He looked at that for a long time. Then he looked up at his brother's face through the glass, and his own face asked the question it could not say.

"The tree's dead," Emeric said, when his helmet was off and he had breath. "Not long. They broke the roof. They didn't know it was a roof." He put the pick head down on the stone of the rim, beside his brother's knee. "It's the sacrifice ground, Zale. It's where it comes from. The name. They cut down into it from above for twenty years, and one day they cut through, and it killed the men on the bench, and they called it that. And then it went on killing the tree, slowly, for years, and nobody knew." He sat down on the rim, beside his brother, with the water running off both of them. "There's nothing left down there. They took the seeds. The ones before. When they went. They swept the room and took every seed and went, and left the tree, and nobody's been in since but us."

Zale looked at the pick head for a long time.

"Took them where," he whispered.

"I don't know."

Zale nodded, slowly. He put out his hand and laid it on the rusted iron, flat, the way he had laid it on the side of the ship under her stern on the quay at home, the way a man lays his hand on a horse.

"I'll find out," he whispered. "One day." He looked at the black water in the shaft. "Somebody always goes somewhere a letter can get from."
