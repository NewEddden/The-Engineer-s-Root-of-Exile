Chapter 238 - In the Water

"We said we'd not do anything about it till we knew," said Ferra.

She said it to her book. It was open on the counting room table in front of her, not at the page for the day, but at a page more than a year back, which she had found without looking for it, the way she found anything in her own books; and Emeric, standing at the end of the table with the plant's last figures in his hand, did not need to read it upside down to know what it said. He had watched her write it, at the long table the autumn before, on a wet night after a burial, with Armand's columns spread between them: two towns on one hill, drinking from different places, and four springs of figures that would not lie down.

E. asked, gift or leash. Said he doesn't know.

She had not written the rest. She had said it, closing the book: don't do anything about it till we do. She had not needed to write it. Neither of them had forgotten it for a day.

"The tank's ready," he said.

"I know the tank's ready. I priced the tank." She did not look up. "The tank's been ready since the plant filled its third rack. You've been waiting for me to say something for a week. I've been waiting for me to say something for a week." She put her finger on the line. "We still don't know."

"No."

"Then we said we'd not do it."

"We did."

Ferra sat and looked at the line for a while. Outside the counting room window the lamps of the east half were coming on along the river in the dusk, every one at once, without a man on a ladder; she did not look at them.

"It was two questions," she said at last. "I wrote it as one. It was two." She turned her pencil in her fingers. "Does a town that's grown on the dose need the dose. That's one. And whoever holds the stones holds the town. That's the other." She looked up at him. "The second one I can't answer and nor can you. It's you. It'll be you until it's somebody else, and then it'll be them. I'll not get an answer to that one this side of my grave." She put the pencil down. "But the first one's a figure. Somebody's got it. It's in Armand's box."



Armand did not have it. Ottoline did, though she did not know it until Ferra asked her the right way.

They sat in the census hall the next afternoon, the three of them and Armand, at Ottoline's long table under the window with the tabulator under its cloth against the wall and the five years of Armand's study in their stacks of cards in their tin boxes, which Ottoline had kept after the June result because she kept everything she had ever punched; and Ferra put the question to her in plain words.

"Men who carried stones and stopped," she said. "Who left the still crew, or the stone shed, or the foundry's stock floor, for some other work. A boat, a farm, the school, the plant floor at Milford. Who'd been read while they carried and then read again after they'd stopped." She put her hand flat on the table. "Did they keep it?"

Ottoline looked at her over her magnifying glass.

"Did they keep what they'd gained," she said. "After."

"After."

"Nobody asked that." Ottoline turned to Armand. "Did anybody ask that?"

"I didn't," said Armand. "I was asking whether they'd gained at all. I was asking whether I was wrong." He looked at the tin boxes. "It didn't occur to me to ask what happened when they stopped. I was busy being wrong."

Ottoline took the cloth off the tabulator.

It took her the rest of the afternoon, with a clerk to read her the trade column off the cards and Ferra checking every name against the works' own rolls, where every man who had ever left the stone shed was written down with the day he left and where he went. There were not many. Men did not often leave work that paid well and did not hurt; but some had. Seventeen. Two who had gone to the ships. Five to Milford with the farm works. Three to the school as teachers. One who had married a woman in Grayrock and gone to keep her father's shop. The rest scattered.

Seventeen men who had carried charged stones in their bare hands for two years, or three, or four, and then put them down and gone to do something else; and Armand had read every one of them every spring since, because he read everybody every spring who would let him, whether they still carried stones or not.

Ottoline laid their cards out on the table in seventeen short rows, each row a man's springs in order, left to right.

Armand put his spectacles on and leaned over them, and Emeric watched his face, and did not look at the cards, because they were Armand's.

"They kept it," said Armand.

He said it quite flatly. He put one finger on the nearest row.

"This man. Three years in the stone shed. Up by a third in those three years. Left for Milford. Read the spring after, the spring after that." He moved his finger along. "Down a hair. A hair. Not back to where he started. Nowhere near. Where he got to, near enough, and stayed." He moved to the next row. "This one, the same. And this." He straightened up and took his spectacles off. "Capacity is grown. I said it at the long table in June; it grows like a smith's arm. I didn't say the rest because I didn't know it. A smith who stops smithing keeps his arm a good while, and loses it slowly. This keeps better than an arm." He looked at the seventeen rows. "Whatever it was they'd grown into, they stayed grown into it. It doesn't drain away when the stones go. It's theirs."

Ferra had not said anything. She was looking at the cards with her hand flat on the table beside them, not touching.

"So it doesn't need the dose," she said. "To keep what it's got."

"It doesn't need the dose to keep what it's got. It needs the dose to get more." Armand put his spectacles in his pocket. "That's not a leash, Chancellor. That's a school. A child who stops going to school doesn't forget how to read."

Ferra sat a long breath longer with her hand by the cards. Then she took it away and opened her book and wrote, small, under the date: Seventeen. Kept it. A.'s reading, O.'s cards. And then nothing else.

"Half," she said, closing the book. "That's half."

"It's the half we could get," said Emeric.

"It's the half we could get," agreed Ferra. She stood up and put her book under her arm. "The other half's you. I'll keep watching it."



The analytical balance, which was what Emeric called it and nobody else would for a week, came out of the cellar on the Wednesday, and Imani did not trust it either.

It was not a large thing. A beam the length of a man's forearm, of a pale hard steel the forge had made, balanced on a knife edge so fine and so hard that it would not wear in a hundred years, with two shallow brass pans hung from smaller knife edges at its ends; and a needle down the middle of the beam over a small ivory scale; and the whole of it in a glass case on brass feet with a door in the front, so that a breath would not move it. The forge had made the beam and the three edges in one night in October, between the runs on the gift list that Emeric was not telling anybody about. Ottoline had made the rest, the case and the feet and the screws to level it, and had made the weights.

The weights were the thing. They sat in a velvet tray beside the case, in rows, each in its own small hollow: brass ones, polished, from the largest the size of a thimble to the smallest the size of a grain of barley; and below them, in their own hollows, thin squares of silver, cut from foil, so small that Ottoline handled them with ivory tweezers and did not breathe on them. Every one of them had been checked against the master set in the standards room, the set the forge had made in the first year of the forge and that every scale in the four baronies had been tested against since. A tenth of the smallest master. A hundredth. A thousandth.

Imani looked at the smallest square in its hollow with her face very close to the tray and said, "That's nothing."

"It's a thousandth of the smallest weight in the standards room," said Ottoline.

"I can't see it."

"The balance can," said Ottoline, and opened the case's door with one finger and put a hair from her own head on the left-hand pan, and closed the door, and stood back.

The needle moved. Not much. It went from the middle of the ivory scale a little to the left, slowly, and stopped, and stayed.

Imani looked at it for a long breath.

"Again," she said.

Ottoline took the hair off. The needle came back to the middle. She put it on. The needle went to the left.

Imani straightened up.

"What do I do with it?" she said.

That was Ivo's part. He had come down from his glass house that morning with a wooden box, and he opened it now on the end of the dispensary bench: long glass tubes, thin as a pencil and as long as a forearm, open at the top and drawn at the bottom to a fine tip with a small glass tap in it; and up the side of each tube, from top to bottom, a row of fine lines etched into the glass, close as the lines on a rule, with a figure at every tenth.

"Burettes," said Emeric.

"Glass with lines on," said Ivo, who did not care what anything was called. "Every line the same as the next. That's the trouble with lines on glass. Anybody can draw lines on a tube. Getting them the same is the trick." He took one of the burettes out and held it up to the window. "I made the marks by weighing water. Fill it to the top. Let a little out into a cup. Weigh the cup on that." He nodded at the balance. "Mark where the water stands. Let a little more out. Weigh it. Mark it. Three days." He put the burette in the stand Ottoline had made for it. "If your water's as heavy as it was yesterday, which it is, every line's worth the same."

Imani looked at the burette. Then at the balance. Then at the weights in their tray.

Then she went to her cupboard and took out the bottle she used every morning for the chlorine in the main, a brown bottle with a starch-and-something in it that went blue when the water had enough in it and stayed clear when it had not, and that she had been using since the spring by the drop from a dropper and her own eye; and she sat down at the bench in front of the burettes and the balance and said nothing at all for the rest of the morning.

By noon her chlorine was a number.

Not enough or too much, as it had been every morning since April when she leaned over the jar and looked at the blue; a number, so many lines of Ivo's burette to turn a measured cup of the main's water blue, written in her book against the date. And beside it, by the afternoon, another number: what the dosing pot at the aqueduct head put into the main every hour, weighed on Ottoline's balance and divided by Linus's figure for what the main carried, which she had to ask Ferra to do because the dividing went into places she did not trust herself in; and the two numbers agreed, near enough, and she looked at them on the page for a while.

"It's been right," she said. "Since April. By eye."

"You've a good eye."

"I've a good eye," said Imani, "and I'll never use it for that again." She closed the book. "An eye can be tired. That can't." She looked at the balance in its case. "What else can it weigh?"

"Anything you can put on the pan."

"And what you can't put on the pan?" She looked at him. "What goes into the towers. That's not a pan of salt."

"No," said Emeric. "That goes in a burette."



Ottoline's other machine finished on the Friday.

It had been running since the harvest, between Armand's cards: the ration count. Every household in the four baronies had a card now, a stiff card with a number on it and a row of names, that the household's eldest brought to the granary at the bay or Grayrock or Milford or the Wayford store once a month and had punched, and that came back to the census hall on the post rider in a sack every week to be read through the tabulator; and because the cards counted mouths and not taxpayers or readers, every name was on them. Every child. Every grandmother in the back room who had never been to a market in her life. Every new family that had come down the road from the north since the war and been given a terrace room and a card in the same afternoon.

Ferra brought the figure up the keep stair herself. She did not send a clerk.

"Thirty-one thousand, one hundred and eighty-six," she said, and put the sheet on his table. "The four baronies. Everybody. Not the readers, not the hearths. Mouths."

He looked at it.

"Inside the walls, five thousand three hundred and forty-two." She put her finger on the line. "The dock quarter, one thousand one hundred and twenty-four. The rest of the Kaldo barony, seven thousand and some. And the other three." She took her finger away. "When I counted the town the first time, with a clerk who couldn't spell and a dog that followed me, I got two thousand eight hundred and seventy-six inside the walls. Nine years." She looked at the figure. "Near enough double."

"You've counted it."

"I've counted it twice. Ottoline counted it twice. The machine counted it four times, and we found a sack of Milford cards that the rider left at Grayrock and counted it again." She sat down across from him. "And I've found where the plan went wrong."

He waited. It was not his plan. It had been hers from the first column; he had given her the granaries and the twentieth rule and the cards, and the rest she had built in a year while he was in the north and the capital and four miles up.

"Grayrock," said Ferra. "The second granary at the pithead. The floor's wet. The masons laid it on the old spoil heap because it was flat, and the spoil heap drains to it, and nobody told them the spoil heap drained to it because nobody who knew was asked." Her voice did not change. "A bay of oats has gone to mould. Three hundred bushels. And Wayford's short, because Wayford's card count came in a month late, from the Lisle road, and the store was filled on last year's figure, and there are four hundred more mouths in Wayford than there were last year." She put a second sheet beside the first. "So Grayrock has oats rotting on a wet floor and Wayford has families on half a ration in the third week of the month. Both on my book."

"What do you want to do?"

"I've done it." She tapped the second sheet. "The mouldy bay's gone to the pig yards at Milford, which is what it's good for, and the rest of that granary's moved to the dry one on the hill, and Bryda has a mason on the floor with a drain under it by midwinter, and he's to ask the old miners where the water goes before he digs anything, and I've written that into the rule for every granary we build from now. The Wayford store's had two carts from the bay since Tuesday on the train to the railhead and the road after." She looked at him. "And the cards come in by the week from every store now, not the month, and if a store's card count is a week late, Ottoline's machine says so in red and I send a rider."

"You didn't ask me."

"You were in a cellar weighing a hair," said Ferra. "And it's not yours. It's the Commonweal's, and the Commonweal's mine." She stood up. "It went wrong. It'll go wrong again. When it does, it'll go wrong on my book, and I'll mend it on my book, and you'll hear about it at the table on a Monday with the rest." She picked up the figure sheet, and then, after a breath, put it down again in front of him and left it. "That one's yours. Keep it. You've never once known how many people you were feeding."

She went to the door. At the door she stopped with her hand on it, and he thought she was going to say something else about Wayford, and she did not; she stood there with her book under her arm and the census hall's cards in her other hand, a small bundle of them tied with tape, the table's own, the long room's people, that Ottoline had sorted out of the town's for her because Ferra liked to check her own people's names by hand; and she was looking at the top card of the bundle, with an expression he could not read.

"What is it?" he said.

"Nothing," said Ferra. "Arithmetic." She put the bundle under her arm with the book. "I'll tell you when I've finished it."



The tower on the hill had been dosing the upper town since the fourth year.

Nobody but the long table knew it. It had been the town's first water tower, and Emeric had put charged stones under the pan where its water was boiled before it went into the tank, because water boiled beside a charged stone kept a little of what the stone gave, and he had wanted to know whether it did anything. It had. Armand's study had read the upper town, which drank from that tower, a shade higher than the lower town every spring since its first sheet, and that shade was how this had begun. The lower town had never had it. The main had come in the spring and fed every tap and standpipe in the lower town through one pipe instead of six wells; but the main ran past the hill tower, not through its pan, and the lower town drank the Mere as the Mere came.

That was what the Saturday changed. Bram had spent October moving the main's riser so that it came up through the floor of the hill tower's base and through a new boiler there and out again to the tank above it and the town below; so that every drop of water any house in Kaldo drew would pass through one pan on its way. And the dose did not thin. Imani had tested it all through the autumn, a jar from the hill tower's tap and a jar a thousand times diluted in the main and a jar boiled again in her own kettle, and Armand had read all three, and they read the same, and she had refused to believe it for a month. A drop of it in the boiler would be a drop in every kettle in the town.

The stones under the pan came out on the Saturday.

In their place, beside the new boiler in the base of the hill tower, where the main came up through the floor and went into the boiler and out again to the tank above, Bram set a canister of the plant's, double-cased, in an iron rack with a slot down its front, on a coupling with two feed tubes; and between the canister and the boiler, in a frame of its own, one of Ivo's burettes, filled from the canister's draw port through a glass tap, with its fine lines down its side and its own glass tap at the foot.

It was a very small thing to look at. A man coming up the tower's stair to check the boiler would see a pipe, a boiler, an iron box on the wall with a slot in it, and a thin glass tube with lines on it in a stand. He would not see anything else unless he put his eye to the slot, and the tower's man had sworn the oath three years ago and had been told he would never be asked to do that.

Imani was in the tower before anybody else.

She had her register. Not her book; the big register from the ward, the one that lived on its own stand in the front room and had every sick day of every soul in Kaldo in it that anybody had brought to her door since the first summer, the coughs and the fevers and the cuts and the births and the deaths, in columns. She had carried it up the tower stair under her arm, which nobody had seen her do before, because it weighed as much as a small child and never left its stand.

She put it on the tower's bench under the window and opened it at a clean page, near the back, where the columns ran on blank to the end of the book. She ruled a heading across the top in her hard upright hand, and dated it, and wrote under the heading the first column's name, and the second's, and the third's; the same columns as every other page in the register, sick days and cuts and coughs and births, so that this page could be laid beside every page before it and read against them. Under the date she wrote nothing at all. There was nothing to write yet. That was the point of writing the date first.

Then she looked up at him.

"Before," she said. "So nobody can say after that I started counting when I knew what I'd find."

"Nobody would say that."

"Somebody will," said Imani. "In ten years. In the capital. When a town that's never had a fever in a winter is standing in front of a court that wants to know why." She put her pencil down on the register's gutter. "They'll have my page. And the date on it will be today's, and the first line under it will be empty, and that's the only thing I'll be able to say for us that they can't argue with."

Ferra came up the stair after her with the book.

She did not say anything. She stood at the top of the stair with the book under her arm and looked at the canister on the wall, and the burette in its stand, and Imani's register open on the bench at its clean page with the date on it and nothing under the date; and then she went and stood by the window, out of the way, as she had stood out of the way in the cellar on the night the bank came out.

Bram had coupled the burette's tap to the boiler's feed. He looked at Emeric.

"How much?" said Bram.

Imani answered. She had the number. She had worked it out at the dispensary bench on the Thursday and the Friday with the balance and the burettes and the plant's figure for what was in a canister and Linus's figure for what the main carried, and Ferra had done the dividing; and she said it, a number of lines on Ivo's glass a day, plainly, as she would have said a dose of foxglove for a man of a certain weight. It was very small.

"That's all?" said Bram.

"That's all. It doesn't thin." Imani did not look away from the burette. "A drop of it in the boiler is a drop of it in every kettle in the town. I'll not put in a hair more than the stones gave, the first month. Then a hair. Then I'll read the register." She put her hand flat on the bench beside the page. "Slowly. It's slow or it's the rite."

Emeric opened the burette's tap.

It ran. A thread of pale blue down the inside of Ivo's glass, faint in the grey light from the tower's window, past one fine line and the next, and the next; and at the line Imani had said he closed it. It was a very small amount. It went down the glass tube into the boiler's feed, and into the boiler, and the boiler gave it to the water, and the water went up the pipe into the tank at the top of the tower, and out of the tank into the main, and the main went down under the inner ward and out under every street in the town, to every tap and every standpipe and every kettle, to the lower ward and the terraces and the north well's lane and the river street and the hospital, to the school and the long room and Maren's kitchen; and nobody drinking from any of them would taste anything at all.

That was all it was. A thread of blue down a glass tube, past a few fine lines, and a man closing a tap.

He felt it go. Not much; the canister was behind iron and the boiler was boiler-iron and the main was iron, and the thread in the glass was the only part of it he could feel without reaching. But he felt it go out of the glass into the boiler, and he followed it as far as he could, into the pipe and up to the tank and out, until it thinned past where his sense would follow; until it was in the town.

Imani wrote the time beside the date. Nothing else.

Ferra, at the window, had her book open.

He did not go and look. He did not need to. She had turned it to the page more than a year back that she had found without looking for it on the Monday, and the line in her hand: E. asked, gift or leash. Said he doesn't know. And under it the half an answer she had written in the census hall on the Tuesday, small: Seventeen. Kept it.

She wrote the day's date under that. Only the date. No word with it.

He saw her do it from across the tower. She saw him see it. She did not say anything and did not close the book, and her face did not do anything at all; and then she looked out of the window at the town, at the roofs going down the hill in the grey to the river and the lamps along the river street, and he understood that she had written it there, under the question and not under an answer, because the question was still open and she wanted anybody who ever read the page to see that they had gone ahead with it open, and on what day, and who had let them.

It was the most honest line in the book. He did not say so. She would have entered it as flattery.



He walked down through the town that evening with Ward, because he could not have sat still in the counting room, and Ward did not ask why.

Nothing had changed. That was the thing he had known would be true and had to go and look at anyway. The lamps came on along the river street at dusk, all at once, and the lamplighter walked under them in his new coat looking up at each one in turn. The lower ward smelled of supper and wet slate. At the corner of the first lane by the old corn exchange a woman was filling a kettle at the standpipe, with a child hanging on her skirt; she filled it, and turned the tap off, and the child put its mouth to the spout to catch the last drops, and she smacked its hand away without looking, and went in. In Bram's lane the bulbs were lit behind the shutters. On the terraces, at the end house of the second row, the eight children were in the yard in the near dark, taking turns at the yard tap with a tin cup, two by two, washing their hands with a cake of the works' plain soap because Imani had told them to and they had decided it was a game.

Down at the river street the wash house across the bridge had its lamps lit and its doors open for the steam, and a line of women stood at the coppers with their sleeves pushed up, and one of them was Wenna Hallam, with her red arms in the rinse water to the elbow, talking over her shoulder to somebody about a street. Outside an alehouse on the near bank a potboy was rinsing cups in a tub filled from the new pipe, and drinking out of one of them between the rinsing, because it was there. Up on the hill the tower's boiler house showed one lit window, and the thin plume from its stack went straight up in the still air and then leaned over toward the lake; and nobody in the lower ward looked up at it, because nobody ever had.

All of them drinking it. All of them, from now. Every cup.

And none of them knew, or would, for years. That was the half Ferra could not answer and had written the date under anyway: a town growing, slowly, in its kettles, without anybody's leave, because a man in a tower had opened a tap.

He stood at the top of the lower ward's steps and looked down at the lit lanes going down to the river.

"You are thinking about the other half," said Ward.

Emeric looked at him.

"Ferra told me," said Ward. "On the stair. She said you would walk the town tonight and look at the children at the taps, and that I was to let you, and then bring you home before you started to think it was a crime." He looked down at the lanes. "It is not a crime. It is water."

"It's not only water."

"No." Ward was quiet a while. "When I was young a mage stood at the head of a stone table and put more into me in a day than a body is built to hold, and most of the men on that floor with me did not get up, and nobody had asked any of us anything but our names." He did not look round. "This is water. Slowly. In a cup. With a healer counting the days." He turned to go back up the steps. "Come home."

Emeric went home.

Fletcher had two thin strips of maple in his steambox at the shed that week, bent round a former on his bench in a shape like a figure eight with its waist pinched in, and when Cade asked him what it was for he said he had not asked and Cade had better not either.



The retrofit went to its full pace that week.

It had been a lane at a time since the summer, one crew learning on the first lane what the tenth lane would teach them in a third of the time; now there were twelve crews, from the Polytechnic's first plumbing class and Tess's linemen and the terrace gangs and every steamfitter's boy who could hold a pipe wrench, and Ferra's count on the noon tape went from two houses a week to twenty-four. The lower ward and the terraces got the whole of it: water in, the closet in the yard, the range, the wire on its knobs and the bulb on its cord. The wards the town's map would pull down in a year or three got what Imani called the floor, and would not let anybody call less: a tap in the house, a closet in the yard once the lane was sewered, a range. Nobody waited for a new house with a well at the end of the street.

Sela's was the first on the north well's lane, on the Wednesday, because Sela had asked him to come, which was how he knew she was not pleased.

The plumber was a narrow young man who had been a steamfitter's boy a year ago and still laughed like one. He had his mate on the floor under the window, packing the hole they had cut through the wall with lime and horsehair round a pipe no thicker than a thumb, grey with its zinc, and he was on his knees at the new sink, wiping the brass of the tap with his sleeve as if it were his own.

"It comes in off the main under the lane," he said, to Sela, because Sela was the one he was afraid of. "There's a cock at the kerb in an iron box with a K on the lid. If it ever runs and won't stop, Missus, you lift the lid and turn that, right round, and run for us."

"And if I can't lift the lid."

"Your boy can."

"My boy's in Ward's department." Sela looked at the sink. It was fireclay from the new kilns by the brickfield, white, with a lip on it, and it sat on two brick piers where the stone trough had been. "Who's going to lift it when he's in the yard being shouted at? Or in the armoury being measured for that suit."

The plumber looked at his mate. His mate went on packing the hole.

"I will," said Emeric.

Sela did not look at him. She looked at the tap. Then she took her bucket off its hook by the door, the oak one with the rope handle that she had carried to the north well and back every morning of her married life and every morning since, and set it in the sink under the tap, and turned the brass head, slowly, as a woman turns a key in somebody else's door.

The pipe knocked once in the wall. Then water came out of the tap, clear and cold, and struck the bottom of the bucket so loudly in the small kitchen that the plumber laughed.

Sela did not laugh. She watched the bucket fill. When it was full she turned the tap off, and it stopped, which seemed to surprise her more than the water had. She lifted the bucket out of the sink and stood holding it in both hands, as she had held it at the well, and looked about the kitchen for somewhere to carry it.

There was nowhere. The kettle was on the hearth. The trough was gone.

"It'll want a new washer in a year," said the plumber, into the quiet. "Leather. It wears. Any of us'll do it."

"I'll do it," said Sela. "Show me."

He showed her. She did it twice, with her sleeves pushed up, the second time without his hands near hers; and then she emptied the bucket into the kettle, filled the kettle at the tap until it was too heavy to lift, and poured half of it back out again. Then she took the empty bucket to the door and hung it on the hook outside, under the eaves.

"That can hold the coal," she said, and came back in and shut the door.
