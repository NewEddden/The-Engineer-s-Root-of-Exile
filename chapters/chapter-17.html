Chapter 17 - The Name

He came down the hill that morning holding the barony's accounts under one arm, and he went past the boy at the door without looking at him.

Here is how the door had come to have that boy on it.

Week three, Declan went to the well dig, because the diggers needed somebody who could read a level and there were two men in the barony who could and one of them was Declan. Emeric said so himself, out loud, in a yard, and was pleased with the arrangement.

Week six, Osric went to the shop rota, because two hundred bars a week were walking out of a back room and somebody had to count them, and Sela had asked for a man who could add.

Week eight, Rylan went out on the door to door with Imani, because the town opened for Rylan and did not open for anybody else, and every hour Rylan spent standing in a corridor was an hour of conversions not happening.

Week ten, Ward went onto Emeric permanently, which was not a decision anyone made. Ward was not going to be anywhere else and everybody in the building had worked that out by the second week.

Every substitution came from the castle's own men.

Every one of them was suggested reasonably, by somebody with a reason, at a moment when Emeric was in the middle of something else. He agreed to all four. Two of the four had been his own idea.

The one arrangement he had made on his first night in Kaldo, standing in front of a baron and saying it in two sentences without a word of accusation in them, had taken twelve weeks to take apart. It had come apart a fortnight at a time. Every step of it had his agreement on it and two of them had his signature.

The boy on the door was nineteen and had a bad shave.

The sword belt sat on him like a borrowed coat. He stood aside with the careful over-correctness of somebody who had been told exactly once how to behave in front of a prince and had been holding onto it ever since.

He had been on that door, or one like it, for something over a fortnight. Emeric could not have said how long, and could not have said which of the rotations he belonged to, and could not have produced his name under any pressure available in the world, because he had never once asked for it.

He would have said, if somebody had put it to him that morning, that the boy was one of Wexler's. He would have been guessing. He had not looked at a garrison roster since the week he arrived and the roster he had looked at then had been handed to him by the steward.

Emeric went past him into the room and did not say a word to him.

Petram did not waste any breath pretending not to know why they had come.

"You're not here to kill me. You'd have done that already."

"No."

"Then you want a name. You know I can't give you the one you actually want. I never met the man. I met the one he sent."

"Give me that one, then."

"What do I get."

"You get to still be alive in a room three months from now, which is more than the men who hired you have offered you since the fire."

He considered it with the thoroughness of a man who had already considered it several hundred times.

"Cobb."

Handed over like the last coin out of a pocket.

A courier's name. Somebody who moves between towns often enough that nobody thinks twice about him turning up in any of them, which is the entire job description of the person you would use for this.

By the door, close enough to be doing his job and not close enough to be obviously listening, the boy went very still.

Emeric caught it.

He caught it clearly, and registered it, and did absolutely nothing with it, because he was holding a name he had wanted for three months and his entire attention had gone to it the way water goes to a drain.

Petram caught it too, and the look he gave the boy was the look of a man filing a problem for later, the way a man in a locked room files everything for later.

Word came before dinner.

He had not gone to relieve the evening rotation. They found him near the mill road, close enough to town that on paper it read as exactly the sort of opportunistic violence a barony this poor produces with grim regularity.

Nothing missing that a robbery would not have taken. Nothing pointing anywhere.

Imani looked at him, because somebody had to and there was nobody else.

One strike. From behind. By a hand that knew where to put it and did not have to try twice.

She said that much and then said she was not going to say any more, because more than that was not hers to say and she had watched what happened in this town to people who guessed out loud.

Rylan had been in that room too. He worked that out standing in the yard about a minute after Emeric did, and Emeric watched him work it out, and neither of them said anything about it for three days.

When Rylan did finally say something it was not about his own door or his own house. It was to ask whether he should stop bringing Nessa's name into conversations in the keep, and Emeric said yes, and that was the end of the discussion and the beginning of a habit that never went away.

Rylan told him the rest in the yard.

Nineteen. Took the guardhouse post in the spring because it paid a half copper over the docks and because his mother had asked him to take something that was not the water. Second son.

"Sela's boy."

The name did what Rylan had known it would do.

A woman carrying water on his fourth day in this town. A nod that had brightened for a guard and died halfway when it reached the man beside him. A bucket going over the rim with every hurried step.

"That's the same woman."

"That's the same woman."

"How many did she have."

"Two."

Somewhere in the next hour he worked out, four months late, that the boy who had stood aside in a corridor that morning was the boy who had pointed him up the hill on his first day and forgotten to bow until they were past him.

Somebody had corrected him once, between the gate and the corridor.

He spent the rest of his short life getting it right.

Emeric told her himself.

Not Rylan, who offered. Not Imani, who was better at it and said so. Him, at the counter of a shop he had set up and did not own, in front of whoever happened to be in the room, because there was no version of doing it privately that was not also doing it conveniently.

She did not scream and she did not hit him.

She asked two factual questions. Where. Whether it had been fast. She got two honest answers and did not ask a third.

Then she asked him to leave her shop, and he did.

She opened the next morning at the usual hour, because there was no version of her life in which she did not, and she went on running the north well, and she never forgave him, and both of those remained true for as long as either of them lived.

Imani went and sat with her that night and did not report any part of it afterward, and when Emeric asked, once, she told him it was not a thing he had a claim on, and he accepted that and did not ask again.

The shop took less money that week than any week since it opened, because half the lower ward found reasons not to walk past a door with that woman behind it, and the following week it took more than it ever had, and Emeric never worked out what had happened in between and knew better than to go and find out.

He did not say Wexler's name.

He wanted to. He could feel the whole shape of the sentence sitting ready in his mouth, complete, with the emphasis already in the right place, and the readiness was itself the reason not to say it.

A conclusion that arrives already assembled is a conclusion somebody else built.

So he did it properly instead, standing in the dark on the stair, and did not enjoy any part of it.

Four people had been inside that room or its doorway when the name was said out loud. Himself. Rylan. Petram, who was locked in it. And the boy, who was now dead.

Three routes out, in four hours.

Somebody was listening whom he had not counted, which means the corridor is not his and has not been his for some time.

Or the boy went and told somebody himself, which a frightened nineteen year old who has just heard a name he recognizes very plausibly does, and would have done to whoever he trusted most in that building.

Or he said nothing at all to anybody, and was killed for a reaction he never spoke.

All three point at somebody with reach inside this castle. There are not many. And one of them had told him three months ago to understand why a thing happened before he decided what it was.

He wrote none of it down.

Then he noticed himself deciding not to, and sat with that, and understood that he had just started keeping a second ledger in his own head.

Which was what everybody else in this building appeared to be doing, and he had learned it in under a season.

Underneath all of it, running on its own and not asking permission, the other part sorted the loss.

What it cost, and what it proved. And the proving came out clean and horrible inside a minute.

The boy heard something worth killing him for, and was dead inside four hours. Which means the name is real. Current. Live enough that somebody with reach moved on it the same day. Worth considerably more than Emeric had let himself hope that morning.

A boy he had walked past twice a day for three weeks had confirmed his lead for him, and had paid for it, and the confirmation was going to be useful.

He took half the slope down to place the rage and it did not land on Wexler.

It landed on the man who had come down that same hill four hours earlier holding a ledger, past a nineteen year old standing at a door, and had not asked his name. Had not asked who was on the roster. Had not once in three months looked up from the numbers long enough to notice that the one arrangement he made on his first night in this place had been dismantled around him a fortnight at a time, with his own agreement, for perfectly good reasons, by people who had perfectly good reasons.

He had seen a man's fall three steps before it happened, once.

He had not seen the car.

At the gatehouse he noticed blood on his palm and could not say when he had made a fist.

Custody was restored that night and it was done properly.

Petram out of the bedroom and into the cell under the keep. Ward's rotation on it. Nobody from the castle staff within thirty feet of the corridor, and the corridor itself emptied and kept empty. All four of his own men back where they had been on the first night.

It cost the well dig two weeks and the shop its counting and Emeric did not care about either.

Bram, when he heard about the two weeks, told him he should have done it in the spring.

Which was unhelpful, and correct, and Emeric did not argue with a word of it.
