Chapter 17 - Consent

Ferra said the rest of it at seven in the morning with the book already open, which meant she had been up before him working out how to say it, and that she had decided to say it before he had eaten rather than after.

"You've made yourself the trunk," she said.

Emeric put the bread down.

"Go on."

"Two days ago I told you a tree with one trunk was a bad design and you agreed with me, and then you signed away a fifth of a harvest and wrote it down that we both knew." She turned the book round the way she did. "Last night you showed me a thing that halves the time to build anything in this barony, and the whole of it is drawn out of one man, and the man is you. Bram cost you a quarter of an hour and you couldn't stand up. I cost you an hour and you couldn't write your own name."

"That'll get better with practice."

"It might. That isn't the point and you know it isn't, so don't hand me the easy half of the argument at seven in the morning." She sat back. "If you break your leg falling off the wall, the ring still gets dug, because the ring is a spade and a slope and a hundred and forty men who know where the stakes are. If you break your leg and this is how we design things, then everything downstream of it stops until you can hold your head up. You have spent four months building a barony that works when nobody is watching. And you've just put the best tool you own inside the one place in Kaldo where nobody but you can go."

Emeric sat with that for a while. It was, he thought, the most useful thing anyone had said to him since Maren at the gate, and the two of them had said it about entirely different things and it was the same sentence underneath.

"Then it gets used for the shape and never for the decision," he said slowly. "Anything that has to be true regardless of me goes on paper, in the book, in the chandler's ledger, on a wall where men can read it. Anything that happens down there is a drawing, and a drawing gets brought out and written down or it never existed."

"Write that down."

"I'm going to."

"Now, and not tonight when you're tired and it comes out generous."

He wrote it down. She watched him do it and did not soften afterward, which he had learned to appreciate and had not yet learned to enjoy.

They went at the furnace again that morning with the book open on the bench before they started, and Bram brought his own hands to it, and inside four hours Emeric had learned three things about the place he had been going to since he was six, none of which he had ever had cause to notice alone.

The first was that the time in there did not keep faith with the time out here.

He had known that vaguely, the way you know your own house is a particular size. Now there were three of them and a book, and Ferra timed it, because Ferra timed things. A quarter of an hour on the forge shelf had been a day and a half inside. That afternoon, fifty minutes outside came to something the three of them separately guessed at four hours, five hours, and half a day, and were not able to reconcile even between the two who had been standing next to each other.

"It isn't a rate," Ferra said afterward, ruling a fresh column. "If it were a rate I could work with it. It's a different rate every time and none of us can tell from inside which one we're in."

"Then we don't rely on it."

"We can't rely on it. It's still the most valuable thing about it and I want that in the same sentence." She wrote both halves down. "It means a day of work costs an hour of daylight, sometimes, and we can never plan on which day. So it goes in the column with the weather."

The second thing was the one that frightened him properly, and it arrived because Bram burned himself on a furnace that was not hot.

They were down at the hearth level, arguing about the tap holes, and Bram put a hand out to brace against the shaft wall and pulled it back with the flat unthinking hiss of a man who has touched a hot thing, and then stood looking at his own palm with an expression Emeric did not like at all.

"That's hot," Bram said.

"It isn't. There's nothing in here. There's no fire, there's no charge, it's a shape."

"My lord, it took the skin." He turned his hand over. There was nothing on it. He looked at the nothing. "It felt like it took the skin."

Emeric put his own hand flat on the same stone and felt cool nothing, the way he always had, the way he had put his hand through the wall the night before to prove a point.

"Ferra," he said. "Come and touch this."

She came and touched it and shrugged.

"Stone," she said. "Cold. What am I looking for?"

The three of them stood there for a moment and it landed on Emeric all at once, and the landing was not pleasant.

"You've stood in front of a furnace every day for forty-one years," he said to Bram. "You have never in your life put a hand on the outside of a working shaft and not been burned by it. Your hands know it. And Ferra has never touched a hot furnace in her life."

Bram looked at the wall.

"It gave me what I know," he said.

"It gave you what you believe. Which is nearly the same thing and is not the same thing, and I need everyone in this room to hold onto the difference, because I have been coming here since I was six and I have been building machines in here for a year, and it has just occurred to me to wonder how many of them worked."

He heard his own voice do something and stopped.

Ferra was watching him with an expression that was, he thought, close to kindness, which from her was alarming.

"Say it in order," she said.

"It has no physics in it. I said that last night and I said it as a limitation, and it isn't a limitation, it's a hazard. There is nothing down here that can tell me I'm wrong. If I build a wheel in here it turns, because I expect a wheel to turn. If I build a wheel in here with the teeth cut backwards it will also turn, because I am the one holding it and I believe wheels turn." He put both hands on the shaft wall and it was cool and obedient and utterly worthless. "It is a place that agrees with me. And I've spent a year in it feeling clever."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Then that's what I'm for," Bram said.

Emeric turned.

"You asked me last night what you were for. I've had a night to be sour about it and now I've had four hours of this." The smith flexed his hand, the unburned one, the one that had told him a lie in a language he had been fluent in for four decades. "You hold a thing in here and it holds. Fine. I've forty-one years of what iron actually does and none of it agrees with anybody, least of all me. You want a man in here whose hands say no. That's not nothing. That's the whole job."

"That's the whole job," Emeric agreed.

"And her numbers."

"And her numbers, and the two of you disagreeing with me in here as loudly as you like, and nothing we build in here going near a stone of Kaldo's until it's been out on paper and argued about in daylight." He looked at the furnace, which was beautiful, and had cost nothing, and proved nothing. "I'd like that to be the second rule."

The third thing they found by accident that night, and it was the one that took something off him.

He had not intended to open it again. He had intended to sleep, having been awake for most of two days, and he had gone down under the way a tired man goes down under, and somewhere in that he had reached for the place the way he reached for it, without deciding to, the way a man's hand goes to a familiar door in the dark.

It was different from sleep.

His body was not standing in a forge with nobody in it. His body was asleep in a bed doing what a body did, and he knew that from inside, and the relief of it was so large that for a while he simply sat in the quiet noticing how much cheaper it felt.

Then Bram was there.

Emeric had not called him. He was sure of that afterward, as sure as he could be about anything from in there, and he went over it a hundred times in the following weeks and never found the moment he had done it. Bram was simply there, in a nightshirt, thoroughly asleep somewhere on the other side of town, blinking.

"Did I do that," Emeric said.

"You keep asking me that as if I'd know."

"I'm going to put us both out right now."

"Don't." Bram sat down on nothing, which he had learned to do that afternoon and was still slightly pleased with. "It's easier this way. Whatever this way is. I've not got the weight on me."

"That's because I'm asleep."

"Are you." Bram looked at him with interest. "You look like you."

And then, because it was that kind of quiet, or because Bram had been carrying it for a day and a half, or because of the thing about that place that neither of them had found out yet, he said:

"Would you have asked me?"

"I couldn't have. It happened in the same breath as the idea. There wasn't a moment in between where asking was available to me."

"That's not what I asked." Bram's face was patient and entirely without malice, which made it worse. "Say the night went the other way. Say you'd worked out you could do it and you'd gone home and thought about it, and you'd come to me in the morning with a plan. Would you have asked me first, or would you have taken me down and told me after?"

Emeric had the answer ready. He had, in fact, had it ready since the previous morning, and it was a good answer, and it was true in most of the ways that mattered, and it went: of course I would have asked you.

He opened his mouth and what came out was:

"No. I'd have taken you down first and I'd have apologised properly afterward, and I'd have meant the apology, and I'd have told myself the furnace was worth it, and it would have been worth it, and I still wouldn't have asked."

The silence after that had a shape to it.

"Christ," Emeric said, in the other language.

"What did you do?"

"I didn't do anything. That's what I'm telling you." He was standing up without having stood up. "I had a different sentence. I had a whole different sentence and it was ready and I have said the other one instead, and I have never in my life failed to say the sentence I meant to say, I am extremely good at this, it's practically the only inherited advantage I've got."

Bram considered him.

"Ask me something," he said.

"No."

"Ask me something you think I'd lie about."

"Bram, I'm not going to do that to you, I've just found out that in here I can't."

"Then I'll do it myself and you can watch." Bram put his hands on his knees. "Am I frightened of the furnace?"

He waited. Emeric watched him try, and watched it not work, and it was almost funny, because Bram had one of those faces that had never successfully concealed anything anyway and it turned out that in here it did not get the chance to try.

"Yes," Bram said. "Not of it cracking. Of it working, and me standing next to the best thing anyone in this family ever made and knowing I'd not have got there in nine lifetimes on my own, and every man in the town knowing it too." His mouth twisted. "That's not what I'd have said in the forge. In the forge I'd have said I was frightened for the lining."

"I know. You did say that."

"I know I did."

They sat with it.

"This one goes in the book," Emeric said eventually, "and it goes in in the strongest words I have. Nobody comes in here while they're asleep. Not for a good reason, not for a fast one, not because I'm tired and it's cheaper. In here a man cannot hold his own tongue, and I will not be a lord who has a room he can take his people into and ask them questions."

"You'd not do that."

"I'd not do it on purpose. I didn't do this on purpose either and you have burned your hand twice today on things that weren't there." He rubbed his face, which did nothing in here and was a habit. "I put my terms in a candle shop so nobody would have to trust me. This is the exact opposite of a candle shop. So it gets a rule, and the rule is a hard one, and the rule holds when it's inconvenient or it isn't a rule."

"Awake or nothing," Bram said.

"Awake, asked, and told what it costs before you answer. All three, every time, for everyone, forever, and if I ever come to you with a reason why not just this once, I want you to say no to me and I want you to be extremely rude about it."

"That I can do."

Emeric put them both out, and lay awake in an actual bed feeling almost well, which was itself information, and got up and wrote three rules on a sheet of paper by candlelight while they were still exact.

One. Nothing decided in there. Shapes only, brought out and written down in daylight, or they never happened.

Two. It agrees with whoever holds it. It is not a test of anything. Bram's hands and Ferra's numbers are the test.

Three. Awake, asked, and told the cost. Never from sleep. Never for a good reason.

Then he sat looking at the fourth thing, and did not write it down.

He had gone deeper, once, after Bram was out and before he woke. Not far. He had wanted to know where the floor of it was, the way he wanted to know where the floor of anything was, and he had gone down past the level where the furnace had been standing and found that it kept going, and that the going was easier the further down he went, which was wrong. Everything in his life that had ever been worth having got harder the further in you went. This had got easier, and there had been a moment, brief, entirely clear, in which he had understood that he could keep going and that going would feel very good and that there was no particular reason to stop.

He had stopped. He had come up fast enough to be embarrassed about it.

He thought about telling Ferra, who would write it down, and Bram, who would tell him not to be a fool, and he did not do either, and the reason he gave himself was that he had nothing to report except a feeling, and a feeling was not a number, and he did not want to hand two people who had just agreed to follow him into a place they could not check a piece of information that amounted to I do not entirely trust myself down there.

He noticed himself constructing that reason. He wrote that down instead, which he considered, at the time, a reasonable compromise.

The other thing, the small one, he did not write anywhere.

Somewhere near the bottom of that easy descent, for less than a breath, he had felt the way a man feels in a dark field when he becomes certain the field is not empty. Nothing had touched him. Nothing had spoken. He had simply had the unmistakable sense of having been, very briefly, noticed.

He had put it down to being tired, which he was, and to a year of doing something strange alone, which he had, and he blew the candle out and slept nine hours for the first time since the spring.
