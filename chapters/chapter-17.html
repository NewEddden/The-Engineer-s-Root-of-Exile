Chapter 17 - The Name

He came up the hill that morning with the soap yard's week of figures under one arm, and he went past the boy at the door without looking at him.

Here is how the door had come to have that boy on it.

Week three, Declan came off it, because somebody had to run the ground for the well sites before Wexler would hear a word about wells, and that wanted a man who could read a level, and there were two men in the barony who could and one of them was Declan. Emeric said so himself, out loud, in a yard, and was pleased with the arrangement.

Week six, Rylan went out on the low streets with Imani, because she had asked for him, and because the town opened for Rylan and did not open for anybody else. The women down there had known his mother.

Week eight, Osric went to the soap yard to sit beside the coin bag at the ash scales, because a bag of coppers in the lower ward wanted a man with a sword near it, and that one was Emeric's idea too.

Week ten, Ward went onto Emeric permanently, which was not a decision anyone made. Ward was not going to be anywhere else and everybody in the building had worked that out by the second week.

Every substitution came from the castle's own men. Hale filled each gap as it opened, quietly and well, and Emeric thanked him each time.

Every one of them was suggested reasonably, by somebody with a reason, at a moment when Emeric was in the middle of something else. He agreed to all four. Two of the four had been his own idea.

The one arrangement he had made on his first night in Kaldo, standing in front of a baron and saying it in three short sentences without a word of accusation in them, had taken twelve weeks to take apart. It had come apart a fortnight at a time. Every step of it had his agreement on it and two of them had his signature.

Declan still looked in most evenings on his way back from the levels, and brought the reports. Petram was eating. Petram was sleeping.

The boy on the door was nineteen and had a bad shave.

The sword belt sat on him like a borrowed coat. He stood aside with the careful over-correctness of somebody who had been told exactly once how to behave in front of a prince and had been holding onto it ever since.

He had been on that door, or one like it, for something over a fortnight. Emeric could not have said how long, and could not have said which of the rotations he belonged to, and could not have produced his name under any pressure available in the world, because he had never once asked for it.

He would have said, if somebody had put it to him that morning, that the boy was one of Wexler's. He would have been guessing. He had not looked at a garrison roster since the week he arrived, and the roster he had looked at then had been handed to him by the steward.

Emeric nodded to him, the way he always did, and went past him into the room without a word.



Since the day with Corliss he had been coming every few days simply to sit with Petram, with the door left open, because an open door was a record. It was mostly dull. Petram talked about the food, or once, for most of an hour, about horses, which it turned out he knew a great deal about. Rylan came when he could, and leaned on the wall, and said even less.

Today Petram did not talk about horses. He was sitting on the edge of the bed with his forearms on his knees, and he did not look up when they came in, and he started before Emeric had sat down.

"I never met him," he said. "The one who paid. I told you that, and it's true. I met the one he sent."

"Give me that one, then."

"What do I get."

"You get to still be alive in a room three months from now, which is more than the men who hired you have offered you since the fire."

"That's a room." Petram looked at his hands. "There'll be a magistrate, sooner or later. I want to stand up in front of him with my neck in one piece and say it myself. That's all. I want to get there."

"I can't promise you a magistrate."

"Then promise me you'll try."

Emeric thought about it for longer than Petram seemed to expect. Rylan shifted against the wall.

"I'll try," Emeric said.

Petram considered it with the thoroughness of a man who had already considered it several hundred times.

"Cobb."

Handed over like the last coin out of a pocket.

"A courier," Petram said. "Rides the south road with letters for whoever pays him. He's the one brought the purse to the stable yard."

A courier's name. Somebody who moves between towns often enough that nobody thinks twice about him turning up in any of them, which is the entire job description of the person you would use for this.

By the door, close enough to be doing his job and not close enough to be obviously listening, the boy went very still.

He had been shifting his weight from foot to foot, the way young men on a door do, and he stopped, the way a man stops when he hears his own name said in the next room. Then he went on shifting.

Emeric caught it.

He caught it clearly, and registered it, and did absolutely nothing with it, because he was holding a name he had wanted for three months and his entire attention had gone to it the way water goes to a drain.

Petram caught it too. His eyes went to the door and came back, and the look he gave afterward was the look of a man filing a problem for later, the way a man in a locked room files everything for later.

Emeric asked what Cobb looked like. Middling height, Petram said. Middling age. A horseman's legs, and a face you would forget while you were still looking at it. After a while Emeric thanked him and went out past the boy, who straightened, and Emeric nodded to him.



Word came before dinner.

He had not come back for the evening rotation. A carter found him on the mill road, where it ran along the bank below the east bridge past the wheels, close enough to town that on paper it read as exactly the sort of opportunistic violence a barony this poor produces with grim regularity. Face down in the dust. Purse cut and gone, boots gone too. Robbed, the carter said, to anyone who would listen, on a summer evening, not a mile from the gate.

Nothing missing that a robbery would not have taken. Nothing pointing anywhere.

By the time Emeric got there Imani was already kneeling in the dust, and somebody had turned the boy over, and Emeric saw the nick on the jaw and stopped where he was.

Imani looked at the boy for some time, because somebody had to and there was nobody else. When she looked up, it was at Emeric, and she said it low enough that it was only for him.

"Once. From behind." She touched the back of her own neck, one place, exactly. "Somebody who's done it before."

"Robbed?"

She looked at the bare feet in the dust. "If you like."

She said that much and then said she was not going to say any more, because more than that was not hers to say and she had watched what happened in this town to people who guessed out loud.

Ward was beside him, though he had not heard Ward arrive. Ward looked at the body, and then up the road toward the keep, and then at Emeric.

Rylan had been in that room too. He worked that out standing at the side of the mill road about a minute after Emeric did, and Emeric watched him work it out, and neither of them said a word about it.

That night Ward slept across Petram's door himself, on the floor, with his sword beside him. Nobody had asked him to.



Emeric found Hale late, in the steward's room, with one candle, doing the next day's accounts.

"The boy on the door," Emeric said. "What was his name?"

He had to ask. He heard himself asking it.

Hale put down his pen.

"Tam," he said. "Sela's boy. The middle one." He looked at the candle rather than at Emeric. "He took the post in the spring. Half a copper a day over what the docks paid. His mother wanted him off the water." A pause. "He was on the gate the day you came in, Your Highness. It was him let you through."

The name did what Hale must have known it would do.

The gate. A boy with a spear somebody had handed him and a bad shave, who had looked at Emeric's eyes and gone red to the ears, and pointed them up the hill, and forgotten to bow until they were past him.

And a woman on his third day in this town, whose face had almost smiled for Rylan and stopped when it reached the man beside him, and a bucket dropped in the lane and left where it fell.

Sela had three sons. He had known about the eldest since that day, taken by the crown for its war and never accounted for, and about the youngest since the fourth week, standing in a lane in the dark while Imani kept him alive. The middle one had been standing at Petram's door, and Emeric had nodded to him every morning.

Twelve weeks. He had walked past that face a dozen times a day for over a fortnight and never once put it with the gate.

Somebody had corrected him once, between the gate and the corridor.

He spent the rest of his short life getting it right.

"Thank you," Emeric said, and went out, and stood in the corridor for some time before he went to bed.



He told her himself.

Not Rylan, who offered. Not Imani, who was better at it and said so. Him, in the morning, at the counter of a shop that had nobody's name on it, in front of whoever happened to be in the room, which was three women and an old man with a basket, because there was no version of doing it privately that was not also doing it conveniently. It had happened in front of the town.

Sela was setting out bars in their rows when he came in. She saw his face, and stopped.

He told her plainly, in a voice the room could hear, because she would want to know it was true and that nobody had softened it.

She did not scream and she did not hit him. She listened without moving, and when he had finished she put down the bar she was holding, square with the others.

"Where."

"On the mill road. Below the bridge."

"Was it fast."

He thought of Imani's hand at the back of her own neck.

"Yes."

Two questions, two honest answers. She did not ask a third. She looked at him, at his face, and then at his eyes, the gold ones, the crown's.

"Get out of my shop," she said.

He went.

She opened the next morning at the usual hour, because there was no version of her life in which she did not. She kept the north well's fire that evening, and the evening after, and went on counting change into people's palms at two coppers a bar the way she always had.

Imani went down and sat with her each night that week and did not report any part of it afterward, and when Emeric asked, once, she told him it was not a thing he had a claim on, and he accepted that and did not ask again.

The shop took less money that week than any week since it opened, because half the lower ward found reasons not to walk past a door with that woman behind it. The following week it took more than it ever had. People bought bars they did not need and put the coppers in her hand, and some of them held on to the hand a moment longer than the coppers needed. That reached Emeric secondhand, and he knew better than to go down and see it for himself.

When he did next come in for soap, she served the man behind him first. Then she took his two coppers without looking at him, and turned to the shelf before he had picked up the bar.



Rylan kept quiet for three days.

He stood his turns at the head of the stair and carried Petram's food down, and he and Emeric passed each other in the corridors and the yard and did not say a word about the mill road.

On the third evening he stopped in Emeric's doorway on his way to bed, and when he did finally say something it was not about his own door or his own house.

"In the keep," he said. "Should I stop saying Nessa's name."

It took Emeric a moment to see what Rylan had been carrying for three days. Then he thought of Petram's fire on the fifth night, and Rylan telling a stranger his wife's name, and his children's ages, and his street.

"Yes," he said.

Rylan nodded, as if it was what he had expected, and went to bed, and that was the end of the discussion and the beginning of a habit.



"Wexler," Ward said.

It was late on the second night, and the door was shut, and Ward said it at the volume of the whisper code, which reached about as far as the bed.

"No."

"He's the man with reach in this house."

"He is." Emeric was at the desk with the notebook shut in front of him. "Which is exactly why I won't say it."

He wanted to. He could feel the whole shape of the sentence sitting ready in his mouth, complete, with the emphasis already in the right place, and the readiness was itself the reason not to say it.

He turned the shut notebook a quarter round on the desk, and back. "A conclusion that arrives already assembled is a conclusion somebody else built."

Ward studied him. Then he said, "All right," which from Ward was a great deal, and went to stand his watch.

So Emeric did it properly instead, out on the stair in the dark, and did not enjoy any part of it.

Four people had been inside that room or its doorway when the name was said out loud. Himself. Rylan. Petram, who was locked in it. And the boy, who was now dead.

Three routes out, in four hours.

Somebody was listening whom he had not counted, which means the corridor is not his and has not been his for some time.

Or the boy went and told somebody himself, which a frightened nineteen year old who has just heard a name he recognizes very plausibly does, and would have done to whoever he trusted most in that building.

Or he said nothing at all to anybody, and what he had heard did not matter, and he was killed to see what Emeric would do next.

All three point at somebody with reach inside this castle. There are not many. None of the three told him which.

He wrote none of it down.

Then he caught himself deciding not to, and sat with that, and understood that he had just started keeping a second ledger in his own head. One for what he knew. One for what he was not going to say until he did.

Which was what everybody else in this building appeared to be doing, and he had learned it in under a season.

Underneath all of it, running on its own and not asking permission, the other part sorted the loss.

What it cost, and what it proved. And the proving came out clean and horrible inside a minute.

The boy heard something worth killing him for, and was dead inside four hours. Which means the name is real. Current. Live enough that somebody with reach moved on it the same day. Worth considerably more than Emeric had let himself hope that morning.

A boy he had walked past a dozen times a day for over a fortnight had confirmed his lead for him, and had paid for it, and the confirmation was going to be useful.

He took half the slope of the inner ward to place the rage, walking down it in the dark, and it did not land on Wexler.

It landed on the man who had come up that same hill with a week of figures under his arm, past a nineteen year old standing at a door, and had not asked his name. Had not asked who was on the roster. Had sat with the door wide open, because an open door was a record, and never asked who was standing in it. Had not once in three months looked up from the numbers long enough to notice that the one arrangement he made on his first night in this place had been dismantled around him a fortnight at a time, with his own agreement, for perfectly good reasons, by people who had perfectly good reasons.

He had seen a man's fall three steps before it happened, once.

He had not seen the car.

At the gatehouse he noticed blood on his palm, four small crescents where his nails had gone in, and could not say when he had closed his hand. He went back up and washed it, in water that had been boiled, because there was no reason not to.



Custody had been restored the morning after the mill road, before he walked down to Sela's counter, and it was done properly.

Petram out of the bedroom and into the cell under the keep, a stone room with one door and one stair. Ward's rotation on it. Nobody from the castle staff within thirty feet of the stair. All four of his own men back where they had been on the first night: Declan off the levels, Osric out of the soap yard, Rylan off the low streets, and Ward over the lot of them. Hale, told this, said, "Of course, Your Highness," and arranged the kitchen so that Petram's food went down by Rylan's hand and nobody else's.

It cost the well dig two weeks and the soap yard its counting, and Emeric did not care about either. Abloh counted the coppers himself, and was slow at it, and said so.

At the end of that week Emeric went down to the forge and told Bram all of it, because Bram would hear it from somebody and he would rather it came from him.

Bram listened without stopping work. He drew a bar out of the fire, looked at the color, and put it back.

"You should have done that in the spring," he said.

Which was unhelpful, and correct, and Emeric did not argue with a word of it.
