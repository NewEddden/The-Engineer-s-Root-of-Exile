Chapter 17 - The Stale Map

They had two days, and Emeric spent the first of them doing the thing he was worst at, which was deciding who would go unprotected.

The foundry had stamped twenty-four harnesses and not one more, and there were more than twenty-four people who were going to stand on that wall. The twenty went into their black steel because the twenty were the line, and the four elites into their Enhanced suits because four terrifying men in the right place were worth more than forty ordinary ones spread thin.

That left the archers. Townsfolk who had volunteered or been volunteered to put arrows over the wall, standing behind stone in whatever they owned.

Bram argued for giving up his own harness to a bowman half his age. Emeric refused, and hated the shape of the refusal, because the arithmetic was ugly and correct. An armored smith who would not be in the melee protected no one, and a smith was harder to replace than a breastplate.

He knew, saying it, that some of the people he was sending up in cloth were going to die of the gap between how fast he had built a foundry and how fast a foundry could arm a town. He filed it where it would not slow his hands and promised himself he would take it back out when there was time to feel it.

He had Petram moved that afternoon from the lower cell to a storeroom above the waterline, on the reasoning that a man who might still be worth something to him was worth not drowning in a flooded cellar, and did not examine the decision any further than that.

Ward set the wall. One landward gate above the ford, the whole town behind a single closed line, the approach to the gate running up open graded ground that Emeric had shaped for water and Ward now read for killing.

Anything coming at the gate had to cross that ground under the wall, in the open, uphill, funneled by the two arms of the river into a front no wider than the town wanted it to be. One road in. One gate. Emeric had built the geography for a harvest and it turned out to be built for this too, which was less luck than he would have liked to claim and more the plain fact that a thing which is hard to leave is hard to enter.

Ward's plan for the gate was not to hold it. It was to let them spend themselves against the approach and the wall until the shape of their charge broke, and then to open the gate and hit what was left with something they had no map for.

He had split the twenty into four files of five, one behind each elite, and drilled the files until they moved as a single thing behind whichever man was at their head. The archers would hold the wall and keep shooting through the sortie. The gate would be open.

If the plan went wrong, the gate would be open, which was the risk, and Ward had said so plainly and Emeric had said it was still the right answer, because a gate that stays shut while the field turns is a gate that traps its own men, and neither of them could afford that.

He set himself on the gate-tower where he could see the whole approach and reach most of it, with the three deadzone stones and the one slug and a satchel of cheap riverbank water-stones at his hip, and he made Bram stay behind the parapet with the reserve, present and stubborn and out of the melee, because Bram would not leave and Emeric would not lose him.

The coalition came up the west road at midmorning of the second day, and it was worse than the number and better than the fear.

Two hundred, near enough, and only half of it meant to fight. Grayrock's men under Baron Renner made the larger part of the front, sixty in loose order. Wayford's forty under Baron Sethe filled in wide of them. Behind both, Milford's banners hung back under Paxton, a hundred more that had come the whole road and looked in no hurry to spend a single man of it.

Five riders in the grey of hedge-mages rode among them, which was the number Ward had guessed and Emeric had hoped he'd guessed high on. And scattered through the front, in harness Emeric knew because he had paid for it, rode a handful of the men he had cast out of Wexler's regiment, carrying the map that had brought all these people here.

He watched the exact moment the map failed them.

They came up expecting a plague town. A ditch and a rabble, a mad prince playing at farming, a granary worth the ride and nothing behind it worth respecting.

What they found when they crested the rise was a closed wall with steel on it, a gate that did not open in panic, an approach graded and clear with no cover on it for a hundred paces, and a stillness that armies learn to be afraid of because it means the other side has decided something.

The front rank slowed. Word went back. The lords conferred, three knots of banners drawing together and apart, and Emeric could not hear it but he could read the shape of it. This is not what we were told, and we are already here, and there are hundreds of us, and we can all count the men on that wall.

They had crossed a great deal of ground on a picture a season out of date. The sunk cost of the ride, and the plain arithmetic of hundreds against a wall that could not hold two dozen proper fighting men and some civilians, decided it. Disbelief lost to numbers. Renner's horn went up, and Grayrock came first.

"They still think it's a rabble," Ward said, low, beside him. "They're crossing in the open like the wall's for show."

"Let them keep thinking it," Emeric said, "for exactly as long as it takes them to reach the marker."

The marker was a fence post Ward had set at the range where a good bow on the wall stopped missing. The coalition reached it in a loose front, still at a walk, still certain, and the wall opened up.

Kaldo's archers were not soldiers. They were farmers and colliers and a fishwife with a heavy draw, standing behind stone, shooting downhill at men in the open with all the time in the world to pick a shaft. It did not have to be marksmanship. It had to be volume onto a lane the enemy could not leave, and the lane was the graded approach, and the enemy could not leave it.

Arrows came off the wall in a rain that thickened as the range closed, and the coalition front discovered the second thing the stale map had gotten wrong.

Their arrows, loosed back uphill, skated off Kaldo's black steel or stood quivering in it without biting. Kaldo's arrows, coming down with the slope behind them, punched through the coalition's lighter harness and found the men inside.

It was not an even trade and was never meant to be. Emeric had inverted the capital's whole logic, poured better steel around ordinary men, and it meant that on the open approach a Kaldo farmer with a bow was worth more than a Grayrock man-at-arms, because the farmer was behind a wall and the man-at-arms was in the open in bad harness.

The front rank came apart in the lane, and the ones behind climbed over them and still came, because hundreds of men who have committed to a wall do not stop for the first rank dying.

Then the mages opened up, and the day turned into the thing only Emeric could answer.

The first fireball came up off a charged crystal in a grey-robe's hand and arced for the gate-tower, and Emeric met it the way he had drilled a hundred times in an empty room, which was not with a bigger fire but with the cheapest possible answer placed exactly.

A thin sheet of water pulled off the wet ground and thrown flat across the fireball's path. Not to drown it, only to rob it of the heat it needed to hold its shape. The fireball guttered and fell short as a gout of steam that scalded no one.

He had not out-burned it. He had spent a mouthful of mana to make its whole charge worthless, and he felt the enemy mage feel it, the particular hitch of a man who has just paid full price for nothing.

That was the shape of the whole duel. They cast big, because big was what they had and what they had been taught to respect, each fireball a fat slow expensive thing off a finite charge. Emeric cast small and fast and exact, delving lending his hand a fineness on the mana while the spell was still forming, so he could shape a counter to fit the threat instead of overpowering it.

A blade of moving air to shove a cast off its line so it burst against empty ground. A slab of stone raised a hand's breadth and dropped, eating a low fireball meant to skip along the parapet. He never threw anything that would have frightened a court mage. He threw the exact small thing at the exact moment, and it kept being enough.

Two of the five went out of the fight without anyone touching them. A charged crystal holds what it holds, and a man who spends his on four big casts that each buy him nothing has an empty stone and no way to fill it standing in a field. Emeric watched one of them look at his own hand, and then at the man beside him, and then start walking his horse backward out of the front, and understood that he had just killed a mage with arithmetic.

A third lost patience. A younger grey-robe, quick and angry at being made cheap, chained a gravity-lightening onto himself and an air-shove under his feet and leapt, meaning to clear the wall and land the duel on the tower where it was personal.

It was the kind of thing raw power made possible and an engineer's eye called a mistake before the man's boots left the ground, because a mage in the air is a mage who has spent his footing and his charge and his options all at once and has nowhere to go but where physics is already taking him.

Emeric did not try to knock him down. He took the man's own arc and helped it, a shove of air at the top of the leap that turned a graceful vault into a tumble, and the grey-robe came down hard and wrong inside Kaldo's wall, alone, among the reserve, and did not get up to cast a third time.

That left their leader and one other, and Emeric watched them do the thing he had been waiting for, which was draw together to combine their casts into something he could not answer piecemeal. Two charges, braided, building toward a single strike big enough to open the gate.

"Bram," Emeric said, without looking away from them. "The stones. Now."

He had one throw and one moment, and he took them.
