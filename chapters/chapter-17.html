Chapter 17 - The Exchange

Ferra's census came back in nineteen days, about two months sooner than she'd said, and it was wrong in a way he hadn't expected.

She'd laid it out at her table in three piles. The tax roll, which the castle had been copying forward every year since before the plague. Her own count, door by door, from the parish book, the tithe lists, the garrison roll, the ash tally, Imani's register and her father's ledgers, checked each against the others until they agreed. And a third pile, thinner, that she put her hand on and didn't explain yet.

"The roll says three thousand four hundred and twelve," she said. "Inside the walls."

"And?"

"Two thousand eight hundred and seventy-six." She tapped her own pile. "The roll's been carrying the dead for four years. Nobody struck them off. Some of them are still being taxed."

He'd expected that. He'd expected the town to be smaller than it said.

He hadn't expected the third pile.

It was the skills: what people could do, whatever they did now. She'd built it from everything, from what a man's father had been in the parish book, from what a woman had been paid for in a ledger fifteen years ago, from who'd signed for what in the garrison stores. A cooper working as a porter on the docks because nobody had needed a barrel since the port died. Two men who'd been masons before the war and were now digging Doran's graves. A woman who'd kept accounts for a wool merchant and now took in washing. Carpenters. A man who'd built boats. Four households that kept bees. A whole street of people working below whatever they'd been trained for, because the work they'd been trained for had gone downriver or into the ground.

"How many can read?" Ferra said.

A town of nearly three thousand. A parish with a book. A castle with clerks. Merchants, once.

"Four hundred?" he said. "Five?"

She turned a page and turned the book round so he could see the figure.

"Ninety-three," she said. "That I can find. Some of those can sign their name and that's all. And three hundred and some who can figure past their fingers, mostly because they've had to, at a stall."

He'd been out by a factor of five. She didn't soften it.

There was one more column on her sheet. He'd asked for it: anyone who could cut a rune. It had a heading in her neat hand, and nothing under it.

"Nobody," she said, when she saw him looking. "Not one. Not in any record I've got. If there's anyone in Kaldo who can do it, they've never been paid for it here."



He walked back to the exchange that afternoon with Bram and Ferra. He hadn't been inside since his second day.

It was as he remembered it. One long room, no posts, the light coming in even and grey through the high windows. The leaves had drifted further under the open bay at the end. Somebody had been sleeping in the corner near the door, and had left, and taken their blanket.

Bram walked into the middle of the floor and stood looking up at the trusses, the way he'd looked at the furnace sheet before he'd called it a shadow.

"Who built this?" he said at last.

"I don't know. Eighty years ago, Rylan thinks."

"Nobody in Kaldo could do that now." Bram pointed up at a joint where three timbers met and were held with nothing but their own cut. "I couldn't. Nobody I know could. Whoever knew how, he never taught anybody." He kept looking. "So that's that. Gone."

Emeric's whole argument, in one beam.

Ferra was pacing the open bay with her book. She came back with a figure. One bay roofed again: lime from his own kiln, timber from the woods with the fine waived, a carpenter off her third pile who'd been mending fishing boxes on the dock for want of anything better.

"Against the shop's takings," she said. "It fits. Just."

"And the rest of it?"

"The rest of it's already right." She looked round at the floor, the windows, the light. "Somebody built it to be read in."



Abloh's mornings moved in first.

He'd been teaching soap-safety in the open end of the yard since June, on a fixed morning, to whoever came: how to make it, how to float the egg, how not to burn yourself or your children. Now he carried his bench and his pots across the town and set them up in the far bay of the exchange, under the new roof, and the first thing in Kaldo that had been taught rather than told was also the first thing with a room.

Reading and figures were to follow, on other mornings. That was the plan. The trouble was who.

Ferra wouldn't. She had the census and the barony's books and her father's forge, and she said so in a voice that closed the subject. Doran could read and had kept the only long record in the town, but Doran was still sitting in kitchens one family at a time, moving the dead, and wouldn't be done before the winter. Emeric could teach, some days, when the barony let him. Some days wasn't a school.

It was Imani who said it.

"Sela," she said.

They were standing outside the exchange. Emeric looked at her.

"She ran the north well," Imani said. "A whole street, fires and rota and wood, and never once let it go out. She runs the shop. She can get forty people to do a thing at the same time without raising her voice." She looked at the building. "You don't need someone to teach. You need someone to run the room. You can teach."

"She won't do anything I ask."

"No," Imani said. "So I'll ask."

She went the next morning. Emeric didn't go with her. She came back at noon and told him, standing in the yard of the keep, exactly what Sela had said, in Sela's order, without changing a word.

Sela would run the room. The hours were hers. The children's places were hers, who sat where and who went home. The food was hers. One of the north-well women would take the shop counter in the mornings, and Sela would take it back at noon. The prince would teach figures, and letters if he must, on the mornings Sela named, and he would come in when the children were seated and leave when she told him, and she wouldn't speak to him in that room about anything that wasn't the lesson.

"And she wants it written down," Imani said, "that it's for Arthur. Not for you."

Arthur was the youngest, the one Imani had sat three nights with in the summer.

"Write it down," Emeric said.



Bram agreed to send Tess two mornings a week.

He grumbled about it the whole time he was agreeing. Every morning Tess spent at the exchange was a morning she wasn't at the forge, and the forge was short-handed already, and who was going to fetch the charcoal, and did Emeric know what it was to train a striker properly. Emeric said he didn't, which was true, and Bram said that was obvious, and sent her anyway.

Tess was fifteen, thin, with a burn scar along one thumb that she'd had since she was eleven and had stopped noticing. She'd been in the forge since before she could lift the small hammer. She came to the exchange on the first of her mornings with charcoal still under her nails and sat at the back, by the wall, where she could see everything.

It was the second morning, in the figures lesson, that she put her hand up.

Emeric had been showing them how to measure a length and set it down as a number. He'd had them measure the table with their thumbs, joint to tip, laid end to end. Tess had done it, and then done it again, and then she'd gone across the room and measured it with Sela's thumb, Sela standing very straight at the wall and letting her, because it was the lesson.

"It's different," Tess said. "Mine says thirty-one. Hers says twenty-eight." She looked at her own thumb. "At the forge Bram's is the thumb. If I tell him a bar's thirty-one thumbs he cuts it with his, and it comes out wrong." She frowned. "Why isn't a thumb in here the same as a thumb at the forge?"

The room waited for the prince to answer.

He opened his mouth. The true answer was because nobody has ever made them the same, which was no answer at all, only the problem. It had been sitting under every drawing he'd made since July.

"I don't know yet," he said. "Write it down. We'll come back to it."

Tess wrote it down. He saw her, at the end of the morning, folding the page small and putting it inside her shirt.



The cost was hands.

He found that out in the first week, at the doors, the way he'd found out about firewood. A child in the exchange for a morning was a child not carrying water, not minding the baby, not sitting on the bank gutting fish for a copper a basket, not picking stones off a field. The households said so, to his face, politely and then less politely. They weren't wrong.

So he did what door to door had taught him. He didn't explain. He asked what the hours would have to be.

They told him. Not at dawn, when the water was carried. Not in the afternoon, when the boats came in. Mornings, late, three days in seven, and never in harvest. He let them set it, and Sela put it on the wall in chalk.

That was the hours. The money was harder.

Every child who came was fed, at the door, before the lesson: bread and something warm. That was easy to decide and Ferra priced it in an afternoon, and it came out of the shop's takings. The other part was not easy. He paid the households for the work the children weren't doing: a sum to the house, never a wage to the child, for every morning the child sat in the exchange instead of on the riverbank, so that sending a child to learn its letters didn't mean the house ate less that week.

Ferra worked that one out too. She worked it out three times, and then she showed him the last line.

"From your purse," she said. "Not the barony's. The barony hasn't got it." She turned the page. "By the end of the winter it's costing you more than your four guards."

"Then it costs that."

She wrote it down, and under it, in smaller writing, the month she thought the purse would run out. He didn't ask what month it was. He could read it upside down.

That night he wrote three rules in the back of Armand's notebook.

No child under twelve at the kiln, the tannery pits, the salt pans or the barges. No child at any work for more than half a day. No child kept from the exchange by an employer who isn't their own house.

He slept on it, and took it to Ferra the next morning.

She read it twice.

"Who checks?" she said.

He didn't know.

"And when the tanner turns a boy off, because a boy who can only work half a day isn't worth hiring, who feeds the house?" She handed it back. "You can make this law tomorrow. You can't pay for it. A law nobody can afford to keep is just a list of people you'll fine."

He folded it and put it back in the notebook. He didn't cross it out.

"When I can pay for it," he said.

"When you can pay for it," Ferra agreed.



Every child, boy or girl, got other mornings too.

That had been Emeric's one firm rule, and Sela had let it stand. A morning with Abloh in the far bay, learning soap and lye and what a burn looked like before it happened. A morning at the forge with Bram, mostly watching, mostly being told to stand back. And a morning in the yard of the keep with Ward, learning to stand, and to fall, and to get up, and to run.

Some of them took to the yard and some of them didn't. The ones who did came back on days that weren't theirs, and Ward let them.

On the third yard morning a boy of about ten, one of the dock quarter's, put up his hand in the middle of a falling drill and asked Ward whether he could be a knight.

"Yes," Ward said.

There was a pause. Then a girl, a little older, at the end of the line, asked the same thing. She asked it with her chin up and her shoulders set, ready to be laughed at.

"Yes," Ward said. Exactly the same way.

The boys laughed anyway. Ward waited for them to finish.

"One of the strongest knights I have ever seen," he said, "is a woman. Queen Rowena of Emberfell. I have been on the ground in front of her." He looked down the line. "Do any of you want to laugh at her?"

Nobody did.

Emeric, watching from the wall, noticed what Ward hadn't said. Knights were made by the rite, in a stone room with a drain, and the rite killed nine in every ten it was done to. Ward had said yes twice and meant it both times, and he hadn't told a yard full of children that. Emeric thought about whether he should have, and decided he didn't know, and let it lie.

Arthur was one of the ones who kept coming back to the yard. Every yard day, and some that weren't. He was quick, and he fell well, and he got up faster than anyone else in the line. Sela sent him to the exchange for his letters three mornings in seven, and stood at the wall while he did them, and did not once mention the yard. If anyone mentioned it to her she didn't hear them.

On his way down to the cell one evening, with the week's figures for the exchange under his arm, Emeric passed the head of the stair and saw that the door at the bottom was open. Corliss was sitting on the stool inside it, and Petram on the bed, and neither of them was talking. They were just sitting. The lamp was between them.

Emeric turned round and went back up, and left them the corridor.



He was tired that night, the good kind, and he practised anyway.

The hover came easily now. He could lift off the boards and hang there for a count of a hundred with his mind half on something else, and come down without thinking about the landing. So he took the candle up with him.

He held himself in the air a hand's width off the floor, and with the other part of his attention he reached for the flame and bent it toward his finger, the first thing he'd ever tried in this world.

Two things at once. Not two halves of one hold, the way he'd tumbled himself in August, but two different workings, his two elements, side by side. The hover wavered. The flame flattened and sprang back. Two small glyphs flickered in the air in front of him, the gravity mark low and pale and the fire mark bright, and for a moment both of them held.

Then the hover went, and he landed on his heels, and the flame stood up straight in its candle as if nothing had happened.

He tried it six more times. On the last one, he held both for a count of eight.

He sat on the bed afterward with the candle in his hand, and the headache coming, and thought about the push he'd felt in his palm when he bent the flame away from himself. Eight was nothing. Eight was a coin at hand height in April.

But it was eight.



The exchange opened on a grey morning in the last week of September, and it was half full.

Emeric didn't go in. It wasn't his morning, and it wasn't his room. He stood across the lane against the wall of a shut cooperage and watched them go through the door: dock children and lower-ward children and two from the high streets whose mothers had walked them down and stood watching too. Each of them took a piece of bread from the basket on the step without being told. Sela stood in the doorway and looked at every one of them as they passed, and said a name, and sometimes a word, and they went in.

He'd learned in the spring, carrying water, that there were times to stand where you could be seen and do nothing.

Imani came up the lane from the river and stopped beside him. She looked at the building, the new roof on the far bay, the high windows, Sela in the door.

Then she looked at him.

"You gave the soap a room," she said, "before you gave the sick one."

He didn't have an answer that wasn't a promise. So he didn't give her one.
