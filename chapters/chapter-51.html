Chapter 51 - The Shortest Day

On the longest night of the year he tried the thing he had written in the back of Armand's notebook in the summer of last year, and never tried.

Fire for thrust. Three words and a question mark, under the night's counts, from a night when he had lain on the floor of this room with his head splitting after the tumble and felt, faintly, the flame push back against his palm as he pushed it away. Hang on gravity. Push with fire. He had been too tired to try it then. Last winter, hanging in the air with the candle bent beside him, he had felt the shove again, and come down, and written Not yet under the question, because alone and at night in a stone room was exactly the wrong way to find out. Since then there had always been something that mattered more, and there still was. The dynamo at the mill stood without its commutator, which was in pieces in the dyehouse. It was the night before the shortest day, and there was nothing he could do about any of it until after midwinter, and he could not sleep.

He closed the shutters and hung the old cloak over them, and moved the table to the wall, and set a candle on the stone of the hearth, because the hearth was the one thing in the room that would not catch, and a full bucket beside it, because he was not.

The hover was easy now. It had been easy since last winter. He let the weight come off his feet, all of it, and the pale glyph opened on the boards under him, and he rose until there was a hand's width of air between his boots and the boards, and stayed there, turning very slowly, the way a thing hung on a thread turns, until he steadied it.

Then the flame. He drew it off the candle into the cup of his right hand, a little tongue of it no bigger than his thumb, and the second glyph came up over his fingers, small and red. A flame he had not had to make cost him almost nothing to hold, the way it cost almost nothing to carry a cup of water across a room. Two things at once was the hard part. He had learned that in his first summer here, on this floor, in the dark, and his body still remembered it. He felt the two holds pull against each other like two dogs on one lead.

He turned his hand so that the flame faced the chimney breast behind him, and pushed it.

The flame went out from his palm in a long thin stream, roaring softly, a hand's length, then two. He felt it push back, about as hard as a man leans on a door he does not expect to open. And very slowly, so slowly that at first he thought it was the turning again, Emeric began to move.

Forward. Away from the flame. Across the room.

He crossed it in about the time it took to say the alphabet twice, at the speed of a tired man looking for his boots, and arrived at the far wall with the flame still streaming out behind him, and behind that, up the stones of the chimney breast where he had started, a long brown tongue of scorch. The headache arrived at the same moment, all at once, behind both eyes. He let the flame go. The hover went with it, and he sat down on the floor harder than he meant to.

It worked. That was the first thing, and he sat on the floor in the dark and let himself have it. The second thing came after: it worked about as well as blowing on a boat. To cross the gate yard that way would have taken him most of an hour, and a tenth of what he had, and anybody in the yard could have walked up beside him and asked what he thought he was doing. A bigger flame would push harder, and a bigger flame he would have to make out of nothing, at ten times the cost, and four afternoons of a much smaller flame at the worm had cost him a fortnight of shaking hands.

One other thing he noticed only because he was sitting still. Near the end, with both hands busy holding the hover level, the push had come up a fraction before his palm finished turning, as if the glyph had heard him before his fingers said it. He lay on the boards and tried to make it happen again, and it would not. Air would have pushed better than fire, his other life said, and air was not his. It would cost him three times what fire did.

He got up, and put the table back, and opened the shutters a crack to let the smell of scorched stone out into the cold.

The brown tongue on the chimney breast was still there in the morning. He looked at it while he dressed. Then he went down to be put on the ground by Ward.



The shortest day came up slow and grey over the east wall, and the sun, when it came, did not clear the battlements of the South Gate until well after the drill had started. The yard was hard with frost. Their breath stood in front of them.

Ward did not ask why the prince was slower than usual. He put him on the ground twice in the first quarter of an hour, once with a turn of the wrist and once without bothering, and stood over him the second time with the practice blade resting on his shoulder.

"You did not sleep," he said.

"I slept."

"Not enough." Ward held out a hand, and pulled him up. "Again."

The third time Emeric lasted long enough to make Ward move his feet, which happened perhaps once in a week, and for which Ward gave no sign of credit at all except to go on longer than he otherwise would have. By the end of it the sun was in the yard, low and white, and Emeric's hands had stopped feeling the cold, and the garrison had come out along the wall of the guardhouse to watch, as they did, because watching the prince lose was the best entertainment in Kaldo and cost nothing.

Bram came up through the gate in the middle of it with a sack over his shoulder.

He waited until Ward had finished, and then he set the sack down on the cobbles and undid the neck of it and took out a helmet, and held it out to Ward without a word.

It was a plain round cap of iron with a narrow brim all the way round, the shape of an upturned basin, dark from the fire and still rough from the hammer, with a leather lining laced in. It had been part of an anchor at the start of the month. There were six of them in the sack.

Ward took it and turned it over in his hands. He put a thumb into the crown and pressed. He looked inside at the lining, and at the lacing, and at the rivets that held the brim on, and counted them. Then he set it on top of a post at the edge of the yard, and stepped back, and hit it with the practice blade, overhand, as hard as Emeric had ever seen him hit anything.

The helmet rang. It stayed on the post. When Ward took it down there was a bright line across the crown where the blade had landed, and no dent that Emeric could see.

Ward ran his thumb along the line.

"Again," he said, to nobody, and put it back on the post, and hit it again.

Then he handed it to the nearest man of the garrison, who happened to be Declan, and Declan put it on, and it came down over his eyes, and the garrison along the wall laughed for the first time all morning.

"Lining's too big," said Bram.

"Head's too small," said Rylan, from the wall.

Bram took the helmet back, and looked at the lining, and said he would have six more by the new year and a better lining in all of them, and breastplates after that out of the hull straps, and that the boys were closing rings on the armory's mail between heats, and that the next thing Ward wanted from him was going to have to wait for a month after that, because a man only had two hands and two boys and one Tess. Emeric asked what the next thing was. Bram said it was the bow for a crossbow, a steel one, if he could make the steel, and that Ward had asked for it in November, and that he had not said he would make it, and that he had a bar drawing down for it in bay three anyway.

Ward did not say anything to that. He took the sack of helmets from Bram and carried it into the guardhouse himself.



The spirit was in a cask in the forge yard, under a sack, where it had been since the end of October.

It was from the second week's runs, the ones that had not gone down to the ward. He had run it through the still a third time, which took the last of the harsh out of it, and cut it with water off the north well until it was strong enough to warm a man and not so strong it would take the skin off his mouth, and put it in the best of the journeyman's casks. He had charred the inside of the cask first with a shaped flame, a slow brown scorch all round the staves, because the only spirit he had ever liked in the other life had been whisky, kept in burned oak, and he had read why, and had not been sure it would do anything in eight weeks. Bram had watched him do it and asked whether he was trying to set fire to a barrel on purpose, and Emeric had said yes, and Bram had gone back to his forge.

On the afternoon of the shortest day they took the cask out from under its sack and knocked a spigot into it and ran a little into a cup.

It came out the pale brown of old honey. It smelled of the forge yard and, under that, of something else, faintly sweet and faintly like smoke. Bram held the cup up to the last of the light, which was going already, though it was barely the middle of the afternoon.

"It's brown," he said.

"It's the oak."

Bram drank. He put the cup down. He did not make Imani's face. He looked at the cup, and Emeric began to wonder whether he was going to say anything at all.

"Bring it in," Bram said. "Bring the jug."



Bram's kitchen at midwinter was the warmest room in Kaldo. It shared its wall with the forge, and Bram had banked the forge fire high before he came in, so that the wall gave off heat like a bread oven, and the stove in the corner was lit as well, and the shutters were closed, and the lamp was on the table. Ferra was at the table. She had the barony's book open in front of her, the year's book, and her pen in her hand, and she did not close the book when they came in, because it was not the last night of the year yet and the totals were not hers to draw until it was.

Bram put the jug down in the middle of the table, on top of the column she was adding, and she moved it.

Emeric went round the table to sit down, and put his hand out for the chair he always took, the one nearest the stove, without thinking about it, because for a year and a half there had been two chairs on that side of the table and one of them had a bad leg.

"The other one's mended," Ferra said.

She did not look up from the column when she said it.

He looked at the other chair. It was the same chair. It stood square now, on four legs, and the fourth was new wood, ash, pale as a peeled stick against the dark of the old ones, pegged and glued and fitted into the seat so cleanly that he would not have seen the join if it had not been for the color. Somebody had taken a great deal of trouble over it. Somebody had done it at night, by the look of the shavings that had been swept into the corner by the stove and not quite all the way out of sight.

He let go of the chair he always took.

Ferra had got up while he was looking at the join. She took the other chair by the top of its back and held it, with her eyes on the new leg.

He sat down in the mended one. It held him. It did not rock.

She kept hold of it until he had sat back against her knuckles, and drew her hand out from behind him, and went back to the year's book.

Nobody said whose it had been. Ferra wrote the next figure in her column, and blotted it. Bram took three cups down off the shelf over the stove and put them on the table and poured into all three, a finger in each, and pushed one across to his daughter and one across to the prince, and kept the third.

Ferra put her pen down for it. She tasted it, and her eyebrows went up, and then she drank the rest of it in one swallow, as she might have taken medicine, and set the cup down, and the color came up into her face under the freckles, from the throat upward.

"That's strong," she said.

"It's watered."

"Then I don't want to meet it before." She looked at the cup. "What does a cask of it fetch?"

"I don't know yet."

"Then find out before you make another." She picked her pen back up. She did not pick up the cup. She did not push it away, either.

Emeric drank his. It was better than the first. Not good. Better. It went down warm, and stayed warm, and somewhere behind the forge yard and the oak there was the smoke, and with it, very briefly, another kitchen in another life, a long way off, and somebody's hand on the bottle. Then the wall was warm against his shoulder again, and the mended chair was solid under him.

Bram did not drink at once. He sat with his cup in his big hand and looked across the table, at his daughter bent over the year's book, and at the prince in the chair with the new leg, and he looked at the two of them, and said nothing about anything.

Then he drank, and poured himself another.
