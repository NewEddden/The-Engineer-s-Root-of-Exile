Chapter 128 - Renner's Box

Bryda received him in the hall, as she had in June of the year before, standing at the end of the table nearest the fire, in a dark dress, very straight and very still.

She had aged. Not much. It was in the grey at the front of her hair, which had spread, and in the way she held her hands, folded in front of her, a little too tight. At the side table under the windows Ilsa sat with her pen and her book, taller than she had been, nearly as tall as her father must have been, and did not write anything, and did not look at him. The steward stood behind her chair.

"Holder of Grayrock," said Bryda.

She said it the way a woman says the name of a disease she has been told her child has.

"Resident in the crown's interest," said Emeric.

Something went across her face. It was gone at once.

"They wrote that to you as well," she said.

"I watched them write it." He did not sit down. Nobody had asked him to. "It means nobody can put you out of this house. Not the crown. Not me. Not anybody who comes after me." He looked at her. "It means nothing else, in the instrument. I'd like it to mean something else in this hall."

Bryda waited.

"You've held Grayrock two years," Emeric said. "You held it well. You gave Kaldo harder terms than anybody's ever given Kaldo and you kept every one, to the day, to the hundredweight, and Ferra has the book to prove it. You refused a factor from the capital a lease on your east working without telling us why, and told us anyway, because the terms said you must." He kept his eyes on hers. "I don't know how to run Grayrock. I don't know its pits or its people or which of its barges leaks. You do." He paused. "I'd like you to go on running it. As steward, under my seal, answering to Ferra's books. At a steward's wage, which Ferra will tell you is not small, because she's set Paxton's at Milford and she won't set yours at less."

Bryda did not say anything for some time.

"And Ilsa," she said at last.

Emeric looked at the side table. The girl was looking at her book, at nothing, with her pen held quite still above the page.

"Her father's line was struck," Emeric said. "In a room in the capital, by a man with ink on his chin. I couldn't have stopped it. I don't think I'd have tried." He did not look away from the girl. "But she keeps Grayrock's books. Ferra says she's kept them since she was fourteen. In a better hand than her father's steward." He saw the steward's face, and went on. "Ferra's counting room has three clerks and an apprentice and a clockmaker's widow who argues with her. It has nobody who knows Grayrock. If Ilsa would keep Grayrock's books, here, under Ferra's office, and come down the river to Kaldo four times a year to sit at Ferra's table and go through them with her, Ferra would be glad of it." He looked at Ferra, who was standing behind him with her own book against her chest. "Wouldn't you?"

"I've already ruled her a column," said Ferra.

The girl at the side table looked up.

She did not look at Emeric. She looked past him at Ferra, the way she had looked at Ferra's hands across this table in June of the year before, and wrote something in her own book that he could not see.

Bryda unfolded her hands.

"Sit down," she said. "All of you. There's food coming." She looked at Vantic, at Ward by the door, at Ferra. "And then I've a thing to show you that I've not shown anybody. Because you're the holder, and it's yours now, and I find I'd rather you had it than the crown."



It was a box.

She brought it down herself after the plates were gone, from a chest in her own bedchamber where it had lain, she said, under the winter blankets, since the week her husband rode south to Kaldo with his levy and did not come back. A plain box of dark wood, iron-cornered, the size of a ledger, with a lock. She had never opened it. She had found the key on his body when it came home to her, with the letter that said by whose order he had died and not how, and she had put the key in the box's own lock and turned it and then turned it back and put the box under the blankets.

"It was his," she said. "He kept it in his study. He never let me see in it. He never let anybody." She put it on the table in front of Emeric and laid the key beside it. "I've thought about burning it every winter for two years. I don't know why I didn't." She sat down. "Open it. I'd like to know what was worth more to him than telling me."

Emeric opened it.

Papers. Not many. A dozen sheets, folded, tied with tape, and under them a single thick roll of heavy paper, and under that a small leather bag that clinked when he moved the box.

He untied the tape on the sheets first.

The first was a survey. He knew it was a survey the moment it was unfolded on the table, because it was the kind of drawing he had made himself a hundred times on his other world and had not seen anyone make on this one: a hill drawn from the side, cut open, like a loaf cut down the middle, with the strata of the rock drawn in layers across it in different inks, and the old workings drawn into it as tunnels, and depths marked beside them in a careful trained hand. Grayrock's east hill. The east working, the one that stood under boards: its shaft going down, and its galleries going off the shaft to either side. And under the deepest of the galleries, below anything Grayrock had ever dug, drawn in a different ink, faint grey, a shape.

A long shape. Lying in the rock along the bottom of the hill, the length of the hill and more, thick in the middle and thinning at the ends, like a seam of coal, or a vein of ore, but not drawn like either. Drawn like nothing. Shaded grey, and beside it, in the trained hand, three words that Emeric read twice before he understood them, because they were in the crown's surveyors' shorthand, which he had seen once, on a map in his father's office, at the age of twelve.

Gravity. Body. Whole.

He did not say anything. He turned the survey over.

Under it were the payments. Six sheets of them, in Renner's own hand, which Emeric knew from Ferra's book of Grayrock's letters: payments to surveyors. Outside surveyors, three of them, named only by their initials, over two years. Each payment larger than the one before. Ferra, at his shoulder, made a very small sound, and he knew without asking that she had added them up as he turned them, and he did not need to ask what the sum was, because he could see her face. It was more than three years of everything Grayrock took in.

"He didn't have that," said Bryda. Her voice was quite steady. "He never had that. We sold ore at a loss three winters running."

"Somebody had it," said Ferra quietly.

The next sheet was an order. Renner's seal on it, the old seal of Grayrock, with his name. Closing the east working. Its shaft to be boarded, its galleries abandoned, no man to go down it on pain of his place. And under it, in Renner's hand, smaller, a note to the steward of that year, not this one: the men would not go back in after the second accident, and he would not make them.

"The curse," said Bryda.

She had not looked at any of the papers. She was looking at the fire.

"Two men died in the east working the winter before he closed it. And three the winter before that. They went down in the morning and didn't come up, and the men who went down after them came up white and sick and said there was nothing down there, no fall, no water, nothing, only the men lying in the gallery as if they'd lain down to sleep." She did not look round. "They called it cursed. The whole town did. He said it was cursed too. He said it at this table." She was quiet. "I believed him."

Emeric looked at the survey again, at the galleries drawn going off the shaft, and at a little note in the trained hand beside the deepest of them, which he had passed over the first time because it was in the same shorthand. Timbering failed and rotting. Air foul below the second level. Candles go out.

"It's not a curse," he said.

Bryda turned from the fire.

"It's the timber," Emeric said. "The props that hold the galleries up. Old wood, down there in the wet and the dark, rotting. Wood that rots in a closed place eats the good out of the air. It doesn't smell. You can't see it. You go down and you breathe it and you feel tired, and you sit down, and you go to sleep, and you don't wake up." He put his finger on the note. "His surveyor knew. He wrote it here. Air foul below the second level. Candles go out. A candle goes out in that air before a man does. Any miner who carries a candle down and watches it knows to come up." He looked at Bryda. "Your husband knew it wasn't a curse. He had this in his study. He let the town call it one because a cursed working is a working nobody goes into."

Bryda looked at the survey.

"Why?" she said.

Emeric put his hand flat on the grey shape at the bottom of the drawing.

"Because of this," he said.



He told her. He told all of them, there at the table, with Ilsa at the side table and the steward behind her chair and Ward by the door and Vantic at the far end of the table with her gloved hands folded on her book, very still, the stillest he had ever seen her.

Gravity stone. The rarest stone there was. The stone that lifted things: the crown's sky-ships in the capital that he had watched as a boy, hanging over the palace on festival days, lifted on gravity stone at two Platinum a pound, and the four in the springs of his own carriage, and almost nothing else in the kingdom, because almost nobody had ever found any. It was not found in seams or veins like other stones. It was found a stone at a time, small, buried, by luck. Nobody had ever found a body of it. Nobody had ever drawn one.

Somebody had drawn one under Grayrock's east hill. The length of the hill and more.

Bryda sat very still.

"He knew," she said.

"He knew. He found out somehow. Or somebody told him. Then he paid surveyors he couldn't afford, with money he didn't have, to find out how big it was. And when they told him, he sealed the working, and let it be cursed, and sold ore at a loss, and kept it in a box in his study." Emeric looked at the payments. "And waited for something."

"For what?"

Emeric did not answer. He unrolled the thick roll of paper from the bottom of the box.

It was a commission. On heavy paper, the kind the chancery used, with a seal at the foot in red wax that had cracked across with the years in the box. It commissioned three surveyors of the crown's own school, by name, on the authority of the commissioner, to make a survey of a hill in the barony of Grayrock for the commissioner's private interest, at the commissioner's expense, and to report to him alone. It was dated. The date was fifteen years ago. Eleven, when Emeric had come to Kaldo. The year that ran through every page of Ferra's first finding at the trestle in the first summer.

And it was signed.

Emeric knew the seal before he read the name. A house said to be as old as the king's. A set of carved wooden soldiers, held out at the banquet, with no cost in it and no craft either. A man standing in the window of the attainder room across the chancery courtyard in August, on the day Grayrock was struck, very still, for longer than a man stands still by accident, and then inclining his head.

Aldane.

He sat with the commission in his hands and did not say anything for so long that Ferra, at his shoulder, put her hand on the back of his chair.

The money had been Aldane's. The surveyors had been Aldane's. Renner had been Aldane's: a baron of a hill barony in the south with a lodestone body under his east hill that the duke had found out about somehow, fifteen years ago, and paid to have measured, and told Renner to sit on. Sit on it, and seal it, and let it be cursed, and wait. And Renner had waited. Fifteen years. And then a prince had come south to the barony next door with fifty Gold Rand and a soap kiln and begun to buy Grayrock's ore, and Grayrock's ore was the hill, and the hill was the stone, and somebody had sent Renner south with his levy and two mages in his pay to burn a town at night.

And when Renner was dead, and his widow held the hill in trust, a factor from a house in the capital had come up the river road and offered to lease the east working for twelve years, and she had said no. Without saying why.

And in August, when the hill came to Kaldo by attainder, the Duke of Aldane had stood in a window and nodded to him.

Not a threat. Not a greeting. The nod of a man across a table to another man who has just been dealt the card he has been holding for fifteen years. You have it now. I know you have it. We will talk.

"Who," said Bryda, very quietly, from across the table, "is that?"

Emeric rolled the commission up again.

"A man your husband worked for," he said. "And didn't tell you about." He put the roll back in the box, carefully, on top of the survey and the payments. "And who knows I've got this hill now. And will be waiting to find out whether I've got this box."

He did not tell her the name. She did not ask it again.

At the far end of the table Vantic had not moved. She sat with her gloved hands folded on her book, and Emeric looked at her down the length of the table, and found her already looking at him, with an expression he had never seen on her face before and could not read at all.

The small leather bag, when he opened it, held three stones. Grey, smooth, each the size of a walnut, and heavy in the bag, and then, when he tipped them out into his palm, not heavy at all. They sat in his palm and pressed, very gently, upward, against nothing. As if they would have liked to rise and were too well brought up to.

The surveyors' samples. From the bottom of the hill. Fifteen years in a box under a widow's winter blankets.

He closed his hand on them, and felt them push, faintly, against his fingers, up; and at the very edge of his sense, where he kept it open, something answered them from inside himself, from very deep down, the heavy quiet thing that Armand had said sounded like a stone falling down a well a long way in the dark. It moved. He felt it turn over, like something asleep turning toward a sound.

He put the stones back in the bag.



Saban's man at Grayrock was the potboy at the tavern on the quay, a sharp-faced boy of fourteen who had been taking Saban's silver since the spring for telling him which barges went where, and who found Emeric in the street outside the hall at dusk and walked beside him without looking at him, the way Saban's people did.

"Master Saban said to show you the wall," he said. "In the tavern. If you came."

"What wall?"

"The back wall. In the long room." The boy shrugged. "There's an old man drew on it. Years ago. They painted it over. Master Saban said you'd want to see where."

The tavern on the quay was low and dark and smelled of beer and wet coats and the river, and the long room at the back had a fire and a few men at the tables and a wall at the far end that had been whitewashed at some point and had not been since, so that the old lime had gone yellow and cracked. And under the yellow, if you stood close and the fire was behind you, you could see lines.

Two of them. Long, straight, parallel, a hand's width apart, running the whole length of the wall from one corner to the other, drawn in charcoal under the wash, and beside them at intervals little marks, and at the right-hand end, where they ran into the corner, a word that had been scraped at before it was washed over. And at the left-hand end, a drawing of a small square box on four round wheels, sitting on the two lines.

Emeric stood in front of the wall.

"Odo Pike," said a voice behind him. "Drew that. Forty years ago, or near it. It's meant to go from here to the capital." A man at the nearest table, a carter by his hands, was grinning at him over his beer. "Iron road, he called it. Iron on iron runs like water on glass, he says. A horse could pull ten carts on it." He drank. "Landlord painted it out when the old man wouldn't stop explaining it to the customers. He's still at it. Sits in that corner there most nights."

There was an old man in the corner by the fire. Emeric had not seen him. He was sitting very crooked on a bench with his back against the wall and a stick between his knees, the crookedness of a back that had been bent under a hill for forty years and had not come all the way straight again, and he was looking at Emeric across the room with small bright pale eyes, not at all friendly.

"They're telling you I'm mad," he said.

Emeric went across the room to him.

"Did you lay the wooden rails?" he said. "In the galleries. In the east hill. For the tubs."

The old man's face changed.

"Who told you that?"

"Nobody. I saw a survey of the east working this afternoon. The galleries had two lines drawn along the floor of every one of them. A surveyor wouldn't draw a thing that wasn't there." Emeric looked at him. "Somebody put rails in those galleries so that one boy could push a tub of ore that would take four men to carry. Before the working was sealed. Forty years ago or near it."

Odo Pike looked at him without answering.

"Oak," he said at last. "Oak rails, pegged to the sleepers. And a tub with four wheels and a lip on the wheels so it'd not come off." He moved his stick between his knees. "One boy. Pushing a ton. Down a gallery a quarter mile long." His voice changed. "And iron's better. Iron on iron. Like water on glass. Lay it from the pithead to the quay, from the quay to the coast, from here to the capital, and a horse could pull what ten horses pull now, all day, without stopping." He looked at the painted wall across the room, at the two lines still showing faintly through the yellow wash. "I told them. Forty years I've told them." He looked back at Emeric. "And they painted it out."

Emeric stood in front of him in the firelit tavern with the carters laughing at the next table.

"People here say you're mad," he said.

"They do."

"I don't think you are." He looked at the old man's crooked back, and his bright pale eyes, and his hands on the stick, the hands of a man who had made a tub with a lip on its wheels so that it would not come off a rail, forty years ago, under a hill, in the dark, because nobody had told him he couldn't. "Do you want to come and work for me?"

The carters at the next table had stopped laughing.

Odo Pike looked up at him a good while. Then he looked across the room again at the wall, at the two lines under the paint.

"What doing?" he said.

"That," said Emeric.
