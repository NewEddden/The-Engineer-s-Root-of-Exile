Chapter 223 - The Mooring

They went up off the lake at six on the morning of the twenty-third of May, eight of them in a boat no longer than a market barge, and Old Edrin began dying at once.

He did it beautifully. He sat in the casting seat in the well amidships with both hands flat on the brass plate and his fingers on the levers underneath its lip, where nobody on the shore could see them, and as the hull came up off the water and the shore dropped away and the town turned slowly under them, his shoulders went down and his breath came hard and high in his chest with a little catch at the top of it, and the colour went out of his long face as though somebody had pulled a plug, and a sweat came out along his hairline and stood there.

Maren, on the bench by the rail with a basket on her knees, watched this for about a quarter of an hour with growing alarm and then got up and went to him with a cup.

"Oh, the poor lamb," she said, very loudly, so that the people on the field below the north gate could hear it, if they had been listening. "Poured right out and it's not seven. Drink this."

"I'm not poured out," said Old Edrin, out of the side of his mouth, without opening his eyes. "There's nobody on the field."

"There'll be people on every field from here to the capital," said Maren, at the same volume. "You'll want to be in practice." And then, lower, holding the cup to his lips with great tenderness: "And drink it anyway, because you've had nothing since four and you're seventy-one and I'll not have you fainting for real."

He drank it. It was broth. He made a face over it that would have convinced any caster in the realm.

Ferra, on the cabin roof with her book, wrote something down.

"What are you writing?" said Emeric.

"Maren's volume," said Ferra. "In case it's wanted for the inquest."

Imani had come up the gangway last, with her bag and a crate of dressings and the face of a woman who had agreed to this in April and was still agreeing to it, one morning at a time. She watched the old man suffer for a while from the cabin step. Then she went across the well and took his wrist, under cover of steadying the cup, and counted, with her eyes on the shore going away.

"Seventy," she said to him, very quietly. "You're a fraud, grandfather."

"Thank you, mistress," said Old Edrin, without opening his eyes, and suffered a little harder.



The hull went north at about the pace of a horse at a gallop, kept up all day, which was the pace capital hulls went, and as much as a man at the casting seat could hold from dawn to the middle of the afternoon if he was young and strong and had slept. She went low, three or four hundred feet over the country, where every farm could see her go over and every posting house could set a boy running to say so. That was the point of her. By noon on the first day there were people out in the fields below them looking up, with their hands over their eyes, and children running along the lanes underneath, and once a man on a cart who stood up on the box and took his hat off.

Nobody on the hull did anything at all, except Old Edrin, who suffered.

Emeric sat in the bows with his back to the stem post and his sense out thin, as he always had it, over the country they were crossing. It was a strange feeling. On the ship he went over the land at a mile's height at two hundred miles in the hour, and the land was a map; here it went under him at a man's walk, field by field, and he could feel every house as it came, a warm smear of a family round a hearth and a few bright points that were lamp stones and once, near a river, the hard high note of a hedge mage in a mill, who turned his head as they went over, Emeric felt him do it, and looked up at the hull, and felt the stone the old man at the seat was pouring into, and was satisfied, and went back to his millstones.

He had not felt the stones under the deck. Emeric had been waiting for it, all the way across the river meadow while the hull came over, with his own sense lying out round the mill like a hand round a cup. The hedge mage had felt a man pouring. He had not felt anything under the iron at all.

Ward, on the bench by the cabin, had been watching Emeric's face the whole time.

"Well?"

"Nothing. He felt Edrin."

"Good," said Ward, and went back to watching the country.



Vantic read.

She had come aboard on the lake shore at a quarter to six with a single bag and a leather case of papers, and Ferra had given her one of the two bunks in the little cabin aft and taken the other, and Imani had looked at the two bunks and the three of them and spread her own blanket on the floor between, without discussion, because she had slept on worse floors in the fever winter; and Vantic had come out again at once and sat on the cabin step in the sun with the case open on her knees and had not looked up since. They were ledgers: the House's own, Emeric supposed, or copies of them, columns and columns of small figures in a dozen different hands, and she went down them with a pencil as Ferra went down a rent roll, without hurrying, making a small mark now and then in a margin.

She was going to her third Reading. He knew that much; she had told him in the counting room in March, in a sentence, the week he came home from the court, that the House would sit on the Tuesday of the same week and that she would be in the capital for it in any case. She had said nothing about it since. She was not saying anything about it now.

In the middle of the afternoon on the first day, over a long flat country of water meadows, Ferra climbed down off the cabin roof with her own book and sat on the step beside her, and they sat there side by side with their two books open, not talking, for an hour; and at the end of the hour Ferra leaned over and put her finger on a line in Vantic's ledger and said something too quiet to carry, and Vantic looked at the line, and then at Ferra, and said, "Yes. That one," and made a mark beside it, and they went on.



They came down for the night at a quarter to four on a farm's home field beside a posting house on the capital road, where the farmer had been told by the posting house, which had been told by Saban's man riding ahead, that a capital hull wanted a field for the night and would pay a Silver for it.

The farmer had told his neighbours. There were about sixty people at the edge of the field when the hull came down, standing well back along the hedge in a long row, with their children held by the collar.

Old Edrin brought her in very slowly, in a long series of small careful corrections, each one of them, to anybody watching, the last drop of a man's strength. Emeric watched him do it from the bows and could not see his fingers move under the lip of the plate at all. The hull touched the grass and bumped and settled on her keel, and the old man took his hands off the plate one at a time, as if they were very heavy, and let them fall into his lap, and sat there with his head hanging.

The row along the hedge made a low sound, the sound a crowd makes at a horse that has come in last but come in.

Maren got down first, with her basket, and went straight to the posting house kitchen to see what they had and say what she thought of it. Ward got down second and stood at the foot of the little gangway, with his arms folded and his back to the hull, and the sixty people along the hedge looked at him and stayed where they were.

Two men from the posting house came out and lifted Old Edrin out of the casting seat between them and walked him across the field to the house with his arms over their shoulders and his feet doing the minimum. As they passed the hedge a woman held out a cup of something to him, and he took it with a shaking hand and drank it and gave it back and said, "Bless you, mother," in a voice like a rusty hinge, and the woman burst into tears.

"He's very good," said Saban, beside Emeric, watching.

"He's frighteningly good."

"He's had fifty years to watch it done." Saban put his hat on. "I'll pay the farmer. You'll want to stay aboard, Highness. Your eyes."

Emeric stayed aboard. Ward slept on the gangway, or sat on it, which with Ward was much the same thing. The three women had the cabin. Saban and Maren had beds in the posting house. Emeric lay on the bench in the bows under a blanket with the stars over him and the farm dogs barking at the hull every hour or so on principle, and lay awake a long time thinking, and was not sure afterward what about.



Three days of it. Two landings. On the second night a churchyard, because there was nothing else flat for ten miles, and the priest came out and blessed the hull, and Old Edrin, carried past him toward the vicarage, raised a feeble hand in thanks and was blessed separately. That night, in the churchyard, with the lamps out and the hull lying among the gravestones like a boat drawn up on a beach of them, Vantic spoke to him for the first time since the lake.

She was on the cabin step with her case shut on her knees. He was coming back from the vicarage pump with a bucket.

"Highness. A question."

He put the bucket down.

"If somebody asked you to explain the worst quarter Kaldo ever had," said Vantic, "in front of people who could take everything from you on your answer. What would you say?"

He thought about it. The plague year, before him; or the year of the siege; or the winter just gone, with its column of the dead in Ferra's book.

"What happened," he said. "And what I'd done that made it worse."

"Would you name anybody else? Somebody who'd made it worse as well? If it were true?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because it was my quarter."

Vantic looked at him for a breath in the dark, between the gravestones. Then she nodded, once, as a clerk nods at a sum that has come out, and got up with her case and went into the cabin, and he heard the bolt.

On the third day, the twenty-fifth, they came over the last ridge before the capital a little after two in the afternoon and saw it spread out ahead of them under its own smoke, the rings and the walls and the cathedral's grey bulk and the white point of the Circle's tower, and over the inner rings, small and dark against the haze, the other hulls, coming and going from the landing tiers.

Old Edrin sat up straighter in the casting seat.

"There they are," he said, very quietly, in his own voice. "Fifty years." He looked at the hulls a while longer. Then he let his shoulders go down again and his breath come hard, and the colour went out of his face. "Right," he said. "Now we're working."



The landing tiers stood on the slope below the inner rings on the city's north side, three long terraces of stone one above another like steps for a giant, each with a row of timber cradles along its edge and a stone house at the end for the tier master. Hulls lay in about half the cradles. Their casters were mostly gone home for the day, but some sat on the terrace in the sun with their backs to the warm wall and their eyes shut, grey, done, as the old man in the hospital at Kaldo had sat, and they opened their eyes as the hull came in over the lowest tier and watched her come down.

Emeric felt them watch. He felt, along the terrace, eight or nine notes with the faculty in them, thin and tired, turn toward the old man at the seat and the stone they could feel him holding, as a cooper hears another cooper's hammer across a yard; and not one of them went any further than that. Nobody went out. Nobody had the strength left to spare, at three in the afternoon, after a day at the plate, to send his sense out after a stranger's hull. They knew how hulls flew.

Old Edrin brought her into an empty cradle at the end of the lowest tier, which Saban's man had kept for them, and set her down in it so gently that the cradle's timbers did not even creak, and took his hands off the plate, and slumped.

A caster on the terrace, a young man, got up off the wall and came over to the cradle's edge and looked down at Old Edrin in the well with an expression of enormous professional sympathy.

"Where've you come from, grandfather?"

"South," said Old Edrin, without lifting his head.

"South where?"

"Kaldo." He lifted his head, slowly. "Three days."

The young caster stared at him. "Three days? On your own? At your age?" He looked at the hull and back. "Who's your lord, to send you three days alone at the plate?"

"A barony that can't afford two of me," said Old Edrin, and let his head fall again.

The young caster looked up from the old man to the rest of them on the deck, and his eyes went along them, the knight, the merchant, the cook, the three women; and then to the man in the bows, and to his eyes; and stopped.

He did not say anything. He stepped back from the cradle's edge. After a breath he went back to the wall and sat down by the others and said something low, and along the terrace, one after another, grey tired faces turned and looked at the hull from Kaldo, and at the old man slumped in the seat of it, and then at the man in the bows with the gold eyes, with an expression Emeric had not seen on any face in the capital nine years ago, and could not name.

"Pity," said Saban, quietly, beside him. "That's what that is. For the old man." He took off his hat. "And for themselves, a bit. They'll be doing that at seventy."



Aldane's sheet was already up on the tier master's door.

It had gone up that morning, Saban's man said; Saban's man was the tier master's clerk, a thin young man with ink on his fingers who came down the steps to the cradle with a paper in his hand and gave it to Saban without being asked and went back up again. It was in Aldane's fine new fount, without a black border this time. It said that His Grace of Aldane, a loyal servant of the crown, would attend the investiture of the Holder of Kaldo on the Thursday as the custom of the peers required, and would bend his knee to the crown's will in the matter as every loyal peer must. And it said, in the last line, in smaller letters, that the realm might observe that the machine duke even came to his girding by machine.

"He's heard about the hull," said Ferra.

"Everybody's heard about the hull," said Saban. "I bought it in the third ring on a Tuesday in front of a notary and a widow and her cook." He folded the sheet. "It's not a bad line. He's had a month to polish it."

"Do we answer it?"

"With what?" Saban put it in his coat. "It's true."



Ward would not hear of a house.

Saban's man had a house in the drapers' street, and Saban knew three more; and there was the palace, where the Holder of Kaldo had had rooms once, nine years ago, and might be supposed to have them still. Ward said no to all of it, standing on the gangway in the late afternoon with the terrace behind him.

"We sleep aboard," he said. "Here. In the cradle. Nobody climbs aboard. Nobody comes up that gangway but the eight of us, by day or by night, for any reason, and I will be on it." He looked at Emeric. "A house has doors, Highness. A hull has one plank."

So they slept aboard. Maren made a kitchen of the tier master's back yard with the tier master's leave, which Saban bought for a Silver and a promise of Maren's pastry, and fed them on the deck at dusk while the city's lamps came on below the tier; and Old Edrin was put to bed at the tier master's house with great ceremony by two of the terrace's casters, who carried him up the steps between them and would not hear of anyone else doing it.

Emeric sat in the bows afterward in the dark, with the city below him.

It came to him slowly, the way a thing comes to a man who has been trying not to look at it. The hull lay in her cradle with the stones under her deck and the levers under the plate, and the old man was asleep in a house at the end of the terrace who could be woken, and there was nobody on the terrace now but the tier master's dog. He could leave. Tonight, now, at any hour. Edrin at the seat, the lever under his fingers, the hull up off the cradle and over the rings and south in the dark, and nobody in the capital could stop it, or would know until morning.

He had come to the capital on a road twice before, with a horse under him that was the only way home. Now he had a way home at his back, and Thursday was two days off, and his father had not sent for him, and his brother had looked at him once across a hall in March and not stopped.

He did not leave. He sat in the bows until the lamps below began to go out, and then he lay down on the bench and pulled the blanket up and looked at the stars over the capital; and he knew, lying there, that every night he stayed now would be a night he had chosen to stay, and that he was going to stay.



Saban's other man came on Monday night, late, to the foot of the gangway, and talked to Saban there in low voices for a quarter of an hour without coming up it.

Saban came and sat down in the bows by Emeric afterward, and did not say anything for a while.

"My man in the palace," he said at last. "He's a clerk in the household's linen office. He sees a great deal of the second floor." He looked at the city. "This afternoon, a little after three, the first queen sent for her son to her own rooms. That's not unusual. They were in there an hour, with the door shut and her women sent out. That's unusual. My man was in the passage with a basket of sheets and couldn't hear the words through the door. He could hear that there were words." He was quiet. "Her voice mostly. Low. His, once or twice, loud. At the end the door opened and His Highness the Lieutenant came out with a face like a slammed shutter and went down the stair without looking at anybody, and the first queen's women went back in, and one of them came out again in a minute for a basin of water."

"About what?"

"My man doesn't know." Saban turned his hat in his hands. "He says the first queen doesn't send for her son. Her son goes to her when he wants something, which is seldom. She hasn't sent for him in a year." He put his hat down on the bench. "So something's worried her enough to send for him three days before your girding, and whatever she told him, he didn't like it, and went out angry." He looked at Emeric. "I don't know what that's worth. I'm telling you because I don't know."

Emeric thought of the woman who had not come down to the hall on the night of his exile, when the court had watched him walk out of it with a chest of crystals and a box of carved soldiers. He had never seen her face close. He had never in his life spoken to her.

"Keep listening," he said.

"I always do," said Saban. "It's the only thing I'm any good at that isn't selling something."



The House carriage came for Vantic at eight on the Tuesday morning.

It came to the bottom of the tier steps and stopped there, because Ward had made it very plain to the tier master that nothing on wheels came onto the lowest terrace: a long black carriage with black horses and no arms on its door, nothing anywhere on it at all to say whose it was, which was how everybody in the capital knew. The coachman did not get down. He sat on the box and waited, looking straight ahead.

Vantic came out of the cabin in grey.

It was the plainest thing Emeric had ever seen her wear, and he had seen her in a working coat in the snow: a dark grey dress with a high neck and long tight sleeves and nothing on it anywhere, not a button that was not needed, and her hair put up as tight as a clerk's knot. She had the leather case of ledgers under her arm. She stopped on the deck and looked down the steps at the carriage, and her face did nothing at all.

Ferra had come out of the cabin behind her. She did not say anything either. She reached up, quite briskly, and took a thread off the shoulder of the grey dress, a single gold thread that must have come off her own sewing in the cabin, and looked at it and put it in her pocket.

Vantic turned her head and looked at her.

"Go on," said Ferra.

Vantic went down the gangway, past Ward, who stood aside for her, and down the steps to the carriage, and the coachman did not get down for her either, and she opened the door herself and got in and shut it, and the carriage went away down the hill toward the inner rings at a walk.

Emeric watched it go.

"Did she say anything to you?" he asked Ferra. "About today?"

"No," said Ferra.

"Do you know what they'll ask her?"

"No." Ferra watched the carriage turn the corner at the foot of the hill and go out of sight. "I know what she'll answer. Whatever they ask her." She turned and went back into the cabin.



Isolde came to the tier at two.

She came on foot up the steps from the street with Jabari a pace behind her, in a dark blue riding coat that was new and a hat with a feather in it that was not, and she stopped at the foot of the gangway and looked up at Ward on it, and then past him at the hull and the old casting seat and the little cabin, with the frank interest of a girl who had been hauled into the sky the first time in a sling and liked it.

"Sir Ward," she said. "I know. Nobody comes up." She looked past him. "Will the Chancellor come down?"

The Chancellor came down. Emeric watched from the bows. The two of them stood on the terrace in the sun at the foot of the gangway with their heads together, and Jabari stood a little way off with his big hands behind his back and looked at the hulls in the other cradles as a man looks at horses he has been told are fast and does not believe it.

Ferra told him afterward. Lord Sallow had come to the capital for the investiture, as the queen's brother had to; he had come a week early, and brought his daughter and his daughter's husband with him, in his own coach, with his own household riding round it. It was the first time in her life that Isolde had ridden in her father's coach beside him and not been delivered somewhere.

"She says he's not said ten words to Jabari the whole way," said Ferra. "And that he took Jabari's arm on the step at the inn the first night when he slipped, and held it till they were inside, and let go as if it had burnt him." She considered. "She says it's his way of saying the blessing out loud. In front of the north. Bringing them to court on his own coach." She looked at the terrace, where Isolde and Jabari were going back down the steps, Isolde talking and Jabari listening with his head bent. "Bastian stayed home."

"Is she afraid?"

"Of the court? No." Ferra opened her book. "She says she spent her whole girlhood being looked at in a hall. She says it's the one thing she's better at than me, and she'll stand where I can see her and I'm to copy what she does with her hands." She wrote something down. "I said I'd consider it."



The carriage brought her back at a quarter to six.

It stopped at the bottom of the steps, as before, and the coachman still did not get down; but this time, Emeric saw from the bows, he turned his head on the box as she got out, and watched her go up the steps, all the way to the top, which he had not done in the morning.

She came up the gangway past Ward with the case under her arm. Her face was exactly as it had been at eight. She looked tired, and she looked as if she had been sitting very still in a hard chair for eight hours; and that was all her face said, to him, and he looked at it as carefully as he had ever looked at anything.

"They sat until four," said Vantic, to nobody in particular, on the deck. "I'm going to lie down."

She went into the cabin. Ferra was standing at the cabin door, and Vantic passed her in the doorway, close, the two of them shoulder to shoulder for a step, and Ferra's eyes went to Vantic's face from a hand's breadth away.

Then the door shut, and Ferra stood outside it with her back to it.

And Ferra's face said something. He saw it. It was there in her mouth and around her eyes for a breath, very bright and very fierce, and gone again before he could get it into words; and then she was looking at him across the deck quite flatly, as she looked at him across the counting room table when there was a figure in her book she had no intention of showing him.

"Don't," she said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to." She sat down on the cabin step with her back against the door. "It's not mine. It's hers. And she's not said it." She opened her book on her knees and did not look at it. "So you'll wait."



They opened the dress boxes on the Wednesday, Abloh's long boxes that had come north under the cabin bunks wrapped in sailcloth, with his chalk marks still on the lids.

Emeric was sent out of the cabin.

He was sent out of it twice, in fact, because the second time he came to the door to ask whether his coat was in the long box or the short one, and Ferra opened the door a hand's width and said the short one and that the short one was on the bench outside and he was to go and sit on it, and shut the door; and he went and sat on it, in the bows, with the box beside him, and listened.

He heard it anyway. The cabin was boards and a door, and the terrace was quiet in the middle of the afternoon with the casters out on their runs, and the three voices came through the door quite clearly, low, the way women talk in a room with the door shut.

"It's the sleeve."

"It's not the sleeve. It's the band. Hold your arm out."

A pause.

"There. It sits a finger low on this side. Look."

"Abloh cut it to a finger. I watched him."

"Abloh cut it for a woman standing straight," said Vantic. "You don't stand straight. You've carried a book on that arm for nine years and that shoulder's lower than the other, and every woman in that hall is going to look at every inch of you to see what Kaldo's Chancellor is, because they've heard about her for years and never seen her. They'll look at the band. And if it's a finger low on one side they'll go home and say the Chancellor was dressed by a sailmaker." A pause. "Hold still. I've a needle."

"She was dressed by a sailmaker," said Imani's voice, from lower down; from the floor, Emeric thought.

"Then they mustn't be able to tell. You sew?" That was Ferra.

"I'm the youngest of three in a house of lenders. I was put to sewing at six, because there wasn't a ledger I was allowed near. I sewed for nine years." A small sound, of thread drawn through silk. "I hated every stitch. I'm very good."

A pause. Then Ferra, in a different voice: "I've never done this."

"Done what?"

"Cared. About a sleeve." Another pause. "I've had two good dresses in my life and they were both for funerals. I wore one of them to the first table, nine years ago, because it was the only thing I had without ink on it, and Bram said I looked like a widow at an auction." A breath. "I don't know how to care about a sleeve. I don't know what you do with your hands."

"I've never owned a sleeve that came below the elbow," said Imani. "They get in the blood."

"Neither of you is helping," said Vantic. "I've only ever been allowed to care about a column." The thread again. "We'll learn on this one. Turn round. No, the other way. There." And then: "Mistress Imani. Stand up."

"Mine fits."

"Yours fits because there's nothing on it to fit. Stand up. The rose is a thumb's width off your heart."

"It is not."

"Where's your heart?"

A silence. Then, with enormous dignity: "A thumb's width to the left."

And then there was a long quiet, broken only by the thread and by small exact sounds of the three of them moving about the narrow cabin, and once a muffled exclamation from Ferra that sounded like a pin; and then, unexpectedly, very quietly, laughter, all three of them, at something he had not caught, going on longer than any of them laughed at anything in a counting room, and stopping, and starting again.

It went on for an hour. He timed it by the sun on the terrace. At the end of the hour the cabin door opened and Ferra put her head out, flushed, with a pin in her mouth, and looked at him on the bench.

"You're still here."

"You told me to sit on the bench."

"I did." She took the pin out of her mouth. "We've done the left sleeve, and moved Imani's heart." She looked back into the cabin, and something in her face was quite open, for once, as it had been at the counting room table in January when Imani told her; and then she looked at him again. "We're going to do the right one now. It's going to take another hour. Go and find Saban."

He went and found Saban.



The peers' drinking was on the Wednesday night, in the long gallery of the chancery's west range, because the custom of the peers said that a man who was to be girded duke on the morrow drank with the peers on the eve, and that the peers who wished to drink with him came, and that those who did not wish to were noticed.

About forty came. Emeric did not count them; Saban did, at the door, and told him afterward. They stood about the gallery in their good coats under the lamps with their cups and watched him come in with Ward at his shoulder and Ferra on his arm in her grey, because the black was for tomorrow, and their talk dropped to nothing and then came back up again a little too loud. Imani had not come. She had said she would go to one thing in the capital and that this was not it, and had stayed on the hull with Maren and a pack of cards. Aldane was not among them. Corbin was not among them. Neither was anyone Emeric had ever exchanged more than a sentence with in his old life, which surprised him for a breath and then did not.

They drank with him. They did it correctly, and briefly, one after another, the old ones and the young ones: a cup raised, his name, the new style, a few words, a bow to the depth the custom asked and not a finger further. Their notes, in his sense, were the hall's notes from March, drawn in, careful, close. The young ones looked at him as if he were a horse they had heard about and wanted to see run. The old ones looked at him as if he were weather.

And at about ten, when the wine had gone round several times and the gallery had loosened a little, an old lord with a red face and a fur collar, whom Emeric had last seen on the benches in March looking at him with unmoving dislike, said it, behind him, to the man beside him, not quite quietly enough.

"Of course the first queen had the other one killed. Everybody said so at the time. Who else gained?"

The man beside him shushed him. He did not shush.

"Twenty years I've said it. A queen with no house and a queen with the north behind her, and one of them dies in the mountains with her whole escort and the other one's son's the heir." He drank. "You don't need a court to add that. A child could add it."

Emeric did not turn round. He felt the old lord's note as he spoke, loose with wine and quite sure of itself, and behind it, in the gallery, a dozen other notes that had heard it and gone very still, listening to see what the man in the gold-eyed body would do.

He did nothing. He went on talking to the young lord in front of him, about the spring floods on the young lord's river, which were bad.

Ward, at his shoulder, did not turn round either. But Emeric felt his note change: the slow wall of it, which never moved, go hard all through at once, like water freezing across a pond in a single breath.

"No," said Ward.

He said it to nobody. He said it quite quietly, flat, looking straight ahead past Emeric's shoulder at the gallery's far window. The old lord behind them did not hear it. Nobody in the gallery heard it but Emeric and Ferra, on his arm, who heard it and did not look round, and whose fingers on his sleeve closed once and opened again.

Later, on the hull, in the dark, she asked him.

"What did he mean?"

"I don't know." Emeric lay on the bench in the bows with his arm under his head, and she sat on the deck beside him with her back against the bench and her knees up. Ward was on the gangway, a dark shape against the tier's one lamp. "He's never said. Not to me. Not once in nine years."

"Then it's his," said Ferra. She was quiet for a while. "He said no as if he knew."

"Yes."

"Not as if he thought. As if he knew." She put her head back against the bench, beside his arm. "I'll not write it down. It's not mine either." Then, after a while, very low: "I'm frightened of tomorrow."

"Of the knife?"

"There isn't a knife. You've had Saban's man and Ward and Bram's steel." She did not move her head. "Of the hall. Of being looked at. I've never been looked at. I've only ever looked." He felt her breathe. "I've got a sleeve now that three women spent two hours on. I'm going to walk up a hall in it in front of your father."

He put his hand down off the bench and found her hair in the dark, pinned up, and the loose strand that was always somewhere, and held it.

"He'll look at the roses," he said.

"I chose them because they're good embroidery."

"I know."

"That's all they are." She turned her head against his hand. "They're just good embroidery."

Below the tier the city's lamps were going out, one by one, ring by ring, and on the gangway Ward did not move, and the hull lay in her cradle with her stones asleep under the iron and the way home at her back, and nobody aboard her used it.
