Chapter 223 - A House Made of Windows

There were two ways to have more control stones, and he had known both of them since the Tuesday in the autumn when Ferra turned the book round and showed him what she had written.

The first was to find them. To send Saban's dealers out through the south and the north and the coast towns, one stone at a time through three different hands, and buy every grey stone that anybody had in a drawer. He had done that. It had given him three in a year, and the dealers had begun to notice that somebody wanted them, and a thing that somebody wants has a price that goes up and a trail that leads back. It was not a way. It was a delay.

The second was to grow them.

He sat at his desk in his new rooms on the first floor of the east range, with the window open onto the gallery and the round room below it, and the long-room table standing in the middle of the stone floor under the open drum, and did the arithmetic again, the way he had done it every night since the move. It did not change. It never did. That was the trouble with arithmetic.

A seed woke in thick air. A seed that woke, if it was planted, split and put up a stem, and the stem became a tree, and the tree, in a year or three or more, made pods, and every pod held eight. That was the book. That was all the book said, in its drawings, in the part of it that Zale could not read and nobody else could either: a seed, a sprout, a tree, pods, and round every picture of the tree the fine wavy lines that the scholar had drawn beside everything that stood in thick air. Nothing about how thick. Nothing about how long, or how much light, or whether it wanted water or hated it. Half a book. The half that showed you what, and not how.

And to grow one you had to plant one. And a seed that was planted was used. It was not a stone any more that you could bind and set in a wall and have do your bidding. It was a tree, or it was dead.

So he had to spend one. And he had one.

He went through them again, the way Ferra would, in order. The wall board had its stones: the ones it had always had, and the dealer's stone that had woken beside his mother's in the band, which had gone into the board the morning after the inventory, as Ferra had said it would, and filled the gap that had been there since the spring. Pull that one out and the wall was a stone short again, and a wall a stone short was a gate that did not know who was at it. The six bands on six wrists at the table were bound, every one, and a bound stone did not wake; he had watched Ward's ride up and down all summer and not change by a grain. The pump house had its own. The relay key's two halves were bound to him and to Ferra. The Mana Forge's deciding stone was the Forge. None of them were free. None of them could be spent.

There had been two in his mother's comb case. One of them, the root, he had taken into the forge in the winter of the Wayford net and shaped into a bead, and it had gone round his wrist on a cord and never come off, and it held the names of every knot in the net. Whatever it had been before the forge, it was not a seed now. He had made it into something else, with his own hands, before he knew what it was.

That left the other one.

The chipped one. Hers. It had lain in a comb case in a drawer in his father's treasury for twenty years after she died, and come south by Ward's road with its pair, and he had never once seen her hold it and never would. The one with the small bite out of its corner and the old break across one face, which had grown a fine grey net across the break in the band last summer, like a scar healing. It was in Ferra's box, in the silk, unbound. It was the only control stone in Kaldo that nobody was using.

He had known that since the autumn. He had not let himself look at it squarely until the winter, until the deck of the ship three miles up and Ward's voice in the dark telling him about a passage and a floor and a woman in grey wool who chose the floor. Before that, it had been a stone. His mother's, and precious, and a stone. After it, he could not pick it up without thinking of her hands.

He did not think she would have kept it in a box.

He did not know that. He knew almost nothing about her, and most of what he knew he had been told in the last three months. But he knew she had taught him at a slate herself, in a plain dress, because a boy could not learn from a crown. He knew she had put a duke on his face in a passage and then gone back in and asked what came after m. And he knew that she had kept a seed in a comb case with her combs, where she would see it every morning, and that he had kept it in a silk in a locked box for years and never once thought of it as anything but a thing to keep.

He took a sheet of paper, and wrote at the top of it, in his own hand, The chipped stone, to be planted. And the date. And took it down the stair to the counting room, and gave it to Ferra, because a thing like that went in the book or it did not happen.

She read it. She did not say anything for a long time. Then she got up and went to the box and unlocked it and took the silk out and unfolded it, and the chipped stone lay in it, grey, with the net across its old broken face.

"She'd have planted it," Ferra said.

He had not told Ferra what Ward said on the deck. He had not told anybody. He looked at her.

"Ward told me," she said. "Not what. That he'd told you. He came to the counting room the morning after and stood in the door for a while and said, I've told him about the passage, and went away again." She folded the silk back over the stone. "I don't need to know what was in the passage. I know what she was, from what you've been since." She looked at the paper. "I'll enter it. When?"

"When there's somewhere to put it," said Emeric.

He built the somewhere from half a book, and said so, at the table, before anybody else could say it for him.

"I don't know how thick the air needs to be," he said. "I don't know how much light. I don't know if the book's tree is the same tree as my stone, or if my stone's even a seed of the kind the book draws, or if it'll grow at all. I'm building a house for a thing I've never seen, from pictures a dead man drew of a thing he'd never seen either, as far as I can tell. And I'm going to put the last of my mother's stones in it."

"That's a very poor way to build anything," said Armand, from the far end.

"Yes," said Emeric. "It is."

Armand looked at him for a moment. Then he nodded, as a man nods at a thing he disapproves of and has no better answer to, and folded his hands.

"Then you'll want readings," he said. "At every stage. From the first day. A control, if there's any way to have one. And you'll want to write down every guess as a guess, so that when it's wrong you know which one it was."

"I know."

"I know you know. I'm saying it so Ferra writes it down."

Ferra had already written it down.

It had to be glass. That was the first thing, and it came from the book, because the book drew the tree in the sun. In every drawing there was the round of the sun above it, or the wavy lines of light coming down, and the tree's leaves turned up to them. A tree needed light. And the air round it needed to be thick, thicker than any air on the ground, as thick as the band or near it, and held that way, day and night, for a year or three, without leaking.

Stone leaked. Brick leaked. Wood leaked. The forge chamber held a flask a quarter hour, because it was brick. Iron held; the Drop House's bottle held its charge for as long as the bottle was shut. But iron was dark, and a tree in an iron box was a tree in the dark.

Glass held. They had proved it in the test row two summers ago: glass held as well as iron, and let the sun in besides. A pane of glass was a window, not a door. Everything he knew said so.

So: a house of glass, round, to sit on the drum. Fifty feet across, which was the drum's width, and rising in a dome to half that height again over the middle. Not one great bowl of glass, which nobody in the world could make, but a great many small flat panes, laid edge to edge on a frame, the way a honeycomb is a great many small flat walls. Triangles. Four kinds of triangle, each a little different from the next in one side's length, so that when enough of them were set together on a steel frame, their edges turned and turned again, a little at every joint, until the whole of them bent round into a dome and closed.

He had drawn the triangles the first evening in his new rooms and sent them to Orrin. He drew the frame now: steel struts, rolled at the rail works, each one cut to one of six lengths, meeting at iron nodes that Bram would cast with sockets at the right angles, so that a man with a spanner and a list could put the whole of it together without knowing what shape he was making until he had made it. He had seen domes like that in his old world, great ones, over gardens, over halls, over nothing at all, and had never once thought about how they were put together. He thought about it now, for a fortnight, with a rule and a pencil and the table's lamp, and filled the floor of his room with sheets.

The glass would be the island's glass. Orrin's new batch, the one with the white crust off the western flat in it, that ran like water in the pot and came out clearer than any glass Kaldo had ever made. Orrin had begun on the triangles without being told what they were for, and had sent up the first twenty in a crate packed in straw, each one ground true on its edges, and a note that said only: Still want to know how many.

Emeric sent back the number. It was large. Orrin sent back one word: When?

"Nobody walks into it," Emeric said.

It was the evening they drew the door, the four of them, at the table under the open drum, in the last of the light: himself and Bram and Ferra and Armand. Ward was in his chair by the door that led into the round room from the hall, where he had put it on the first day, as though the round room had a door that needed watching. It did now.

"Why not?" said Bram.

"Because it'll be the band in there. Or near it. Air as thick as three miles up, sitting on a stone floor in the middle of the city, day and night." He looked at Armand. "You've felt what the band does to a man in a night. You came down from the Hod feeling thirty."

"Forty," said Armand. "I was being modest."

"A man who walked into that dome every morning to water a tree would be walking into the band every morning. For years. We don't know what that does. We know what a summer of it did to the crew. And every time he opened the door he'd let a roomful of it out into the palace, and the palace would have it in its walls, and anybody in the city who could feel a stone would feel it." He drew a box on the sheet, at the dome's foot, where the glass met the drum. "So nobody walks in. Things go in. Water, and soil, and tools, through a box with two doors, glass and iron, one to the court and one to the dome. You put the thing in, and shut the outer door, and open the inner one, and reach through with gloves on long sleeves sealed into the glass, and set it down. Nothing opens straight from one side to the other. Ever."

"And if the tree needs a man?" said Ferra. "In there. With his hands."

"Then we draw it down first. Out of the dome into empty stones, until the air in there is only air. Then a man goes in, and does what he has to, and comes out, and we fill it again." He did not say how long a tree could stand thin air without dying. He did not know.

Bram was looking at the drawing of the box. He looked at it for a long time, with his head on one side.

"I've built this," he said.

They looked at him.

"The lock at the Drop House. The flask lock." He put his finger on the sheet. "Two doors. The outer one to the room, the inner one to the bottle. You put the flask in the lock, and shut the outer door, and open it into the bottle with the inner, and nothing gets out, because the doors are never open at once. That's this. It's the same thing. It's only that there, the charge goes in and the man stays out, and here." He stopped. "Here it's the same. The charge stays in and the man stays out. It's the Drop House lock with the dome on the other side of it instead of the bottle."

"It's the Drop House lock turned inside out," said Emeric.

"Turned inside out," Bram agreed, and took the sheet, and began to draw on it himself, which he did not often do on the prince's sheets. "And your charge. How does the charge go in? You're not opening flasks into it through the box. You'd be opening one a day."

"Not flasks." Emeric turned to a clean sheet. "The rig on the ship. The vial rig. But bigger. A cartridge. A glass tube the length of your forearm, as thick as your wrist, filled in the band like the vials, sealed like the vials, in a steel sleeve. And in the shell of the dome, low down, an iron collar, set into the frame, with a thread cut in it. The cartridge goes in the collar, and you turn it down the thread to a mark on the collar, the zero mark, and at the zero a spike at the bottom of the collar punches the glass, and the charge goes out of the cartridge and into the dome. And the thread's sealed round with the island's pitch, so nothing comes back out the way it went in."

Bram looked at that too.

"One cartridge a day?"

"I don't know. Until we know how fast the dome leaks, and how fast the tree drinks, I don't know." He said it again, because it needed saying again. "I don't know."

Ferra wrote it down. Rate of fill: not known.

There was one more thing, and Imani found it, not him.

He had asked her for beds. Nothing else. He had come to the ward one afternoon and asked whether she would like a glass house at the palace, a warm one, in the middle of the city, where she could grow what she grew in pots on the ward's sills all the year round, winter and summer, and have beds of it instead of pots. She had looked at him for a long time with her sleeves rolled and said yes, she would like that very much, and what was it for. He had said it was for her beds. She had said that was not what she had asked. He had said it was all he could tell her yet. She had said, in that case, she would have it, and she would ask again, and she would like the answer before she was old.

So the dome would have beds. Low ones, round the edge, under the glass, in good earth from the old farm, with Imani's feverfew and foxglove and willow cuttings and the grey shrub from the island in them. That was what the city would see, if the city ever saw inside it. A winter garden, for the physician, at the palace, which was the kind of thing a lord did for his physician. The yard men who carried the struts up the palace stair were already calling it that. The winter garden.

And in the middle, in a bed of its own, a stone.

"It'll glow," Imani said.

She said it at the table, on the evening they showed her the drawings, the whole table, with Ward at the door. She had looked at them for a long time, the triangles and the frame and the pass-box and the collar, and the beds round the edge with her own name written on them in Ferra's hand, and the space in the middle with nothing written in it, and then she had put her finger on the space.

"Whatever you're putting there. If it's thick air, like the band, it'll glow. Not much. Ned told me the deckhouse lamps come up on their own the moment the ship's in the band. A lamp stone glows brighter in thick air than on the ground." She looked up. "You're putting a thing that glows under a glass roof in the middle of a city, at the top of the highest building in it, where anybody on the wall can see it."

Nobody said anything. Emeric had not thought of it. He sat and looked at her and felt the particular cold that came when somebody at the table saw a thing that was in front of him and he had not seen.

"Screens," said Bram, after a moment. "Canvas. On rails round the drum's head, inside, under the glass. Drawn round at dusk, drawn back at dawn. Like a ship's deadlights."

"Every night," said Ferra. "For years."

"Every night." Emeric took the sheet and drew the rail, round the inside of the drum's head, and the canvas hanging from it. "Somebody draws them. At dusk. And writes it in a book that they did." He looked at Imani. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me," said Imani. "Tell me what it's for."

"Soon."

"You said that about the water," she said, and got up, and went back to the ward.

The last thing was the table.

He had known it would have to be, from the evening he drew the triangles. He had not said so. He had let the table stand under the open drum for a month and sat at it every evening with the others and looked up at the sky over it and not said anything, because once he said it, it was done.

He said it on the evening the first of the steel struts came up the palace stair on the yard men's shoulders.

"The table goes to the east range," he said. "The ground floor. The room beside the counting room, with the windows onto the court."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"We've been here a month," said Saban.

"I know."

"You drew this room for it. Years ago. You drew the palace round this room so the table would be in the middle of it and everybody in the city could look up and see the drum and know the book was under it." Saban looked up at the ring of stone and the sky. "You said so. At this table. In the old room."

"I know."

"Then why?"

Emeric did not answer. He could not, not with the yard men on the stair outside and the struts leaning in the hall and the whole palace full of masons and clerks and carters. He looked at the drum, and at the sky in it, and at the table under it, and did not give a reason, because there was none he could say out loud in that room.

Ferra closed her book.

"Twice in a year," she said.

It was the only thing anybody said about it, then or afterwards. She stood up, and picked up her chair, and carried it out of the round room and across the hall and down the east range, to the room beside the counting room, with the windows onto the court. After a moment Bram got up and picked up his, and followed her. Then Imani, then Saban, then Armand, with some difficulty, and Ward last, carrying his own chair and the prince's.

They carried the table in the morning, eight men and Bram. It went through the hall's doors with a hand to spare. They set it down in the room beside the counting room, under the windows onto the court, and Ferra put her book on it in the worn place, and opened it.

By the end of the week everybody in the palace called the room beside the counting room the long room. It was not long. It was square. Nobody remarked on it. The name had come with the table from the keep, and it went where the table went.

Weekes came up to see him about the dome.

The stone one. It had been on Weekes's list for four years, at the top of the palace sheet: the dome over the drum, in dressed stone, coffered, with a lantern at its crown. It was the last great piece of masonry in the city, and Weekes had been saving the best stone from the ridge quarry for it, and had drawn the centring for it himself, and was not a man who liked to have his list touched.

"It comes off," Emeric said.

Weekes looked at him.

"The stone dome comes off the list. There'll be a ring on the drum's head instead. Steel. Bolted to the stone, all the way round. And on the ring, a frame, and on the frame, glass." He showed him the drawings. "It's a garden. For the physician. A winter garden."

Weekes looked at the drawings for a long time. He was a man who had built walls and gates and towers and a palace in dressed stone for nine years, and had never in his life put a pane of glass on anything bigger than a window. He looked at the triangles, and the frame, and the ring.

"It'll leak," he said.

"It'll be tight."

"Every roof leaks."

"This one won't," said Emeric. "It can't."

Weekes looked at him a moment longer, as if there were something in the word can't that he wanted to ask about. He did not ask. He took the drawing of the ring and folded it and put it in his coat.

"The palace was always going to have a dome," he said, at the door. "I'll give it that. I'd drawn it for four years. I'd have liked to have built it." He went out, and then put his head back round the door. "Nobody knew it'd be glass," he said, as if that settled something, and went.

The ring went on the drum's head in the middle of the spring, bolted to the stone all the way round, with a crane on the drum's top that Fletcher lent from the dock. And the first of the struts went up on it the next morning, in the sun, with the yard men calling to each other across the top of the drum, and the first node, and the first three triangles of Orrin's glass, set in their seats and bedded in the island's pitch, catching the light, the way the new windows in the first quarter had caught it when the sun was low, and made the whole of the city glitter.

Emeric stood in his window in the east range and watched them go on, three panes, and then six, and then a row, and thought about his mother's hands.
