Chapter 197 - Tons

The first line Ferra wrote about the still week was a wage.

Hand pump, second tower. Four men, three shifts. Ward's. She wrote it on the second morning, at the table in the counting room, with the window open on an island that was already hot at the hour when it should have been cool, and the smoke from the bakehouse chimney going straight up into the sky like a plumb line. By the fourth morning the line said hired instead of Ward's, because Ward needed his men back on the wall and the gates, and there was a price beside it. Two Copper the shift, the man, paid at the end of it. She had set the price herself, on the flat, with the ganger's slate in one hand and her own pencil in the other, a Copper over what the night gang drew at the works, because the night gang could sit down between loads and the men on the beam could not.

By the seventh morning she had written the line six times, and the wind had not come back.

She walked up to the tower that evening because she wanted to see the dusk draw for herself. A figure somebody else wrote down was a figure. A figure she had stood beside was a fact.

The second tower stood at the low corner of the first quarter, where the ring canal came round below the last of the new streets, on the course Weekes had laid again in the spring after the frost took the first. It was a good tower. Everybody said so. It was stone to the tank and timber above, with the wind pump on its mast at the top and the vane on the mast, and at its foot the hand pump, and beside the hand pump the little square pump house with its slate roof and its door shut, which nobody went into, because there was nothing in it but a wooden plug in the mouth of a clay pipe.

The vane had not moved in seven days. It stood out from the mast pointing north-east, where the last breath of the last wind had left it, and the sails of the wind pump hung under it like washing.

On the beam of the hand pump there were four men, two to a side, and they were not talking. She knew two of them. One was a carter off the Grayrock road who had come in for the harvest last year and stayed. One was a lad from the island's tannery row whose mother had asked Ilsa in the spring when they would be drawn, and been told, and had not yet been able to go. They went up and down with the beam, slowly, in the heat, with their shirts off and wet through, and every stroke made a sound in the rising main like somebody swallowing.

The ganger stood by the tower with his slate. On the tower's side, at a height a man on the ground could read, was the float gauge Bram had fixed there in the spring, a needle on a painted board that climbed as the tank filled. The ganger had chalked a line on the board beside the needle, and a date, every dusk since the wind died. The chalk lines went down the board like steps.

"Where is it?" Ferra said.

The ganger showed her. The needle stood a hand below the mark, where a full tank would put it, and it was not rising, though four men were pumping. It was holding. That was all four men could do: hold it, against the taps in the old streets of the corner below, where people had lived since the second draw and were cooking their suppers.

"And at dawn?"

"Lower," said the ganger. "Always lower at dawn. They fill everything they've got at night, the women, against the morning. I would." He looked at the new streets beyond the corner, the fourth draw's streets, forty doors with new paint on them and nobody behind any of them. "Those taps give a trickle at dusk, mistress. At dawn they don't give anything. They're at the end of the main. They get what's left."

Ferra looked at the forty doors. Ilsa had the keys. Ilsa had had the keys for a fortnight, in a box in her room over the old weigh house, forty keys on forty tags with forty names, and the forty households on the island who went with the names had their beds roped and their chests packed and had been told to wait. They had waited. Every morning since the wind stopped, somebody from one of them had walked the four miles up from the island to look at the vane, and walked back down.

Imani was already there. Ferra had not seen her come. She was standing at the first of the new doors, the one nearest the tower, with the tap on the wall beside it open, and she was holding the back of her hand under it. A thread of water came out, and broke, and came again, and stopped.

Imani shut the tap and wiped her hand on her skirt. She did not say anything about it. She came across to Ferra and the ganger and looked at the needle on the board.

"Give me your book," she said.

Ferra gave it to her. It was the draw book, the one with the names in it, the one that went back to Ilsa every night. Imani turned to the fourth draw's page and took the pencil out of Ferra's hand, which nobody else at the table would have done, and wrote under the forty names, in her small square hand, without stopping to think about the words, as if she had written them already in her head on the walk up.

Stayed. No household into the tower's streets until the tank stands at its mark at dawn with no man on the beam. And her name, and the day.

She gave the book back. "Forty households in the heat on a trickle," she said. "By the end of the first week the women are carrying. By the second they're carrying from the ring, because it's nearer, and the ring isn't treated, it's canal, and there are children in thirty of those houses. You know what that is in summer. I've buried it." She looked up at the vane. "I'll not have it in a street I signed for. I'm sorry for them. I'll tell them myself, if Ilsa wants."

"She'll want," Ferra said. She read the stay again, and countersigned it, and closed the book on the pencil to keep the page.

The men on the beam changed over while they stood there. Four went off and four came on, and the four who went off sat down in the tower's shadow with their backs to the stone and did not get up. One of them had his hands open on his knees, palms up, and Ferra saw the blisters on them from where she stood, and wrote the wage in her head for the fourth time that day, because writing it was the only thing she could do about them.

She brought the coal sum to the table that night.

She had shown it to Emeric once already, in the thaw, in the long room with the red copper on the table in front of him and his plans for it spread out round it like a hand of cards. A wheel that did not saw. A shed that ran all day. Wire to the tower. A little machine in every pump house. And one day, lamps. She had asked him what turned it, and he had said coal, and she had turned the book round and shown him the coal line, and he had looked at it and said nothing, which was how she had known he understood it.

That had been between the two of them. This was for the table.

They were all there. Bram with his forearms on the table. Saban at the end with his chair tilted, which meant he was listening properly. Imani, who had come straight from the ward and had not taken her apron off. Armand in his chair by the hearth with the rug over his knees, though the room was warm, because he was always cold now in the evenings. Ward by the door. Emeric at the head of the table with his hands folded in front of him and his eyes on her book, not on her.

"The line brings in what it brings in," Ferra said. She did not open the book. She did not need to. "So many tons a day off the Grayrock hill, and every ton of it has somewhere to go before it's off the wagon. The converter. The rail works. The lung. The fireboxes in the keep and the ward. The back boilers in the first quarter." She said them in the order she had them written, which was the order they had asked. "In the thaw the third stand at the rail works went quiet, and gave back a little over three tons a day. That's the only coal in the barony that nobody's burning. I wrote it against the pumps in the spring, and I told Imani she'd have her taps."

"You did," said Imani.

"So." Ferra looked at Emeric. "A coal engine can turn every pump in New Kaldo. The second tower and every tower after it. Three tons a day will do that, Bram says, with some over. It can keep the telegraph's crocks up, if they ever need it, which they don't. The line can carry that. I've checked it twice, and Saban's checked it, and Tarrant's checked what he can dig." She put her hand flat on the book. "It cannot light the city. Not the quarters. Not the dock, when Fletcher wants to work it at night. Not the works on the night shift. That's another eleven tons a day, or twelve, and there aren't eleven tons a day, and there won't be till Tarrant opens the second pit, and he says two years, and he's never once said two years and meant less."

Nobody said anything.

"The other thing can," Ferra said. "The pair. The big one, when there is a big one. It'll light what you like, as long as it's fed. And it's fed on what comes down out of the sky on a clear night, and on nights that aren't clear nothing comes down." She looked at the window, where the night was as still as the day had been. "And on a night like this, it doesn't matter either way, because a night like this, nobody's pumping but four men at two Copper the shift. I've paid for thirty-six shifts. I'll pay for more tomorrow."

She stopped there. It was not her place to say what should be done. It was her place to say what things cost, and she had.

Emeric had not moved while she talked. He moved now. He took a clean sheet from the pile at his elbow and his pencil out of his coat, and wrote on it, not fast, one line, and turned it round on the table so they could all see.

What must not stop goes on what cannot stop.

Ferra knew the line. He had said it at this table in the spring, over the Milford plan, about straw and wind and a hand pump. He had said something like it years before that, in other words, the summer the water first ran down the collar joints from the tower on the western rise. She had written it down both times.

"The pumps go on coal," Emeric said. "Always. Every pump in the city, on a coal engine, whatever else is on the same shaft. The telegraph keeps its crocks and wants nothing from anybody. The sawmill wheel stays what it is, timber by day and the quarry by night." He put his finger on the sheet. "And everything else goes on the drops. The dock. The works at night. The lamps in the quarters, when there are lamps in the quarters. And when the drops stop, because it's raining, or blowing, or because we've run the stock down, then everything else stops." He looked round the table. "And the city goes back to what it was the week before. Not worse. Nobody's tap goes dry because a hull couldn't fly. Nobody's tap ever depends on a hull flying."

"And the ward?" said Imani.

"Your ward's on the island. It was never on a wire and it's never going to be, not the one you've got. It's got its stones and its fires and the gas. Nothing I build on the flat touches it." He looked at her. "When you've got the other one, in the city, it goes on the pumps' side of the line. With the water. I'll write that down too, if you like."

"Write it down," said Imani.

He wrote it, under the first line, smaller.

Bram had been looking at the sheet since it was turned round. He looked up now.

"One house, then," he said. "Not two."

"One house."

"One dynamo," said Bram, slowly, the way he said a thing he was building in his head as he said it. "The coal engine on one side of it and the other on the other side. And a clutch between them." He looked at Emeric. "The rolls' wheel. Wire by night and rail by day, and throw one out and the other in without stopping the wheel. That's what the second clutch was for."

"That's what the second clutch was for."

"The coal engine stands warm," Bram said, "with the clutch out. Banked, and in steam, and turning nothing, so it'll take the shaft the minute somebody throws it in. And a watcher at its gauge, the same as every boiler." He did not say it as a question. There had been a watcher at every boiler in Kaldo since the winter Rudd died, and there would be one at this one if it never drove anything in its life. "And when the needle on the other side goes down and doesn't come back up, your pourer throws out the drops and throws in the coal and fires her hard, and the pumps never know."

"And the dock goes dark."

"And the dock goes dark," said Bram. "And Fletcher swears. That's all."

Saban let his chair down onto its front legs. "Where?"

"Not the flat," Emeric said. "Not anywhere a street goes past it." He looked at Ferra. "I want to walk it tomorrow with Fletcher. It's his ground."

Ferra wrote Fletcher, the morning in the margin of her book, and under it, because it was what she did, a copy of the sheet's two lines. She looked at the second copy of them, in her own hand, and thought of the plan in the tin tube that had gone west with Farris, where the same rule was drawn in water and wind pumps and straw. She made a third copy on a slip, to go in the Saturday wagon to Milford, because Farris ought to know it was a rule and not only a drawing.

"The wire," she said, before they rose. "From wherever it is to the tower. It's a long way for copper."

"It'll lose some on the road," Emeric said. "Every wire does. The longer it is, the more. We'll draw it thick, and it'll still lose some."

"How much?"

"I don't know yet. Enough that you'll want a line for it." He almost smiled. "You'll book it."

She would. She wrote Loss on the wire at the foot of the page, and left the figure blank, and drew a ring round the blank.

Fletcher walked them round the yard wall from the inside, in the morning, in the heat, with his hat in his hand.

The wall had gone up the summer before, on the rest days of two of the earth mages, and it had been built for one reason, which was that the mages on the flat should not be able to see what was under the canvas in Fletcher's yard. It did that. It ran from the ridge rock down to the water on both sides of the levelled flat, higher than a man standing on another man's shoulders, and inside it you could have been anywhere, or nowhere, except for the sound of the lower water slapping the slips and the smell of tar. Hull two lay along the far wall under her canvas like a long hill. At the ridge foot, where the rock came down, was the outline of the dock, cut a hand deep into the grey stone and going nowhere yet, with Bram's first courses of pumice mortar standing along one side of it where the gang had begun them in the spring.

Fletcher listened to what Emeric wanted with his head on one side. A house. An engine house, a boiler and an engine and a dynamo, the same as the works, and a wall across the back of it, and behind the wall a thing Fletcher was not shown on a sheet and did not ask to be. Somewhere a cart could bring coal to, and somewhere a man could carry a sack of flasks to from the quay without going through a gate that anybody else used.

"Upper corner," Fletcher said. "Against the rock."

He said it before Emeric had finished. He pointed with his hat.

"The rock's your back wall. It'll not shake, and nothing's coming through it. It's away from the dock, so when the dock's full of men and mortar and I've got a gate I'm trying to hang, they're not climbing over your coal. The carts come in by the gate and go straight up the side, and never cross the slips." He put his hat back on. "And the flasks come in off the quay a hundred paces from it, and never see a street."

"And the dock wants light," Emeric said.

"The dock wants light," said Fletcher. "If you've got a way to give it light at night, I've got a way to build it in one summer instead of three. You gave me a mortar that sets in the sea. Give me the night as well, and I'll have walls on her before the frost." He looked at the corner. "How far to the city from here? The south gate?"

"About a mile," said Ferra, who had walked it.

"A mile of wire, then, and more round the ring road." Fletcher looked at her. "That's your column, not mine."

She had it already. She had had it since the night before, with a ring round it.

Emeric stood at the corner for a while, with his hand flat on the rock. Ferra had seen him do that before, at the pump house on the lake farm, at the dock outline in the winter. She did not know what he felt through his hand, or whether he felt anything, and she had stopped asking. When he took his hand away he nodded, once, and that was the house's place decided, as much as anything was ever decided in Kaldo before it was written down.

"And who pours," Fletcher said, "in my yard?"

He said it the way he said How many? about a ship: a question that came before the others because the others did not matter without it.

Emeric looked past him, down the yard, to the second slip, where Fletcher's second was standing in a boat's ribs with an adze, and had been standing there since before they came in the gate, and had not looked up once, though he had certainly heard every word.

"Cade," said Emeric.

Fletcher followed his look, and said nothing for a moment, and then said a word Ferra did not write down.

"He's my second," Fletcher said. "I've a dock to build. I've a lighter to float in it when it's built, and a ship after that I'll be building till I'm dead."

"He's inside every secret your yard has," Emeric said. "He's been inside them since the first ship. He held the Hod in the sky with Tobe, with his hands on a rail, till the skin came off them, and he didn't let go. He knows what a flask is and where it comes from and what it does if you're a fool with it. If I bring a works man down here to pour, that's one more man through your gate who has to be told what's under your canvas." He paused. "And I'd have to tell him. And every man I tell is a man."

Fletcher chewed on that. He was not a man who liked being argued with in his own yard, and he liked less being argued with by somebody who was right.

"He'll want a second of his own," he said at last. "For the nights."

"He'll have one. Wes teaches him first."

"The boy from the Hod." Fletcher grunted. "The one who blew the box."

"The one who'll never blow one again," Emeric said. "The one who knows what it looks like from the lock just before it goes. Nobody else in Kaldo does."

Fletcher called down the yard, not loudly. Cade put the adze down in the boat's ribs and came up the slip, wiping his hands on his breeches, and stood in front of them, a thickset quiet man with the fine pale lines across both his palms that Ferra had seen on Tobe's.

Emeric told him. Cade listened. When Emeric had finished, he looked at Fletcher, and Fletcher looked at the dock outline and did not look back, which was as much as Fletcher was going to say.

"When?" Cade said.

"When there's a house. Before that, go up to the shed behind the foundry and stand at the pair's lock with Wes, nights, and watch. Don't touch it. Watch."

Cade nodded. He did not ask anything else. He went back down the slip and picked up the adze and went on with the boat's ribs, and Ferra, watching him, thought that he had taken it the way he would have taken being told the tide had turned: as a thing that was so, and would want doing.

She wrote his name that afternoon, at the long-room table, in the roll where she kept every man the barony paid and what it paid him for. She gave him a fresh page, and wrote Wes on it first, because Wes had been doing it since the vent, and Cade under him. Above both of them, where a heading went, she hesitated with the pen, because she had never had to call them anything.

They were not stokers. A stoker fed a fire. They were not mages. They were not engine men, because Bram's engine men were the ones who stood at the coal. What they did was take a flask out of a rack and put it in a lock and turn a key, and what was in the flask went out of it into the box.

Pourers, she wrote, because that was what they did.

Armand was in his chair by the hearth. He had his eyes shut, and she had thought he was asleep. He was not. He had opened his eyes when her pen stopped, and he was looking at the word upside down across the table.

"Pourers," he said.

"It's what they do."

"It is." He looked at the word a moment longer, and something went across his face that she did not know how to read, and was gone. "They'll be the first in the trade," he said, "who don't pour anything of their own."

She thought he meant the wage, because a pourer's wage would be a works wage and not a mage's, and she said so. Armand said yes, the wage, and shut his eyes again, and did not explain, and she went back to the page.

Saban costed the flasks the next day, in the counting room, with the window shut against the heat, which did not help.

He had costed the Hod this way the first summer she flew: not in Rands, because nobody sold what she brought down, but in nights. A night was a night. It had a crew and a tow and a risk and a weather, and it either happened or it did not.

"Hull two, a full night, clear," Saban said. "Twelve flasks down, off her racks. The Hod, a full night, four." He wrote them one above the other on Ferra's slate, which he borrowed without asking, the way Imani borrowed her pencil. "The handcart first. The mages at noon, on the flat, their flask each with Hobb writing the name and the mark. That's first, always, because Armand says so and because if it isn't, the wall stops." He wrote a figure under the twelve and took it away. "What's left of a hull-two night, after the handcart, is eight, give or take. The pair eats one a day and a night, at the bench. The house will eat, what does he say?"

"Four a day," Ferra said. "Near enough. At the load he's drawn."

"Four a day." Saban looked at it. "So hull two goes up every second clear night all summer, not two in seven. And the Hod on the nights between, when Ned can spare her. And in a wet week nothing goes up, and you're eating what's on the shelf." He put the chalk down. "You can't get ahead of it. Not by much. And if you could, you couldn't stay ahead."

"Bram's pitch."

"Bram's pitch. A month, and then the lid weeps. You can't put by a winter's flasks against a winter. You can put by a few days." He looked at her. "How big is the store?"

"One room. Iron, behind the lock, the way he's drawn it. It'll take a rack of twelve."

"Three days, then."

"Three days," said Ferra, and wrote it, and under it, not more, because she knew somebody would ask one day why there were not more, and it would be easier to point at a line than to say it again.

Saban got up to go, and stopped at the door, as he always did.

"Who flies all those nights?" he said. "Ned's got one crew for hull two. He'll want two, if she's up every second night all summer. That's more men who know."

"I know," said Ferra. It was in her book already, under the flagship's page, in a line that said Crew: not known. It had been there since the winter. It had not got shorter.

When he had gone she sat for a while with the works book open in front of her, at the page where she had ruled a column in the spring, when the pair first knocked, beside the coal. Flasks. She had headed it herself. She had been proud of it. It was the first time anything that came down out of the sky had been written in the same book as anything that came up out of the ground.

She looked at it now, and thought about who read the works book, and on what day, and where the copy went on the Thursday tide.

Then she took a new book off her own shelf, plain, bound in board, the twin of the one by the pair's lock, and ruled it, and copied the Flasks column into it, line by line, from the first night of the pair to the night before, every figure, checked twice. And in the works book, beside the column she had been proud of, she wrote a line saying where it had gone, in the barony's own form, which no creditor had ever asked to see, because no creditor had ever lent against the sky. The flasks were not the works'. The works did not buy them. They were the barony's, and the Crown had never counted what came down out of the band, and she had never been asked to.

The pourers' wages she put in the works' general line, with the night gang's. They were works wages. It was true.

It was all true. That was what she sat with, when she had done it. Every line in both books was true.

On the ninth night she went up to the long room late, with the works book, and found him there alone.

He was at the table with the house drawn on four sheets in front of him, and a fifth sheet with the wall across the back of it that he had drawn over and over in the margin, thicker each time, a line of iron and a line of sand and a line of iron. He looked up when she came in and did not say anything, and she sat down across from him and opened the works book to the page she had been looking at all evening, and turned it round, and put it between them.

It was the coal page. The coal line for the works, by the week, and beside it, in a column of its own, the lamps they burned now: the two at the quarry on the sawmill wheel's current, which were in the book as carbons, so many to the week, because a carbon rod burned down and had to be bought. It was a small page. It added up.

"This is the book Vantic reads," she said. "Every quarter. Line by line, because it's the works, and the works are what the House lent against. She copies what the instrument asks for, and the instrument asks for this." She put her finger on the coal line. "In the autumn there'll be a new line on this page. An engine house, at the yard. It'll say what it burns, because it's a boiler and it burns, and the coal's bought and carried on the line and paid for, and there's no way to have a boiler in Kaldo and not have its coal in this book. And under it, another line. What it lights. Carbons for the dock, four lamps all night. Carbons for the works. Wire."

He looked at the page. He did not say anything.

"The coal will be small," Ferra said. "Because it's banked. Because it's standing warm with the clutch out, turning nothing, waiting for the needle. And the lamps will be large." She took her finger away. "A house with a stack that lights a city and burns what it burns will not add up. Not in my book. And my book is copied."

She did not say who by. She did not say where the copy went, or whose table it ended on, a day past Milford, in a house on the coast. They had both known that since the winter, and so had the whole table, and every one of them had agreed, because he asked it, to know it and leave it alone.

Emeric looked at the page for a long time.

"The coal engine's real," he said. "It's a real engine. It'll burn real coal, every week, and some weeks a great deal of it. There's nothing on that line that won't be true."

"That isn't what I said."

"No," he said, after a moment. "It isn't."

She waited. She had learned, in all the years of the table, that he nearly always had an answer, and that the answer was usually better than the question had deserved, and that the time to worry was the time he did not have one and said something anyway to fill the room.

He did not say anything. He sat with his hands flat on his drawings and looked at her page, at the small line and the space under it where the large one would go, and she watched him look for the answer and not find it, and not pretend he had.

"I don't know," he said at last. "I don't know what to do about that yet."

"Then I'll write it down that you don't."

"Yes," said Emeric. "Do."

She did not, in the end. There was no column for it. She closed the book on the coal page and took it down to the counting room and put it in the press and locked it, and went to bed, and did not sleep for a long while, listening to the stillness over the island, which was so complete that she could hear, from her window, a dog barking on the far shore of the lake.

The wind came back on the tenth morning, a little before dawn.

She heard about it from the ganger, who sent a boy down to the island with it because he thought she would want to know before anybody else did, and he was right. It came in off the sea, not much of it, a cool breath from the south that stirred the washing on the lines in the tannery row and put a wrinkle on the lake. Up on the flat, at the low corner of the first quarter, the vane on the second tower's mast swung round off north-east where it had stood for ten days, and hung, and swung again, and the sails of the wind pump turned over once, stiffly, like a man stretching, and then again, and then went round.

The men on the beam stopped pumping. The ganger told her they had not stopped at once. They had kept on for a dozen strokes after the sails went round, because their arms did not believe it yet, and then the last four had let go of the beam and stood back from it and looked up, and one of them had laughed.

She walked up and paid the last shift herself, at the tower, out of the purse, and wrote the names, and then sat on the pump house step and added the page. There were twenty-nine names by the end, and seventy-two hired shifts, and the figure at the foot of the page was a hundred and forty-four Copper, which was fourteen Silver and four, which was one Gold, four Silver and four Copper, for six days of four men keeping a tank from going down. Ward's men's three days were in Ward's roll and not on the page at all. She knew what it would cost to keep them on the beam for a winter of still weeks, because she had worked it out on the fifth night, and she did not write that down.

The needle on Bram's board had come up a finger's breadth since dawn. It would not reach the mark today. Perhaps tomorrow, if the wind held.

Imani's stay stood in the draw book, under the forty names, and would stand. The tank at its mark at dawn with no man on the beam. The wind would not give her that, not every dawn, not in a summer like this one, and she knew it, and so did Imani, and so did the forty households on the island with their beds roped.

Ferra stood by the tower a while after the men had gone, in the new breeze, with the book shut in her hand, and looked down the slope to the south, over the fold of the land, to where the yard lay at the ridge foot behind its wall. She could not see the upper corner from here. She could see the smoke from Fletcher's tar kettle going up over the wall and bending, now, bending north with the wind.

Then she went down to the island to find Ilsa, and tell her about the stay, and about how long.

"The autumn," Ferra said. "Late summer, if Bram's quick. Not before."

Ilsa wrote it in her own book, the census book, in the margin beside the fourth draw. Then she looked at it for a moment, and at Ferra, and went and got her shawl, and went out to tell forty households, one door at a time, that they were going to have to wait for a house that did not exist yet, in a corner of a yard none of them had ever been inside, to be fed by something that came down out of the sky, which she did not know, and which Ferra could not tell her.
