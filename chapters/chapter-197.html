Chapter 197 - Salt Water

The second coaster came up on the twenty-sixth of January with a passenger.

Saban's man on the Emberfell quay had put him on the manifest that rode up the road the night before, at the very bottom, under the empty sulphur sacks going back and three crates of Emberfell vine cuttings that Rowena had sent for the castle garden without asking anybody: one gentleman, foreign papers, paid his passage in Emberfell gold. Saban had read it out at supper. Nobody had thought anything of it. Gentlemen with foreign papers came up the river on the coasters now, two or three a month, to look at the iron road and the lamps and go home and tell people.

So Emeric was not on the quay. He was in the works yard with Bram and the straw tower, which was three courses of brick high and leaning, in the cold, arguing about the leaning; and the first he knew of it was Cade, running across the yard from the gate with his cap in his hand, which Cade did not do.

"Highness," said Cade. "There's a man on the slip asking for you."

"Saban can see him."

"Saban's seen him." Cade got his breath. "Saban sent me. He says you'll want to come yourself." He hesitated. "He says the man says he's your brother."

Bram stopped with a brick in each hand.

Emeric stood in the yard with the leaning tower beside him and the cold coming up through his boots, and for a while he could not think which brother. Corbin would not come up a river in a coaster; Corbin would not come at all. And then he could, and he put the trowel down on the tower's top course, carefully, so that it would not fall, and went.



Zale was standing on the slip with his back to the river, looking up at the town.

Not at the castle. At the town: at the wall with its arc lamps on their iron arms, unlit in the daylight, and the wire going away on its poles over the fields toward Wayford, and the works chimney beyond the east bridge with its smoke laid flat on the wind, and the long new roofs of the lower ward over the wall, slate and tile, row on row, and below them the dock quarter, still half thatch. He had a sea bag at his feet and a long leather case under one arm, and he stood with his weight on one leg and his head a little on one side, the way a man stands in front of a picture he has been told about and is deciding whether the telling was fair.

The sun had got at him. That was the first thing. He had been a pale boy, and his face and neck had gone the red-brown of a ship's rail, dry across the nose and peeling a little at the tops of his ears, with fine white lines fanned at the corners of his eyes where he had squinted at a great deal of bright water. Where his collar sat open the brown stopped as sharp as a tide mark, and below it he was as white as he had ever been. His hair had gone grey at the sides, the rest bleached at the ends to the colour of old rope, and he wore it short, like a sailor. The hand on the strap of the leather case was thick across the palm and dark in every crease with something that was not dirt, and on the back of it, gone soft and blue at the edges, somebody had pricked a small anchor. He had a coat of a cut Emeric had never seen, long and dark and buttoned on the wrong side, and a small gold ring in one ear. He was older than Emeric remembered, which was stupid; he had been twenty, and it had been fifteen years.

He was bigger, too. The tall boy in the nursery doorway had grown into the rest of his height: broad through the shoulders and deep in the chest, with a deckhand's forearms and the loose balance of a man who had spent years standing on things that moved. The strange coat hung off him the way coats hang off men tailors like to dress.

He turned round.

He had their father's nose, and Rowena's mouth. Emeric had forgotten that. He had forgotten all of it, and it came back in one piece on the slip, like a door opening onto a room he had once lived in: the nursery corridor, and a tall boy of twenty in a travelling cloak in a doorway, and a cup in his own hand.

And he was handsome. That was the unfair part. Fifteen years of salt and glare ought to have cured him like a side of bacon. Emeric knew what the sea did to men; the coaster masters who came up Kaldo's river were creased like old boots by forty, pink at the rims of the eyes, short of teeth, and the books from his other life had been no kinder about sailors. Zale had the creases at his eyes and nowhere else. The weather had been at his skin and stopped there, as if somebody had shown it where the line was. Under the burn he had the face the capital's painters had been paid to give their father, and had never quite managed.

How, Emeric thought. And then, because he had been in this world long enough to know where to look, he looked. His sense was open, as it always was now, and Zale sat in it like a banked furnace: deep and quiet and steady, a great deal more than any sailor had a right to. The son of the king and of Rowena, who could put Ward on his back. In this world the years went slower for the people who held the most. It answered the question, nearly. It did not explain the teeth.

"Emmy," said Zale.

Nobody had called him that in Kaldo. Rowena had called him it in Emberfell, more than once, and on her quay at the last. He found he did not mind it.

"Zale."

They stood and looked at each other. Saban had gone a little way off up the slip, and was very busy with the coaster's master over a sack of something; and Ward had come down from the gate behind Emeric and stopped at the top of the slip, where he could see everything and be seen to be seeing it.

"Mother said you'd stopped," said Zale.

"Did she."

"She said you'd stopped. And I said she'd said that before, the year you were sixteen. And she said, not like this." He looked at Emeric's face, carefully, as if he were reading it. "She said, he doesn't drink at all now, and he hasn't for years, and he never really did, and if you go up there expecting the boy with the cup you'll make a fool of yourself in front of his people, and I won't have it." He paused. "She was very definite. You know how she is."

"I know how she is."

"I believed her," said Zale. "Mostly." He looked up at the town again, at the wall and the wire and the chimney. "And then I came round the last bend this morning, and saw all this, and stopped believing anybody about anything." He picked up his sea bag. "Four baronies, Emmy. An iron road. Lamps on the wall that Mother says you can read by at midnight on the far bank." He shouldered the bag. "I've been to the far end of the world and back, and nothing I saw out there frightened me as much as this does."

"Frightened you?"

"Not for me," said Zale. "For you." He looked at Ward, at the top of the slip, and nodded to him, as one man to another; and Ward, after a moment, nodded back. "Is there anywhere in this town a man can get a cup of something hot that isn't sulphur? I've smelled nothing else for a month."



He had brought gifts. He laid them out after supper, on the long table in the hall, under the lamps, one after another out of the sea bag, like a pedlar who had been told he would be fed whatever happened.

The first was a box. Cedar, the size of a loaf, with a sliding lid, and when he slid it there were little bags inside, two dozen of them, of oiled cloth tied with coloured thread; and when he untied one, the smell came out across the table so strong that Maren, who had come out of the kitchen with the last dish and stopped to look, put the dish down on the nearest corner without looking where it went.

"Bark," said Zale. "From a tree I couldn't tell you the name of in any language you'd know. You grate it." He untied another. Little black seeds, wrinkled. "These you crack, and they taste of smoke and lemons both." Another: green pods. "These make your tongue go numb, and then you want more of them, and nobody can tell you why." Another, and another. "I bought them in a market where nobody spoke a word I knew, by pointing, and paid too much for every one. Mother wouldn't take any. She said Emberfell has enough things that burn."

Maren had come round the table. She stood over the box and put her nose to the bark, and to the black seeds, and to the green pods, and then she straightened up and looked at Zale with a face Emeric had last seen on her when the first crate of oranges came up the river.

"Who cooks for you," she said. "Out there."

"Nobody, mostly."

"It shows," said Maren, and took the whole box, and went back into the kitchen with it held against her front in both arms; and they heard her through the door, a minute later, say something to the kitchen boy at the top of her voice that made him drop a pan.

Zale watched her go with an expression of great peace.

The second gift was a bolt of cloth. Blue, a deep blue that went nearly black in the folds, so evenly dyed that Imani, across the table, reached out without meaning to and rubbed the edge of it between her finger and thumb.

"For your wife," said Zale. "If you'd got one. I didn't know." He smoothed it on the table. "And then Mother said," and he stopped.

He looked at Imani, whose fingers were still on the cloth. He looked at Ferra, beside Emeric, with her book closed under her hand, who was looking at the cloth too, and not at him. He looked at Emeric.

"Mother said there were two of them," said Zale. "I took her to mean two you couldn't choose between."

"He chose," said Ferra.

"Both," said Imani.

There was a short silence at the table. Bram, at the far end of it, made a sound into his cup. Saban looked at the ceiling. Armand, in his chair by the fire with the blanket over his knees, opened one eye and shut it again.

Zale looked down at the single bolt of blue cloth on the table between them, and then up at his brother, with a face so exactly like their father's in the moment before he laughed at a council table that Emeric's chest went tight with it.

"I've brought one," said Zale.

"We'll cut it," said Imani.

"Lengthwise," said Ferra. She had opened her book. "It's a long bolt. If it's cut across, one of us has the end with the selvage flaw." She did not look up. "I'll measure it in the morning."

Zale sat back in his chair and laughed until he had to wipe his eyes, and nobody at the table, not even Ferra, minded.



The third gift he took out more carefully. A little pouch of oilcloth, the size of a purse, tied at the neck, and he did not untie it on the table but held it in his hand a while, weighing it.

"These are the stupid ones," he said. "Every soldier on the north coast has a dozen. I know." He untied it and tipped it out onto the dark wood.

Flakes. Seven of them, thin, the size of a thumbnail, with sharp curved edges like shell. Emeric had seen flakes like them on a beach in the dark, a month ago, in their thousands; and had one in his own pouch upstairs that nobody at this table but two people knew he had.

These were not grey.

They lay on the table under the lamp and the lamp went through them. Clear, nearly; the faintest blue at the thick edge, the colour of new ice on a pond in the first hour of a frost, and as clean. Not a mark on them. Not the dull, scuffed, salt-bitten grey of the beach.

"Shard stone," said Zale. "Off the Isle. Dead, before you ask, the man swore it, and I believe him, because I've been carrying them for a year and they've never done anything." He turned one over with a finger. "A Kesrath sailor sold me them in a port on the far side of the western sea, drunk, for a meal. He'd been a diver once, for the old quarry families on the Isle, when he was a boy. Down at the west cliffs. Under the water." He tapped the flake. "He said the soldiers' ones come off the beach, and the beach ones are grey, because the sea's been grinding them since before anybody's grandfather. And these come up from under. From the foot of the cliffs, under the water, where the old quarrymen never went because they couldn't breathe down there." He shrugged. "He said they're the same stone, and they're dead too, and they're worth nothing more than the grey ones. But they were pretty. And he was hungry. And I thought of you."

Ward had come away from the fire. He stood behind Emeric's chair and looked down at the flakes on the table.

"Strain shields," said Ward. "The army cuts a sign on them. A man presses into it, and it holds off one arrow, and then it goes to dust in his hand." He looked at Zale. "I have seen it done. Once. It works. Once."

"Then they're not quite nothing," said Zale cheerfully, "and I've not wasted the meal."

Emeric did not touch them. He sat with his hands on the table on either side of the seven clear flakes and his sense open, as it always was now, and felt nothing from them at all; nothing, the same nothing he felt from the grey one upstairs. Dead, as the drunk diver had sworn.

From under the water. At the foot of the west cliffs. Where nobody could breathe.

He gathered them up, one by one, into their oilcloth, and tied the neck, and put the pouch inside his coat.

"Thank you," he said.

Zale looked at him, at his face, and at the place in his coat where the pouch had gone; and Emeric saw him see something, and decide not to ask what. Then Zale reached down beside his chair for the long leather case, and put it on the table, and did not open it.

"This is the real one," he said.



It was a book.

Big, the size of a ledger, bound in boards covered with a leather that had been dark red once and was brown now at every edge where hands had held it. It had no title on its spine. Zale undid the case's straps and slid it out and laid it on the table in front of Emeric, and opened it at the first page, and sat back.

Carving. That was what it looked like at first: not writing but carving, copied onto paper; rows of marks drawn very carefully with a fine pen, each one an outline, as if the man who drew them had been tracing something cut into stone and had wanted to show the depth of every cut. Angular marks, and round ones, and marks that were both, in long rows across the page from edge to edge. And between the rows, and up the margins, and crowded into every space the carving had left, a second hand: small, sloped, quick, in an ink gone brown, in letters Emeric did not know either, though they were plainly letters, plainly a man's notes to himself.

He turned the page. More carving. Another. A drawing, halfway down: a wall, in outline, with the carved rows on it, and a little figure of a man standing at its foot for scale. He turned on. Pages and pages. Rows of the angular marks, and the sloped brown notes swarming round them.

"His name was Ansel Vey," said Zale. "That's the one word on it I can read, and only because he wrote it on the first leaf in three alphabets, as if he was afraid nobody would know it was his." He turned back to the first leaf to show it: a line at the top, small, three times over, in three scripts. "He was a scholar. From a country past the end of every map you've got. He found a ruin, somewhere, with all that cut into its walls, and copied every line of it, and wrote down what he thought about it as he went. And he made a fair copy of the whole thing, this one, and sent it home." He shut the book. "And then he went off again, to look for where the writing came from, and he never came home himself. His family kept it thirty years. His granddaughter sold it, in the end, to a bookseller in the port where I was wintering, because the roof needed doing." He put his hand flat on the cover. "Nobody could read it. Not the bookseller, not the scholars at the college there, not a priest of their god who looked at it for a week and gave it back and wouldn't say why." He looked at Emeric. "I thought of you."

"Of me."

"The nursery lamp," said Zale. "You were six. You took it apart with a fruit knife because you wanted to see what the runes on the stone said, and you put it back together wrong, and it lit, and it went on lighting for a year, and nobody could ever make it go off again." He smiled. "I was already too big for the nursery chairs. I sat on the floor and watched you do it. I thought you were going to be a mage like your mother. A great one. And then you weren't anything, and I went away, and I'd forgotten." He tapped the book. "And then a bookseller put this in front of me in a cold shop in a strange port, and I remembered the lamp."

Emeric did not remember the lamp at all. He did not remember anything from before he was seven; there was a door there that did not open. He knew he had done it, now, because his brother said so, and he sat with his hand on the book and felt the strangeness of being handed a piece of himself across a table by someone who had kept it for him, without knowing, for more than twenty years.

"Armand," he said.

The old man was already holding out his hands.

They carried the book across to him by the fire, and Saban held a lamp, and Armand put on his spectacles and turned the pages one by one on the blanket over his knees, slowly, with his long fingers on the very corners; and the hall waited. He had handled books, Emeric knew, from every kingdom on the map and some that were not on it, in the tower's library, for sixty years. He turned a page. He turned another. He stopped at the drawing of the wall with the little man at its foot and looked at it for a long while.

"No," said Armand.

He turned another page.

"No. Not the carving, not the hand." He took off his spectacles. "I've never seen either. Not in the tower, not anywhere." He looked at the page under his hand with an expression Emeric had not seen on him before, something almost like offence, as of a man who has been told there is a room in his own house he has never been into. "Not one line."

"Nobody can," said Zale, from the table.

"Nobody can yet," said Armand. He put his spectacles back on.



Zale talked to him about Corbin in the long room, late, with the fire down and everybody gone to bed but Ward, who was at the window and did not count.

He did it as he had done everything since the slip, easily, with his feet up on the fender and a cup of Maren's tea gone cold beside him, as if they were discussing the weather in a town neither of them lived in. He did not ask about the works, or the wire, or the iron road. He did not ask about the bronze boat with the dome on her back that he had passed in the inlet below his mother's city, running down on three long legs with spray coming off her like a swan's, though he mentioned it once, idly, as a strange thing he had seen; and Emeric said that Kaldo built boats, which was true, and Zale said so he'd heard, and let it go, and did not look at him while he let it go.

"He'll answer it," said Zale. "Corbin. All this." He moved a hand at the window, at the dark town and the lamps on the wall. "You know he will. You've got four baronies and a road and a light nobody else has got, and Father likes you better than he did, which Corbin will have noticed before Father did." He drank the cold tea and made a face. "I worked him out when I was nineteen. He hasn't changed. He doesn't hit you when you're strong. He waits till you're looking somewhere else, and then he hits whatever you were looking away from."

"I know."

"I know you know. You were there longer than I was." Zale looked at the fire. "That's why I came. I could have written. Mother said write. But a letter goes up the post road through four baronies and a crossing house and whoever's paid to read it, and I didn't want to be the one who taught Corbin something about you he hadn't worked out for himself." He paused. "So I came, and I'll go, and anyone who's watching saw a brother come up the river with a bag of spices for the cook. That's all."

"Is anyone watching?"

"Somebody's always watching," said Zale, "or I'd have come home years ago." He put the cup down. "Where's your cup, Emmy?"

"Gone."

"The night I left. I said something to you in your doorway." He did not look round. "Do you remember it?"

"You're good at it," said Emeric. "Don't get too good."

Zale nodded slowly at the fire.

"I thought you'd heard it wrong," he said. "I thought about it for years, on ships. That you'd have heard it as me sneering at you, and gone on drinking to spite me, and I'd never be able to tell you." He was quiet. "I meant: you're good at being nothing. Be careful you don't turn into it."

"I heard it wrong."

"I know. I could see it on the slip." Zale smiled, a little. "You looked at me like a man who'd been owed an apology for years and had just found out he owed one."

The fire settled. Ward, at the window, did not move.

"You didn't turn into it," said Zale.

"No."

"You turned into something else." He looked round at last, at Emeric, and his face in the firelight was entirely serious. "I've no idea what. I'm not going to ask. Mother didn't tell me, and I could see in her face there was a great deal to tell, and I'm not going to ask you either, because whatever it is, the less I know about it the less anybody can get out of me." He stood up, and stretched, a tall sunburnt man in a foreign coat in the long room of a castle he had never been in. "Goodnight, Emmy. I sail on the morning tide."

"So soon?"

"Brothers who stay make people wonder what they've come for," said Zale, and went to bed.



Ferra was still in the long room when he came back down for the book.

He had meant to take it upstairs. He found it on the long table instead, open, under the lamp, with two sheets of paper beside it, and a pencil, and Ferra in her chair at the end with her shawl round her shoulders and her chin on her hand, looking at the first page of carving the way she looked at a ledger somebody had tried to hide something in.

"I couldn't sleep," she said.

He sat down across from her.

She had not looked at him longer than the business needed for a week. She looked at the book now, and not at him, and turned the sheet of paper toward him: a column of the angular marks, copied in pencil, one under the other, with a little stroke beside each.

"There aren't many of them," she said. "The carved ones. I thought there'd be hundreds. I've done four pages and there aren't. They come round again." She tapped the column. "Thirty-four, so far. That's all. Some of them a great deal and some hardly ever."

He looked at the column, and at her, and something in his chest that had been held shut for a week opened, very slightly, without asking him.

"That's how you do it," he said.

"Do what?"

"Read a thing nobody can read." He took the second sheet of paper. "In my other life, there were men who did it. Twice, that I know of. Once with a script on a stone that had a translation beside it, and once with a script that had nothing beside it at all, on clay tablets. The second man was an architect. He did it in his spare time." He drew a line down the paper. "He counted. Every sign, how often it came. If there are thirty-odd, it isn't letters for every word, it's sounds; a syllable each, or a letter each. If there are hundreds, it's words. You count them, and you find the ones that come at the starts of rows and the ends of rows, and the ones that come next to the drawings." He looked at the page with the wall on it. "And you find the numbers first. Numbers are always the easiest. There's always a list somewhere, and things in a list get counted."

Ferra looked at him for a breath.

"Then count," she said. "From page five. I'll go on from where I am."

They counted.

It was the most peaceful hour he had spent in Kaldo since the ship came home. The castle asleep around them, the fire down to red, the lamp hissing; the old book open between them under the light, with its brown margins swarming round the carved rows, and the two of them bent over it on either side of the table, each with a pencil, each with a sheet, making little strokes. He took page five and page six. He found the drawing of the wall again and counted the marks on the carved wall in the drawing, which were the same marks as the rows. He found a run of the same sign, five times, at the end of a row, and marked it. His strokes went down his sheet in fives, the way he had learned to tally on Earth, four uprights and a bar across.

She was faster.

He knew it by the second candle. He had done three pages; she had done six. She did not tally in fives. She did not tally at all, as far as he could see; she looked at a row, the whole of it at once, the way she looked down a column, and wrote a number beside each sign in her list, and turned the page. When he looked up, the third time, she had finished the eighth page and was waiting for him with her pencil laid down across her sheet, and an expression on her face that she was keeping very carefully off it.

"How many have you got for that one?" She pointed with her chin at a sign on his sheet: a square with a stroke through its corner, one of the commonest.

"Forty-four."

"In pages five to seven."

"Yes."

"It's forty-nine," said Ferra. "You've missed the ones in the drawing. And you've counted the one on page six twice, because it comes at the bottom of the page and again at the top of seven, and it's the same one, he's carried the row over." She held out her hand for his sheet. "Do it again."

"It's a tally, Ferra. It's not a ledger."

"Everything's a ledger." She did not take her hand back. "If you get the counts wrong you'll find the wrong numbers first, and then you'll read the whole book wrong, and it'll be forty-four's fault." She waited. "Again."

He did it again. It was forty-nine.

She took his sheet and laid it on top of hers and went down both together with her finger, sign by sign, and put her own figure beside his where they differed, and he watched her do it: her hand on the paper, the lamp on her hair, red going dark in the low light, the freckles across the back of her wrist where the shawl had slipped. The little line between her brows that came when a figure was not right yet. It went away when the last figure was right, and she sat back.

"Thirty-four signs," she said. "In eight pages. No new ones since page four." She put her pencil down. "So it's sounds. Like you said."

"Like the architect said."

"Like you said," said Ferra. "I don't know any architects." She looked at the sheet, and then, at last, at him. Properly; the way she had not looked at him for a week. "I'm not angry any more."

He did not say anything.

"I said I'd tell you," said Ferra. "When I wasn't. So I'm telling you." She squared the sheets on the table, edge to edge, without looking down at them. "I stopped on page six. When you missed the carried row." Her mouth moved, very slightly, at the corner. "It's hard to stay angry with a man who can't count."

"I can count."

"Forty-four," said Ferra.

She stood, and put the sheets inside the book's front board, and closed it, and pushed it across the table to him. Then she came round the end of the table, on her way to the door, and stopped beside his chair, and put her hand on the top of his head, briefly, the way she might have steadied a stack of paper before she took her hand off it; and took it off, and went to bed.



The coaster went down on the morning tide at a little after seven, in a thin cold mist off the river, with the vine crates gone and the sulphur sacks in their bundles on her deck and Zale at her rail in his foreign coat.

He had come down to the slip at six with his sea bag, and found Emeric there already, and they had stood together at the foot of the slip in the half-dark without saying much while the coaster's boy cast off her stern line. Then Zale had taken a folded sheet out of his coat and given it to him.

"The margins," said Zale. "The little brown hand. It's a language I picked up some of, three winters ago, from a man I shipped with who came from somewhere near where Vey did. Not much of it. Enough to buy bread and get cheated." He nodded at the paper. "I wrote down what I could last night. Their numbers, one to twenty, the way they write them. And a dozen words I'd swear to. Water. Stone. Wall. Old. Under." He shrugged. "It won't read the carving. But if your man wrote 'under the old wall' in his margin, you'll know."

Emeric put the sheet inside his coat, beside the oilcloth pouch.

The coaster's boy called from the rail. Zale picked up his bag.

"One more thing," he said. "And then I'll go and be seasick." He looked up the slip, at the town coming out of the mist, the wall and the lamps and the wire. "Corbin's waiting for the drunk. He's been waiting for seven years. Every report he gets from down here, he reads looking for the boy with the cup. He'd sleep better if he found him." He looked at Emeric. "Everybody who's afraid of you would sleep better if they found him. You might remember that, one day, when you want somebody to sleep."

He went up the plank. The coaster's engine coughed, and caught, and she backed off the slip into the river with the water churning white under her stern, and swung, and went away down into the mist with Zale at her stern rail with one hand up.

Emeric stood on the slip until the mist had her.

Ward came down from the gate and stood beside him.

"Your brother," said Ward, "notices a great deal."

"Yes."

"He did not ask about any of it."

"No."

Ward looked at the river where the coaster had gone.

"Her Grace's son," said Ward, which was the most Emeric had ever heard him say in praise of anybody; and went back up the slip to the gate, to count.
