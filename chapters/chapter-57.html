Chapter 57 - A Letter in a Hide Bale

Hollis sowed his barley in the second week of April, on the strip that had stayed bare all winter.

Emeric saw him from the lane, on his way out to the granary: a small brown man walking the bottom of his strip by the hedge with a sack at his hip, throwing seed in long even swings, left hand, right hand, not hurrying. Two men from the west side were leaning on the gate at the top of the field, watching him. Hollis did not look at them. He walked the strip to the end, and turned, and walked it back, and the seed went out from his hand in a fan each time and fell, and the men at the gate watched it fall, and in the end they straightened up and went on along the lane without saying anything, which was what Hollis had wanted, and was the whole of what he had wanted.

Emeric did not stop.



The hides came from Milford on the tenth, on a carter's flat wagon, eight bales of them roped under a cloth.

Bram had ordered them in March, after the second week with no barge, for the linings and the straps. Every breastplate wanted two straps and a buckle and a pad between the iron and the man, and every helmet wanted its lining, and Milford had leather when nobody else did, because Milford had cattle when nobody else did. The carter was a slow, cheerful man who had never been to Kaldo before and kept looking at the iron hoods on the walls. He was paid at the exchange door and given a cup of the spirit by Rylan, who wanted to see his face when he drank it, and he went back over the moat bridge in the afternoon with the empty wagon, whistling.

Bram opened the bales in bay three. He slit the cords on each one and unrolled it on the floor, hide by hide, to see what he had paid for, and in the third bale, between the fourth hide and the fifth, there was a packet the size of a man's hand, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with plain cord.

He did not send it up the hill. He brought it himself, across the east bridge and up through the lower ward to the dyehouse, with the oilcloth still on it, and put it down on the bench in front of Emeric and stood back.

"It was in the hides," he said. "Between two of them. Flat. Somebody put it there that knew how a bale gets rolled." He looked at it. "The carter didn't. He'd have been looking for a cup of something, not a letter."

Under the oilcloth was a letter folded small and sealed with plain wax, no mark in it. On the outside, in a square heavy hand, it said: To the prince at Kaldo. By no hand but his.

Emeric broke the seal.



It was not a long letter. It had been written by a man who did not write many, and who had thought about each sentence before he put it down and had not crossed anything out.

It said that Renner of Grayrock meant to come against Kaldo before midsummer. That he had asked Milford for its levy, and that Milford would give it. That the writer had no quarrel with Kaldo and wished it none, and that this changed nothing, because Milford was a plain of grass with no wall round any of it, and the baron who refused Renner his men this year was the baron Renner would come for the year after.

It said that everybody who had come into the writer's hall from the south in the last year had told him the same thing: that Kaldo's walls were kept by forty men, half of them a dead baron's; that it was the plague town still, and would always be; that the prince who held it was a young man with a fire trick and a great many lamps, who had spent a good deal of money in two years and would spend the rest. It said that nine men of Kaldo's own garrison had come through Milford last summer, on their way north, with a map of the town off their own guardroom wall, and the word of men who had walked its walls for years, and had sold the map twice in Milford and once more before they left.

It said that the writer believed them.

It said that he wrote so that the prince could do what a man does when he has been told, whatever that turned out to be. It did not say when Renner would come, or how many, or by which road, and it said why: the writer did not know, and would not have written it if he had, because a letter that said too much was a letter that hanged somebody.

The last line said: I am aware of what this makes me.

It was signed Paxton, of Milford. Nothing else.

Emeric read it twice. Then he put it down on the bench, and looked at the brown pots along the wall, and said a short word aloud in a language nobody else in Kaldo had ever heard spoken.

Bram, who had not left, waited.

"Paxton," Emeric said. "Milford."

"I know who Paxton is. I've bought his leather twenty years. Sends it on the day. Never short. Never once asked what I wanted it for." Bram looked at the letter without reaching for it. "Is it what I think?"

"It's what you think."

Bram nodded, slowly, and stood there with his hands hanging, and then went back down the hill to bay three, where there were eight bales of hides on the floor and a great deal more need of them than there had been an hour before.



He showed it to two people.

Ferra read it at her table, once, and turned it over to see whether there was anything on the back, which there was not, and laid it flat between her hands.

"Nine," she said. "The nine men. That's where the old map went."

"Yes."

"Through Milford. Sold three times." She looked at the letter. "He believes them. He says so." She was quiet. "He'd be a fool not to. It was a true map when they took it."

"It was a true map."

She did not say anything else about the map. She took out the page she had made at the end of March, with Bram's number for the breastplates on it and the garrison's under it and the line between them, and put it beside the letter, and looked at the two together.

"Then it's the iron," she said. "Before it's anything."

Ward read it on the wall walk above the South Gate, in the last of the light, holding it close to his face, and gave it back.

"When," he said.

"It doesn't say."

"They will not have decided." Ward looked out at the road. "A man who has decided does not ask a neighbor for his levy in the same letter as he names the day. He asks first. Then he counts what comes. Then he decides." He was quiet. "So we have until he has counted."

The letter went into a locked drawer in Emeric's room, with the key on his own neck, and not into the keep's chest with the barony's papers. A year ago he would have left it in the chest. A year ago perhaps three men in the garrison could have read it. Now most of them could, and some of them read anything they found lying on a table, out of pride, and he had taught them to.

Afterward he walked the wall alone, the whole circuit, under the lamps, and tried to see it with the map's eyes.

The map had come off the guardroom wall, a dead baron's clerk's drawing, and the men who carried it out had walked this circuit for years. Everything on it had been true last June: the gate, the towers, the stairs, the place above the lower ward where the walk narrowed to a man's width, the water gate at the foot of the east bridge. What it did not have on it was everything since. No lamps; there had been none. No wire. No line to the gate. No iron on anybody's head. Forty men in a dead baron's coats, half of them drunk by dark, and a gate that opened for anybody with a coin in his hand. That was the town Renner was counting his men against. Walking under the eighth lamp with its new carbons, Emeric could not decide whether that was the best thing he knew about the coming summer or the worst.

He wrote one answer, that night, and burned two drafts of it first. It said that Kaldo was not what the prince's neighbor had been told, and that the prince would not send proof of it, because proof traveled. It said that if Milford's colors were somewhere at the back on the day, and stayed there, nobody from Kaldo would go looking for them. It did not thank him.

It went west at the end of the week at the bottom of a cask of spirit, under a false floor Bram cut and the cooper's journeyman fitted, consigned to the steward's house at Milford in the ordinary way, on the wagon of the same slow, cheerful carter, who had come back for more spirit and was given a cask of it to carry and no idea what else he carried.



Bay three did not stop after that.

The works' wheel ran the hammer from first light until the river door was black, and some nights after, by lamp, with Tess at the bellows lever and the two boys at the sledges and Bram at the anvil in a leather apron gone hard as board with scale. The breastplates were simple. A front and a back, each one raised out of a single plate, curved to stand off a man's chest by a finger and let a blade skate, joined at the shoulders and the sides by straps of Milford leather. Bram made the first one for Declan, because Declan was the widest man in the garrison and if it fitted Declan it could be cut down to fit anybody, and Declan stood in the middle of bay three in it while Bram hit it with a hammer, front and back, to show him, and Declan said afterward that it was the worst thing anybody had ever done to him on purpose, and he had been trained by Ward.

By the end of April a dozen of the garrison were drilling in them at dawn, and Ward's yard sounded different: a dull knock when a practice blade landed, and a grunt, and no blood. Ward made them run the wall walk in them, the whole circuit, twice, and then once more, to find out which men could not, while there was still time to do something about it.

The Grayrock bar went into them a plate at a time, and the hull straps from the dead boats, and when Bram measured the stack each morning now he said the number out loud, and it was smaller every day.

The bows were harder.

Ward had wanted crossbows since November. A crossbow wanted a bow of steel that would bend and come back, a thousand times, and not take a set, and there was no steel in Kaldo but what Bram made, a little at a time, by packing bars of the river iron in charcoal in a sealed clay box and keeping the box at a red heat in the corner of the hearth for a week. What came out was blistered and uneven and had to be folded and welded and drawn under the hammer before it was any use at all. Bram swore at it in a steady undertone that the boys learned from and Tess did not.

"How many?" Emeric asked, in the third week.

"Bows? Twenty, maybe, by the end of May, if the boxes don't crack. Thirty if I don't sleep."

"And for the town? If men from the town wanted to stand on the wall with them."

Ward was in bay three that afternoon, looking at the bows, and answered before Bram could.

"The garrison," he said. "Only."

Emeric looked at him.

"I can teach a man to load and shoot a crossbow in a month." Ward picked up one of the bows Bram had finished, and bent it a little across his knee, and let it come back. "I cannot teach him in a month to stand on a wall at night with men coming at it and keep loading. He runs, and the man beside him sees him run." He put the bow down. "Give the town the water. The buckets. The lamps. The carrying. They will do that well, and it is not less."

Bram looked at the bow Ward had put down, and then at Ward, and said that was the most words he had ever heard Ward use at once, and Ward did not answer that either.



The line went upriver in the last week of April.

Ward wanted eyes at the bend, where the river came round out of the north and could be seen for a mile above it, and he wanted what the eyes saw to be at the keep before a rider could have got his boots on. So the gate crew dug holes up the river road for two miles, one every sixty paces, and the carpenter set larch poles in them out of the baron's woods, under a waiver Hale wrote without being asked, and the iron wire went up on the potter's bell knobs from the river tower to a stone hut on the high bank at the bend that had been a shepherd's once and had no roof. The carpenter gave it one.

The man who asked to keep the key there was the wall-crew man who had read his own name aloud in Doran's class last summer, and learned the six pictures in an afternoon. He went up with a second man, a blanket each, a week of bread, and a crossbow between them that Ward signed for in the book, and on the first evening, a little after sunset, the needle in the line room at the top of the keep swung once, slowly, to the left, and back.

All well.

Emeric was in the line room when it came. So was Ward, which surprised him. Ward stood at the window with his back to the needle and his face to the north, where the river went up into the dark between its banks toward the bend, and did not say anything until the lamp needed trimming.

"Two miles," he said at last. "It will not be enough."

"It's more than we had."

"Yes," said Ward. "That is why I asked for it."
