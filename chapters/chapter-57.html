Chapter 57 - The Place It Broke

He woke under three blankets with a fever coming up in him and Imani's fingers on his wrist.

"A day," she said. "In this bed. I am not asking."

He was up inside the hour. She said two sentences about that which he deserved and did not answer, and then she put a hot stone in the pocket of his coat, on the other side from his mother's, and did not say anything about doing it.

Ward gave him the night in order, in the keep, standing, with his hands behind him because they were cut.

The men in the treeline had not fought. They had not tried to fight. By the fourth hour they had stopped even pretending to come down the slope, and Ward had stood in a frozen field looking at fires that were being fed by somebody who wanted them seen, and the thing he had filed nine days ago and been told to leave alone had refused to stay filed.

So he had come back over the bridge with four men and left the rest out there.

The gatehouse was empty. The chair was empty. Nine was flickering, and while he stood and watched it, nine went dark, and then flickered again, which was in no card anybody had written.

The card said wake the prince. The prince was not in the keep. Hollis was asleep in his own bed with his arm strapped, and had not been sent for by anybody.

Ward had taken two men and the fastest boat in the river port and gone east round the island, and he had not known what he was looking for until he came round the point and saw the light on the water.

At first light the watch found the water gate standing open and the bar tidy against the wall, off its pegs. They found the ring bolt in the ledge stone, and a cord, and on the end of the cord a charged blank the size of a fist, still swinging in and out of a field it had been put there to lie to. The hull had burned to the waterline and gone down in the channel with two men in it. Doran had seen two men pass his window at the late bell and a lantern out on the east water afterwards, and had taken it for fishermen, and had gone back to his book.

Emeric sat with the flicker for a while, because it was the only part of the night he had not worked out yet, and then he had it, and it was simple and he did not enjoy it.

A dead zone fills back in from the edges. The node at nine had been sitting on the edge of one for the better part of an hour, going out and coming back and going out again as the world crept in around it, and every time it came back it had reported the fact faithfully to a stone behind a board in an empty room.

The wall had done everything he built it to do. It had done it to nobody.

Ferra came in mid-morning with the lamp log open on her arm and put it down in front of him without a word.

Otter, nine. Otter, nine. Pressed hard enough that the nine had gone through to the next page.

He remembered signing the sheet beside it. He remembered thinking about the first autumn and the runner coming up out of the stair swearing it was the same otter, and being pleased with the log, and being pleased with Ferra for making him keep it.

"When?" he said.

"Both on his nights. There are no others on the east run this quarter in any hand at all." She closed the book. "I did not see it either."

He went out to the market square before he went to the hall, because the town had seen a boat burn on its own water in the dark and a town that is told nothing invents something worse and then acts on it.

He gave them four sentences off the steps by the payroll board.

Men had come by water in the night to take him. They had failed. Two of them were dead and the rest had gone downriver before dawn. Anybody who saw a boat on either branch that they did not know by name was to send word to the gate, at any hour, and would be thanked for it whether it turned out to be anything or not.

He did not say a word about a stair, or a guard, or a boy.

They took it the way they took his numbers, which was without noise and with a good deal of looking at each other afterward, and by noon there were four fishermen on the east branch with nothing to do who had found a reason to be there anyway.

The hall took the rest of the morning.

The garrison was in it, because the garrison already knew. Ward's two men had sat in a boat and heard a knight say what he had done, and the file that walked the wall at first light had seen the bar standing against the wall, and no order Emeric could give was going to put either of those back.

Ferra sat at the end of the long table with the book open. Bram stood at the wall because Emeric had asked him to come and had not explained why. Declan and Osric stood with the line.

Rylan stood in the middle of the floor in a clean shirt, washed, with his hands loose at his sides. He had plainly spent what was left of the night deciding how a man ought to stand at the end of a rope, and had arrived at an answer, and was using it.

He gave his account the way he had once given the story of how he became a knight, which was in order, at a level pitch, without asking anybody in the room for anything.

Finn had gone out to Milford on a contract cart in the second week of winter, which was his own idea and Nessa's and had nothing in it but a boy's chest.

Eleven days later he had come in off a shift and found on his bench in the gatehouse a pine horse with one ear longer than the other, which had been in his son's pocket for four years, and under it a paper with a place on it and an hour.

He had gone. A man he never saw clearly told him that Finn was warm and had been fed and was sleeping in a bed, and that he would be home when two things had happened: when Rylan understood the board well enough to say how it was woken and by whom, and when a certain gate was left off its bar on a night he would be told about.

The prince, the man said, would be held and not hurt. A prince alive is worth a great deal to somebody and a prince dead is worth a hanging.

"I believed him," Rylan said. "There is no better answer than that one. He said a thing I needed to be true and I took it, and I have been taking it every day since, because the days you do not take it are the days you go home and look at your wife."

Every question he had asked in the gatehouse, he had asked for them. He had sat two nights that were not his because he had been told to learn the board and the only way to learn a board is to sit at it. He had logged the otters. He had lifted the bar at the ninth hour. He had gone up the hill to the keep and said the word flicker.

He did not ask for anything at the end of it. He stopped talking and stood there.

Ward put the case for hanging him, and he put it well, because Ward did not put cases he did not believe.

"Every man in this hall knows he opened that gate," he said. "If he walks out of here in his own boots, then what this garrison has been told this morning is what the gate is worth, and the answer is one child. Any man on that wall with a wife in this town now knows the price of his own post and so does everybody who might ever want to pay it."

"He was coerced."

"He was. And a man who was bought with fear once can be bought with fear again, and the next one will know exactly where to stand and what to take, because we will have shown him." Ward did not raise his voice at any point. "And there is the other thing, which nobody in this room has said out loud. Rylan is not on Kaldo's roll and never has been. He is crown household on a warrant out of the capital, the same as me, and what happened on that stair was an attempt on the life of a prince of the blood. If the capital ever hears of it, my lord, the sentence is not yours to give."

"No," Emeric said. "It is not."

He got up and came round the end of the table, which put him on the same floor as the man in the middle of it.

"I am not going to argue with any of that, because all of it is true," he said. "I am going to argue about where the gate was opened."

He laid it out the way he laid out anything he had built, which was in parts, and he did it looking at the room rather than at Rylan.

The system sent every real fault in the ring to one man, in person, wherever he was, at any hour.

The system published the way it was woken, on a card, at eye height, in the gatehouse, so that the men sitting the chair could do their work properly. Which was correct. Which he would do again tomorrow.

And the system ran through a town where the men who guarded it had wives and children living nine minutes' walk from the wall, which had been true since the first night he slept on the island and which he had known was true, because he had been told so on a road six years ago by a man who had explained to him, patiently, that the value of family in Kaldo to an organisation like that one is that family in Kaldo can be reached.

"There is one man in this barony who can mend a node," he said, "and everyone who works on that wall knows it, and now everybody who wants to take me knows it, and I built that on purpose and wrote the word deferred beside it in my own book, in my own hand, fourteen months ago."

Then he looked at Rylan.

"You didn't break my system. You were the place my system broke."

Nobody in the hall said anything.

"You will go home today," Emeric said. "You will be at home for seven days and the barony will pay you for all seven." He turned to Ferra without waiting. "Not leave. Owed. Put it in the book under the heading and read the heading out."

Ferra read it out. It was two words and it was the column where the barony wrote down what it had taken from a man and had not yet paid back.

"On the eighth day you will come back and I will give you work," Emeric said. "Come back as the man who came out of that boat. Not as the man who lifted the bar. I have the second one's name and the date and what it cost, and I will keep them, and you will never hear them from me again."

He did it in private with Ward afterward, upstairs, with the door shut.

"It is a crown matter," Ward said.

"It is."

"And you are not going to write it."

"No." He put three reasons on the table and he had them in order because he had been assembling them since the boat. "The capital is where they have always had reach. Whoever paid that level voice did not find him on a road outside Kaldo, and a packet describing exactly how close it came, with names in it, goes north into a building where I cannot see who reads it.

"The only proof I have is a confession out of the mouth of the man who carried my hands out of a fire when he could have had five minutes and a clean story instead. A court would take that as the whole case and hang him on it, and be correct in law.

"And the capital has never once answered a line I sent it. Not the census correction, not the war, not the ore. Four years of packets and I have had rulings and receipts and no letters. I am not putting the one thing that could hang a man of mine into a stream that runs one way."

Ward thought about it for a while.

"Then it never happened," he said.

"Then it is written down once, by me, and it never happened."

He wrote it that evening in the back of the book that had nothing else in it except a sentence about a rite and a man in vestments, and he put the date on it, and he sat and looked at the two entries together for rather longer than he meant to.

The yard did not like it, and did not hide that it did not like it, which was the best thing about the yard.

Declan said one careful sentence at the armoury door about what the line men would do with it, and did not repeat it. Osric did not make a single joke for two days, which was worse than anything Osric could have said. A man from the west file spat on the ground when Emeric came through the gate, and looked at him while he did it, and Emeric let it go past and told Ward that night to leave the man alone and to make sure the man knew he had been left alone.

Late, when there was nobody else in the gatehouse, he said the other thing.

"You were right."

"I know," Ward said.

Neither of them said anything about a yard three winters ago and a name said out loud in it, and neither of them needed to.

He went down to the lane on the second day.

Nessa had had it from Rylan, all of it, and her face had not decided yet what it thought of anybody, including her husband, including the prince standing in her doorway with a hot stone in his pocket because a healer was managing him.

Una's letter had come that morning on the Milford cart, four lines, asking whether Finn had got home safe for midwinter and whether he had remembered to give Nessa the cheese.

Nessa put it in his hand and let him read it and took it back.

"Write to her," Emeric said. "Tell her he is home and he is well. Nothing else, nothing about any of it, and not because I am hiding it from her." He was careful with this part. "Those men stood at her door and she opened it, and she has no wall and no watch and no guard on the road. The less she carries, the less anyone can take out of her. When it is safe to tell her the whole of it, I will come out there and tell her myself."

Nessa looked at him for a moment.

"She will want to know why the cheese never came," she said.

Wren came round the corner of the table then and asked whether he had brought his crown this time, and he said it was in the carriage, and she said there was no carriage, which was the furthest the argument had ever got.

The stone on the lane took him half a morning.

He took a node out of the board's spare box, which was full to twenty since the bench, and set it under the eave of the house on the corner where the lane turned, so that its field lay across the whole width of the approach. Inside, on the shelf by the door, he put one of Nevin's lamps, which lit when anything with weight and blood in it came up the lane and went out again when it had gone.

It was thirty seconds of warning. He said so, out loud, twice, because he did not want anybody in that house believing it was anything else.

Ferra had already confirmed the only part of it that could have been a problem: no money moved, and the stone was on the board's inventory, and a stone moved from one inventory to another by the man who owned both was not a new work under anybody's reading of the covenant, including the factor's.

He walked up the lane himself four times so Wren could watch the lamp do it, and on the fourth one she stopped watching the lamp and watched him instead, which was the end of that as a game.

Finn did not sleep.

Three nights. He went down easily enough and then he was up at every noise the house made, and at some point in each of them he asked whether the boat was burning, in a voice that expected an answer.

On the fourth day Emeric went to Nessa and asked her first, standing in the kitchen with his hat in his hand like a man asking for a great deal more than he was asking for.

"There is something I can do," he said. "I put a hand on a man and I can find the fear in him and take the edge off it. Not the memory. Not the reason. The edge. He would sleep tonight. It is not medicine and it does not last and I have only ever done it in Imani's ward with men who were going to die of being afraid before they died of anything else." He made himself say the rest. "I will do it once, if you tell me to, and I will not do it again after that, and I want you to hear the second half before you answer the first."

Nessa asked him three questions, and they were better questions than the ones the keep asked him, and then she said yes.

He sat on the edge of the boy's bed with his hand on the boy's shoulder through the blanket and found it the way he had found it in a bird with a broken wing in a box in his rooms, which was without difficulty, because it was the loudest thing in there by a very wide margin.

He took the edge off it.

Finn slept eleven hours, and woke up hungry, and ate like a hired man.

In the morning Emeric told Nessa exactly what he had done, and that he would not do it again.

"He has to put it down himself," he said, "with you and with his father, over whatever time it takes him. If the only way he can sleep is a hand from me, then I have given him one more thing to be afraid of losing, and I would be doing it because it is easier to watch."

"I understand you," Nessa said.

He was not certain she did. He was fairly certain she had decided to let him leave believing she did, which was a different thing and was her right.

On the eighth day he was at the gatehouse before light, because he had not been sleeping either, and because the first permitted hour was the first permitted hour and he was not going to be the one who was late for it.

It was still dark when the knock came.

Rylan was in harness. He had shaved. By the look of him he had been awake since midnight and standing outside since the hour before, waiting for the bell that made it legal.

"Tell me the work."

Emeric had a sheet of paper on the table behind him with eleven lines on it and none of them finished, because every time he had got to the fourth one he had gone back and started again.

"It is not written yet," he said. "Be in the yard at dawn tomorrow. It will be."
