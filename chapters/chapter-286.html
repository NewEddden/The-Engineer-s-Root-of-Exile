Chapter 286 - Coded

The tide went out on the morning of the twenty-fourth at a little before nine, and Emeric and Arthur walked out to the stone along the reef.

It was further than it looked from the dune. The reef went out from the beach's southern end in a long ragged crescent of black rock, round the lagoon's seaward side, wet and weed-hung and cut every few yards with channels where the water still ran through at the bottom of the ebb; and they went along it carefully, from rock to rock, with the surf booming on their left hand over the reef's outer edge and the lagoon lying still and pale on their right, in the low cold winter sun, for half an hour, until they came to the ring.

It stood up out of the lagoon's shallows a little inside the reef: the round of squared stones Emeric had seen from the dune, much bigger close to. A wall. A low round wall of great dressed blocks, each the size of a cart, fitted without mortar, going round in a circle the size of the castle's inner ward; broken now, in a dozen places, so that it was more gap than wall on the seaward side, where the reef had grown up into it and over it and through it, black rock and white shell knitting the old blocks into itself as if it were trying to swallow them. On the landward side it still stood. At the bottom of the ebb, its tops were a foot out of the water.

A pen. He knew what it was the moment he saw it close. A fish pen, a tidal weir, the kind of thing the fishermen of Fletcher's village built in the mud of the north-east arm out of stakes and wattle, to let the tide in over it and the fish with it, and hold the fish when the tide went out. This one was stone. And a hundred times the size.

And in its middle, in the dark water, the stone.

Only the top of it showed. A great rough grey shoulder of rock coming up out of the black pool at the pen's heart, at a slant, like the back of a whale; wet, weed-hung, crusted with shell; its upper end standing a man's height out of the water and pointing out to sea, north-west, over the reef, toward Kaldo. The rest of it went down into the dark at the slant, a long way. Emeric felt it go down. Forty feet of it, near enough, and fifteen through, set into the bottom of the lagoon at an angle in a socket cut out of the bedrock, gripped there by the rock the way a tooth is gripped by the jaw.

He stood on the pen's wall and felt it.

A gravity stone. Of course. The same deep slow weight in it that every gravity stone had, that the four under his carriage had had on the road south, that the Grayrock crater's had, that his own body knew from the inside. But bigger. Enormously bigger. And empty, very nearly; it had spent itself on the Hod, all of it, in one long pull, and since then it had been taking in the lagoon's thin mana again, slowly, a trickle, the way a dry well fills from the ground. In six weeks, he thought, perhaps, it would be full. In six weeks it would be waiting again.

"Is it alive?" said Arthur.

"No." Emeric thought about how to say it. "It's not alive. It's not a control stone. It's an ordinary stone. A very big one." He looked at it. "And there's nothing on it."

That was the thing. He had come along the reef expecting to see marks: a stone that did a thing, that pulled along a line and stopped and pulled again, had to have been told to do it, and the way you told a stone to do a thing, if you were anybody in the world but a delver, was you cut the signs on its face. A glyph, a ring of them, the way a commoner could etch a fire stone, the way the tower etched its watch stones on the treasury doors. He looked at the great grey shoulder of rock coming up out of the water at the pen's heart, at every inch of it that the tide had uncovered, under the weed, under the shell, and there was nothing.

No marks. No signs. Nothing cut on it at all.

"Then how does it know to pull?" said Arthur.

"I don't know," said Emeric.

He stood on the wall and thought about it.

A raw stone did nothing. Every mage in the world knew that. A raw stone sat in the ground, or in a crater, or at the bottom of a lake, and held what it held, and did nothing with it, ever, until somebody told it what to do. That was the whole of the stone trade. That was why mages were paid. But perhaps that was only what every mage in the world knew; and perhaps out here, at the end of everything, in a place nobody had ever come back from, there was a kind of stone nobody had ever seen, a kind that did something on its own because it had sat in one place in the sea for ten thousand years and learned to.

It would be a new thing. It would be a thing the tower would give its teeth for.

He did not believe it. He wanted to, a little, the way you want a strange story to be true; and he did not believe it, because the pull had been too exact. A line. Ruled. Along one axis, one way, at one pace, from full to empty. Nothing in the world did a thing that exactly by accident.

"I'm going to go in," he said.

Arthur looked at him.

"Into it," said Emeric. "The way I go into a control stone. If I can. If it has a room." He looked at the shoulder of rock. "If it hasn't, I'll know it's what it looks like. A big stone in the sea. And if it has."

He did not finish.

"Highness," said Arthur. "The hill."

"I know."

"Bram's still in bed."

"I know." He took off his coat, and folded it, and gave it to Arthur. "So this is how. I go in. I read. I don't change anything. Not one mark. I don't cut, I don't write, I don't touch a line. I read every line, the ones I understand and the ones I don't. And I come out." He looked at Arthur. "If anything moves. If the stone does anything. If the pull comes back. You don't wait for me. You don't try to get me off it. You go back along the reef as fast as you can, and you tell Ned to get her up, bent fin or no."

Arthur looked at him for a long moment with no expression on his face at all.

"No," he said.

"Arthur."

"I'll stand off. On the wall. Out of its line." He put Emeric's coat over his own shoulder. "Forty paces. Like Ward on the hill. And if it does something, I'll come and get you off it, and then we'll both go back along the reef as fast as we can." He paused. "You can tell Ward I wouldn't do as I was told. He'll understand. He'll have wondered why you asked me."

Emeric looked at him. Arthur at twenty-two, on the old wall of a fish pen at the end of the world, with the surf behind him and Emeric's coat on his shoulder; who had been eleven, a boy at Sela's skirts, the year Emeric came to Kaldo; who had carried Edric out of the Isle's pits with Emeric beside him; and who had said no to him, just now, flatly, for the first time in eleven years.

"Forty paces," said Emeric.

"Forty paces," said Arthur, and went.



He went down off the pen's wall into the water, to the waist, cold enough to stop his breath; and waded to the stone's shoulder where it came up out of the black pool at the pen's heart; and put both hands flat on it, on the wet rough rock under the weed; and closed his eyes.

And it had a room.

He knew it at once. The way you know, when you put your hand on a door in the dark, that there is a house on the other side of it and not a cliff. A stone with nothing in it was a wall; you put your hand on it and felt the stone and nothing behind the stone. This had a behind. He went into it the way he went into his own stones, slowly, carefully, the way he would go into a room in a strange house at night without a lamp, feeling for the floor; and the floor was there, and he was in.

It was coded.

Somebody had been in this stone. Somebody had gone into it from the inside, as he went into his, and cut it, in there, where no surface glyph could reach and no tower mage could ever see: the lines he cut himself into his own stones with his own hand, the inside cut, the thing only a delver could do. That was why there was nothing on its face. There did not need to be. Everything it knew was written inside.

He stood in the room of the stone with that for a moment.

Nobody delved. That was what the world believed, the little of the world that knew there was such a thing as delving at all: the high Church at Ostrel; whoever it was that hid from the Church; Kaldo. Three. A handful of people, in all the kingdoms, who could go into a stone and write in it from inside. And one of them had been here. Out here, on this reef at the end of the world, standing in the water to the waist as he was, with both hands on this rock, writing.

A man, he thought. A delver from the kingdoms. Long ago. A tower master who had found the way in before the Church buried it; or one of the Church's own, sent out here for some reason he could not guess; a man who had come to this island in a boat, and found the fish pen, and written the stone to fill it, and lived here, and died.

Then he looked at the lines.

He did not touch them. He kept his hands off them, in there, the way he had told Arthur he would; he looked. He went along the first of them as he had gone along the sleeping stone's, all autumn, in his own study with the golem's chest stone under his hand, reading, slowly, every mark; and the marks were small, and dense, and close, and turned their corners in a way he knew.

He knew them.

Not the meaning, at first. The hand. The way a man knows his mother's writing on a letter before he has read a word of it. The small dense strokes. The way a line went down and back and down again before it turned. The little hooks. He had been learning that hand since August, in the dark of the sleeping stone, and had nearly killed Bram with it in November; and here it was again, in a fish trap on a reef twelve hundred miles from the cave where he had first seen it. The same hand.

Not a man from the kingdoms. Not a man at all.

The old makers. The people of the swept city under the Isle; the ones who had carved the hall with the lists of the dead, and set the grey figures at the doors, and written the sleeping stone in the golem's chest a thousand years ago. They had been here too. They had been at both ends of the line on the rock in the bay. In the north, under the Isle, and here, at the bottom of the world, in the south; twelve hundred miles apart. They had been everywhere. They had been across the whole of it, the whole coast, and they had been good at this, better than anybody alive; and then they had gone, and left their stones behind them in their caves and their reefs, still doing what they had been told.

He stood in the room of the stone for a long time.

Then he read it.



It was simple. That was the first thing, and the strangest. After the sleeping stone, with its register and its routines and its joints and its guard, he had expected something as dense, as tangled; and this was a few lines. A child's lines, almost. A job.

Fill. He knew that one; it was in the sleeping stone too, the mark that told a stone to take in what it could. Fill, from the water, slowly, until full.

Then wait.

And while waiting, feel. That was the second line, and he read it three times to be sure, because it was a thing he had done himself, in his own hand, in the wall sensors on Kaldo's walls, years ago: feel the mana in the field round the stone. Not a man's note, not who; how much. How dense. A reading, like the wall sensor's needle, of how much mana there was in the stone's line.

And a threshold. A number, written in the old makers' marks for numbers, which he had learned in the hall under the Isle from the lists: if anything came into the stone's line that was denser than this, then pull.

Pull along the axis. Toward the stone. Hard, and steadily, all of it, everything the stone held, until there was nothing left; and then fill again.

That was all.

A fish trap. A stone set in the sea at the heart of a stone pen, tilted, so that its line ran out of the pen and out over the reef and away along the current; and filled, and waited, and felt; and when a shoal came down the current, a great shoal, dense in the water, densest thing in the sea, it pulled it in. Along the line, through the gap in the pen's wall, into the pen. And the tide went out, and the fish were in the pen, and somebody came down to the pen's wall with a basket.

He understood, standing in the room, why it had taken them.

The threshold was the old makers' threshold. They had set it for the world they lived in, a thousand years ago or more, when the mana in the world was denser, and everything in it denser; when a shoal of fish coming down the current was dense enough to trip it and the sea round it was not. Now the world was thin. Now the fish were thin, and fell under the line, and swam past the pen in their thousands and the stone did not feel them; and the stone sat full in its pen for years at a time, waiting, for something dense enough to cross it.

And then something did. A whale, now and then. A boat, once in a long while, if it came close, with men in it, a little denser than the sea. And once in a thousand years, a ship. A Kaldo ship, out of nowhere, low over the water at dusk, with nine people in her and two of them in suits full of charged stones, and racks full from a morning in the Band, and canisters of liquid mana behind iron in her hold; the densest thing the stone had felt in all the centuries since its makers left. The biggest fish there had ever been.

It had done exactly what it was told.

He went on reading. He read every line. He read the ones he understood and then he went back and read the ones he did not, slowly, mark by mark, the way he had not with the sleeping stone, because he would never in his life again step over a line in a stone because it looked like nothing.

There were dead lines.

That was the rest of it, under the fish trap's few lines; and they were most of the stone. Lines that went nowhere. Lines that had been written to do something and no longer did it, because the thing they were written for was not there to be done to: the way a bell rope in a house still hangs in the hall long after the bell has been taken down. He read them carefully, and slowly, with his head beginning to ache.

Sixteen of them were the same. Sixteen lines, each one reaching out of the stone toward something, and finding nothing, and ending. Sixteen. He counted twice. And all sixteen reached out toward one place, east, over the hills, across the island; and he knew, with a small cold feeling in the room of the stone, before he read a word of what the sixteen were for, what they had reached for. Sixteen sleeping stones round the rim of a slab six hundred feet long, in a bay on the far side of the island.

And a seventeenth line, longer than the sixteen, that reached toward the same place, and had written in it, among its marks, a mark he knew from the sleeping stone, from the cave's walls, from the lists in the hall. Not a number. A word. He had found it nine times in the sleeping stone's routines and had read it, after a month, as a place: the place where a thing that walked stopped, and stood, and was set down.

Here, written beside the long flat thing in the bay, it could only mean one thing.

The landing.

He stood in the room of the stone with his head aching and read the seventeenth line over and over. The landing. The long flat thing in the bay with sixteen stones round its rim was a landing. A place where something came down. Something big. Something that needed six hundred feet of flat stone to come down on, and sixteen stones round it to hold it, or lift it, or bring it in; and a stone in a reef on the far side of the island had been written, once, to talk to it.

And the fish trap was the last thing left. The smallest, cheapest job of the whole place; the thing that fed the people who kept the landing. When they had gone, they had taken the stones out of the dockhands at the quay, and left the landing's sixteen asleep; and this one they had left awake, doing the only thing it still could, filling and waiting and pulling, for a thousand years, for nobody.

He came out.



Arthur was there. Not forty paces off. Ten. Standing in the water to his knees beside the pen's broken wall with Emeric's coat on his shoulder and his face perfectly still, and when Emeric opened his eyes Arthur said, "Two hundred and ninety," which was the count.

"I said forty paces."

"You'd been in a long time." Arthur held out the coat. "You were bleeding a little. From your nose."

Emeric put his hand to his lip. It came away red.

"I've had worse," he said.

"I know," said Arthur. "I was on the hill path at midnight. With the stretcher." He looked at the stone. "Well?"

Emeric told him. He told him on the reef, walking back, slowly, with his head pounding and the cold water drying on his trousers in the wind: the room, the hand, the trap, the threshold, the sixteen and the seventeenth and the landing. Arthur listened to all of it without a word, the way he did, picking his way from rock to rock ahead of Emeric and turning, at the worst gaps, to give him a hand across without being asked.

At the reef's root, where it came back to the beach, he stopped.

"They were here," he said. "The ones from the cave. The ones on the wall in the delve that you showed us. The ones nobody knows about." He looked back along the reef at the pen. "They had a harbour. And a landing. And a fish trap for the people who kept it. And they went." He looked at Emeric. "Where did they go?"

"I don't know."

"Are they coming back?"

"I don't know that either," said Emeric. "I don't think so. I think..." He thought about the hall under the Isle. The lists, each smaller than the last. The last row, unfinished, half a row in. "I think they didn't go anywhere, Arthur. I think they stopped."

Arthur was quiet a long time.

"Then who's the landing for," he said, at last.

Emeric did not have an answer to that, and did not try to give one.



They found the boats on the way back to the Hod, in the dunes, at the bottom of the island's long north-west face, where the reef came in to the land.

Emeric had felt nothing there on the first night, because there was nothing to feel. Wood held no mana. Old wood held none at all. But Arthur saw them, from the dune's top in the clear noon light: a rib, black, standing up out of the sand between two dunes like the bone of some great animal. And then, when they went down to it, another, and the stub of a stem post, and further along the hollow, half buried, the whole of a hull, a little one, its planks gone grey and soft and split, lying on its side in the sand with the dune grass growing up through it.

There were eleven of them.

They walked the hollow between the dunes for an hour and counted. Eleven boats, along a mile of the coast behind the reef's root, where the current that came down past the reef from the north-west, Emeric supposed, came in close; where a boat that had been pulled across the reef by a stone in a fish pen would come ashore, if it came ashore at all. Old ones and older ones. Some were a few ribs in the sand, so old that the wood had gone to something that was hardly wood. One was a keel and nothing else, a long black spine lying in the grass. Three were nearly whole. All were small: fishing boats, a man's boats, two or three men's at the most, the kind that went out from a coast a day and came home at night, if nothing took them.

And there was a hut.

It had been a hut. It was at the end of the hollow, in the lee of the biggest dune, on a scrap of firm ground looking out toward the reef and the pen: four walls of driftwood logs and turf, fallen in, and a roof of something that had been turf and planks from the boats and was now a heap. A ring of blackened stones in front of it where a fire had been, a great many fires. A pile of shells beside it as high as a man's waist, mussel and limpet and oyster, bleached white. And beyond it, in a row, on the dune's slope, low cairns of beach stones, eight of them, each the length of a man; with a driftwood post at the head of each, weathered grey and silver, and on two of the posts, cut deep, marks that might once have been letters and were now only grooves.

Nobody said anything.

They stood in front of the fallen hut in the noon sun, Emeric and Arthur, with the surf booming on the reef behind them and the gulls crying, and looked at the eight cairns on the dune's slope.

"Castaways," said Arthur.

"Castaways."

"Pulled in." Arthur looked out at the pen in the lagoon, its broken wall showing at the tide's bottom. "Like us. When it was full. And they couldn't get off." He looked at the boats in the hollow behind them. "The current comes in. It won't let you back out. They'd have known that. Fishermen'd know that the first time they tried." He was quiet. "And they lived here. In that. On shellfish." He looked at the cairns. "And buried each other."

"Until the last one," said Emeric.

"Until the last one."

The newest boat was by the hut. Emeric had not seen it at first because it was the hut; or part of it. Its planks were in the hut's walls and its roof, some of them, and its keel lay along the hut's back, and its stern, the square transom board at its back end, was set up in front of the hut's door like a step, half sunk in the sand, its paint gone and its wood gone grey but still sound, still a board. It was newer than the others. Much newer. Its nails were iron and had rusted, and the others' nails, where they had any, were wood.

And cut into the stern board, deep, with a knife, filled with sand, there was a mark.

A fish, lying on a hook. Emeric knew it. Every boat in Fletcher's village had a mark like it cut into her stern, her man's mark, so that a boat that came ashore somewhere on its own could be known and sent home. He had seen them on the boats drawn up in the reeds at the village a hundred times. Fletcher's own boats had a ship's wheel. This one had a fish on a hook.

He knelt down beside it in the sand.

And by the hut's fallen door, propped against the doorpost that still stood, in the lee of the turf where the rain did not reach it, there was a board. A plank, from the boat, a forearm long, grey and smooth with handling. And down both its edges, and across its face in rows, cut into it with a knife, small and neat and close: notches.

Arthur picked it up.

He turned it in his hands in the noon light. He looked at the notches. Then, because he was Arthur, and because nobody else was going to do it, he began to count them aloud.

He counted the first edge. Then the second. Then the rows across the face, one row after another, slowly, his finger moving along them, his voice even and low in the wind off the sea. Ten. Twenty. Thirty. Emeric knelt by the stern board and listened. A hundred. A hundred and fifty. The rows got less neat as they went on, as if the hand cutting them had changed, or got tired, or got cold. Two hundred. Two hundred and five. Two hundred and ten.

"Two hundred and eleven," said Arthur. "Two hundred and twelve."

He stopped.

His finger rested on the last notch. It was not like the others. It was not deep. It was a scratch, short, shallow, as if the knife had been put to the wood and drawn across it once and then not again.

Arthur did not say anything else. He stood there by the fallen hut with the board in his two hands and his finger on the last notch, and did not move.

Two hundred and twelve days. Seven months, a little more.

Forty-one years ago this spring, old Wicket had said, at the inn, at the next table, on the first night of September, the night Zale came to say goodbye. His brother Davin went out after mackerel, south-east past the bar, the way nobody went, and the water took him. And every time they turned their boats toward him to go after him, the water turned them back. And the next morning, there in the haze, the drowned land. And then not.

Emeric put his hand on the fish on its hook, in the stern board, and felt the sand in the cut, and the cold.

"I know whose this is," he said.



They did not dig. Emeric would not let anybody dig; there were eight cairns on the dune's slope, and whichever one of them was Davin's, if any was, had been piled up by somebody else's hands forty years ago, and that was where he was, and that was where he would stay. But the board, he took. And the knife, which Arthur found under the fallen roof when they lifted the end of a plank: a fisherman's knife, its blade worn to a sliver by forty years of rust and before that by seven months of cutting notches and opening shells, its handle wrapped with tarred twine that still held.

He wrapped them both in his coat, and carried them back to the Hod.

That afternoon, sitting on the last dune above the camp, with the board across his knees and the sun going down over the marsh, he named the island.

He had not meant to. He had not thought that anybody should; a place that big had a name, surely, somewhere, in the old makers' marks on the rock in the bay, and it was not his to give it another. But it would need one. Ferra would need one for her book. Kaldo would need one, to say to itself, quietly, among the few who would know; and he sat on the dune and looked out at the lagoon in the long light, at the pen's broken wall and the grey shoulder of the stone coming up out of the black water at its heart, pointing out to sea, north-west, toward everything, as it had pointed for a thousand years and more. Waiting. Watching the sea for the shoal that would come down the current. A watchman on a wall that nobody had stood on for longer than anyone had kept a calendar.

At the far end of the line on the rock in the bay, twelve hundred miles north, there was another place, under another island, where the same people had kept another watch at another door. Here was the southern end.

"South Sentinel," he said, aloud, to nobody.

Arthur, who was sitting a little way down the dune with Declan's sleeping bird in his hand, turning it over, heard him, and said it back, quietly, trying it; and then went down to the camp and told Ned, and by supper the whole crew was saying it as though they had always known it was called that.



Ned had the fin straight by noon on the twenty-fifth.

It was not straight. Ned said so himself, at length, with his arms black to the shoulders and his hands raw: it was bent back to within a hair of where it had been, and a hair was a hair, and she would fly a point to starboard all the way home and he would have to hold her off it with his own hands for every mile, and if anybody aboard said one word about it he would put them out over the marsh. But it was sound. The root had not cracked. Cade had gone over every rivet. Bren had worked the jack for a day and a half without stopping and had not said a single word about tents.

They struck the camp in an hour. Abloh's tents came down and went into their bags, all eight, without a hole in any of them, which Emeric made a note of for Abloh. The driftwood fire was put out and buried. The trench was filled. Mira went along the dunes' foot with a sack and picked up every scrap of anything they had dropped, every rind and cloth and oatcake crumb, and when Sully asked her why, since there was nobody here, she said that was exactly why.

And then they went thin.

That was Emeric's order, and Ned agreed with it without a word. The stone in the lagoon was empty; he had felt it again that morning from the dune, and it was barely more than it had been on the twenty-third. It would not pull. But he did not know how it felt, how sharply, or how far, and he was not going to take a ship out across its line for the second time in his life with her racks full and her canisters open to the air on the strength of something he did not know. So the canisters went behind the iron in the hold, every one, and the hold's doors were shut on them; and the racks were drawn down to the least that would lift her, the rest drained into the spent stones in the scrubber's box; and Declan and Osric unshipped their suits' stones and put them in the iron locker; and the Hod lifted off the marsh at a little after two in the afternoon on the twenty-fifth of December, slowly, thinly, on next to nothing, with Ned at the wheel holding her off her bent fin and swearing under his breath, and went out over the dunes, and over the lagoon, and over the pen and the grey shoulder of the stone coming up out of its black water, low.

It did not move.

Emeric stood under the cupola with his sense pushed out through the house's iron, paying for it behind the eyes, and felt it go by under them: the great dense shape in its socket in the bedrock, nearly empty, filling, slowly, a trickle; feeling the sea round it with its old makers' threshold, and finding nothing in its line dense enough to wake for. A thin ship, on thin stones. A small fish.

They went out over the reef.

"Bring her up," said Emeric, when the reef was a mile behind them; and Ned brought her racks up, and her lift came back under her, and she climbed, and turned north-west, and went home.



They came down on the Mere at a quarter to nine on Long Table night, by the jetty, in the dark.

The town was lit. They saw it from twenty miles off, over the sea: Kaldo on its corner of the coast, every street of it lit, the avenues' long lines of lamps and the dome glowing in the middle like a lantern, and the station's chimneys, and the port; and closer, coming in over the marsh, in the lanes, the long tables, end to end down the middle of every street in the lower town, with their own lamps on them, a line of small lights down every lane like beads on a string, and people at them. The Long Table. Every lane in Kaldo set one table down its length on the twenty-fifth and ate at it together, and toasted the year's decadays, and had done since the first one, two years ago. From the air, in the dark, it looked as if somebody had drawn the town in gold thread.

Nobody in the Hod said anything as they came in over it. Sully had his face against the after window like a child.

There was a lamp on the jetty's end.

One. A battery lamp, a hand-lamp from the works, held up, steady; and as the Hod came down over the Mere's black water toward the jetty it did not move, and Emeric, at the cupola, felt the note of the woman holding it before he could see her, and the note of the woman beside her; and found that his hands, on the cupola's rail, had shut.

Ned put her down by the jetty so softly that the water hardly moved, bent fin and all, and held her there while Cade jumped for the jetty with the line. And the hatch came open, and Emeric went down the ladder, and onto the jetty's boards, in the cold.

Ferra was standing at the jetty's end with the lamp in one hand and her book in the other, in her grey coat, with her hair coming down.

She did not say anything. She looked at him, in the lamplight, very steadily, from his boots to his face; and then past him, at the Hod's hatch, where Arthur was coming down the ladder, and Mira, and Bren behind her, and Sully; and counted them. He saw her do it. Her lips moved. Nine. And then she looked back at him.

"You didn't turn when you said," she said.

"Ned did. Something else turned us."

"You've been nearly three days." Her voice was perfectly level. "Two days and twenty hours and three-quarters, from the time you were due. I had the figure. I had your water and your food and your charge. I knew you had another day, more if you'd been sparing, and you're always sparing." She looked at the book in her hand as though she had forgotten it was there. "I did the sum two hundred times. Every time it came out that you were down, not dead. Every time." She looked up at him. "It didn't help."

"I know."

"Don't," said Ferra. "Don't say you know."

And then she put the lamp down on the jetty's boards, carefully, so that it stood upright, and the book on top of it; and came the two steps across the boards to him, and took his face in her two cold hands, and kissed him, hard, on the jetty, in front of Ned and Cade and the whole crew coming down the ladder, and did not care in the least who saw; and did not stop for a long time; and when she did stop she did not let go of his face.

"Never," she said, against his mouth. "Never again. Not three days. Not without a word."

"The set broke."

"Then build one that doesn't," said Ferra, "before you go anywhere again." And kissed him again, shorter, as if putting a full stop at the end of a line.

Imani had not moved.

She stood a pace behind Ferra on the jetty, with her bag at her feet, in her scrubs under her coat, with her arms folded tight across her chest; and when Ferra let go of him at last and stood back, and picked up her lamp and her book, Imani was looking at him over Ferra's shoulder with a face Emeric had seen on her only once before, years ago, in the cellar, with her head on Ferra's shoulder, when she asked him to play it on the day.

She unfolded her arms. She came to him, and put her hand flat on his chest, inside his coat, over his heart, the way she always did, and held it there; and did not count. He felt her not count. And then she put her forehead against his shoulder, and stood there, for a long moment, without a word; and he put his arms round her and held her, and felt her shaking, a little, all through, the way she never shook, not over a table, not over anything.

"The beds are made up," she said into his coat, very low. "At the ward. Nine. Since the twenty-second, at midnight. Bettany's sat up with them three nights." She drew a breath. "I didn't know what I'd need them for."

"Nobody's hurt."

"I know. I can feel." She lifted her head. Her eyes were wet and her face was furious. "Mira's bled. And the boy. And Bren. I can feel it on them from here."

"It's all on your paper."

He took it out of his coat and gave it to her: the nine names in her square hand and the columns in his, every half hour, every pulse, every handkerchief, from nine on the twenty-second to the last count at two. She held it up to Ferra's lamp and read it, there on the jetty, in the cold, every line, with her lips pressed together; and at the bottom, under Bring them home. All of them, and Bring yourself, he had written, at midnight on the twenty-second, on the marsh, by the shuttered lamp in the Hod's house, with Declan whittling across the table: All nine. Down, not dead. Coming.

Imani read that three times.

Then she folded the paper, very small, and put it inside her scrubs, against her heart; and picked up her bag, and turned round to the crew coming along the jetty, and said, "Right. Mira. Bren. Sully. With me. Now," in her ward voice, and they went with her without a word, all three, because nobody in Kaldo argued with that voice.

Ferra stood on the jetty with her lamp and watched them go. Then she looked at him.

"What turned you," she said.

"An island."

Ferra looked at him for a long moment.

"There's no island," she said. "South-east. There's nothing south-east. I've done every chart."

"There is now," said Emeric.



Maren had kept them plates.

She had kept nine of them, in the castle kitchen, under cloths, on the back of the gas range where the warmth was; and had sent Saban to the jetty at nine to say so, and to say that if the crew went to the ward first she would understand, and if they did not go to the kitchen second she would not; and they went to the ward first, and then to the kitchen, every one, Ned and Cade and Declan and Osric and Arthur and Mira and Bren and Sully, at ten o'clock on Long Table night, and sat at the kitchen's long table in their marsh clothes with the mud still on them and ate Maren's Long Table supper off Maren's plates, cold goose and hot gravy and roast roots and a plum pudding the size of a man's head, while Maren stood at the range with her arms folded and watched every mouthful go down.

Osric ate with Tess beside him. She had come up the hill in her chair at a quarter past nine, at a speed nobody had ever seen the chair go, and stopped in the kitchen door, and looked at him, and said, "You were on watch the whole time and couldn't have flown it if you'd wanted to"; and Declan, at the table's end, had said, without looking up from his goose, "He wasn't"; and Tess had looked at Declan, and at Osric, and come in, and parked her chair beside Osric's stool, and put her hand on his arm on the table, and left it there the whole of supper, and said nothing else at all.

The rest of the castle's Long Table had eaten at eight. The six had eaten it, with the household and the knights and their families, at the long table in the great hall, and Bram had been carried down to it in a chair by four apprentices against Imani's orders and had sat at its head with his sling and his bandage and a glass of Kaldo spirit, and had made the toasts himself, in his own way, which was to stand up, say the name of everybody in the room who had a decaday that year, and sit down again. Ferra said he had said nine names that were not in the room. Nine. The Hod's. He had said them in the middle of the others, without stopping, as if they were there.

The great hall was empty by eleven, its long table cleared and its lamps out. Emeric went up through it, in the dark.



He played it in Ferra's room, on the warm floor.

He had not meant to play at all. He had meant to sleep; he had been awake, near enough, since the twenty-second. But he had come up the keep stair at a little after eleven to Ferra's room, because it was the warmest room in the keep and because Ferra was in it, and Imani had come up from the ward behind him ten minutes later with her shoes in her hand; and they had lain down on the quilts on the warm floor in front of the cold hearth, the three of them, as they had a month ago, in the dark; and Ferra had said into his shoulder, with her eyes shut, "You didn't play it."

"Play what?"

"The song. On the day. Imani asked you to play it on the day, the first year, and you did, and Doran sat with his hat in his lap. You didn't play it this year." She did not open her eyes. "Because you weren't here."

"It's still the day," said Imani, from his other side. "For three-quarters of an hour."

So he got up, and found the guitar in its felt case behind Ferra's door where he had left it on the twenty-first, and sat down on the warm floor with his back against the bed's foot, between them, and took it out.

No horn. No box. Just the guitar, as it had been in the cellar the first night, the wood and the strings, quiet; and he tuned it by ear in the dark, slowly, while the two of them lay on the quilts on either side of him with their heads against his hips and their eyes shut and waited.

Then he played the quiet one.

The midwinter one. From home. The song about a night, a still one, a long one, that everybody had sung once a year on the other side of everything, in the dark, with candles, badly; his father worst of all. He played it very softly, so softly that the pipes ticking under the floor were nearly as loud. He did not sing it. He had not sung it the first time either, in the cellar, years ago; the words were in his own tongue, that nobody in this world but the two of them had ever heard, the one Imani had said sounded like a man chopping wood. Tonight, again, he only played it, and let them hear the words in it if they remembered them.

Ferra was asleep before the end of the first verse.

He felt her go. Her head got heavier against his hip, and her breathing changed, and her hand, which had been holding a fold of his shirt, let go of it; between one chord and the next, the way she always slept, as if somebody had shut a book.

Imani held on to the second verse. She lay with her cheek against his other hip and her hand flat on his knee, and he felt her listening, the way she listened to a chest; and partway through the second verse, at the place where the tune came down and stayed down, low, for a long time, as if it were looking at something very small, he felt her hand on his knee move, a little, keeping time. And then not.

He played it to the end anyway. All of it. For nobody, in the dark, on the warm floor, very quietly, with the two of them asleep against him and the pipes ticking under the oak and, somewhere far below, through the keep's thick walls, very faint, the last of the lower town's Long Table singing something else, badly, in the lanes.

When it was done he put his hand flat on the strings, and the room was quiet.

He did not put the guitar away. He did not want to move. He sat on the warm floor with his back against the bed's foot and the guitar across his knees and their two heads on his hips, and looked at the dark window, where the stars were, over the sea, to the south-east.

Somewhere out there, two hundred miles over the black water, a stone the size of a house sat in a fish pen at the heart of a reef, filling, slowly, a trickle at a time; feeling the sea round it with a threshold set a thousand years ago for a world that was not there any more; watching. And beyond it, on the far side of the island, in a bay a fleet could lie in, sixteen sleeping stones round the rim of a landing that nobody had landed on since before anybody kept a calendar.

And in his coat, over the chair by Ferra's door, wrapped in a fold of it, a grey board with two hundred and twelve notches in it, for an old man in Fletcher's village who had waited forty-one years to know.

The clock in the ward below struck midnight. The day was over. He had played it on the day.

He fell asleep sitting up, with the guitar across his knees; and for once in his life, beside the two of them, he did not lie awake at all.
