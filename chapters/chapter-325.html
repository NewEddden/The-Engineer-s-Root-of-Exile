Chapter 325 - Noon Tomorrow

The last of the Church's lights went over the northern rim of the sky at twenty to five, and for a while after that Emeric went on feeling them.

Five hulls. The Lantern first, big and slow and lit from end to end like a church on a feast night; and four behind her in a ragged line, one of them low and labouring, held up by men pouring into her stones by hand. He followed them up the Ostrel road until they were a thread at the far edge of his sense, and then nothing; and opened his eyes, and found that his hands were locked on the K Discovery's rail and would not come off it for a breath when he told them to.

The capital lay to the north-east in its bend of river with every lamp in it lit, as it had been lit all night: a hundred and twenty thousand people who had stood at their windows since dusk and watched the sky burn over them. In the river below the walls, and in the fields along it, there were seven small red lights that were not lamps. He knew where every one was. He had counted them down.

The king was at the rail beside him in his plain coat. He had been there since the Lantern's bow went past in the dark with the can screaming red behind them, and he had not gone below, though Fletcher had asked him twice.

"Five," he said.

"Five. And eight of ours in the sky. Escort two's on the bottom of the river with nobody in her." Emeric made his hands let go. "Nobody dead. Of ours."

The king heard the last two words and did not ask. Emeric had felt it happen, on the K Amara's deck, in the worst of the boarding: a stranger's note, white with fright, very close to a boy's note he knew; and then the stranger's going out, as a lamp goes out when a door opens, and the boy's going on. The king would hear it at breakfast, as a figure. One.

The radio clicked in its box by the helm. Not the fleet's line; the long one, the home set, that Ambrose had tuned to the line room at the top of the keep seven hundred miles south. All night it had spoken only on the hour, to say that Kaldo was quiet. It was Tess now, too fast, and then slower.

"Kaldo for Discovery. They're on the south road. A mile out. Mistress Ferra says she's on the wall and you're not to talk." A pause. "She said it twice."

Emeric went to the box and put the brass cup to his ear, and did not talk.



Saban's wire had come at a quarter to five the morning before, from the halt at Wend's Cross on the Wayford line, in his man's figures: COLUMN SOUTH FROM LISLE. HORSE AND FOOT. GREY ROBES TWO. C. WITH THEM. A second slip said the man had ridden a horse to death buying them the day, because Saban's men put everything in. Ferra wrote the horse in the book before she did anything else.

Then she did what Ward had left her. Three sheets in his small square hand, sealed, in the drawer of her desk, KALDO HELD on the outside. Who held the walls. Which wards went down and in what order. Where the knights waited. And the last sheet, which was the gate: the passage between its two doors, what lay in the old towers' feet below the chalk line behind iron, and the lever in the east chamber under its steel cover. Who could throw it: any one of the three holding Kaldo, on Ferra's word.

She gave it to Waylon.

Armand raised his eyebrows at that, in the long room at six, and looked at Waylon standing by the door in a riding coat with straw on it, caught on his way to the stable; and said nothing.

"They'll come on horses," Ferra said. "The front of it. You'll know when a horse means it."

Waylon thought about that the way he thought about everything, as if it were a leg he was running his hand down.

"Yes," he said.

The three wards nearest the South Gate went down at ten that night, and the lodging houses along the Gate Avenue, and every house on the tram's last stops. She had argued it with herself for an hour and lost. If the gate held, it was a night's sleep for nine thousand people. If it did not, it was the difference between nine thousand people in their beds and nine thousand under four feet of concrete with the steel doors shut.

At midnight she went down to the big shelter under Lantern Square to see the doors shut. She did not need to.

It was warm down there and smelled of wet coats and lamp paraffin and four hundred people's breath, and it was very loud. They had drilled it every term since the campus opened and the children knew it better than their parents; they had gone down the dog-leg stair at a run, the older ones timing themselves against the clock at the stair's foot and shouting their times back up, and the benches had come down into bunks without anybody being told, and somebody had already been sick, and somebody had forgotten their coat. Somebody always forgot their coat. A girl of nine in a nightgown and one boot was telling a warden it was her brother's fault, he had been standing on it.

Sela sat under the far lamp with the south wards' roll, reading it a name at a time as she read it at Commencement. Each child that was there put up a hand or was held up to show her. Each one that was not, she read twice, and waited.

"Three missing," she said, without stopping. "Two at their gran's in ward five. The third's a Hobb. A grandson. He'll be on a stair somewhere watching the gate. They always are."

"I'll send someone."

"I have," said Sela, and read the next name.

Bram had built the steel doors on counterweights so that a child could shut them, and Ferra stood by them and watched a child do it: a boy of seven, chosen because he had asked, both hands on the bar and his whole weight behind it, the door going over slow and heavy and closing on the stair like a vault. He looked round to see who had watched, and found her, and went red. She nodded at him, as she would have nodded at a clerk who had got a column right.

Emeric had told her once, the night they costed the shelters, that where he came from children did this every term, against fire and the ground shaking and things that fell from the sky, and against men who came into schools with guns. She had written the cost at the foot of the column and not argued with a figure of it. She had told him the cost was not the thing she hated. He had said he knew.



Armand was on the wall walk over the South Gate without a coat, between two arc lamps turned down to a glow as Ward's sheet said, with his hands on the parapet and his sense out down the dark road.

He had looked about thirty-two since the stone. Ferra had not got used to it. He had been an old man at her table for four years, with a stick and a way of lowering himself into a chair in stages, and now he went up the wall stair two at a time and stood at a parapet in December in his shirt. Only the voice was the same.

"A hundred and thirty-eight," he said, before she reached him. "Forty-one horse in front. The foot in fours behind; they've walked all night. Two grey mages in the middle of the foot, carrying something each in a bag that I'd rather they weren't."

"Forcing stones."

"For the castle, I'd think. They've been told a great deal by somebody who's walked our streets." He was quiet. "I'd forgotten how a grey mage feels. Like a man holding a bucket he's been told is full of water."

"Callis?"

"Somebody at the front who knows the road. Frightened and not showing it. I can't give you a name from here." He turned his head. "Where's the boy?"

Waylon was thirty-one. Armand had called him the boy since August and showed no sign of stopping.

Ferra looked down into the gatehouse yard. The east chamber's door stood open with a lamp inside, the steel cover up on its hinge, the lever under it, a plain iron bar with a wooden grip worn by nobody's hand; and Waylon on an upturned bucket beside it with his elbows on his knees and a straw in his mouth, and a big grey Grayrock sergeant with a rifle across his lap. They were not talking.

The passage ran under the gatehouse between its two doors, the length of a cart and team and half again, with the tram platform along its west side, and both doors stood open. That had been the hardest line in Ward's sheets. The South Gate had stood open every night for six years for the last tram and the milk carts, and somebody who had walked Kaldo's streets would have said so. So it stood open tonight, with its lamps low, and the moat bridge going away into the dark beyond.

From the set at the stair head, Tess: "They're moving."

"I know," said Armand.

Ferra took out her watch and opened it on her palm. Seven minutes past five.



She heard them first: horses on a made road at a trot, a great many, like rain coming across a field; and under it the foot, men who had walked all night being made to run the last of it. Then the front came into the glow at the bridge's far end, four abreast, dark cloaks, no colours, no lamps, and a man on a tall chestnut in the middle of the rank with his hand up.

They saw the gate open. The front rank checked, the way a man checks at a door he was told would be unlocked and finds standing wide; and the man on the chestnut turned and said something, and the man beside him laughed, and the hand came down.

They came over the bridge at a canter.

In the east chamber Waylon was on his feet with his left hand on the grip and his right flat on top of the bar, as if it were a horse's neck. He was not looking at the lever. He was looking through the chamber's inner window at the outer door.

The front rank came off the bridge into the door, and Ferra saw the horses' ears go back, all four. They had heard their own hooves come back off the passage stone, or seen the tram rails shine, and they did not like it, and their heads came up. For a breath the front hung in the door on the edge of balking.

"Now," said Armand, very low, to nobody.

Waylon did not throw it.

The second rank came up into the first and pushed it in under the arch; a horse shied at the platform's edge and was kicked straight; and then the four heads went down a little and forward, together, and the riders sat deeper, and the front of the column came into the passage at a canter, committed, eight horses, twelve, a length from the inner door.

Waylon threw the lever.

She felt it in her feet: a single deep blow through the whole wall, like a ram on a door from the inside, and the arc lamps shook in their brackets. Down in the passage the light changed. The air in it was full of something, the way water is full of a current, and everything in it was going one way.

Outward.

The front of the column came back out of the door. Not running; thrown. Horses on their haunches and their sides with their legs going, men off them and over them, a man still in his saddle with his horse sliding backward under him on the cobbles and sparks coming off its shoes; all of it going back out onto the bridge into the next rank and the next, so that the column folded up on itself like a cloth pushed along a table. A horse went over the parapet into the moat and a man went with it. The sound came up a breath late, metal and hooves and bodies on stone, and screaming, men and horses both.

Waylon held the lever down, his whole weight on it. She could see his lips moving. At ten he let it up, and the passage was empty, and the air in it was only air.

Nobody in Kaldo had ever seen it work.

"The mages," said Armand. "Ferra. The mages."



The knights came up out of the ground on both sides of the road.

There were field shelters under the meadows either side of the Wayford road, as under every field round the town, turf over steel hatches that the farmers ploughed round and the children used for forts. Twenty of the sixty knights left at home had gone down into them at midnight in their dark plate and shut the hatches and waited five hours with Armand's numbers coming down a wire through the turf. Now the hatches went back, a hundred yards out, behind the place where the foot had stopped dead in a mass; and the knights went across the grass at a run, straight for the two places Armand had given them.

The first grey mage never knew. A knight was on him and his bag was torn out of his hands and thrown into the ditch, and the foot round him scattered like starlings.

The second one knew. Armand said "Oh," quietly, like a man who has dropped a cup. On the road a small frightened man in grey had his bag open, with a knight ten yards off and closing, and had been told what to do if anything went wrong; and his hands came together in front of his chest round something, and pressed.

The light stones in the gatehouse yard went out all at once, and the passage lamps, which were stones. The knight ten yards off stumbled, as if the ground had dropped an inch under him, and went on. The mage fell down. And on the parapet either side of Ferra the two arc lamps came up together, white and hard, as the sergeant threw the switch Ward's sheet said to throw; because the lamps on Kaldo's wall ran on a wire from Bram's dynamos on the river, and Bram was at the works tonight with his stokers, and a dead field could not find coal.

The white light went out over the moat and the bridge and the road for three hundred yards, and every man on that road stood in it.

Ferra looked at Armand. He was standing with his hands at his sides and his face very still.

"I can't feel anything," he said, in the voice he used to read a column. "Not you. Not the road. Not the boy." He put one hand back on the parapet, carefully, as if he were not sure where it was. "So that's what it's like."

She took his hand off the parapet and held it, as she would have held a child's on a stair, and did not look at him, because she did not think he would want to be looked at. She looked at the road instead, and counted, because somebody had to and he could not.

The column had been finished when Waylon threw the lever; the rest was the men on the road finding it out. The chestnut was down on the bridge, kicking. The foot was a mass with no front and no back, men looking round for an officer and finding a knight; and the knights went through them, not fast, because Kaldo's knights kept their strength in a dead field and the men on the road were tired commoners who had walked all night. They took the pikes out of men's hands and threw them in the ditch and put the men on their faces and went on to the next. Some ran north past the edge of the light, and Ward's sheet said let them, and the knights let them. Some of Lisle's horse got clear of the bridge and rode their own foot down to get away. Along the wall a hundred rifles of the garrison lay on the parapet in the white light, and nobody fired one.

It was four minutes by her watch. The yard's stones came back below her with a flicker, like a held breath let go, and Armand's hand closed hard on her fingers, once.

"There," he said. "There you all are."



The chestnut had broken both forelegs.

When the knights had the road Ferra went out through the passage with a lamp, and Waylon was already on the bridge, kneeling at the horse's head on the wet stone with his hand on its neck, talking to it in the low running nonsense with no words in it that she had heard him use in the stable yard. It had stopped trying to rise. Its rider sat against the parapet with his hands tied in front of him and blood in his hair, a lean man of thirty-four in a forester's leather coat, staring at it.

Waylon looked at the forelegs a long moment in the lamplight and did not touch them.

"Give me that," he said to the sergeant, and held out his hand. He shot the horse once behind the ear, and gave the rifle back, and stood looking down at it.

The man against the parapet said something hoarse. Waylon looked at him.

"You rode him in the mews yard every week in the spring, with the dispatches," he said. "He's got a split in the off fore you never had seen to. I told your farrier." He looked at the man's face. "You're Lisle's forester."

The man did not answer. He did not need to. Ferra had her book open on her knee on the cold stone and had already written it: Callis. Taken at the bridge. Known by W. She looked at the man, who was looking at the dead horse and not at either of them, and did not feel what she had expected. She found she wanted his name spelled right, and the horse in the book, and that was all.

"Two out of the moat," said a knight beside her, wet to the chest. "One's breathing badly. The mage who broke his stone's having a fit on the road. He's alive."

"Hospital. All of them that can't walk, in carts." She wrote. "Waylon. You held it for ten."

"Ward's sheet says ten." He took the straw out of his mouth, which he had forgotten was there, and looked at it. "If I'd thrown it when they first came in the door, the front would have had their heads up and the rest would've balked on the bridge, and we'd have thrown eight men and had a hundred on the bridge deciding what to do next." He put the straw back. "I waited till they meant it."

"Yes," said Ferra, and wrote that down too.



Emeric heard it through a brass cup seven hundred miles away, with his hand flat on the box.

He heard Tess say they were on the bridge. Then he heard her stop, in the middle of a word, and say nothing, and go on saying nothing; the set hissing and her breath in it, quick, because the gate yard under the line room window had gone dark and she did not know why. Four minutes by the clock over the helm. The king, who could not hear the cup, watched him watch the clock and did not ask. Then Tess said a word Bram would not have liked, and then, in another voice, that the wall lamps were on, both, white, they had never gone off; and then that she did not know, Mistress Ferra had gone down with a lamp; and then Ambrose took the cup from her and held the line open, and there was nothing but Ambrose breathing for a long time.

At twenty past six Ferra's voice came up it, flat and square and out of breath from the keep stair.

"Kaldo for Discovery. The gate held. The set worked. Waylon threw it and held it for ten. One grey mage forced his stone on the road; it closed in four minutes; the wall lamps stayed on. A hundred and three taken, nine hurt, two out of the moat. About thirty ran north and were let. Two horses dead. Ours, a knight with a cut hand." The pen. "Callis is in the keep. Waylon knew his horse."

Emeric shut his eyes.

"The children came up at six. Sela read them out. The Hobb boy was on a stair after all." A pause. "Aldane's colours weren't there. I looked."

"Ferra..."

"You're not to talk. You've a rod to drop. Go and drop it." The pen. "I'll be on the line for the naming. Written."

He told the king, in her order, because it was the right one. At the lever the king turned his head and looked at him. At the horse, and the forester, and the mews yard, he looked away, south, toward a place he could not see.

"Ten," he said at last. "He'll have counted it under his breath. He counted the strokes of a brush like that, when he was small." He put his hands in his coat. "I'd forgotten."



First light came up grey over the eastern hills at a quarter past seven, and the castle began to empty.

Fletcher had brought the K Discovery in to a mile and a half over the city's south side, outside the towers' reach, bow to the hill. Six hundred yards. Emeric had drawn the ring on the sand table in September with a string and a pin and stood over it every night since; and he had looked at the cathedral, and the close and its gatehouse and the chests on its two towers, and the streets the leaflet named. And the ring went out from the summit and took in the top of the Circle's yard on the north-west slope, and on the east shoulder, beside the close wall, it took in the castle. All of it. The keep, the gardens, the long gallery where Waylon had searched for a picture at eight.

He felt it go out room by room. The kitchens first, a hundred notes in the heat of the fires, going out of the kitchen door in a stream with what they could carry. The laundries; the stables, the horses' big slow notes going under the arch on long reins. The guard came off the walls. On the close gatehouse next door the breakers got up off their straw ticks by their chests and stood a moment, and went down the tower stairs with the guard, and left the chests asleep with nobody to wake them. The east wing. The audience hall. Corridor after corridor that he had grown up in, going dark, as if a man were walking through the house ahead of him blowing out candles.

"It takes in the castle," said Saban, beside him at the rail, with his eyes shut.

Every soul at the long table had seen it on Hobb's map. Not one of them had said it aloud, in two weeks.

"Yes," said the king. He had Ivo's long glass from the rack by the helm at his eye and did not take it down. "I paced it on the quarry floor. In my head, from the rim. I know how far it is from the altar rail to my bedroom window. I have walked it every feast day for thirty years."

By eight there was a knot of guards by the keep door, and one note in the keep, high up, in the royal apartments on the south side, that was not moving.

Emeric would have known it in a crowd of a hundred thousand. He had felt it across the hall at his farewell banquet thirteen years ago and across another at the investiture: steady and hard and very controlled, a great deal held down under a great deal more. It stood still in his father's rooms for a long time, and the guards by the door waited for it.

Then it came down through the keep a stair at a time, not hurrying, and out of the south door onto the top terrace, and the guards fell in round it. And it went down the gardens' stair. Terrace by terrace down the south slope, between the walled beds and the clipped yews, past the fountain, out of the gate in the lowest wall, and down the lane toward the edge of the dotted line, at a walk, with six men round it: the last note to leave his father's house, because a sheet of paper out of the sky had told it to.

The king had the glass on the stair. Emeric did not need one. A small dark figure among others on the white stone, going down. Not looking back.

Neither of them said anything.

Corbin's note crossed the line. The castle was empty. The king lowered the glass and gave it to Fletcher's mate, and went on looking at the hill.



Ward's voice came up the fleet's line at a quarter to eight, from the K Amara's belly hatch four miles above the city.

"Drop sight's on. Wind's what Linus said." The creak of him shifting on the hatch floor with his eye to Ivo's tube. "Name it."

It had been Ward's condition at the long table in August before it was the fleet's rule, and Ward held him to it by saying it once and waiting. Emeric put his sense down on the drill yard.

It lay on the east side of the lower city inside the outer wall, four hundred yards of beaten earth between the barracks and the wall, where the crown's foot had drilled in lines every morning of his childhood; he had watched them from the castle's east windows at six in their blue, wheeling and stamping, before his tutors came. He went over it end to end for a dog, a drunk asleep against the wall, a child. There was a pump, and a row of posts for the bayonet drill, and the barracks' blank back wall, and nothing living; the barracks' men had gone out to the gates at first light. He read the barracks room by room to be sure, and then the yard again.

"The crown's drill yard," he said. "East side of the lower city. Empty to its edges. Named."

"Heard," said Ward.

And seven hundred miles south, after a breath, in the cup by his ear: "Written."

Ward said now, to somebody on the hatch beside him. Emeric felt the rod leave the K Amara's belly, a long cold thing with its stone pushing, nose down, the fins holding its tail behind it; faster and faster, until it was faster than anything he had ever felt that was not lightning; a long time going down, and no time at all.

The sound came up long after: the deep thud he knew from the quarry, like a door shut very far underground. Then the earth of the yard jumped in one piece, and a column of earth and stones went up out of its middle two hundred feet, brown and slow, and stood against the grey morning, and came apart. The barracks' back windows went in. Every window on the city's east side shivered in its frame; he felt the shiver go out through the roofs. Then there was a hole in the middle of the crown's drill yard that a man could not see the bottom of, and a dirty cloud drifting east over the wall.

And the city moved. All night it had been at its windows and on its roofs, looking up. Now it turned, the way a field of barley turns under a wind: toward the sound, and then, after a breath, away from the hill. Out of the streets inside the dotted line that had waited, that had watched the lights burn in the sky all night and told each other it was a trick, a sheet of paper, nobody pulls down a cathedral from the sky. They came out of their doors with their children and their old people and their bundles, and went down the hill.

"They believe it," said Saban.

"They watched the sky all night," said the king.

Saban opened his eyes and looked west, past the keel, at the river where it came round the city, and the old bridge, and the dark line across the water under it.

"Boom's still manned. Two boats on it, men sat in their coats since yesterday waiting for us to come up the river." He shut his eyes. "My man's still believed."



The K Endeavor's cells were full by eight.

Imani had asked for him on the fleet's set, in four words, and Fletcher put the K Discovery alongside her over the fields west of the city, where the hospital hull hung with the red cross on her flat black flank. Her after hold was the cells, plain, steel-walled, benches and blankets and buckets; and in the passage outside them, at a crate for a desk, sat two of Ferra's clerks from the records hall, a young man and an older woman, with cards. Every man who came down the ladder had stood at the crate and given a name and a ship and a rank in the Church, if he would, before Imani's people looked at him.

"Three hundred and forty-one by the cards," said the older woman, before he asked. She had been Ferra's for six years and sounded it. "Twenty-two more on the K Amara and hull four not across yet. Forty-six in the ward forward. Ninety-one won't give a name; we've written them by ship and a number." She turned a card. "Eleven gave a name and asked for a room."

She laid the eleven out on the crate, apart. Nine were bleeders, the men who had sat in the holds drawing the cabins' mana off into stones by hand in shifts so the crews would not sicken; years of it, on the ships and before that at the mother house in Ostrel. "Mistress Imani says some have been drawn to nothing so long they've forgotten what it was like not to be." She touched another card. "That one had a sheet come through his ship's dome in the night. He asked me if it was true, the rite, the nine in ten. I said I only write the cards. He said he'd had a brother." She looked up. "He asked if the duke's town gave rooms to Church men the way it did the men off the Isle. I said I'd write it down."

Doran was in the end cell.

He had asked for it at the long table the night before the fleet went, as he had offered his letter: that if men of his Church were taken up off those ships, somebody should be with them who was a priest and not their enemy. Imani had said he would be sick in the Band. He had been, most of the night. He sat now on the bench among twenty men in torn white surcoats, the ones who would not give names, with a blanket round his shoulders over his patched coat, eating bread.

Emeric felt them as he came to the grating: frightened, angry, very tired, and held tight in a way he had felt before on the Isle, in the wardens they kept alive; a thing in the note that would not let go because letting go was the sin. Their eyes went to his eyes and away. One of them said a word under his breath. Demon.

"Leave them be," said Doran, to the bread. "Nobody's preaching. Nobody's asking anything. They've had a bad night." He held half the bread out sideways without looking to the young man beside him, who after a long moment took it. "We were talking about bells. His mother house has a peal of eight. My parish had one. Cracked." He ate. "Go away, Emeric."

Emeric went away.



Imani's ward was the forward hold, lit white, the mana racks along both walls in their plated sleeves with Hester's jars of broth among them, and forty-six men on cots in their white: burned, most of them, from their own oak, and broken from their own decks when the deck hold went out from under them. She had been on her feet since dusk and looked it.

"Wash your hands," she said, without looking round. "Then come here."

The man on the cot was perhaps twenty, cropped, a deck brother's brown under his torn white, his lips blue at the edges. He lay propped on his right side very still, the way a man lies who has found that every breath hurts and has decided to take as few as possible. His name was Piran. He had given it.

"His hull came down in the shallows below the east wall in the night," said Imani. "He went over the rail when she hit and his mates pulled him out of the river by his collar and sat on him on the wreck till our knights came down the bank at first light. Two ribs, left side. Not through anything. But they ride on each other every time he breathes, so he's stopped breathing, nearly; and if he goes on not breathing he'll have a lung full by tomorrow and be dead by Thursday." She looked at him. "I've told him that."

"She has," whispered Piran. His eyes were on Emeric's. Every man on the Church's ships had been told about the gold eyes.

"I've told him what I want to try," said Imani. "That it's me, and not the Light, and not God. That it'll be warm. That it'll tire me and might do very little. That he can say no." She looked at Piran. "You can say no."

"The brothers said..." He stopped, because it hurt. "Only the high clergy. The holy." A short breath, and his face went white with it. "Are you holy?"

"No," said Imani. "I'm the surgeon."

Piran thought about that for a long time, for a man who could not breathe.

"All right," he said.

"Bind him," Imani said to Emeric, and held out a roll of flannel from her apron. "Broad, round, not tight. Enough to stop the ends riding when he moves. If you bind him tight he won't breathe at all, and that's the lung the other way. I'll be in him while you do it. Don't talk to me."

So he bound him. He sat Piran forward with one hand on his chest and one on his back, the way he would have sat up a child, and passed the flannel round under his arms, broad and flat, while the man held his breath against it and let it out in a long shaking whistle; and Imani laid her hand on his side below the flannel's edge, and went in.

He had his sense on her, as she had asked him to on the twenty-third and never told him to stop. She went in slowly, hollow, reading: through the bruised muscle to the two ribs, the ends grinding a hair's breadth on each other, the blood gathered round them, the flesh round them hot and already working in its slow way to begin. And she found the room before she did anything. A commoner's, thin; a deck brother fed on bread and drawn on for years in a ship's hold, with almost nothing to spare. She read it a long breath, and he felt her decide.

Then a trickle. A thread of her own out of her palm, along the road her reading had made; not to the bone, which she could not knit and did not try to, but to the living stuff round it that was trying. She fed it at the rate the man's body would take it, which was very little, for a slow count of a hundred, while Emeric held him up and did not talk.

She took her hand away and stood with her other hand on the cot rail and her eyes shut, and put the heel of her hand into one eye, hard, and then the other, as if something were pressing on them from behind.

"Behind the eyes," she said. "Like the morning after wine. On the ward it was like running." She opened them. "He's less room than the dock man. Much less. I had to go slower, and slower cost more." She looked at Piran. "Breathe for me. As deep as you can. Don't be brave."

Piran breathed. It was not deep. But it was deeper than the last, and his face did not go white at the top of it, and he looked down at the flannel, surprised.

"It's the bandage," he whispered.

"Some of it," said Imani, and took out her book and wrote against the cot rail in her capitals, and Emeric read it upside down as he always did. RIBS 2, LEFT. DECK BROTHER, RIVER. ROOM LOW. FED 100. GAIN SMALL: BREATH DEEPER, HEAT LESS ROUND THE ENDS. NOT THE BINDING: BOUND ALONE A QUARTER HOUR FIRST, NO CHANGE. COST: BEHIND THE EYES. And under it, smaller: first one off the ward.

"Again at noon, if you'll let me," she said to Piran. "And at dusk."

"You're not holy," said Piran, as if he were checking.

"No."

"All right," he said, and shut his eyes, and breathed.

Imani took Emeric's wrist between finger and thumb, briefly, hard, and let it go.

"Go and count your cathedral," she said. "Eat something first." She was already looking down the row. "Forty-five."



The K Discovery came down over the close at half past nine with her shell up and every eye in the city on her.

Fletcher brought her in from the south at four hundred feet, over the emptied streets with their doors left open, past the castle long and pale and dark on its shoulder with its banners still up, and hung her over the west court with her bow to the three doors; close enough that Emeric could see the saints in their rows round the arches and the great round window and the charcoal grey two hundred years of smoke had made of the stone. The spire went up past her keel from the crossing, very thin, a hundred feet above her rail, with the Gilded Eye at its tip in the grey light.

The brass horns were on a frame on her bow, four of them, flared four ways. They had called the hours of every launch at the yard since the first lift, and the whole of Kaldo knew their sound. The capital had never heard them.

Fletcher's mate held out the mouthpiece on its cord, and the king took it and looked at it a moment, as a man looks at a pen he has been handed to sign something he has already read.

Nothing on the leaflet had said he would be there. For nine months the Lieutenant's criers had told the city that the king was the traitor duke's prisoner in the south, sick, or mad, or dead; and the decoy hull with his standard had gone up over the Wayford road a week ago in front of every watcher on it.

"This is Harrold," said the king.

It went out of the four horns over the close and down the hill into the city: the voice that had said the coronation oath under that roof, made very large and a little strange by the brass. Emeric felt the city hear it. From the square to the outer wall, a hundred and twenty thousand notes went still, the way a room goes still when a door opens on a man everyone thought was dead.

"I am your king, I am alive, and I am above you," said the king. He did not raise his voice. "At noon this house will fall. You have read the sheet. Go six hundred yards from it, and further if you can. Take your old people and your sick and your children. Do not run. There is time." He paused. "Every gate of this city is open."

And he named them. Every gate in the wall, round from the South Gate in order, as a boy learns them at a tutor's knee; the bridge gate, the river stairs, the gates of the second ring, the roads out of the close. At each name Emeric felt the notes near that gate turn toward it and move. The king did not hurry it.

"Nobody will stop you. Nobody who goes will be hurt. Kaldo makes no war on you." He lowered the mouthpiece, and raised it again. "I was crowned in this house. I am sorry for it."

He gave the mouthpiece back. The city was very quiet. Then, from every street at once, it was very loud.



The tower by the old wool hall answered first.

It stood forty yards outside the ring on the second wall, south-west, a red pin on the sand table. Its crew had heard the horns like everybody else, and they had a dead-zone stone in a throwing frame and a grey mage beside it, and orders. Emeric felt the frame swing round.

"South-west. The wool hall tower. They're swinging onto us."

Fletcher had the brass guard up before he finished, and his hand on the lever under it; and looked at the dial by his knee. Emeric saw it from the rail. The needle should have been lying cold on the left of its face, six hours after the Lantern. It was on the right, in the red. The can had burned twice in the night, close together, longer than the cutoff liked, because there had been no other way; and its firebrick lining drank heat and gave it back for hours, and the strip on the shell read what the firebrick gave it. With the strip in the red the cutoff would not let the fire stones seat. It had been built so that no man at a helm could argue with it.

Fletcher put the lever over anyway. Nothing.

"Matrix," he said. "Up. All of it."

She went up the way she had gone up every day of her life before the can, on her stones, straight, at the pace of a man climbing a ladder; and the tower's frame swung its arm over, and the dead-zone stone came up off it in a long lob toward where she had been and very nearly still was.

It fell a hundred yards short, on the empty lanes of the second ring between the close and the castle, and burst; and a patch of the hill went out of Emeric's sense like a hole cut in a map, roofs and lanes and an open door, nothing, and his stomach turned over at the edge of it. The K Discovery climbed on above it, slowly, out of its reach.

The grey mage on the tower did not wait for the frame to be wound. He broke what was in his hand, a sour small note pressing on a stone, and what was in it was lightning. It came off the tower top in a white crack that lit the whole south face of the cathedral, and it did not go at the shell, because a shell was for things that flew. It went where lightning goes, to the tallest iron on the ship: the staff on her bow, with a copper strap down it to the keel, put there for exactly this and nothing else.

The whole ship rang. The speaking tubes rang in their brackets like struck bells and went on ringing; the deck jumped under his boots; the horns on their frame hummed for a breath like a man humming through his teeth; and the smell came up off the bow, hot copper and scorched paint. Then it was gone down the strap and out of her, and she went on climbing, and nobody aboard was hurt.

The king had not moved from the rail. He looked at his hand on it, and then at the bow staff, smoking a little at its tip.

Fletcher looked at the dial. Still in the red.

"You lying, useless, brass-faced little bastard," he said to it, quietly, with feeling. "You sat in the red all night and all morning, and the one time I want you out of it..." He rapped its glass twice with a knuckle. The needle did not move. He let the guard down over the lever with a clap. "I'm having that strip off the can when we're home. I'm having it framed. I'll hang it over my bed so I can look at it."

"It did what it was built to," said Saban, with his eyes shut.

"So did the staff. I've no complaints about the staff."

Then the bells began.

Saban's people had been in the belfry at the spire's foot since before dawn, five of them, up the crossing stair by a door whose key Saban's man had bought in October. The big bell first, the one that had told the city its hours for two hundred years, a great slow stroke that came up through the keel and the deck and his boots into his teeth; then the three small ones after it, falling, as they fell for the noon office. Then the big one again. Over and over, not the office and not the hours and not any peal the city had ever heard rung: the cathedral's own bells, going and going in its own voice, so that every soul in the city heard the house itself say it.

It was ten o'clock. They rang for a quarter of an hour.

And the last of the ring went. The ones who had heard the king and the rod and still sat behind their shut doors saying it was a trick came out into the lanes all at once, too many at once, and piled into the gates of the second ring, a press of frightened notes jammed in the gate passages. He felt a cart go over in it, and a man put against a gatepost; then the press break and go through and down. He held the rail and read it and could do nothing about any of it, and the bells went on under his feet.



Osric's team came down hull four's long ladder onto the close's south lawn at twenty past ten, forty of them in their grey with rifles slung and shields on their arms, and went in by the west doors and began to walk it.

Two files, one up each aisle, every chapel, every tomb, every door, as Osric had drilled them on a chalked plan of the nave on the cobbles at Kaldo; and the short set on his signaller's back with Emeric's voice in it from four hundred feet, telling them where every note in the building was before they reached it. That was the count. Emeric reading it, and Saban beside him with his eyes shut, and Ward from four miles up, and Imani when she had a minute; and Osric's forty walking every room they read, because Ward had said at the table that a man reading from the sky believes his sense and a man on the floor believes his eyes, and he wanted both.

Two thousand had stood in the nave at the noon office on the twenty-first. At first light there had been perhaps six hundred. By the end of the bells, a hundred and some in the side chapels and the choir; and when the bells stopped, most of those got up and went out of the north door in twos and threes, past Osric's men, who stood aside and did not speak to them, as the order said. Eighty. Fifty. Thirty-one.

He said each number into the fleet's line. Ward said heard. Ferra said it back from seven hundred miles, and then, every time, written.

At half past eleven there were seventeen, and they had stopped going. Nine canons in the choir behind the screen, saying the office over and over with their books open. A sacristan in a vestry off the south choir aisle, putting away the plate very slowly, who had told Osric's man through a shut door that he would go when it was put away and not before. Two old women in a chapel on the south side on their knees before a saint, who would not look round. Three in the passage inside the north wall, walking, with lamps, as on the twenty-first. One in the room over the north porch, asleep. One at the foot of the south-west stair, behind the door, faint and very still.

"Seventeen," he said.

"Written," said Ferra.

At a quarter to twelve the north wall fired on Osric's team.

He felt it before he understood it: the three in the passage stopping and going to one place, and one of them doing something small and quick and spent, like a man striking a match. Then fire out of the wall, out of a slit in the moulding over the north aisle's third arch that he had not known was a slit, at the second man in the file. Osric said one word up the short set, cover, not loud; and the forty were behind the piers, every man, before he had finished saying it. The second man's shield had come up late. Emeric felt the burn go into his forearm over the rim.

"Precast," said Saban, with his eyes shut. "Thumb on a stone. The priory lads."

"Nobody goes near the north side," said Osric's voice, "till I know where they are. Tell me when they move. Every time."

"Every time," said Emeric.

Noon came.

He heard it before he felt it: the parish bells across the lower town, a spire every few hundred yards, ringing twelve as they had rung it every day of his life, ragged, out of step, one after another from the outer wall inward, as if they did not know what else to do. And under them the city, a hundred and twenty thousand, outside the ring now, on the walls and the roofs and the far bank of the river, every soul turned toward the hill. Waiting for it.

Nothing fell.

"Seventeen," he said into the fleet's line. "Three in the wall, firing. The rest where they were. Not empty."

"Heard," said Ward. He did not say anything else. He had said it all on the wall walk in August.

"Written," said Ferra. Then, in the other voice, the one for her table at midnight: "You'll be there all afternoon."

"Yes."

"Then I'll be on the line all afternoon. Waylon's asleep in the stable loft. Armand's gone to the hospital to sit with the grey mage. Bram says his dynamos ran all night and he'd like that remembered." The pen. "I'll read you the count back. Every time you say it."

The set clicked.

He stood at the rail with the whole hill under him and the city round it waiting, and the K Discovery hanging in plain sight with her can in the red and every tower outside the ring winding its frame toward her; the king beside him not speaking, Saban on his other side counting; and he went back down into the stone, into the thick north wall and the long dark nave with Osric's forty behind its piers, and began to read it again from the beginning, slowly, as if he had never read it before.

He had said, at the long table, however long. He found that he still meant it.
