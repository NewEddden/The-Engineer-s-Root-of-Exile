Chapter 160 - The Lockbox

He heard at the marsh.

The Emberfell came in to the marsh mouth on the sixth evening with her escort, and Rowena's grey sloops hove to off the bar and dipped their flags and turned for home, and the coaster went on in alone up the channel on the last of the flood. At the head of the channel, where the marsh met the lower water, there was a relay post on a post-and-timber tower with a keeper's cottage under it, the last post on Kaldo's line and the first. The keeper was on the landing stage when they came alongside, with a slip of paper in his hand that he had been holding, he said afterward, for eight days.

It was in Ferra's hand. It had come down the line in lamp-marks and been written out fair by the keeper, but the keeper had copied her words, and they were hers.

RAIL WORKS. BOILER. RUDD DEAD. TWO BURNED, LIVING. WATCHER CALLED STOP, OVERRIDDEN. WORKS SHUT BY A. UNTIL B SAYS. B HAS WRITTEN. F.

Emeric read it twice on the landing stage in the dusk with Ward reading it over his shoulder. Then he folded it and put it in his coat, and went back aboard, and told Kerrow they would go on up to the quay tonight, in the dark, and Kerrow did not argue.

Rudd was in the churchyard by the east wall.

He went there the next morning before he went anywhere else. The grave was new and the turf on it had not knitted, and there was a wooden marker at the head with the name cut in it and a date, and somebody had put a length of iron at the foot of it, a short bar with the scale still on it, bent. He did not know who had put it there. He did not ask.

He stood there for a while.

He thought about Rudd reading the watcher's rule in the engine shed three years ago, holding it at arm's length because he had learned his letters at forty-five and did not trust them close up. Nothing, Rudd had said then, when he had read it twice, and meant that he had no objection, and folded it, and gone to his fire. He had been the first watcher. He had held the first engine for three years and never once had to use the rule against anybody, because the man who had to be stopped had never been anybody but himself, and nobody had ever thought to write a line for that.

Emeric had not thought to. He had written a rule for a man who would be stopped. He had never written one for a man who would stop himself too late.

He went to the pattern store after.

Bram was at his bench. He looked up when Emeric came in and did not say anything, and reached under the bench and brought out the sheet and put it on the wood between them, turned so that it could be read.

The prince's rule on the front. The man who watches the fire holds the works. And on the back, Bram's line from the spring, and under it the new one, in Bram's big square slow hand, with his name signed at the foot and the date. Nobody overrides the watcher. Not the driver. Not the master of the works. Not the holder of Kaldo. Not me.

Emeric read it through.

There was nothing he could add to it. He had come down the channel in the dark with things he meant to say about the override, and about the bar through the valve lever, and about Kester, and about what a rule was for if a man could be talked out of it by an older man he loved. He had turned them over all night. He found, reading Bram's page, that Bram had already said all of them, in fewer words, and signed them.

"The stays?" he said.

"New steel. Full thread. None of them cut by one man alone."

"And the boy?"

"Kester's at the rail works gauge," said Bram. "He's been there since it reopened. I asked him. He said he'd understand if I put someone else on it. I said I'd understand if he didn't want it. He said he wanted it." Bram put the sheet back under the bench. "He'll stop the next one. He'll stop it if it's me at the fire. He'll stop it if it's you."

"I know."

"I know you know," said Bram. "I'm saying it so it's said."

Emeric did not say anything about the override. There was nothing left to say about it that Bram had not already put in writing, and a man who had lost the best driver he had ever had did not need the holder of Kaldo standing in his pattern store telling him what he had already written down.

Bram told him the other thing that evening.

He did it at the long-room table, before the others came up, with the lamps being lit and the windows open on the summer dusk and nobody in the room but the two of them and the girl with the taper. He did it plainly, the way he said anything.

"I went in," he said. "On my own. The night after."

Emeric looked at him.

"Nobody opened it for me," said Bram. "I sat at the bench and tried to see the room, the way you told us, and I couldn't. I've never been able to see the room. So I stopped trying and thought about the boiler instead, the way I'd made it, one stay at a time. And it opened round the boiler." He turned his cup on the table. "I walked round it. I saw the stay that went. I saw the bar. I saw it go." He looked up. "Ferra was there when I came out. She'd been counting. Four minutes and nearly five."

Emeric sat very still.

He was glad. That came first, and it was real, and it was large. It went through him like warmth. He had waited for this for a year and more, since the first seating, since he had first put a stone in each of their hands and told them what it might do if they were patient. Bram had been first to feel it and first to follow him in. It was right that Bram should be the first to go in alone. It was right that it should be out of a boiler.

And under the gladness, he found, there was something else.

He did not like it. He looked at it straight on, sitting at the table with Bram across from him and the girl with the taper at the far end lighting the last lamp, and made himself see it. It was small, and it was mean, and it was his. For eleven years he had been the door. Every time any of them had gone into the delve, he had opened it for them and walked them through and been in the room when they were in it. He had been the only way in. He had built half of what Kaldo was on the fact that he was the only way in. And he had told himself for a year that the thing he wanted most was for them to find their own way, and he had meant it, and now Bram had done it and there was a part of him that did not entirely want it to have happened.

Ten years making himself the one door. And the correct answer had always been to stop being it.

"That's good," he said. "Bram. That's very good."

"I know it's good," said Bram. "I'm telling you so you know it's happened. I don't know if I can do it again. I don't know if it's only boilers." He looked at his band. "I don't know what I can see, now, that I couldn't. Or what anybody can see of mine."

Ferra asked it at the table an hour later, with everybody there, and she asked it because Bram had.

It was practical. It was the most practical question anybody had asked about the delve since the first seating, and Ferra asked it the way she asked about the price of oak, with her book open and her pen in her hand.

"When you're in," she said to Emeric, "and one of us is in, in our own room. The way Bram was. What can you see of it?"

The table went quiet.

"When it was only yours," said Ferra, "it was your house. We came into it as guests. You told us at the seating that anything we'd seen sat on a shelf in the room while we were in it, and that you weren't taking ours down, and that it was a promise and not a lock, because you hadn't got a lock to give us." She did not look at her book. She had it by heart. "Now there are rooms that aren't yours. Bram has one. The rest of us will, if the bands do what you say. So I'm asking what the promise covers. If you're in the house, and Bram's in his room, and there's a boiler in it. Can you see the boiler?"

He could have answered quickly. He did not.

"I don't know," he said.

Imani looked up.

"I don't know all of it," said Emeric. "I know what I've tried. When I'm in my own room I can feel the others, when they're in the house. I can feel where their doors are. I've never tried to go through one without being asked. I've never tried to look." He made himself say the rest. "I don't know if I could. I don't know if the room would let me. I didn't build it. I found it. There are things about it I've learned by accident and things I've learned by being wrong, and I haven't learned all of them, and I'm not going to pretend at this table that I have."

All of it was true. He had never gone through one of their doors, and he had never tried to look into one of their rooms. He thought of the warden's room at the Staple, and a book-man's shelf coming off a wall into his hands, and a man going slack under his palm, and he did not say it, because it was not what she had asked. Marrow had been in Emeric's room, not a room of his own. He knew it was the kind of line a clerk draws to make a column balance that does not. He drew it anyway.

"Then you might be able to," said Ferra.

"I might."

"Without the person in the room knowing."

"I don't know that either."

Ferra wrote something. Saban was not looking at anybody. Ward was looking at Emeric. Armand had his hands folded on the table and was very still. Bram was looking at his own wrist.

"Then build us a lock," said Imani.

She had not said a word at the table to him directly in two months that was not about the ward. She said this across the table in the same voice she used for a dose.

"You haven't got a lock to give us," said Imani. "Build one. A thing in each room that only the person whose room it is can open. Where we can put what we don't want seen." She did not look away from him. "Including by you."

He built it that month.

He did not know how when he began, and he said so. He knew what it had to do. It had to be a thing in the room, owned by the room's owner, which nobody else could see into. Not a door that could be shut, because a door was a thing the room's master already had, and Ferra's question was not about the door. It was about what a man could see of a room once he was in the house at all, and whether a room's master could keep a part of her room from a guest, or from a stranger, or from the man who had found the house.

He built it the way he built everything in the delve: by making a thing and then finding out what it did.

The first thing he made was a box. Plain, iron-coloured, the size of a strongbox, with a lid. In his own room he put a thing in it, a drawing of a bearing, and shut the lid, and then went out of his own room and came back in as if he were somebody else, the way he had learned to do years ago when he wanted to see a thing fresh. The box was there. He opened it. The drawing was inside.

That was no good. A box he could open from outside was only a box.

It took him nine nights. On the ninth he understood that he had been building it backwards. He had been trying to make a box that kept people out. What he needed was a box that let one person in, and only that one, and did not know how to let anybody else in because nobody else had ever been written into it. It was the same thing he had done with the board's stone years ago, with the binding: a stone that was his, the way a dog was somebody's, not because it was locked against everybody else but because it did not know anybody else was there.

He made it that way. A box in the room that knew one person. The room's owner. And when anybody else put a hand on the lid, the lid did not refuse them. It was simply not a lid, to them. There was nothing to open.

He tested it on himself. He made one in his own room, bound to himself, and put the bearing drawing in it. Then he made a second self, a copy, as he did when he rehearsed, and sent the copy to open it. The copy put its hand where the lid was and there was nothing there.

Then he did the thing that mattered. He asked Bram.

Bram went into his own room, the boiler room, on a night with Ferra counting at the bench. And inside it, while Emeric waited in the house outside, Bram made a box the way Emeric had shown him and put something in it that Emeric did not know, and shut it. Then Bram let Emeric in through his door. And Emeric stood in Bram's room, with the boiler round him, and saw the box sitting on the boiler's crown where Bram had put it, and put out his hand to it.

There was nothing there. He could see it. He could not touch it. It was a picture of a box, painted on the air.

He took his hand back.

"Well?" said Bram, in the room.

"I can't," said Emeric.

"You're sure?"

"I'm sure."

Bram looked at the box for a while. "Good," he said.

He never told Emeric what was in it. Emeric never asked.

The backfire he wrote the same month, and he did it because the lockbox was not enough, and because Ferra had asked the question under the question.

The lockbox kept a thing from being seen. It did not stop a man being forced. He had thought about that for a long time, since the Deep Channel, since the hold and the hood and the level voice, since Rylan on the stair. A delver could be taken. A delver could be held, in the real world, and made to go into the house, and made to open his door, and made to let somebody through it. Or somebody who could delve could try to force a door that was not his.

He could not do anything about the first. He had thought about it for nights and there was nothing. The house could not know what was being done to a man's body outside it. He would not pretend it could.

He knew better than anybody what could be done to it. A man held down and made afraid enough came in through his fear whether he wanted to or not, and what was on his shelf could be taken off it, a ledger at a time, until he put himself out rather than give up the last one. Emeric had done it on a floor in the ash with Rylan's knee in the man's back. If it could be done to a book-man in Wayford, it could be done to Ferra.

He could do something about the second.

He had drawn a lock for the stones years ago, when he first wrote the network, and never had cause to finish it. Three stages. A warning first, a pressure, the feeling of pushing against a door that is not going to open. Then, if a man kept pushing, ejection: the house put him out, the way a body puts out a splinter. And then, if he came back and pushed again, the release, which was worse, a forced letting-go of everything he had open, all at once, which left a man flat on his back in the real world with a headache like a hammer and nothing in his hands.

He finished it now, and wrote it into the house. Into every door.

A delver who tried to go through a door that was not his, without its owner's leave, would feel the warning. If he kept on, he would be put out. If he came back, the release. And the same for the other side of it: a delver forced into somebody else's room, pushed through a door by somebody who had got hold of him, would be put out the same way, because the room did not know him and had not been asked.

It said nothing about a stranger pulled into the master's own room by the master's own hand. There was no door in that, to write a lock into. He knew that when he finished, and he did not write anything to cover it.

He wrote it. He told the table he had written it, and what it did, in three sentences.

Nobody asked to test it.

He did not offer. He could not test it himself, because he was the only one who could try to force a door, and he would know he was testing, and the house would know he knew. And he would not ask any of the six to try to force another's, because he did not want any of them to find out what they could do if they tried. He had found out what he could do, and he had not liked how little he minded. It would have to be tested by something real. He hoped it would not be soon, and he did not write down how he knew that it would one day be tested, because he did not know. He only knew that everything he had ever built had been tested in the end by something he had not planned for.

Marrow was in the keep.

He had been in the keep since the autumn, since the column came back down the Wayford road with the five in the churchyard behind it and one man in a closed cart. He was in the old strongroom under the east tower, which was dry and had a window high up with bars in it, and a bed and a table and a chair and a lamp, and books if he asked for them, which he did not. Rylan had brought him down. Rylan went down once a week and looked at him through the grille, and did not speak to him, and came back up.

He had woken on the second evening after the Staple, in Pennock's silver room behind the relay, and asked Rylan through the door for water. That was the first thing anybody knew of it. Emeric had watched him, in the months since, by every report Rylan and the turnkeys brought up the stair, for some sign that he had come back less than he went down. There was none. He knew the date and his own name and the names of the men who guarded him. Whatever going out like a lamp had cost him, it had not been anything he used.

Emeric went down with Saban on the third day after the table.

Marrow was standing by the wall under the window. He looked much as he had looked in the strongroom at the Staple with Rylan's knee in his back: medium height, grey at the sides, a face you would forget on a quay. His coat was buttoned to the throat, as it always was. He did not come to the table when they came in. He looked at Emeric's hands before he looked at his face.

Emeric sat down at the table. Saban stayed by the door.

"You told me nothing at Wayford," said Emeric. "You've told the keep nothing since. That's your right, and I'm not here to ask you to change your mind about it."

Marrow looked at him and waited.

Emeric put the coin on the table.

He had had it from Saban on the coaster. It lay on the wood in the lamplight: thin silver, a little larger than a Silver Rand, the tower or the gate on its face with the thing over it that might be a crown or a flame, rubbed nearly smooth.

"This came out of the purse of a man who boarded a boat of mine," said Emeric, "two days out of Emberfell, and killed six of her crew, and put her cargo at the bottom of the sea. He's in the sailors' ground above Rowena's harbour. I don't know his name."

Marrow did not come any closer to it. He looked at it from the wall.

He looked at it for a long time. Emeric watched him look. He had watched Marrow's face at the Staple and seen nothing in it, and he had put the sense over him then and felt nothing he knew, a stranger's grain. He did not put the sense out now. He watched with his eyes. And he watched a man who had spent the better part of a year in a strongroom saying nothing look at a thin silver coin on a table, and something in the face moved, very slightly, around the mouth, and was put back.

"The men who boarded your boat," said Marrow, "were paid out of the same purse that paid me."

His voice was hoarse and low, with the flat northern vowels. It was the first full sentence he had spoken since the Staple.

"Whose purse?" said Emeric.

Marrow looked up from the coin at him. Then he looked at the wall.

He did not say anything else. He did not say anything else for the rest of the quarter hour Emeric sat there, or when Saban asked him the same question in a different way, or when Emeric picked up the coin and put it back in his coat and stood. He stood under the window with his coat buttoned to the throat and looked at the wall.

On the stair, going up, Saban said, "Same purse."

"Yes."

"He wanted you to know that." Saban was quiet for a few steps. "He's been sitting down there the best part of a year wanting us to know something. That's the thing he picked."

"Can you find it?"

"The purse?" Saban stopped on the stair. "The coin first. Then who has coins like it. Then who pays men with them." He held out his hand. Emeric gave him the coin. Saban looked at it in the light from the stair window. "It's not from anywhere I've traded," he said. "Which means it's from somewhere I haven't. That's a short list, and it's a long way off."

He put it back in his purse, and did up the string, and went on up.

They finished the lane at the table in the last week of midsummer.

It had been argued since the Emberfell came in, in pieces, at every table, and it was finished in one because Saban came up from the lower water with the figures and put them down and said, "It's two boats a month now, with escort. It'll be four by next summer. Four boats is four more chances for somebody with a lugsail and twenty men and a purse. Rowena's sloops can't be everywhere and I can't buy enough of them to be."

"So what do you want?" said Ferra.

"I don't want anything," said Saban. "I want to know what the prince wants. Because I know what I'd want, and I'm not allowed to say it."

Emeric had been waiting for somebody to say it.

"The ship carries people," he said. "That's what she was built for. Fifteen, seventeen, a hold for their gear, rooms for them to sleep. She's not a cargo hull and I'm not going to make her one." He put his hand flat on the table. "But a lane that can be cut at sea needs something that can't be. Something that goes over the sea and not on it. Not for everything. For the things that can't be lost."

"A second ship," said Ward.

"A second hull. A cargo hull. Longer, broader, uglier, with nowhere for anybody to sleep but her crew and nowhere for anything to go but the hold." He looked round the table. "She carries what the lane can't risk. The powder materials when there are powder materials. The plate, when Rowena wants plate. The lead. Anything that's worth more than a boat."

"And she flies," said Saban.

"And she flies."

"And the stone?" said Ferra.

She had her pen in her hand and had not written anything yet. It was the only question at the table that mattered, and they all knew it.

"Grayrock raises gravity stone twice a year," said Emeric. "The Crown takes its share of everything sold out of the barony, at two Platinum the pound, and the rest goes to the House's account and ours. That's the covenant. It binds what's sold." He looked at her. "It doesn't bind what's kept. A barony can keep its own stone. It always could. It's never had a reason."

Ferra looked at him for a long moment.

"Every pound," she said.

"Every pound. Raised, kept, set, in the book."

"In the book," said Ferra. "Not the box." She began to write. "If the Crown's assessor ever asks me how much gravity stone Kaldo's raised and how much it sold, I'll show him the book, and the book'll have every pound of it, and the difference, and the hull it went into." She did not look up. "Lawful. All of it. I'll make sure it's lawful. I'm not going to make sure it's comfortable."

Fletcher laid the keel a week later, on the slip where the Emberfell had stood.

His yard men were told she was a second coaster. They believed it; there was no reason they should not. She had a keel like a coaster's, of the converter's bar, long and straight, and she would have frames like a coaster's, and plate. They did not know that she would be twenty feet longer than the Emberfell and nearly half again as broad, because Fletcher had not told them and would not until the frames were up. They did not know that there were places marked on the keel's upper face with a chisel, at intervals, in pairs, where nothing a coaster needed would ever go.

Only Fletcher knew what those were for.

He had been the only one among the shipwrights who knew the first time. He had built the ship's hull knowing, and stood at her wheel when she left the water, and had his hands go slack on the spokes when there was no water on the rudder. He had not told a soul. He had not told Abloh. He did not tell anybody now.

Emeric came down to the lower water on the evening after the keel was laid, alone, at dusk, and stood at the head of the slip.

The yard men had gone. The keel lay on its blocks down the length of the slip in the last light, black, straight, with the stem not yet raised at one end and the sternpost not yet at the other, and nothing on it but a row of chalk marks where the frames would go.

And Fletcher was on it.

He was standing on the keel itself, halfway down its length, in his coat, with his hands behind his back, looking down at the upper face of it between his feet. At the chiselled marks. The pairs of them, going away from him down the keel toward the stern in the dusk, at intervals, where nothing a coaster needed would ever go, and where, in a year, if the frames went up and the plate went on and Bram and Emeric came down at night with a cart under a cloth, something would.

Fletcher did not look up. He did not know Emeric was there. He stood alone on the keel in the dusk and looked at the slots nobody else on the lower water could read, and after a while he walked on down the keel to the next pair, slowly, and stood and looked at those.
