Chapter 160 - Drain Tile

Orrin held the first firing of the Milford tile kiln for nine hours on the eighth of July, sitting on a stool at the firebox door with his hands in his lap, and at the end of it he said it was the dullest thing he had ever done for money and he would like to do it again.

The kiln stood at the foot of the bench above the bay, where the clay was: a squat round tower of firebrick the height of two men, built in a fortnight by Bram's masons from Linus's map, with its firebox at the bottom and its chimney at the top and the tiles stacked inside it on edge in rings, three thousand of them, grey and soft and wet, each one a half-round pipe of clay a foot long, for laying end to end under the bottoms in the trenches. Drain tile. Every one of them had been pressed in a wooden mould by a Milford girl or boy at a penny a hundred, which was the first wage most of them had ever had, and every one had to come out of the kiln hard and rung like a bell, or it would melt back into the mud it came from in its first wet winter.

That was the trouble. The heat. Too cool, and the clay would come out soft. Too hot, and it would slump and stick, and you would have a kiln full of one grey lump. And the right heat had to be held, not reached. Held for hours, until the clay through the whole stack had come to it, the middle as well as the outside.

Nobody in Milford had ever fired a kiln. Nobody in Kaldo had ever fired one this size. So Orrin held it.

He was a fire mage. A hedge mage, the tower would have called him, with one element and not much of it, and he had kept the school's stove going for six years and the glassworks' furnace for two, and he had taught Haddon at the foundry to tell iron's heat by its colour against what Orrin could feel. He could feel a fire the way Ivo could feel a lens. He sat on his stool at the firebox door with the coal burning behind it, and put out one hand, and felt the heat of the whole stack through the firebrick, the way a man feels a horse's temper through the reins; and when it rose too fast he took a little of it out and sent it up the chimney, and when it sagged he leaned on the coal and made it burn, and he held it there, the right heat, the colour of the inside of a peach, for nine hours.

When the kiln was opened two days later and the tiles came out, they rang. All three thousand. A boy went down the rows on the drying floor with a stick tapping them, and every one of them gave back the same clear dull note, like a cow bell a long way off, and he went down all of them twice because he liked the sound.

"There," said Orrin. He was sitting on the drying floor with his back against the kiln's warm side. "Now somebody has to do that without me."



That was the second kiln.

It stood beside the first, the same squat tower, the same firebox, and it had no mage on a stool at its door. It had a stoker, a Milford man who had stoked the baron's bread ovens, and a pile of Grayrock coal, and a damper on its chimney that opened and shut on a lever, and nothing else. And it had to do what Orrin had done. Hold the inside of a peach for nine hours, with nobody in it who could feel a fire.

"He can't," said Orrin, at the long table, which was the first time he had ever sat at it. "The stoker. He can't. Not because he's a fool. He's not. Because he's got to go by the colour, through a spy hole, and the colour of a kiln at the right heat and the colour of a kiln just past it are the same colour to anybody's eye but mine." He spread his hands. "I can tell you the difference. I can't show it you. There's nothing to point at."

"Then we make something to point at," said Emeric.

The forge made the start of it, on the twelfth, in the cellar: two coils of wire, each of a different metal, each run to recipe exactly, and drawn through the same die to the same width, a hair thicker than a horse's tail. That was all. Tess took the rest.

She knew what it was before he had finished telling her, because she had been there for every instrument that the telegraph had ever needed, and she knew the galvanometer better than anybody in four baronies: the coil of wire round a little compass needle in a box, that swung when a current went through the coil, and swung further when the current was stronger. Two different metals, Emeric told her, twisted together at one end and that end put into the fire, and their other ends put to the galvanometer. Heat the joined end, and a current goes round. Not much of one. A trickle, too small to ring a bell or move a sounder. But the hotter the joined end, the more of it, and the galvanometer's needle would go round on its card further for every degree of heat; and the needle did not care what colour anything was.

"A thermocouple," he said. "The joined wires. And with the galvanometer, a pyrometer. A fire-measure."

Tess did not say anything. She twisted the two wires together at one end with a pair of pliers, and put the twist into the flame of her bench lamp, and screwed the other ends into the terminals of the galvanometer from the line room's spare board; and the needle moved. Off its pin. A little way round its card, and stopped, and stayed there while the twist was in the flame, and went back when she took it out.

She put it back in. The needle went round. She took it out. She did that nine or ten times, without speaking, watching the needle.

"It's not a number yet," she said.

"No. It needs a card."

The card took a week. Tess and Bram made it in the works the way you made any scale that had to be true: by things that did not lie. Tin melts at one heat and no other, every time, everywhere. So does lead. So does zinc, and silver, and the brass for Ottoline's irises, and salt. They put the twist into a crucible of each, in turn, in the works' furnace, and watched the metal in the crucible, and the instant it went from solid to liquid Tess marked where the needle stood on the card. Tin. Lead. Zinc. Salt. Silver. A row of marks round the card's edge, each with a name; and between the marks, Tess divided the card into degrees with Ivo's dividing glass. And at the end of the week the needle on the galvanometer in its box, with the two long wires running from it, could tell anybody who could read a clock how hot anything was, in numbers, to a degree or two, without a mage in the room.

She ran the long wires up to Milford on the empty line. The twisted end went into the second kiln through a hole in the firebrick, in a sleeve of fired clay, into the middle of the stack. The galvanometer went on a shelf by the stoker's door, in a box with a glass lid, and the card behind its needle had a red line drawn on it by Orrin's own hand, where the needle had stood in the first kiln when Orrin, with his hand out at the firebox door, had said: there.

"Keep it on the line," said Tess, to the stoker. "Open the damper if it goes over. Close it if it goes under. That's all."

The stoker looked at the needle. He looked at the red line. He was a man who had judged his bread ovens all his life by putting his arm in to the elbow and counting. He looked at Orrin, standing in the door of the drying shed with his arms folded.

"It'll go off," said the stoker. "In the night. When you're not looking. Like an oven does."

"Then you'll see it go off," said Tess, "because it's a needle and not your arm. And you'll fix it." She turned her chair round. "And in the morning you'll show me where it went and when. That's the other thing it's for."

The second kiln fired on the twentieth, for nine hours, with the stoker on a stool at the firebox door and his eyes on the needle and his hand on the damper lever, and nobody else there at all. Orrin had been told to stay away and had gone fishing. The needle wandered twice in the night, once over and once under; the stoker saw it both times and moved his lever, and the needle went back to the red line and stayed there.

The tiles rang. All three thousand.

Orrin came up from the river with two trout and stood in the drying shed door while the boy with the stick went down the rows tapping them, and listened. Then he went over to the galvanometer on its shelf and looked at the red line he had drawn on the card, as though it were a portrait of somebody he had known.

"That's me," he said. "In a box." He did not sound unhappy about it. He sounded, Emeric thought, like a man who had been told his son had passed an examination. "Nine years I've been the only man in a hundred miles who could do that. Now any fool with eyes can." He put a finger on the glass over the needle. "Good."

Ivo came up a week later with the same two wires and a different card, much smaller, in a box no bigger than a snuff box, and a row of glass tubes in a leather case: clinical thermometers, sealed glass with mercury in a fine bore, for Imani, to go under a fever's tongue. He had made them in the spring, and they had read differently from each other by as much as a degree, and he had been ashamed of them. He tried every one against the pyrometer's little card in a basin of warm water, and scratched a new mark on every tube that was out, and threw away the three that were too far out to save. Imani took the case from him at the hospital door, and opened it, and looked at the tubes in their slots, each with a tiny scratch at the place where a man's blood ran right; and did not say anything for so long that Ivo began, nervously, to explain the card.

"I know what they are," said Imani. "I've been guessing a fever with my wrist on a man's forehead since I was a girl." She shut the case. "Thank you."



The tiles went into the bottoms in the last week of July, in trenches.

The trenches ran where Linus's map said the water stood, which was where it had always stood and where nobody had ever known it stood, all at once, because nobody had ever seen it from above. Long straight trenches across the low ground in a pattern like the ribs of a leaf, every one of them falling, a hand in a chain, toward a main trench along the foot of the bottoms that fell to the river. The tiles went into the bottom of every trench end to end, open side down, with a flat tile under the joint, and the trenches were filled again over them; and the water that had stood on the bottoms since Milford was Milford went down into the gravel round the tiles, and in at the joints, and along the tiles under the ground, and out at the river.

Paskin dug the main trenches. He did it in the clay with three of his students, the way he had opened the first cutting on the Milford line, and the levy and the Milford gangs dug the side trenches by hand behind them, which was most of the work, and which was the way that would still work.

And on the third day of it, in the afternoon, Paskin stood at the end of a side trench in the black wet ground of the bottoms where the Milford gang had just begun, and looked down at the sedge and the standing water, and then at Emeric, who was standing beside him.

"You'll want this one," Paskin said. "For the thing you won't tell me what it's for."

Emeric looked at him.

"The starting block," said Paskin. "On the grade. You asked me how to make the ground stand up. Every man who asks me how to make the ground stand up asks me, a month later, how to make it lie down." He put his pipe in his mouth. "So. Lie down."

He put his boot on the bank of the trench, on a patch of firm turf above the wet, and pressed. Nothing happened. Then he took his boot away and crouched and put his palm on the turf where the boot had been, and leaned, and a small amber glyph came up off his hand; and Emeric, watching, saw the turf darken. Water came up into it. Not over it; into it, from below, out of the wet ground underneath, the way water comes up into a sponge laid on a wet table. The turf went dark and shining and soft, a patch the size of a door, in the time it took to say so.

Paskin stood up and put his boot back on it. His boot went in to the ankle.

"Here," said Paskin, round his pipe, "it's easy. The ground's half water already. You don't make it soft. You let the water up into it and get out of the way." He pulled his boot out with a long suck. "On dry ground it's harder, and it costs, and on rock it's nothing, and on a flagged yard it's less than nothing, because there's no ground under the flags to soften, only sand. Remember that." He wiped his boot on the turf. "Go on."

Emeric crouched on the firm turf a yard further along the bank and put his hand down.

He leaned. He did not lean the way he leaned for the ridge, up and against; he leaned down, and let go, the way a man lets go of a rope he has been holding to let a weight drop, and felt the wet under the turf like a cold hand pressing up against his palm through the grass, waiting. He let it up. It came. The glyph wobbled amber off his fingers and closed, and it cost him, the dull push behind the eyes that earth always cost him; but it came. The turf under his hand darkened.

A patch the size of a plate.

Paskin looked at it. Then, without a word, he put his boot on it, and his boot went in to the instep, and stopped, and he stood there with one foot in the plate of mud and looked at Emeric with an expression of great patience.

"A door," said Paskin. "I said a door. Not a plate. A man doesn't put his foot on a plate unless you've told him where it is."

"I'm not trying to make a door."

"What are you trying to make?"

Emeric looked at the plate of mud with Paskin's boot in it. He thought about Declan in the yard, saying feet first. About the lean in a man's note a breath before his foot moved, earlier than the foot and noisier. About the place where a man is going to put his foot, which is a plate, not a door, if you know where it is.

"The place where a man's going to step," he said, "before he steps there."

Paskin took his boot out of the mud, slowly. He looked at the plate. He looked at Emeric.

"Then that's a good plate," he said. "But you're slow. By the time you've made it, he's stepped." He put his pipe back in his mouth and walked off along the trench toward his students. "Faster," he said, over his shoulder. "And smaller glyph. You're asking the water. You're not ringing for it."



Hollis and Farris went west with the tiles.

They had not wanted to. Farris had said that the west-side farm would go to ruin without him, and Hollis, who was nearly seventy, had said that he had not left Kaldo's fields since the war and saw no reason to start; and then Ferra had told them what Milford's farmers were saying, in the alehouse by the steward's house, about Kaldo men coming to tell Milford how to farm its own land, and both of them had packed that evening.

Milford's farmers argued with them. That was the whole of July. They did not argue with Emeric; he was not there to argue with, most of the time, and when he was they were careful with him, in the way that men are careful with a prince who has bought their baron's debts. They argued with Hollis and Farris. In the fields, and in the alehouse, and at the steward's door in the mornings, at length and in detail and with a great deal of feeling, because Hollis and Farris were farmers, and farmers can be told by other farmers that they are wrong, and will answer back.

The rotation was the worst of it. Four fields, in turn: wheat, then turnips, then barley with clover sown under it, then the clover alone; and round again, so that no field was ever left bare and resting for a year, as Milford had always left a third of its land, and every field gave something every year. And the turnips and the clover and the hay off the drained bottoms went to the cattle through the winter, in the byres, instead of the cattle being killed in the autumn for want of anything to feed them, as Milford had killed two in three of its beasts every November since the first Milford baron.

"You'll not keep beasts through the winter," a Milford man said in the alehouse, to Hollis, on the first night. "Nobody keeps beasts through a winter. You kill them at Martinmas and salt them. My father did, and his."

"Your father killed them," said Hollis, "because he'd nothing to feed them. Nor had mine. Nor had I, till a man from the capital made me grow turnips in a field my father left empty, and I told him he was a fool, in that alehouse at Kaldo, every night for a year." He drank. "Now I've forty head through every winter, and I sell milk in March when nobody else has any, and beef in May when it's dear." He put his cup down. "He was a fool about the seed, mind. I told him that too."

Paxton argued the winter feed. He did it at his own table, in the steward's house, with his book open and Ferra's figures open beside it and Hollis on one side of him and Farris on the other, over three evenings, and it was the most enjoyable argument, Ferra said afterward, that she had read about in years. Paxton wanted to know how many bales of hay and how many cartloads of turnips it took to keep one beast from November to May. Farris told him. Paxton said that was too many. Farris said it was not. Paxton went out to his own byre with a lantern and a measuring stick and measured how much a cow ate in a night, three nights running, and came back and said that Farris's number was a fifth too high. Farris said Paxton's cows were Milford cows and ate like it. Paxton said that was the first sensible thing anybody from Kaldo had said to him; and they settled it at a number between the two, which went into Paxton's book in Paxton's hand, and Paxton reported it up the wire on the Monday as if he had thought of it himself.

Hollis came back to Kaldo at the end of the month thinner, and sunburnt, and in a better temper than Emeric had seen him in years.

"They're fools," he said, at the works gate. "Every one of them. Stubborn as mules." He looked back west, along the line toward Milford, with something in his face that might have been pride. "They'll be the best farmers in four baronies in five years. You see if they're not."



The fertilizer works was at Kaldo, below the tannery, downwind of everything, and it smelled worse than anything Kaldo had ever built, which was a considerable claim.

It was a shed with a stone floor, and a mill for grinding, and a row of lead-lined troughs, and a stack of bones. The bones came from the tannery and the slaughter yards and the soap works, which had been throwing them in the river for five years or selling them to a man in the dock quarter who boiled them for glue. They were ground in the mill to a grey grit, and the grit went into the lead troughs, and onto the grit, from carboys, slowly, by men in leather aprons and leather gloves and the dark glass masks from the arc, went the acid works' oil of vitriol, the sulfuric acid that the works made now by the barrel from the dyehouse's brimstone; and the grit and the acid together hissed and steamed and stank and grew warm and set, in a day, to a grey crumbly cake. The cake was broken up and sacked. A sack of it on an acre was worth, Emeric said, a cartload of muck.

Superphosphate, he told them, which was its name, and the men in the leather aprons called it bone-vitriol, and the farmers called it the stink.

Hollis would not have it. He came to the shed on the day the first sacks were filled, and stood in the door with his hand over his nose, and said that he had put muck on his fields for sixty years, good muck from good beasts, and he was not putting a sack of dead men's bones boiled in the devil's acid on any field of his, and neither was Farris.

"Cows' bones," said Emeric. "Not men's."

"I know whose bones they are," said Hollis. "It's the principle."

Farris put it on a field anyway, in August, a strip of it, a sack to the acre, across the middle of his turnips, without telling Hollis. He did it the way Emeric had done the seed on the first strip, five years ago: one strip, beside a strip with nothing, and let the field decide.

By September the turnips in the strip were half again the size of the turnips on either side of it, and the line between the two was as sharp as if it had been drawn with a rule. Hollis walked along it, slowly, with his stick, looking down, from one end of the field to the other.

"It's the principle," he said, at the end of it, to nobody. And the next spring he bought six sacks.
