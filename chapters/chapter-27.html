Chapter 27 - The Assault

They came over the rise at first light and it took eleven minutes for all of them to finish coming over it.

Emeric had read the number off Rylan's mouth four days earlier and had believed it, and had drawn against it, and had sited stones against it, and none of that turned out to be the same as watching a slope fill.

"Say something useful to me," he said.

"They've no carts," Ward said.

"They've no carts."

"Nine wagons and two of those are Renner's own comfort. Five hundred men and nine wagons." Ward had not moved from the parapet and had not put his helm on. "That's a day. That's one day of bread on the man and a day and a half in the wagons if the miners have been honest with each other about what they're carrying, which they haven't."

"He can forage."

"Off what." Ward nodded down the slope at two thousand acres of stubble, cut and carried and gone in six days, the divisions lying flat and yellow all the way to the fence line. "There's not a stalk out there. There's no standing crop in this barony and there's no grain in a field and there's nothing in that plain for five hundred men or six hundred horses, and every mouth he's brought has to eat tonight and again tomorrow and he cannot buy, because he's ridden three days to a place he's declared war on."

Emeric stood with that for a moment and found that his hands had gone cold.

"He can't besiege me."

"He cannot besiege you. He can stand out there for one day and then he starts sending men home in tens, and every man that goes home tells the hill what he saw, and Renner cannot have that." Ward finally turned. "You want to know what you did on the seventh day when you got that harvest under a roof. That's what you did. You didn't save your winter. You took the only thing he was any good at away from him, and he's been riding three days toward a siege he can't have and he'll not have worked it out until about an hour ago."

"Then he has to come up the hill today."

"He has to come up the hill today. Once, in daylight, with everything, at whatever we've built."

The herald came at seven and Emeric let him talk.

The terms were the foundry, the works on the eastern arm entire, the harnesses, and the prince himself to come out onto the road and answer for a season of unlicensed manufacture in a border barony. It was read out well. Whoever had drafted it had used the word regularise twice.

"He wants me to come out," Emeric said, afterward, to nobody.

"He wants to be seen wanting it." Ward watched the herald ride back down. "Look at where he's put himself."

Renner was at the front. Not behind the front, not on the flank with the household men where a baron with four thousand people on a hill ought to be. He sat his horse eighty paces behind the first rank in a grey coat with the Grayrock device on the breast, in the open, on the graded approach, and he did not move from there all morning, and Emeric understood the shape of the whole day out of that one fact.

"He has to be at the front of it," Emeric said. "It's not courage."

"It's never courage when it's on a slope like that."

"He's emptied his workings, he's borrowed against a harvest he hasn't cut, and he's brought two other barons out here on a picture of a granary that doesn't exist any more. If he stands at the back and this goes badly, he's a man who spent Grayrock's miners on a mistake." Emeric put his hands on the stone. "If he stands at the front, he's a baron who led. He needs to be in front of it more than he needs to win it."

"Then he'll be in front of it when it breaks," Ward said. "Remember you said that."

They came at nine and the crossbows opened at a hundred and twenty paces.

Sixty men in shirts on a wall, most of whom had been in a field cutting grain nine days earlier, shooting a weapon a farmer can be taught in an afternoon down a slope that had been cleared and graded and measured and marked with a line of whitewashed stones at exactly the distance the bolts wanted.

Emeric had costed the bolt going through a Grayrock harness in March, on paper, off a sample of Grayrock plate that Saban had brought him for two shillings and a laugh.

It went through.

The Grayrock men were not wearing harness. That was the part the paper had not told him. Two hundred and fifty men off a mining face and a sorting floor, called up in August at three days' notice, came up that slope in leather and quilt and mail where a man owned mail, because there is no armoury on a hill for two hundred and fifty men who are not soldiers. Renner's household men had plate. There were forty of them.

The first rank did not reach the whitewashed stones.

Emeric watched it and did not feel any of the things he had prepared to feel. What he felt was the specific flat horror of arithmetic coming out right, and underneath it, further down, something worse, which was that he had built this and it was working.

"Slow them," Ward said, to the ward, to no one, and fifty-seven men who had been drilling since April slowed them.

The push at the joint came at half past ten and it was not stupid.

Somebody down there knew the wall. Somebody down there knew that the new stone met the old stone eleven paces east of the second tower, and that the courses did not tie properly for about four feet of it because Emeric had run out of season and had chosen a water wheel instead, and that a barony that has extended a wall in one summer has one seam in it and only one.

Corliss had known it. Corliss had paid a forearm to say four words about it in a long room in July, and Ward had put the four black suits there and had not let anybody talk him out of it.

Ward, Rylan, Osric and Declan held the joint for two hours against a hundred and forty men.

Emeric only saw parts of it. He was on the tower and then on the eastern stretch and then back, and what he retained afterward was in pieces: Rylan taking a spear on the pauldron and the layered lower edge sending it out and away exactly as Bram had said it would; a Grayrock miner with a pick, which was the only thing on that slope all day that Emeric was frightened of, because a pick is not a weapon and does not care what a plate is for; Osric standing on the seam itself with his feet apart for so long that men began to go around him.

Arrows came up and did nothing at all. They came up in quantity and they hit black steel and went sideways off it, and Emeric heard a sound he did not recognise for some seconds and then identified as the men on the wall laughing.

He wrote that down afterward and did not know what to do with it.

The grey-robes came up behind the second push and there were three of them.

He had known there would be. Grayrock kept two on the hill for the deep workings and Wayford's household ran one, and he had asked Rylan for that number in June and had got it, and the number had been sitting in a margin ever since with nothing written under it, because there had been nothing to write.

He came down off the tower and walked out onto the eastern stretch alone, because there was no version of it where anybody stood next to him and was useful, and he did the three things he had done two hundred times a night in an empty room since May.

The first one put a wall of fire out at the base of the joint and Emeric pulled water thin across the front of it, a sheet a hand wide and no thicker than a nail, held for four seconds, and the fire went out with a sound like a door closing and the steam went into the faces of the men who had been walking behind it.

He did not put out the fire. He had never once, in two hundred repetitions, tried to put out a fire. He had practised a sheet of water at a particular thinness because thin was what a flame needed and no more, and the flame had been somebody else's and the thinness had been his, and the whole of it cost him about what lifting a full bucket costs a man.

The second one was slower and better and put a spear of shaped stone up out of the slope at him, and Emeric was not fast enough for it and did not try to be. He put a blade of moving air a hand long into the shaft of the thing at the point where it left the ground, held flat, held still, and the spear broke off its own base and went over the wall behind him and killed nobody.

The third one he never saw properly.

What he saw was a man in grey stepping up onto the graded approach eleven paces from the joint with both hands out, and what he did was raise a slab of stone the size of a door a hand's breadth off the ground under him and drop it.

A hand's breadth is nothing. A hand's breadth is a step off a kerb, and a man who has just had the ground under him rise and fall a hand's breadth while he is standing on one foot with both hands occupied does not fall over dramatically. He stumbles, and puts a hand down, and gets up, and is four seconds late.

Osric killed him in the four seconds.

Emeric sat down on the walk of the eastern stretch afterward for a moment with his back against the parapet and his hands shaking, and thought, with total clarity, that he had spent a season being told by everyone including himself that he had a tenth of a mage's output, and that he had just spent about a third of what he had and stopped three mages, and that the reason was not a secret and was not clever. It was that he had known in advance exactly what each of the three things cost, and had never once in his life been able to afford to find out the hard way.

He got up and went to the tower and threw the stones.

That was the decision he had been holding since nine. The three deadzone stones sat in a line across the approach at a hundred and ten paces, moved out from eighty on the tenth day because eighty had opened Declan's coupling in a drill in front of Bram, and lighting them killed every working inside their reach and cost him most of the effect on the slope for it.

He lit them because there were two grey-robes left and one of them had just put a spear of stone through his own wall's parapet.

Declan's breaker threw open sixty seconds later.

He heard it. That was the thing that stayed with him. Across a hundred and something paces of shouting and iron he heard the sound of a dropped latch, because he had been listening for it since the moment he put his hand on the third stone, and he had been listening for it because he had known it was coming and had thrown them anyway.

Declan's suit went dead on him at the joint. Fifty-one pounds of Kaldo plate with nothing in it, on a man standing on the one seam in the wall, holding the position that Corliss had bought with his arm.

Ward and Rylan and Osric closed over him and Declan fought the rest of it in dead steel, which he told Emeric afterward, at some length, was no worse than what he had worn for eleven years in the capital.

The crystal lived.

Bram was on the charging floor when the stones went out and did not know any of it until evening, and Emeric went and found him himself rather than send anyone.

"It opened," Emeric said. "At the joint. Sixty seconds after I lit them and about four feet from a man with a pick."

Bram put down what he was holding.

"Say the rest of it."

"He stood in dead plate for an hour and a half with three men over him and he has a broken hand and he is going to be entirely fine, and the stone is in the housing and it will feed a suit tomorrow." Emeric said it flat, without decoration. "And you were right for four days in June and I want that said in front of somebody who isn't me. You told me I was putting a thing that can come apart into the middle of a thing whose job is not coming apart, and it came apart, on a man, in a fight, at the worst possible moment, exactly the way you said it would."

"And you'd do it again."

"I'd do it again tonight."

"Aye," Bram said. "Well. That's the two of us right, then, and I'd rather have been wrong."

Renner came forward at one o'clock because he had to.

The second push had failed at the joint and the third had not formed, and men were sitting down on the slope out of bowshot, which is the thing an army does before it stops being one, and Renner rode up through them to the front to be seen doing it.

He stopped at about a hundred and forty paces, inside the line of the deadzone stones, on ground he had chosen because it was the closest a man could sit a horse and be looked at by both his own men and the wall.

Emeric was in the gate tower with a thing that had a barrel and a compressed-air crystal in it and had been made once and could not be made again.

He had test-fired it eleven times in July into an inch of oak at a hundred and forty paces, and had recorded the drop, and had built the sight himself, and had never once let himself think past the oak.

The stones did not reach the tower. He had sited them at a hundred and ten from the joint and the tower stood behind that, which meant the workings in the tower were live and the workings on the slope were not, and the coat Renner was wearing had something in it that would have turned a bolt and did not have anything in it at all where he was sitting.

Emeric was aware of all of that. He had been aware of it since the tenth day, when he had moved three stones out to a hundred and ten and stood looking at a slope and put a thing in a column.

He shot Baron Renner of Grayrock off a horse at a hundred and thirty-eight paces at one o'clock in the afternoon with his own hands, and it was the only man he killed all day, and he was steady for the shot and not afterward.

Grayrock broke inside a quarter of an hour.

Not all at once and not away. Two hundred and fifty men who had been called off a mining face nine days earlier, who had come up a graded slope twice into bolts that went through what they were wearing, watched the man who had brought them there go off his horse in the open, and stopped.

Sethe took Wayford off in order.

That was the part Ward respected and said so. A hundred and twenty men, formed, in line, at a walk, down the slope and onto the road and gone, having lost eleven, having spent an entire morning wide on the western approach in a manner that had committed nothing and threatened everything, and Emeric understood by three o'clock that Wayford had never intended to come up that hill at all.

Milford never moved.

A hundred and fifty men stood on the plain from first light to the middle of the afternoon and did not advance, did not deploy, did not send a rider, and did not answer the two the wall sent down. At four they struck camp, and at five they were on the Milford road, and Paxton had spent nothing and lost nothing and had been present for the whole of it.

Emeric watched them go and thought about a contract with a third taken in advance, and about a border with Grayrock, and about a man with a fifth of what Grayrock had, and could not decide for a long time afterward whether he had been saved by a friend or spared by a man keeping his options open, and eventually wrote both down and left the space between them empty.

He came down off the wall at half past five.

Ward was at the bottom of the steps with his helm under his arm and blood on the plate that was not his, and he said something that Emeric did not hear, because Emeric had just come round the shoulder of the tower and could see over the roofs of the lower town to the mill.

There was a column of smoke standing straight up out of it in the still evening air, and it had been there long enough to be grey at the top.

Forty paces from the mill, under one thatch, was everything the barony of Kaldo had.

Nobody on that wall had been looking behind them since nine o'clock in the morning.
