Chapter 27 - Pebbles

He had gone out to the west farms with Rylan in the second week of October, the same week the mason first walked out to the span, and he had seen what was wrong with them in about twenty minutes.

Two fields. Every strip on the west side was cropped one year and left bare the next, and the bare half was not fed with anything but the weeds that came up on it and the few sheep that ate the weeds. The arches strode over all of it thirty feet up, and under them the farmers carried water in buckets in a dry spring, and took whatever the ground gave them in the autumn as weather is taken, as a thing nobody chooses. A good year was a gift. A bad one was a judgment. Four seeds back for every one sown was a fair year, Rylan said, and three was not unusual.

He knew what to do about it. He had known by the time they reached the end of the first lane. He had read it, once, in a thick agronomy text for an elective he had taken to fill a gap in a timetable, and he could see the page.

He said none of it.

"You've got it already," Rylan said, watching his face. "Haven't you."

"Most of it."

"And you're not going to tell them."

"Not yet." Emeric looked down the line of strips toward the hedges and the farmhouses beyond. "Who do they watch?"

Rylan did not need to think about it. "Farris. Hollis. Geddes." He counted them off. "Farris because he's young and he's got the biggest yard and the road goes past it. Hollis because he's been right about seed for fifty years. Geddes because..." He stopped, and shrugged. "Everybody watches Geddes. To see what not to do."



They met in Farris's yard, because it was the biggest and nearest the road, and because Farris had said so.

Farris was the youngest of the three, thirty or so, heavy in the jaw, and he looked at the sky while other people talked, as though he was listening to it as well. Hollis was the old man from the muster, small and brown, and so still that a hen walked past his boot without changing course. Geddes was the big one, between the other two in age, and he stood a little behind them both and watched Emeric's face the whole time and said nothing at all.

Emeric did not tell them anything. He asked. What they sowed and when. What they got back. What a bad year looked like, and how many of them there had been since the war. They answered the way men answer a tax assessor, which was fairly, and as briefly as they could manage.

At the end of it Farris said what all three of them were thinking.

"One harvest a year." He was still looking at the sky. "You try a thing and it goes wrong, that's not a lesson. That's a winter."

"And it's our winter," Hollis said. "Not yours."

"Then let it be mine." Emeric had thought about this one on the walk out. "One strip each. One. The rest you farm your own way, the way you always have. On the strip, we try it my way, and if the strip does worse than the strip beside it, I pay the difference. Out of my own purse. Not the barony's."

Geddes spoke for the first time.

"Your pocket."

"My pocket."

Geddes went on looking at him. "The last lord who had ideas about our fields was nobody," he said. "Nobody ever came."

"What are you promising?" Farris said.

"Six to the seed. Not four." He had considered promising more and decided against it. "Not this year. When it's running."

Hollis made a small noise that might have been a laugh, if anybody had ever heard him laugh, and said that he had heard six to the seed promised before, by his own grandfather, about a field that was woods now.



The arguing took the rest of October.

It happened in Farris's barn, in the evenings, after the light had gone and the work with it, with a lantern on a hook and the three of them on upturned buckets and Emeric on a sack, and Rylan in the doorway enjoying himself, and Ward somewhere outside in the dark. Emeric lost nearly all of it.

He lost the beans first. He wanted beans or peas on a strip before the wheat went into it, and they asked him why, and he tried to tell them. He got as far as something in the bean's roots taking a good thing out of the air and leaving it in the ground for whatever came next, and heard the shape the words were making in the barn, and stopped. He had done this once before, on a road, with a pot of boiling water, and it had gone exactly as badly.

"I can't tell you why," he said. "I can tell you that it does."

"Can you," said Hollis.

He lost the clover to Farris. Clover in the fallow, to feed the beasts through the winter, and Farris said that a cow turned into wet clover would blow up like a bladder and die in an afternoon, and he had seen it, and so had his father. It was true, and the book had said so too, in a paragraph Emeric had not thought hard enough about. Graze it dry, then, and mixed with grass, and never in the dew. Farris nodded as though he had been asked to agree to the obvious.

He lost the turnips to Hollis, and it was not about turnips at all.

"Who hoes them?"

"The household."

"You've got the household's children at your school three mornings in seven." Hollis did not raise his voice. He never did. "You pay us for the hands. You said. Do you pay us for the hoeing as well?"

So the turnips would go on the baron's farm first, where the reeve was paid by the barony and the hoeing could be paid from the same place, and the strips would wait a year for them.

And he lost the seed, on the fourth evening, which was the one he had been sure of.

Seed from the best heads, he said, picked by hand. Hollis listened to the whole of it with his hands on his knees.

"Where in the field?"

"The best heads. Wherever they are."

"Not the edge." Hollis turned his head and looked at him properly for the first time in four evenings. "Wind lodges the edge. Knocks the heads down and they fill half chaff. You go three paces in. And never from a wet corner. My grandmother picked seed and my mother picked it and I've picked it since I was eight, and none of us ever took a head from a wet corner." He waited. "Your book know that?"

"No," Emeric said.

"Then I'll pick the seed."

Walking back that night Rylan asked him whether he had won anything at all in four evenings in that barn, and Emeric said he had not, and was surprised to find that he did not mind.

The trouble was that losing was not getting him a single strip.

He said so at Ferra's table, on the Sunday, between two of her columns. She asked him one question, which was whether anybody on the west side had ever counted anything, and he did not know, and she went back to her columns and said nothing else about it. On the Monday she went down to the parish house and did not come back until dark.



Ferra came out on the last evening of October, with a sack.

She had spent a week on it. The reeve who had worked the baron's farm for Wexler had brought her his books with the air of a man surrendering a weapon: seed sown and grain taken, strip by strip, for as far back as he had kept them. And Doran had the tithe.

The tithe had always been taken in the field, every tenth sheaf, counted where it stood, and for every strip in every year the parish clerk had cut the count into a hazel stick and tied the sticks in bundles by the year. Nobody had thrown them out. Nobody had thrown anything out at the parish house in twenty-six years, and in the plague years nobody had been left to tidy. Ferra had gone through the bundles one stick at a time in Doran's back room, with Doran bringing her his terrible wine and apologizing for it.

She did not bring her book into the barn.

"They can't read it," she said, when Emeric asked. "They can count stones."

She swept a space on the barn floor clear with her foot and drew the west farms in the dirt with a stick, from the old tithe map she had by heart: the road, the strips running down from it in their long bands, the hedges, the farmhouses with their middens, the wood along the western edge, the arches over all of it. The three farmers watched her draw their land, and did not correct a line.

Then she opened the sack and began laying pebbles.

One pebble for every sheaf the tithe had taken off a strip in an ordinary year. She went down the strips one at a time and counted them out, three, five, four, six, and the barn was quiet enough to hear each one land.

It took her most of an hour. Nobody got up and nobody left.

Below the middens the pebbles were thick: every strip that took the runoff from a yard did better than the strip beside it that did not. At the ends of the strips, on the headlands where the plow team turned and trod the ground hard, they thinned out. Where a strip had grown beans or peas the year before, she laid that following year's pebbles in a second heap beside the first, and every second heap was bigger than the first. Every one.

And along the wood edge, where the trees took the sun in the afternoon and the water from the roots and the deer took whatever was left, there were hardly any pebbles at all.

Those were Geddes's strips.

Ferra did not look at him. She put down the last of them and said, to the floor, "Two other men had that edge before him. The sticks have their marks on them." She tapped the wood edge with her own stick. "Same count. Both of them. Whoever farms there gets that."

Nobody said anything.

Emeric was not looking at the map, and had not been for some of that hour. He was looking at the woman on her knees beside it, with barn dust to her wrists and the lantern making her paler than she was, counting stones out loud for three men who could not read a word she wrote, and getting every one of them to lean in.

Hollis was the one who broke it. He leaned forward on his bucket and put one brown finger on a strip near the road, below his own midden, thick with pebbles.

"That's the year we had peas in the low strip."

"Yes," said Ferra.

"And the wheat after."

"Yes."

Hollis looked at the second heap. "My strip does well," he said. "Always has."

"It does well because it's under your midden," Emeric said. "And because you put peas in it one year. If every strip had its turn under the peas, the others would stop doing badly. Yours wouldn't do worse. That's all a rotation is. Everybody gets that year in turn."



He drew it two mornings later in Farris's yard with a stick, because Farris asked him to, and because by then they wanted to see the whole of it.

Four boxes in a ring. Wheat. Then turnips. Then barley, with clover sown underneath it. Then the clover by itself. And round again. From the turnips and the clover he drew a line out to a pen: the beasts, fed through the winter, so that nobody had to kill half of them when the grass gave out. From the pen a line back into the ring: the dung, spread on the ground in spring. And from the ring a line out to the barn: the grain.

More beasts. More dung. More grain. Every box fed the next one, and the last one fed the first.

Farris looked at it with his heavy jaw working. Then he looked at the sky.

"One strip," he said. "To start."

"One strip," said Hollis. "And not an inch more. Beside my own, where anybody at my hedge can see the both of them."

Geddes said nothing. He took no strip, and he refused nothing, and when the others turned to go he did not go with them.

Ferra had come out that morning for her pebbles, and was standing at the barn door with the empty sack over her arm. Emeric turned to her.

"That was her map," he said, to the three of them, and to the yard. "Not mine. I've sat in that barn three weeks telling you what a book says. She showed you what your own ground says."

Ferra's hands, which had been smoothing the sack, stopped, and then went on smoothing it.

"Bram's girl?" said Hollis, from the gate.

"Ferra," said Ferra, without looking up.

Hollis considered that, and nodded, as if he had been given the right name for a field.

The pebbles were still on the barn floor where she had left them. When they went in, Geddes was already there, standing over the wood edge. He bent, slowly, the way a big man bends, and picked up one pebble from his own strip, and looked at it, and put it in his pocket. Then he went out past them without a word and down the road toward his own hedge.

Ferra watched him go. She did not say anything, and she did not replace the pebble. She swept the rest into the sack with the side of her hand.



Rylan waited until they were halfway back to the town.

"Good map."

"It was."

"How would you know?"

"I was watching their faces."

"Course you were," Rylan said, and walked the rest of the way whistling.

He brought it up again on the Monday, and on the Wednesday, and on the Thursday he brought it up in front of Declan, who did not know what it was about and laughed anyway. On the Friday, at the gate, with Emeric standing right there, he asked Ward.

"Ward. In the barn. Where was he looking?"

Ward considered Rylan the way he considered weather.

"At the map," he said.
