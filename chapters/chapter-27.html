Chapter 27 - The Works

The problem was the year, and there was not enough of it.

The farm needed cast iron, because a plow head cut out of wrought bar by a man with a hammer is a plow head that costs four days and bends in the third season. The foundry needed brick, in quantities nobody in Kaldo had ever fired at once. The kiln needed fuel. The fuel supply needed woodcutters working four months ahead of anybody wanting charcoal, in a barony where nobody had made charcoal at scale since the war and where the three men who knew how were all past fifty.

And the harvest was coming in the autumn whether any of that happened or not.

He could not build them in sequence. He sat with it for two evenings looking for a way and there was no way. Built in order, the chain came out at something over three years, and he did not have three years, because Ferra had already told him what he had, in writing, and it was one season with a bad month at the end of it.

So everything started in the same month.

He worked the dependencies on paper, properly, the way he had once worked things that mattered less, and the paper came back with three items on it that decided everything.

Excavation took the longest of anything and could start the day the ground gave. Charcoal took the longest to accumulate and nobody was making any. And the foundry had to be lit before the farm needed a single cast part, because a farm waiting on iron in September is a farm that has missed September.

Everything else could slip. Those three could not.

Ferra kept the schedule. She built it out of her own sheets in four days, on the biggest piece of paper anybody in the castle could find, and hung it on the wall of the small room off the hall, and within a fortnight every crew boss in the barony had stopped coming up the hill to find Emeric and had started coming up the hill to find her.

Emeric noticed that happening and did nothing to stop it, which was the most useful decision he made all spring.

The drawings failed in the second week and they failed carefully, which was worse than failing carelessly.

He handed out the first sheet on the pump-house frame. Clean lines, every dimension marked, a ratio stated in the corner, and he walked away from it feeling better about the season than he had felt since the thaw.

They built it wrong. And they built it wrong with enormous attention. Four men had spent two days getting a thing exactly right that was not the thing on the sheet, and when Emeric came down and stood in front of it they were visibly pleased, because they had worked hard and had measured everything and had not made a single mistake that they could identify.

They could read the rule. They could read a figure. Ferra had put that into three hundred people over a winter in a parish hall and it had held.

What none of them had was the habit of a plan.

A wall in Kaldo goes up because a man who has built walls stands on the ground and decides where it goes. He does not consult anything. There is nothing to consult. The craft in this kingdom transmits by standing next to somebody for four years and being shouted at, and it transmits well, and it has never once in its history passed through a piece of paper.

The fix took him a week and came in three parts, and he got all three of them wrong once before he got them right.

Plan and elevation, on the same sheet. One view is not enough. Two views are enough, and the reason two are enough is that a man can hold two and check them against each other, and he cannot do that with one.

A scale bar drawn on the sheet rather than a ratio written in the corner. A ratio requires arithmetic. A bar requires a stick. A bar survives a man who cannot do arithmetic, and it survives a sheet that has been rained on and redrawn by somebody else.

And numbered parts, one operation to a sheet, with every dimension given from the same edge. That last one was the expensive lesson. Dimensions given part-to-part walk an error down the whole piece, so that a sixteenth wrong at the first joint is an inch and a half wrong at the last, and every man in the chain did his job correctly.

He said the other thing in the yard in the third week, with some satisfaction, because he had been thinking it since the muster and it had been sounding better every time.

"You do not need skilled labor. You need people willing to do the work, and a sheet that tells them what the work is."

Bram let him have it for about a week.

Then he took it apart, in the yard, in front of the pump-house frame, which was still standing in its wrong shape because Emeric had decided to leave it there for a fortnight as an argument.

"Say that again with this behind you."

Emeric said it again, less well.

"You need both." Bram did not raise his voice and did not look at the men, who had stopped working anyway. "Your sheet does the fetching, the cutting to a mark and the laying to a line. That is most of it. I will give you that, and I did not expect to give you that, and you were right about it."

"But."

"But there is a part of every job where a man has to look at the thing in front of him and say that it is not right yet, and nothing on your sheet is going to tell him so. That is judgment. It comes out of four years of somebody hitting you for getting it wrong." He put his hand flat on the bad frame. "Six men built this. Every one of them did what he was told. Not one of them had anything in him that said stop."

"How many people in this barony have it."

Bram thought about it for longer than Emeric wanted him to.

"Nine. Maybe eleven. I can name them."

He named them, and it took under a minute, and two of them were dead within the decade and one of them was Colm.

So Emeric revised the method rather than the drawings. One experienced hand to a crew and six willing ones. And the experienced hand's job was not to build. It was to say no.

He wrote that down and put it on the wall next to Ferra's schedule, in her handwriting because his was worse, and it was the only instruction on that wall that he never afterward changed.

The overlay arrived by accident and by bad temper, over a flue.

They had been arguing about it for three days. Emeric could see it. Bram could see it. They were not seeing the same object, and they had proved that four times by both agreeing to something and then discovering an hour later that they had agreed to different things, and the sheet was not settling it because the sheet was the problem, because a flue is a solid shape doing a job in three directions at once and a flue drawn flat is a suggestion.

Emeric reached for the delve out of pure frustration, intending to go down and look at it properly and come back up with an answer.

He did not go anywhere.

The room stayed. Bram stayed, mid-sentence, with a piece of chalk. The half-built shell of the foundry stayed exactly where it was, in the light, with a crew on the far wall and a cart coming up the track.

And the flue was standing inside it at full size, in the air, with the real brick visible behind it.

Emeric said a word he had not said out loud in this kingdom.

Bram, who had been in the delving space twice and had strong private opinions about it that he had never shared with anyone, walked around the thing once, put his hand through the middle of it, ducked under it, came out the other side, and settled a three-day argument in about ninety seconds.

"Your throat's too tight and it's too far forward. Move it back a hand and open it two fingers."

"That is what I have been saying."

"No it isn't. You have been saying a hand and a half."

It was a hand and a half. Emeric checked that evening and did not mention it again.

They used it for the rest of the build and the rules of it got set in that first hour and never changed afterward.

It showed shape, size and position, in place, at full scale, and it showed them perfectly. It showed nothing at all about whether a thing would stand, hold heat, carry a load, drain, or survive a winter.

And it rendered whatever he understood with total confidence, which meant that a flue he had got wrong looked exactly as solid and exactly as convincing as a flue he had got right. There was no version of it that came out hazy where he was uncertain. The uncertainty simply did not render.

He wrote that limitation down himself, that night, in the driest words he could find for it, because the mode was seductive and he had known it was seductive inside about four minutes.

Ferra put it in her log under a different mark from the ordinary delve, because the cost was not the same and she had noticed that before he had.

Bram took his apprentices in the fifth week and neither of them was who Emeric would have chosen.

He had assumed one of the smith's boys off the forge district, and there were four of those, and two were good, and Bram did not take either.

He took Jorrin, who was seventeen and not particularly strong and had been on the quarry gang doing badly. And he took Neta, who was twenty-two, who had been on the shop rota counting and wrapping soap since the summer, and who had never held a hammer in her life.

Emeric asked, once, on what grounds.

"Her hands don't shake." Bram considered whether there was more. "And she listens."

That was the entire answer and Emeric did not get another one in ten years.

Colm was the brickmaker and Colm was the best craftsman on the kiln crew and Colm could not make two of anything the same.

He said so himself, without embarrassment, in the second week, when Emeric asked him about the variation in the courses. He had thrown pots for twenty-six years. Every pot he had ever made had been its own object, judged on its own, sold on its own, and nobody in twenty-six years had asked him to make one the same as another one, because why on earth would they.

A brick that is a quarter inch proud does not look wrong. It gets shimmed. A wall built out of six hundred shimmed bricks is a wall a man has to think about every time he adds a course, and thinking about every course is how you build forty feet of wall in a season instead of a fortnight.

The fix was a master form and a ring.

Bram turned the ring out of iron in an afternoon and it was the least impressive object made in Kaldo that year. A hoop. Everything passed the hoop or went back in the clay.

Colm hated it for four days and said so at volume to anyone in earshot.

On the fifth day he started rejecting his own work before anyone asked him to. By the end of the month he had made a second gauge, without being told, for collar depth on the pipe sections, because the first one had shown him what a gauge was for and he had gone and found the next thing that needed one.

Emeric gave him the reject pile to sell as drain tile and took no share of it, which was the cheapest thing he did all year and bought more than the wage board had.

The ring was the actual invention of that season and almost nobody in Kaldo noticed. It is what made a plow head that could be cast twice. It is what made brick that coursed without shimming and pipe that joined without packing, and it is why, eventually, a suit of plate made for one man fit a different man.

What went up that year went up all at once and the site was never quiet.

The foundry. Brick over stone and timber, two storeys, a steep many-planed roof to throw the weather off a shape that had too many corners in it, three chimneys, framed for three furnaces and two wheels.

The wheel. Vertical, open-spoked, set off the race where the eastern branch ran hardest, driving the bellows and the trip hammers through gearing that a millwright out of Wayford was paid a great deal to cut properly. It turned whether or not a single crystal in the barony held any charge at all. Only one of the two framed wheels went in, because the second one wanted an axle that had to be cast in the foundry that was not lit yet, which Emeric found funnier than Ferra did.

The roof crane. The same rotation thrown onto a hoist drum, lifting charging skips to the second floor. No stones anywhere in it. Hand-haulable if the wheel stopped, because it was a drum and a rope and a man.

The pump-house. A slat-bladed wheel sitting in the current, turning a lift pump, feeding a high timber tank on the slope above the west shore. No stone in the original build at all. Redundancy three deep, which the crew thought was funny: the tank held a head against a dry week, a hand crank took over when the current quit, and bolted to the frame was a pedal rig built off a cartwright's drawing so that a man's legs could do the river's work when his arms gave out.

The pedal rig looked absurd and it was the layer that made the crew like the whole thing. A rota formed on it inside a fortnight without anybody organizing one, and there was an argument by midsummer about who was fastest, and Emeric stayed entirely out of that.

The drying shed. Low, long, slatted floor, a brick heat channel running underneath it, sited on the shoulder where the prevailing draft came up off the water and did half the work for nothing.

The silo bins. Many small sealed bins rather than one large vault. Limed, damp-sealed, and set apart from one another, so that a failure cost one bin and not a winter.

The farmland. Excavation and sectioning to the pegs from March. Trench lines cut, clay-lined and graded, parallel runs rather than one main channel, and dug deeper and wider than irrigation had any need of.

Farris asked why, on the third day, leaning on a spade.

"Because I would rather dig once."

Nobody pressed him on it. The ditches carried open water for a season and a half before anything else went into them.

The town's reaction was complicated and Emeric spent the summer failing to simplify it.

There had not been this much paid work in Kaldo in eleven years, and people were glad of it, and the gladness had an edge on it that took him a while to locate.

Some of the money going out was money that used to be skimmed. Enough people worked that out that it started being said aloud in the market, and once it was said aloud it was said with a particular tone, which was not gratitude. It was the tone of a man who has just realized how long he was being robbed and by whom, and who cannot do anything about it, because the whom is dead.

And the Works and the farm were competing for the same hands, and the competition got worse every week rather than better, and by August Emeric was choosing every few days which thing did not get built this fortnight.

Hollis took the work and was on the quarry gang and then the kiln haul and did both well. He said one thing to Emeric all summer, in passing, without heat, which was that the school was still not built.

Emeric's own failure came in September and it was entirely his and it was extremely boring.

He built the foundry shell before the charcoal existed.

The kiln was at capacity. The quarry gang was at capacity. The carters were at capacity and had been since June. And the woodcutters were three men over fifty who had started six weeks late because in March he had been choosing between excavation and fuel and had chosen excavation, and excavation was the right choice, and he had then failed to go back and fix the other side of it.

So he ended the season with a finished foundry, a turning wheel, a cut hearth, a stack that drew, and nothing whatsoever to burn in it for five weeks.

It was not dramatic. Nobody was hurt and nothing fell down. It was the most ordinary kind of project failure there is, which is a thing that took longer than a person believed while he was looking somewhere else.

He wrote it down in his terms, which were that he had scheduled the visible thing and not the slow one.

Ferra wrote it in hers, which were shorter.

Then the bill came due, on schedule, exactly as she had said it would at the muster.

Five weeks of an idle foundry. A payroll that did not pause for any of it. A harvest not yet in and therefore not yet worth anything. And a set of promises made on a set of steps in front of most of the town.

She gave him the month rather than the week. She had said she would and she did.

He held the wage. He held the injury pay. He cut everything else, including the thing he wanted, which was the second charge of brick for the school foundation he had not announced to anybody and had been quietly ordering against since June.

It was visibly a choice rather than a comfortable decision and he was not gracious about it for about two days.

Nobody on the Works ever found out how close it came.

The foundry lit late in the season, in front of most of the town, because nobody in Kaldo had ever seen the inside of a working foundry and everybody had watched it go up.

There was no pour. No liquid iron, no sparks, nothing anyone could take home and describe.

A stack drawing properly. A wheel turning a set of bellows. And heat coming off a hearth inside a building that had not existed in the spring.

Bram stood in front of it for a long time without saying anything at all, with his cap in his hand, which Emeric had seen him do exactly once before, in a church.

Ferra wrote down the date.

What Emeric felt was not triumph. It was the specific relief of a man who has carried a schedule in his head for seven months and can finally put one line of it down.

And underneath that, standing in the heat with his hands behind him and the whole town making noise around him, the knowledge that he had no ore contract that would outlast the decade, and that the diggings ran out in a few years by the foreman's own arithmetic, and that he had just built the most expensive object in the barony's history around a supply he did not control.

He knew it while he was standing there and he stayed until the crowd went home.
