Chapter 27 - The Man Who Watched

The coalition hardened the week Grayrock changed sides, the way a thing hardens when it loses a piece of itself and the pieces that remain draw closer to cover the gap.

Saban brought the shape of it. The soft price got harder, not softer, which was not what Emeric had expected and was, once he thought it through, obvious.

He had taken the weakest member off the board. The ones left had deeper purses and less to lose, and they answered losing Grayrock's ore not by folding but by leaning heavier, closing ranks, matching each other's prices down further than before to prove the defection had cost them nothing.

It had cost them something. The hardening was the proof it had cost them something. It also meant the pressure on Kaldo was worse this month than last, and a man reading only the pressure and not the structure under it would have concluded the split had backfired.

"It didn't backfire," Emeric told the room. "It's working. You don't squeeze harder on a thing that isn't hurting you. They're hurting. A bloc that was leaning lazy is leaning with its whole weight now, because it has to, because it lost the one member who had the thing they actually needed. That isn't a bloc winning. That's a bloc that's been made to spend, and a thing that has to spend to hold its line is a thing you can eventually make spend more than it has."

He believed it. He also knew that believing a thing and surviving the season it took to prove true were different problems, and the debt did not care which of them he was right about.

And into that hardening rode a rider from the west, out of Milford, carrying no threat and no price and no demand. Only a request that Baron Paxton be received, at Kaldo, at the prince's convenience, on a matter Paxton preferred to put in person.

Emeric spent the day before the meeting deciding how to handle a man he did not understand.

He understood the others. Sethe was a beaten baron leaning because leaning was all a beaten man had left. The coast money was greed with good manners, buying low because low was there to be bought. Bryda was a widow counting a cold sum, and even her he had read only by not trying to read her and trusting the arithmetic instead.

Paxton he could not place at all, and the not-placing had been gnawing at him since the spring. The man had ridden up the road with a hundred men and then not spent them, had sat his banners back on the hill and watched Kaldo break the rest of the coalition and had ridden home with his strength whole.

A man who brings an army and does not use it is a man running a calculation Emeric had not been able to reconstruct, and an unreconstructed calculation in another man's head was the exact thing that had cost him nine graves at the lake and would cost him more before he was done.

So he did the thing he had learned to do in place of reading people, which was to prepare for every version of the man and let the meeting tell him which one had ridden in.

Ward vetted the escort. Saban pulled together every scrap of what Milford was. Plains country west of Kaldo, cattle and grain-fed beef and wool, no mine and no great wealth but no debt either. A solid unglamorous barony that fed itself and sold its surplus and had never once in living memory reached above its station.

A barony, in other words, with every reason to keep its head down and no obvious reason to send its baron alone to the door of a prince the whole region was busy leaning on.

Emeric built his read three ways. Paxton comes to squeeze, quietly, a friendlier face on the same pressure. Paxton comes to sound him out for the coalition, a scout with a diplomat's manners. Or Paxton comes for himself, some Milford interest Emeric could not yet see.

He did not, in any of the three, land on the version that turned out to be true, because the true version required him to believe a thing he found hard to believe, which was that a man might simply have understood him.

Paxton was older than Emeric had pictured. Grey and square and unhurried, with the weathered plainness of a man who had spent his life around cattle and account books and had never needed to be more impressive than the work required. He came with two men and left them at the door. He looked around the hall once, the way a man appraises a room he has heard about, and then he looked at Emeric, and the first thing he said was not a greeting.

"You met fire with a handful of water. On the wall, in the spring. I watched you do it."

He said it flatly, a fact laid down the way Bryda had laid down her dead husband.

"Everyone else on that field saw a mad prince throwing a cup of water at a fireball and thought you'd lost your nerve or your mind. I saw a man who knew exactly how much water it takes to kill a fire if you put it in the one place the fire lives, and not a drop more, because you didn't have a drop more to waste. I've thought about that handful of water for two seasons. I came to talk to the man who threw it."

Emeric said nothing for a moment, because the read he had built three ways had just been answered by a fourth he had not built, and he needed a breath to catch up to it.

"You held your men back," he said finally.

"I did."

"Why."

"Because I could count." Paxton lowered himself into the chair across the table without being asked, an old man's ease, past the age of standing on ceremony.

"A hundred against a wall two dozen were holding, and holding easy, on ground shaped so the hundred had to come uphill into a chute. I've sat on hills my whole life. I know what a bad piece of ground looks like from the top of it, and that was the worst piece of ground I ever saw an army asked to climb, and it had been made that way on purpose by someone who understood that dirt shaped right is worth more than any number of men."

"Renner didn't see it. Sethe didn't see it. They saw a plague town and a farm and a soft target, because that was the picture they'd been sold, and they charged the picture instead of the ground. I saw the ground."

He folded his hands. "I don't spend my men on ground like that for a picture somebody handed me. So I held them, and I watched, and I learned the thing your allies died not knowing."

"Which was."

"That you build like a man with nothing, when you're a man with everything."

Paxton's eyes did not leave him. "You've got a mage's blood. You've got crystals and runes and whatever that flying thing was that took Renner off his horse. And you lean on dirt and water and shaped ground and honest steel for every last thing you can, and only reach for the magic when the mundane runs out. I couldn't work out why, riding home. A man with your gifts, spending his cleverness on trenches."

"Then I had it, about a mile past your river." He leaned forward slightly. "Because dirt doesn't run out. A trench doesn't go dead when a stone cracks. Shaped ground can't be turned off by an enemy who kills your magic, the way you killed theirs with that thrown stone. You've built a barony that holds when the magic fails, and every other lord in this kingdom has built one that falls the moment it does."

"That's the smartest thing I've seen a lord do in thirty years, and my allies rode up your road calling you mad and died of it. I decided on the way home I'd be on the right side of you before somebody made me choose. Grayrock going over to you this week is somebody making me choose. So here I am, choosing, before the choosing gets made for me on worse terms."

It should have been the easiest alliance Emeric had ever made, and in the arithmetic it was.

Milford had beef and wool and a solid full barony and no debt, and it sat on Kaldo's western flank where a friend was worth more than a wall. Paxton asked for little. Standing trade, mutual defence, the ordinary machinery of two baronies deciding to face the same direction.

There was no widow's grief to work around, no dead husband on the table, no unreadable cold sum. Paxton had come pre-decided, had done the deciding himself two seasons ago on the ride home, and all Emeric had to do was say yes.

And yet he sat with it after Paxton had gone up to his rooms, turning it over, because something in the meeting had unsettled him and he had learned that an unsettlement was information he could not afford to ignore.

It took him a while to find it, and when he found it, it was almost funny in the bleak way the true things about himself tended to be.

Paxton had read him perfectly. Had watched one gesture on a wall, a handful of water thrown at a fire, and reconstructed from it the entire philosophy Emeric had built his barony on. The mundane-first discipline, the reasons under the reasons, the whole engine of how he thought.

Paxton had done to Emeric, from a hilltop, at a hundred paces, in one afternoon, the thing Emeric could not do to any of them across a table, which was read the man and get him right.

And Emeric, sitting opposite the one person in the whole affair who actually understood him, had spent the meeting braced for a squeeze or a scout. He had built three careful wrong readings and missed the true one entirely, because the true one required him to recognise being understood, and being understood was a thing he had no practice reading, having so rarely been on the receiving end of it.

The best ally he had made in the entire coalition fight was the one he had come closest to misjudging, and he had misjudged him in the friendly direction, which was its own kind of danger.

He had got lucky. Paxton had come as a friend and Emeric had prepared for an enemy, and the gap between them had cost nothing this time because the man on the other side of it was better than Emeric's read of him. A gap that costs nothing when the other man is good is a gap that costs everything when he is not.

He filed it beside the widow and the courier and the nine graves. Once again he had solved a problem by arithmetic and been handed the human half of it as a gift he had not earned and could not count on being given twice.

He had Milford now, and Grayrock, and a coalition down two members leaning with its whole weight to hide the loss. He had the ore and the western flank and a full granary and a growing case sealed in a stranger's strongbox near the capital. The pieces were moving the way he had meant them to move.

And the man who understood him best in all of it was a cattle baron he had nearly mistaken for a spy, which told him something he already knew and kept having to learn. The parts of this he could count were going his way. The parts he could not count were still the parts that would decide it.
