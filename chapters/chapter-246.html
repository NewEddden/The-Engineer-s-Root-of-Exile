Chapter 246 - Carbide

The furnace in bay three was fired for the first time on the morning after the stewards' reckoning, at a quarter past nine, and by ten Bram had taken his lunch outside.

He took it outside at ten, which was two hours before he ate it. He picked up Maren's basket off the shelf by the door where it sat every morning, and looked at it, and looked at the furnace, and carried the basket out into the yard and put it on the stone mounting block by the gate in the cold, with a sack over it, and came back in, and said that a man's dinner ought not to taste of whatever that was, and that if it did he would rather have no dinner.

Whatever that was came out of the furnace's door in a thin grey-yellow smoke whenever Tess opened it to look, and hung in the bay's roof under the lamps, and smelled of garlic and rotten fish and something under both of them like a lucifer match struck in a cellar.

Tess did not seem to notice it. Tess was in her chair at the furnace's control board with her sleeves pinned back and Osric's old leather apron over her knees, and her eyes on the needle of the meter that told her what the powerhouse was sending down the cable to her, and she noticed nothing at all that was not the needle.

The furnace was hers. She had drawn it from his sketch in January and built it with Bram's men in a fortnight, at the end of bay three where the powerhouse's thickest cable came in off its poles through a hole in the wall: a squat round pot of firebrick in an iron jacket, the height of a man's chest, with a lid of firebrick and two black rods of carbon as thick as a man's arm coming down through the lid on chains, so that they could be lowered and raised. The powerhouse's current came down one rod and went up the other; and between their tips, inside the pot, when Tess brought them down close enough and threw her switch, it jumped the gap.

An arc. The same thing as the lamps on the wall, the first light Kaldo had ever had that was not a flame or a stone; but a lamp's arc was the size of a fingernail, and this was the size of a man's fist, and so bright that nobody looked at it except through the little window of smoked glass in the furnace's side, and so hot that nothing in the world that Kaldo knew of could stand in it and stay what it was.

In the bottom of the pot, under the arc, Tess had put lime, burned white in the kilns, and coke from the gas works, crushed, mixed, a barrowful. The arc melted them. In its heat they did something to each other that they would not do in any fire, and became something else: a grey stone, dark, crystalline, that Tess's men tapped out of a hole in the bottom of the pot at noon into an iron tray, glowing, and let cool, and broke up with hammers into lumps the size of a fist.

Calcium carbide. He had told her the name in January and she had written it on the drawing in her small square hand, and on the side of the tray in chalk.

It looked like nothing. Grey rock. Tess picked a lump of it out of the tray with the tongs when it was cold enough and held it up in the bay's lamplight, turning it, and looked at him over it.

"Now?" she said.

"Now."

She dropped it in the bucket of water by her chair.

The water went white and seething, and the lump fizzed in the bottom of it like a fistful of lime in a pail, and up out of the bucket came a gas, a great deal of gas, out of one grey stone; and with it the smell, ten times worse, so that Osric, who was at the bay door, went out of it and did not come back for a quarter of an hour.

Tess held a taper over the bucket.

It lit. A flame stood up off the water, tall and yellow and so sooty that a black thread of smoke went up out of the top of it to the roof, and so bright that the lamps beside it looked brown.

"Acetylene," said Emeric.

Tess watched it burn. Then she put the lid on the bucket, and the flame went out, and she wrote the word on the drawing under the other one, carefully, and sat looking at it.

"A rock that makes fire out of water," she said.

"A rock that makes a gas out of water, that burns hotter than anything you've ever lit."

"Hotter than the arc?"

"No. Nothing's hotter than the arc. But you can't carry the arc to a ship's frame in a hose." He looked at the bucket. "You can carry this."



He tried the arc himself, that first afternoon, while Tess's men were raking out the pot for the second charge.

He had not meant to. He stood beside the furnace with the cable humming at his elbow and the arc's light still printed blue on the inside of his eyes from the smoked window, and it came to him that he had never once in nine years tried to do by hand the thing that lit every lamp in Kaldo; and he put out his hand, palm down, a foot above the brick of the furnace's lid, between the two carbon rods, and called it.

Lightning was the weakest thing he had. It always had been, the one that should have been easiest for an electrical engineer and was hardest of all: everything outside fire and weight cost him three times, and lightning cost him three times and then refused to stay. He called it anyway. The air between his palm and the brick went tight, and the hair on his arms stood up, and the glyph came up off his hand, and with a crack like a whip and a flash that put spots in front of everybody's eyes in the bay, a spark jumped from his palm to the left-hand rod.

And was gone.

A burst. The length of a heartbeat. He tried to hold it, to keep the path open, to make it run from his palm to the rod and stay running as the arc in the pot stayed running all day; and it would not. It was all there at once and then it was not there at all. He tried again. A crack, a flash, a smell of hot air. Nothing steady. He tried a third time and got a spark the length of his finger that left his palm numb to the wrist and did not light anything.

Tess, in her chair, had watched all three.

"It doesn't stay," she said.

"It doesn't stay. It never has. A cast lightning's a burst. All of it at once, and gone." He shook his hand out. "What comes down that cable from the powerhouse is a current. It stays as long as the engine turns. It's the same thing as what came off my hand, and it's nothing like it."

Tess looked at the furnace, which had done all day without anybody's hand what his hand could not do for the length of a heartbeat.

"I'll take the cable," she said.



The stewards came through the works that afternoon, at two, with Ferra; it was the day after the reckoning, the day of the Stewards' Council's first sitting, and Ferra had said at its end that if they were going to tell him what the holdings needed they had better see what Kaldo had, and had walked them down the hill.

They smelled bay three from the lane.

So did the lane. The bay's big doors were open for the smoke, and the smoke went out of them and down the lane behind the works between the old terraces, where the washing hung in the yards on lines; and two women at a gate had their aprons over their noses and were saying to anybody who passed that it was the third time since midwinter the works had poisoned their washing, and who did the duke think he was.

Bryda Renner stood at the bay's door with her hand over her nose and looked at the furnace, and the lane, and the women at the gate, and the washing.

"You'd not put that next to my rows," she said.

"No."

"You've put it next to theirs."

He looked at the lane. It was true. The works had grown where it had grown, nine years, out of the old forge yard behind Bram's house and along the river, a bay at a time as each thing was needed, wherever there was room for it; and the terraces had grown round it, wherever there was room for them; and the furnace that stank like a dead fish's cellar stood forty yards from a line of washing because that was where the cable came in.

Paxton, at Bryda's shoulder, said that at Milford they had the tannery upstream of the village, on the beck, because that was where it had always been, and the whole village drank what came down past it, and always had.

"And the slaughter yard?" said Emeric.

"Behind the inn," said Paxton. "Where else?"

He wrote the Siting Code that night, at his own table, in an hour, on one sheet.

It was not long. It did not need to be. He had known every line of it for nine years, and had broken half of them himself, a bay at a time, because there had always been something more urgent; and he wrote it down now plainly, for all four baronies, as law.

Drink upstream. Discharge downstream. Every town takes its water above itself and puts its waste below itself, and nothing that makes waste is built above any town's intake.

Smoke goes downwind. The wind in all four baronies is from the west three days in four; anything that smokes or stinks is built east of where people sleep.

Heavy freight beside rail and water. Whatever needs coal and ore brought in by the ton is built where the rails and the river can bring it, and nowhere else.

Nothing heavy inside a wall.

And under those, a schedule, by holding: Grayrock's smelting moved below the rows and downwind of them; Milford's tannery below the village on the beck, and its slaughter yard out past the last house; Wayford's dye vats out of the posting street; and Kaldo's own old works, the bay at a time it had grown in, moved bay by bay to the new ground beside the port, north-east and downwind, over years, as each one wore out.

At the bottom he wrote one exception, and did not explain it, because the people who needed it explained already knew it. The Forge stays where it is.

Ferra read it in the morning and copied it into the table's book under the stewards' first sitting, and sent fair copies up the line to all three holdings by the noon train; and at the end of the month, when the trainee lists came down from the holdings, Bryda's had a note on its back in her own blunt hand: Smelter moving. Rows first. Your Code.

Grayrock's wagon shop had a letter the same week, in Ferra's office hand, asking what it would charge to build coaches. Not freight wagons. Coaches with seats, for people, a dozen to start, for the line.



The carbide stank all the rest of February.

Tess ran the furnace every day, and every day it stank, and Bram ate his lunch on the mounting block in the cold, and the women in the lane hung their washing on the other side of their houses; and Tess got the charge right by the third week, so that a tray of carbide came out of the pot every noon that broke clean and grey and gave its gas freely in the bucket, and the gas holders she had built beside the furnace, two upturned iron bells floating in tanks of water that rose as they filled, stood higher every afternoon.

Osric came to bay three every noon with the basket.

He had come every noon since the war, with Maren's basket packed for two, and put it on the end of Tess's bench, and eaten his half standing up while she ate hers at the board without looking up from the needle; and on the twenty-third, when Bram was in the bay for once, standing at the furnace's other side with his collar over his nose looking at the gas bells, Osric put the basket down on the bench and did not take his half out of it.

He went round the furnace instead, to Bram.

Emeric was at the bench with the oxygen bottles. He saw Osric go, and saw his face, which was the face of a man walking up to a wall in a dead zone, and kept his own eyes on the bottles.

"Master Bram," said Osric.

Bram took his collar off his nose.

"I'd ask your leave," said Osric, "to walk out with your daughter."

He said it all at once, and stood there after it, very straight, in his plate, with his big hands at his sides; and he was a knight of Kaldo who had fought in the war and dropped off the wall in a suit with Rylan and stood in the dead field at the stream ground, and his ears were the colour of a forge.

Bram looked at him for some time.

Then Bram did what he had done nine years ago, at his anvil, without stopping his hammer, when a prince three weeks in Kaldo had walked into the forge and asked his leave to put Ferra to work on the barony's books. He looked over Osric's shoulder at his daughter in her chair at the board.

"Ask her," said Bram, and put his collar back over his nose.

Osric turned round.

Tess had not taken her eyes off the needle. She did not take them off it now. She sat in her chair at the board with the arc's light coming blue through the smoked window onto the side of her face and the garlic stink hanging in the roof, and said, to the needle, in the flat voice she used when something mattered and she was measuring it:

"You've been walking out with me since the fire drills."

Osric opened his mouth.

"Since the fire drills," said Tess. "Three years. You carry the basket. You fetch my lamp oil before I've noticed it's low, and when I ask why, you say you were passing. You push my chair up the hill when the motor's charged and doesn't need pushing. Maren packs for two. Everybody in this bay knows it. Everybody in the works knows it." She turned her head at last and looked at him. "You're the last to know."

Osric stood in the middle of bay three in his plate with his mouth open.

"Well," he said. "Then."

"Then," said Tess, and turned back to the needle, and put out her hand without looking, palm up, toward the bench.

Osric went and got the basket and put her half in it.

Bram, at the furnace, watched the gas bells rise, with his collar over his nose and his shoulders doing something under his apron that might have been a cough.



The first flame was wrong.

It was wrong in the way he had known it would be, and he did not tell her, because he had decided in January that the flame was hers, and that he would stand at her shoulder and keep his mouth shut until it was lit or she asked.

The torch was Bram's, from his drawing: a brass handle with two valves, one for each gas, and two hoses running from it, one to the acetylene bell and one to a bottle of oxygen from the plant at the secret dock, where Tobe had been making it since the autumn out of the same cold and pressure that turned the Band's mana into a blue pool in a flask. Air, squeezed and cooled until it ran like water, and then let warm slowly, so that the parts of it came off one at a time; and the part that came off last, pale blue and heavy, was the part that made a fire roar. He had had Tess run it in November for her own reasons. This was the reason.

The gases met in the torch's neck and came out of its tip together. An oxy-acetylene torch, he had written across the top of Bram's drawing; the yard would be calling it the gas torch inside a month. Tess opened the acetylene and lit it with a spark-striker, and a long yellow flame came off the tip, smoky, ragged, with a black thread off its end that went up to the roof; and she opened the oxygen, a little, and the flame went shorter and whiter, and she opened it more.

The flame snapped.

It went back. A pop like a cork, sharp, and the flame was not at the tip any more but inside the torch, burning back down the neck into the handle with a shriek, and the brass went hot in her hand all at once; and Tess, without a sound, shut both valves, oxygen first, so fast that her hand was a blur, and the shriek stopped.

The bay was very quiet. Osric was halfway across it, with his hand out. Bram had come round the furnace, and had hold of the back of her chair, and did not seem to know that he had.

Tess sat with the torch across her knees and looked at it.

Emeric opened his mouth.

"Wait," said Tess.

He shut it.

She sat with the torch on her knees for some time. Then she unscrewed the tip and looked down its bore, and looked at the two valves, and at the hoses, and back down the bore; and he watched her measure it in her head the way she measured everything, with her own body, as she had measured the spring of a gate latch at fifteen by how far it pushed her thumb.

"It went back up the gas," she said. "Faster than the gas came down. The mix in the neck caught, because the gas wasn't coming out quick enough to keep the flame out at the tip." She turned the tip over in her fingers. "Too little gas, too much air. Too slow out of the tip." She looked up at Bram. "I want a smaller hole in this. And a pressure on the gas that doesn't drop when the bell does. And something in each hose that lets gas go one way and not the other, so if it ever goes back again it stops in the hose and not in my hand."

Bram looked at the tip in her fingers. Then at Emeric, who said nothing at all.

"A check valve," said Bram slowly. "A ball. On a seat. Gas pushes it off, it goes; gas comes back, it sits." He took the tip from her. "Tomorrow."

It was two days. Then she lit it again, with the smaller tip and Bram's ball valves in both hoses and a weighted regulator on the acetylene that held its pressure; and the flame came off the tip yellow and smoky again, and she opened the oxygen, a little, and a little more, slowly, with her eyes on the flame and not on anything else in the world.

The yellow went out of it. The smoke went out of it. The flame drew in, and in, and inside the pale outer feather of it something formed at the tip: a small cone, a thumb's length, sharp-edged, a blue so intense and so clean that it looked solid, like a shard of glass held to the light.

Tess stopped turning the valve. She held it there.

The cone did not move. It stood on the tip, perfectly still, roaring very softly, and the air round it shook with its heat.

"There," said Tess.

She did not look at him. She did not need to. She held the torch out over the boiler plate that Bram's men had laid across two firebricks on the floor of the bay, a sheet of iron as thick as his thumb, the kind it took a man half a day to cut with a hacksaw; and she put the blue cone's tip to its edge, and held it, and the iron went red, and orange, and white, in a spot the size of a coin. Then she reached for the second lever on the handle that Bram had put there from Emeric's drawing without knowing what it was for, and pressed it, and a jet of pure oxygen went down through the middle of the flame onto the white-hot spot.

The iron burned.

Not melted; burned, like a log, in a shower of sparks that went across the bay floor in a fan; and the torch went through the plate. She drew it along, slowly, steadily, her hand perfectly still on the handle, and a line of fire and sparks went across the boiler plate behind the torch's tip, and the plate opened behind it like cloth under a knife.

Like a knife through cheese. He had read that line in a book once, about the first men who did this, and thought it a cheap way to say it, and stood in bay three now with the sparks going past his boots and found that it was exactly right and he could not think of a better.

Tess drew the torch off the far edge. The cut piece dropped onto the floor with a clang. She shut the oxygen jet, and then the oxygen, and then the gas, in that order, and the flame went out, and the bay rang.

Osric said a word that a knight should not say in front of the woman he had been walking out with for three years.

Bram picked up the cut piece in his apron and turned it over and looked along the cut edge. It was straight. It was clean. It had taken her less time to cut a hand's length of boiler plate than it would have taken him to find his hacksaw.

"Ships," said Bram. Then, to himself, very low: "Frames. Bridges. The truss rods." Then he looked at his daughter. "How did you know when to stop turning it?"

"It looked right," said Tess.

Emeric had not said one word since the flame snapped back. He did not say one now. He went back to the bench with the oxygen bottles, and wrote in his notebook only the date, and Tess's name, and under it nothing at all.



The furnace had other work in it, and he gave it to her the next week, because a pot that could make carbide could make anything that needed heat more than a fire could give.

The first was steel. Kaldo had made steel since the second year and made it well, Bram's way, in the old furnaces with coal; and the trouble with steel made in coal was the carbon, which got into it from the fire whether you wanted it or not and made it hard and brittle and quick to rust. In the arc there was no coal and no flame, only the current, and a melt could be held there with its carbon as low as a man wanted it; and with the carbon held down, and the grey chrome ore that came down from Grayrock with the iron melted into it, the steel that came out of Tess's pot did not rust.

The Forge had made it before. Maren's kitchen was made of it, and Imani's instruments, and Ferra's calculator's wheels, and every one of those had come out of the cellar, one at a time, under his hand. This came out of a pot in bay three, under Tess's hand, by the hundredweight, in an afternoon, and nobody had to be in the cellar for it.

The first melt went three ways, by Ferra's book. To the hospital, for basins and trays and the tall steaming kettles Imani boiled her linen in, that rusted through every year and were patched and rusted through again. To Maren's kitchens, for the big pans the castle cooked for the garrison in, which were iron and black and tasted of it. And to the acid works by the river, for pipe, because the acid ate every pipe Kaldo had ever laid for it and would not eat this.

Stainless steel. He had said the words to Maren at midwinter over a roll of knives. He said them to Bram now over a tray of grey ingots in bay three, and Bram picked one up in his black-seamed hand and turned it over, and held it to the light from the door, and turned it over again.

"Out of a pot," said Bram.

"Out of a pot."

"Not out of the cellar." Bram put the ingot down on the tray, very carefully, with the others. "Good."



The second thing he gave her was harder, and he gave it to her on the first of March, with a jar.

The jar had come up from the cellar the night before in his coat. In it, set hard and white like a cake of salt, was something the Forge had made in a run of its own: a pale crystalline mineral that existed in only one place on his other world, a cold coast in the far north, and in no place in this one that he knew of, which the Forge had built for him in a night out of fluorspar and soda ash and the alum from the dyehouse, a thing at a time. Cryolite. He did not say the word yet. He put the jar on Tess's bench beside the small iron motor she had taken apart at midwinter and put back together four times since, and sat down on the stool beside her chair.

"There's a metal," he said, "in every clay bank in the four baronies."

Tess put down her pencil.

"In every clay pot. Every brick. In the alum the dyers use. In the ruby on a ring. It's the commonest metal in the ground anywhere, more than iron. And nobody has ever had a pound of it." He touched the jar. "Because it holds on to what it's mixed with harder than anything. No fire will get it out. Coal won't take it off. You can heat the clay till it runs and it's still clay." He looked at the furnace. "Only a current will."

"What's it called?"

"Aluminum."

Tess wrote it down. She spelled it the way he said it, carefully.

"You melt this," he said, and tapped the jar. "In an iron pot lined with carbon. Not in the arc; on a current through it, the pot one side and a carbon rod the other. It melts at a heat the furnace can give it easily, and when it's molten you stir in the white dust you get from the alum, and it dissolves, the way salt dissolves in water. And then the current goes through the melt, and the metal comes out of it, and sinks, and lies on the carbon at the bottom of the pot." He sat back. "A little at a time. A great deal of current for a very little metal."

"How little?"

"Today? A button."

It was a button.

It took her all day. She built the pot herself, in the morning, from a crucible and a carbon lining Bram's men cut from the ends of old furnace rods, and set it on the bench beside the board with its own cable; and melted the white cake in it at noon, and stirred in the alum dust, which Ivo had roasted for her, a spoonful at a time, and dissolved; and threw her switch at one, and sat with her eyes on the needle and her hand on the switch, and the pot hissed and fumed very faintly, a sharp thin fume that Emeric moved her chair back from and opened the high window for, until the light went.

At five she shut it off and let it cool. At six she broke the crust off the top with a small hammer and reached into the bottom of the pot with the tongs.

What came out was the size of a thumbnail. A rough round button, grey-white, dull, lumpy, like a drop of tin that had fallen on a hearth. She held it in the tongs and looked at it. Then she put the tongs down, and took it in her bare palm.

Her hand went up.

It went up an inch, all on its own, the way a hand goes up when it braces for a weight and the weight is not there; and Tess looked at the button in her palm, and then put it on the little brass balance on her bench, Ottoline's, that weighed her charges, and read the beam.

She took it off. She put it back on. She read the beam again.

"It weighs nothing," she said.

"A third of iron, for the size."

"It weighs nothing." She weighed it a third time. Then she held it in her palm again and closed her fingers on it and sat in her chair with her fist in her lap and her eyes on the pot, and he watched her see it. Not the button. What the button was. A metal that weighed a third of iron; frames for a thing that had to fly, and wire for a line that had to be hung from poles across four baronies, and the chair under her, and the arm of a man who had lost his hand.

"More current," said Tess.

"A great deal more."

"A powerhouse's worth."

"A powerhouse of its own, one day. Pots in rows. And a dam."

Tess opened her fist and looked at the button.

"Then I'll want a dam," she said.



Abel Dunning came into bay three while the button was still cooling on the balance pan, to have the spring on his hook renewed.

He came every few months. The spring that closed the hook wore out, as springs did, and Tess had made the hook with Bram the year the fifth fuse went early on the quarry road, after Imani took what was left of the hand above the wrist; she renewed the spring herself, always, in a quarter of an hour, at her bench, and Abel sat on the stool and talked to her about the quarry while she did it, because Fennick still paid his wage and he still went up to the quarry every day to sit in the hut and tally the stone, and liked to have somebody to tell about it.

He sat on the stool now, with his sleeve rolled up and the leather cup off his forearm on the bench, and talked about the new face Fennick was opening, while Tess wound the new spring; and Emeric, who had been about to go up the hill, did not go.

He looked at the hook on the bench. A split hook of steel, two curved fingers that opened when Abel shrugged his shoulder against a strap that ran across his back, and closed on a spring when he let it go. It was a good hook. It had cost Kaldo a fortnight and a little iron, and Abel could hold a cup with it, and a pen, and his tally board. He could not do up his own buttons with it; he had told Imani that once, at the ward, without meaning to.

And beside the hook on the bench lay the motor. Tess's midwinter present, the little round iron one with no brushes, nothing touching, that ran on the alternator's back-and-forth current, which she had had apart four times. It was the size of a fist.

He looked from the motor to the hook, and back, and began without meaning to.

A hand. Not a hook. Four fingers and a thumb, jointed, of the new light metal, if there were ever enough of it. A tendon for each finger, a cord in a sleeve, as a hand had, running down into the forearm. A motor in the forearm to pull the cords. A battery for the motor. And the thing that told the motor when to pull: a man thought close and the hand closed; thought open, and it opened.

And there it stopped.

It stopped at the same place every time he walked it through, and he walked it through three times, sitting on the stool by Tess's bench while she wound the spring. The thinking. Something had to hear Abel think close, through the skin of his forearm, where the muscles he had left still moved when he tried to move the hand he did not have; something had to hear that faint flicker, and decide it meant close, and tell the motor. Something had to choose.

A control stone would do it. A grey seed, bound to the arm, written inside by a delver with what close meant and what open meant and the difference between Abel lifting a cup and Abel's arm twitching in his sleep. There was one grey seed spare, the last, and he would not spend the last on a first try. Every other one in Kaldo was in somebody's band, or the Forge, or the gate, or the printer, and the one in the brooch was going in the ground.

And the other road, the one with no stone in it, needed a way to make the faint flicker in Abel's forearm big enough to drive a motor. An amplifier. A thing that took a whisper of current and made it a shout. Tess's carbon repeater on the north line did it, a little, badly, for a voice; it would not do it for this. What would do it was a glass valve he could draw in his sleep and Kaldo could not yet make, because Ivo could not yet pull a vacuum hard enough or seal a wire through glass well enough to keep it.

Two walls. And behind the first two, a third: the motor itself. A motor in a man's forearm was too heavy, and too loud, and too hot, and too slow, all at once. On his other world there had been muscles made of wire, fibres that drew in when a current went through them as a muscle draws in, but they wanted a finer forge than Kaldo's and a voltage that would kill a man if the sleeve split.

He sat on the stool and looked at it all, the three walls, one behind the other.

Then he took a pencil off Tess's bench and the back of one of her carbide drawings, and drew the one thing in the whole arm that he could build. At the elbow. A gearbox for the joint: a disc with a wavy rim, like a flower with rounded petals, rolling round inside a ring of pins, driven by a crank set off its centre. Each turn of the crank walked the disc back by one petal. A big turn slow, from a small fast one, in a box as flat as a biscuit tin, with no play in it, that would hold its place when the motor stopped. A cycloidal drive. He wrote the name under it, and drew the disc's profile out once, carefully, from the rule he had for it; and left the sheet on Tess's bench under the motor, face up, without a word.

Tess, winding the last turn of Abel's spring, looked at the sheet under the motor, and at him, and did not say anything either.

Abel shrugged the hook on and opened it, and closed it, and opened it again, and said it was better than new.

"Thank you, miss," he said, and then, to Emeric, on his way to the door, cheerfully, because he was a cheerful man: "Your Grace was looking at my hook a while there."

"I was."

"Thinking of making me a better one?"

Emeric did not answer at once.

"Thinking," he said. "Only thinking. I'll not promise you anything I can't build."

"Nobody ever has," said Abel, quite cheerfully, and went out with his hook in his pocket and his other hand in the other, whistling.

Emeric sat on the stool. How far did control go? A stone that chose. A hand that heard a man think. A suit of plate at a High mage's door that could not tell an apprentice from a thief. He did not know. He sat in bay three with the button cooling on the balance pan, and did not know, and found that he wanted very badly to find out.



Lantern Night was on the sixth of March, a Saturday, clear and very cold.

It had been clear and cold for a week, the hardest frost of the winter coming late, as it sometimes did; and the river was black and moving between banks of white, and the canal that ran west out of the town past the ice house toward the lock villages was frozen from bank to bank, so hard that the ice house had cut its last blocks from it on the Thursday and the boys of the lower town had been skating on it all week.

The town came out onto the river wall at dusk, as it had every year since the first, with its lanterns.

He stood on the wall among them, as he had every year, with Ferra on one side and Imani on the other and Ward a pace behind, and watched the town light them: paper and cane and a little cup of fat in the bottom of each, held up over the water by somebody's hands until the hot air filled it, and let go. Each one carried a name. Each household wrote on its lantern the name of somebody it had lost, and sent it up over the river, and watched it go west on the wind until it was a spark among the stars, and then nothing. The first year there had been three hundred, from the wall alone. This year Ferra had counted the paper sold, and it was more than nine hundred.

Below the wall, along the river and up the lanes, he could see the town lit under them. That was the other thing Lantern Night was for, now, though nobody had decided it should be. You looked down from the river wall as the lanterns went up, and you saw what was lit below them that had not been lit the year before: the lamps on their posts along the new streets of the lower town; the terraces' windows, each with its bulb; the hospital on its hill, lit end to end. And you saw how many roofs were still thatch, dark, with a tallow candle in a window. Fewer than last year. A good many fewer. He counted, without meaning to, and lost count, and was glad to.

Ferra's lantern was white, and plain, and small. She had written on it in her square capitals, small, one name; she had shown it to him at the counting room table that afternoon, the only time she ever had, and he had read it and said nothing, and she had folded it shut again. Her mother's name. She did not say it aloud, and nobody else on the wall ever saw it, because she held it with the writing turned in toward her, until the hot air had it.

She let it go from the river wall at full dark, without a word. It went up slowly, turning, a small white light among the hundreds, and the wind took it out over the black river, west; and Ferra stood with her hands still up where she had let it go, and watched it, and he watched her, until she put her hands down.

Further along the wall Tess let hers go from her chair, with Osric beside her holding the hoop steady while it filled, and the two names on it went up after Ferra's.

Then Ferra turned to him.

"Now," she said.



They slipped away as the last of the lanterns went up and the wall began to break up into the crowds going home and the crowds going to Maren's barrow on the quay; and Ward, who knew where they were going, because Emeric had told him that afternoon, stayed on the wall with Imani and watched them go, and did not follow.

Emeric had his sense open, as he always had it open. He would know if anybody came near them in the dark. Ward knew he would. That was the only reason Ward stayed on the wall.

The sled was at the ice house, where he had left it in the afternoon. It was a lock-keeper's sled, the kind the canal men used in a hard winter to go up and down the ice to the locks and back: a little chair of ash on two long iron runners, with a rail behind it for a man on skates to push. Ferra sat in the chair in her furs with a rug over her knees. He strapped the skates to his boots and put his hands on the rail, and pushed off.

The canal went away west in front of them under the stars, a long straight road of black ice between two white banks, with the willows along it hung with frost and the town's glow falling away behind; and the sled ran on its iron runners with a long hissing sound, and he pushed it, long strokes, faster, with the cold coming past his face so hard it made his eyes run. Over their heads the lanterns went west with them on the wind, hundreds, a slow river of small lights going the same way they were going, a little higher than the willows.

Ferra put her head back against the chair's back and looked up at them.

"Faster," she said.

He went faster.

The aqueduct came up out of the dark at two miles: the long line of its arches across the valley floor, black against the stars, carrying the water from the hills to the town's tower as it had carried it for two hundred years before it broke, and for nine years since it was mended. The canal ran under it, through the third arch, and beyond the arch, on the far side, there was a place on the bank where the ground rose and a man could stand and see the whole of the valley back to the town and the whole of the sky above it, and every lantern in it, and nobody on the river wall could see him at all.

That was where they were going. He had found it in the autumn, walking the aqueduct with Linus, and had not told anybody, and had meant ever since to take Ferra there on Lantern Night where nobody would be looking at the prince.

The culvert was just short of the arch.

He knew it was there. He had walked the bank in the autumn. A stream came down off the valley's side and went under the canal in a stone pipe, and came out on the far side and went on down to the river; and where it ran under the canal, the canal's water moved, a little, over the pipe, all winter long. Moving water froze thin. He knew that. He had known it all week, and had known it on the wall, and had known it skating, and had looked up at the lanterns going over and at Ferra's face turned up to them in the chair, white and laughing, and had forgotten it entirely.

The ice did not crack. It gave. There was a sound under the runners like a long sigh, and the sled's front went down, quite gently, as a boat's bow goes down into a wave; and then everything went down, and the black water came up over the runners and the chair and the rug and Ferra and him, and closed.



It was so cold that it was not cold. It was a blow. It hit every part of him at once, and drove the breath out of his chest in one great gasp that took in water instead of air; and it was black, entirely black, black above and below, with the sled gone somewhere under him and his coat and his skates dragging him down and his ears full of a roaring that was his own blood.

Ferra.

He did not see her. He could not see anything. He did not need to see her. His sense had her, as it had everything, always: a bright quick note in the black water an arm's length from him and going down, tangled in the rug, kicking, not yet panicking, very nearly.

He did the only thing the rules let him.

Weight was his. Weight on himself, the first thing he had ever learned to do with it, on the floor of his room behind the shutters with his heart going: he took his own weight off himself, all of it, and put it back on upside down. He went up out of the canal as if he had been thrown, straight up, through the hole in the ice in a burst of black water, and hung there, over it, in the starlight, soaked, gasping, a man's height above the hole with the water streaming off him. Then he put his hand down at the water, at the bright note in it, and pulled.

Not her weight. He could not lift another's weight with his own gravity; the rules did not allow it, and the mana in her would not have let it. But toward his hand, he could pull: a living body that held little mana, toward the hand that called it, as he had pulled a coin across a table and a tool off Bram's wall into his hand a thousand times. She held so little. He pulled, and the black water at the hole's edge broke, and Ferra came up out of it in a rush of water and rug, as if somebody below had lifted her, straight toward his outstretched hand; and he caught her wrist, and then the rest of her, and held her.

He brought them both down on the bank in the snow. Not gently. He did not have gentle left. They came down on the frozen grass in a heap, soaked, and lay there.

The canal's black hole steamed behind them in the starlight. The sled was gone under the ice. Over their heads, the last of the lanterns went west.



Ferra was coughing. Coughing and shaking, already, on her hands and knees in the snow with the water running off her, so hard he could hear her teeth.

He was shaking too. He could feel the cold going into him from the wet clothes, deeper with every breath, and knew exactly what it was and how fast it went, two miles from the walls, at night, in March, soaked to the skin in a frost that had frozen a canal from bank to bank. The trial had put the numbers in his head and he wished it had not.

Fire first.

There was a dead willow on the bank, three paces off, fallen in some old winter, grey and dry under its frost. He got to it on his knees and put both hands on the trunk where it lay, and called his fire, his own, the one thing besides weight that had always been his, and the willow went up in one great roar of yellow light, the whole length of it, so suddenly that Ferra flinched back from it in the snow.

It was not enough. He knew it as soon as he felt it. A fire warmed your front and froze your back, and their clothes were full of water that would take an hour to dry and steal every bit of heat out of them while it did; and Ferra had stopped coughing and started shaking in a way he did not like, a deep slow shudder that came up from her middle.

So he did the other thing.

She had known about it for three years. Since the glasshouse, and the truth; since he had told her and Imani in one room that he was not one thing but two, a mage and a knight both, and had seen their faces take it. She had seen him use the knight's side in the yard, at the drills, and on the stair once carrying her. She had never seen him use it for this. Nobody had. He had never tried it.

A knight in the cold did not shiver. Everybody knew that. Ward had stood guard on a wall in a January wind in his shirt and not felt it, because a knight sent his mana inward, into his own body, where it cost almost nothing, and the body did with it what the body needed. Strength, if he was lifting. Speed, if he was running. And heat, if he was cold. The body knew how. It burned what it was given, as a stove burns coal, and the knight stood on the wall and was warm.

He pushed his mana inward.

Not out, as a mage did, through the hand and into the world. In. Down, into his chest and his arms and his legs and his belly, everywhere at once, the way he had pushed it into his wrists to toss a pan the size of a cartwheel at midwinter, and told it nothing at all except: you are cold. And the body took it, as Armand's heart had taken his mana in the Hod and done with it what it needed; took it, and burned it.

The heat came up in him like a tide.

It started in his chest, a warmth like a hot drink going down, and then spread, outward, fast, into his arms and his hands and down his legs, and kept coming; past warm, past the warmth of a body, until the wet shirt on his back began to steam in the firelight and the snow under his knees went to water.

"Off," he said. "The wet. All of it. Now."

Ferra did not argue. That was how he knew how cold she was. She got her furs off with shaking hands, and her dress, which was wool and weighed what a sack of meal weighs when it is full of canal, and he got his coat off and his shirt, and the wet things went on the snow in a heap; and his cloak, which had been on his back under the coat, was the only thing either of them had that was not soaked through, because it had been rolled under the coat's collar for the wind. It was wet at the hem. It was the best thing in the world.

He sat down on the dry side of the fire with his back to the willow's root and pulled her down into his lap, her back against his chest, and wrapped the cloak round both of them, and held her.

The heat went into her.

He felt it go. He was burning, inside, steady, like a hearth stone at the back of a range that has had a fire on it all day; and she was ice, all down the front of him, from her shoulders to her knees, and he held her against it and the heat went out of him into her as water goes downhill. He felt her shudder. He felt the shudder go on, and then go on less, and then come in waves with longer gaps between, and then stop; and her breath, which had been short and catching, went long, and deep, and slow.

The fire roared in the willow. Overhead, the sky was full of lanterns.

"You're a stove," said Ferra, after a long time, into the cloak.

"Hybrid," said Emeric.

Ferra laughed. It was a very small laugh, and it shook, and at the end of it she turned her head against his shoulder and put her cold nose into the side of his neck, under his jaw, where the heat was greatest, and kept it there.

"I always wanted to know what that was for," she said. "The other half."

"This."

"Just this?"

"It's the first time I've done it."

"Then I'll write it down," said Ferra, "when I've a book that isn't at the bottom of a canal."

Her book. He felt her remember it, and felt her decide not to mind, which for Ferra was a thing he had never seen. She lay against him with her eyes shut in the firelight, warm now, warm all through, her skin against his skin under the one cloak; and after a while her hand came up out of the cloak's edge and found his face, and turned it down to her.

She kissed him slowly. She did everything slowly, when she meant it. Her mouth was cold and then it was not, and her hand went from his face to the back of his neck, and held him there; and then she turned in his lap, inside the cloak, to face him, carefully, so as not to let the cold in at the edges, and the heat that came off him went into all of her at once.

"You're very warm," she said, against his mouth.

"I can't stop it."

"Don't," said Ferra.

The fire burned down in the willow. The lanterns went over, and over, and thinned, and were a few, and then were none; the last of them went over the black line of the hills to the west, small and far, and was gone. Under the third arch of the aqueduct, on a bank where nobody could see them, two people lay in a cloak by a burned-down fire in the snow, and the heat came off the one of them like a hearth, and the other did not let go of it.



They walked home along the towpath at two in the morning, under the stars, in his cloak and her furs, which had dried by the fire to a stiff steaming board, and their wet clothes in a bundle on his back. He ran hot the whole way. He could not have stopped it if he had tried, and he did not try; Ferra walked inside the cloak against his side, and the frost on the towpath went to water under his boots, and behind them on the path there was a line of dark wet footprints in the white all the way back from the aqueduct, his only, with hers beside them dry.

Ward was at the gate. He had been at the gate, the watch said afterward, since midnight, without a lantern, and had not asked any of them anything.

He looked at the two of them come up out of the dark. At their hair, and the bundle, and the steam coming off Emeric's shoulders in the gate lamp's light like smoke off a horse.

"The culvert," said Ward.

"The culvert."

Ward looked at him a breath longer. Then he stood aside.



He slept for fourteen hours.

He did not remember getting into bed. He remembered the stair, and Ferra's hand on his back on it, and then he remembered nothing at all until two in the afternoon of the next day, when he woke in Ferra's bed with the shutter full of grey light and the smell of soup, so hungry that his stomach hurt, and lay there trying to work out where he was and why he felt so well.

He felt better than he had felt in a year. He felt as though he had slept a week.

Ferra was in the chair by the cold hearth, in her shawl, with a new book on her knees, unopened. Imani was on the end of the bed, still in her ward clothes, with the stethoscope round her neck. Both of them had dark rings under their eyes like bruises, and their hair was coming down, and they were looking at him with an expression he had not seen on either face before.

"He's awake," said Imani.

"He's awake," Ferra agreed, without any warmth at all.

"What's the matter?" he said.

"What's the matter," said Imani. "He says what's the matter." She leaned forward and put the silver cup on his chest without asking and listened, and took it away. "Sixty. Slow. Steady. Like a man on a feather bed with nothing on his mind." She sat back. "You went to sleep at half past two in the morning and you didn't move. You didn't turn over. You didn't snore. I've watched men die in my ward who made more noise than you did."

"Imani came up at four," said Ferra. "Because Ward sent for her. Because the watch told him you'd come in steaming." She opened her new book, and closed it again. "She listened to your chest. Then she went down to the ward for her lamp and her sheet and came back up. And we sat."

"You sat."

"Every half hour," said Imani, "one of us got up and put a hand on your chest to see if it was going up and down. Every half hour. All night. Because you were hot to the touch, at four in the morning, hotter than a fever, and then you weren't, and then you were so still." She folded her arms. "And at nine this morning I sent down to the ward that I'd not be in, for the first time in a year, because I'd been up all night watching a man sleep."

He looked from one of them to the other. Their faces were grey. Ferra's eyes were red at the rims. Imani had a crease on her cheek where she had leaned it on her hand.

He had never in his life felt so well.

He began to laugh. He tried not to, and it was no use; it came up out of him from the warm rested middle of him and would not stop, and he lay in Ferra's bed in the grey afternoon light and laughed until his stomach hurt worse than the hunger, while the two women who had sat up all night over him looked at him with tired, dark-ringed, absolute contempt.

"Soup," said Imani at last, when he had stopped. She got up and fetched the bowl from the hearth's side, where Maren had sent it up an hour before and it had been kept warm on a stone, and put it in his hands, and stood over him. "Two bowls. Then bread. Then two more bowls. You'll be hungry like this for two days, because you burned a week's worth of what you are in one night to keep her warm, and the body wants it back." She looked down at him. "And contempt. From both of us. As much as you can take. Doctor's orders."

"How much contempt?"

"All of it," said Ferra, from the chair, and opened her new book, and wrote the date at the top of the first page, and under it, in her square capitals, small, a line he could read upside down from the bed: THE CULVERT. And under that, smaller: hybrid.

She looked at it, and at him, over the book, with her red-rimmed eyes.

"It was for something," she said. "The other half. I wanted that written down." Then, to the page: "And you forgot about the culvert."

"I forgot about the culvert."

"You walked that bank in the autumn and you knew it was there, and you forgot, because you were looking at the lanterns."

"I was looking at you."

Ferra's pencil stopped.

"That's worse," she said, and wrote it down.
