Chapter 246 - The Tribunal

The roads north had opened in the second week of the new year, and the lords who had not come in the winter came now, and the hall was fuller than Keller had seen it since the duke's file.

He came in early, as he always did, and stood at the Crown's table and watched the grey young men put everybody in their places. He watched them the way he had watched them at the elevation, with the same admiration and a new attention. Somebody had given them a list. Somebody had sat down with the rolls of the peerage and a pen, and worked out where every man in the kingdom who had a voice should sit to give it, and had done it so well that no man in the hall would ever know he had been placed. Keller thought he knew who. He looked for her at the foot of the gallery stair, and she was there, in grey, with a single sheet in her hand, not looking at it.

He did not look at her for long. He looked at the benches instead, because the benches were the case, and he had known that since the day the king stood up.

They were not bought. That was the first thing he saw, and he saw it plainly, because it would have been easier if they had been. A bought bench sits wrong: a man who has been paid sits among men who have not, and looks at them, and they look at him. There was none of that. Every man in the hall was sitting exactly where his seat put him, and every seat was already a vote.

The lords spiritual on the east bench, all of them, the bishops and the abbots and the two old priors from the north who never came south for anything, with Augustine at their head in the red. Those were the Church's voices, and they would go the way the Church went, and nobody would call it anything but conscience.

The lords temporal under the windows. Keller knew them, most of them, from one end or the other of a suit, and he went along the benches in his head as a clerk goes along a column. Harlow's father, whose son had died charging at nineteen. Two earls whose eldest sons had come out of the chamber at sixteen marked to the elbow, and been knighted the next spring, and cost their fathers a year's rents each. A baron who had borrowed against his wife's land to put his second son through it, and whose second son had not come out at all. Man after man on those benches had paid the Church for a son's capacity, in coin and some of them in worse, and had been told it was the only road there was. And a barony in the south had printed on good paper, for anyone to read, that there was another road, and that it ran through a school on an island that took a fee by the quarter.

They were not bought. They did not need to be. Every one of them had already paid.

On the first bench, west side, among the old blood, Aldane. He had come south through the snow for this with thirty men, and he sat the bench the way he sat everything, as though expecting to be looked at, and he did not look at the dais once.

And on the second bench, in a good coat going soft at the seams, Grier.

Keller noticed him because he was pale. He had never seen Grier pale. At every sitting Keller could remember Grier had been red, with a cup, holding it out before the jug reached him. This morning one of the grey young men came along the second bench with the jug at the start of the hour, as they always did, and Grier put his hand over his cup, and shook his head, and the young man went on.

Keller filed it. He did not know yet where.

The king was in the chair.

That had not been certain. There was no form for a king in the Church's keeping to sit in his own council, because there had never been a king in the Church's keeping. Keller had spent two nights looking. In the end the king had simply come, at the ninth bell, by the door behind the dais, as he always came, and sat, as he always sat, and two priests of the close in black had come in behind him and taken their places at the foot of the dais steps, one on either side, with their hands folded, and stood there. The Church asks only to be at the door. They were at the door.

The king looked as he had always looked. The beard was white. The hands were on the arms of the chair. He did not look at the priests, and he did not look at the benches, and he did not look at the prince Corbin, who stood a pace behind the chair at his right hand, where he had always stood, with his face arranged into the grave attention of a son at his father's trial.

He looked, once, at the Crown's table. Keller met it. Then the king looked at the south door, and did not look at anything else for the rest of the morning.

The close had sent an advocate.

Keller had expected that. Augustine had spoken for himself in the winter because the winter had only been a finding, and a finding was the lords spiritual's office and nobody else's. This was a question put to the whole council, touching the king's person, and it wanted an advocate, and the close had one: a canon of the cathedral, a tall stooped man in black with a chancery voice and a memory for precedent that Keller had crossed twice before and respected both times. He stood on the floor in the dark between the bars of light, where parties stood. He had a single sheet in his hand. He did not look at it.

The question was read by the chancellor. Whether the king, having taken upon the Crown the practice at Kaldo that the lords spiritual have found to be heresy, has kept his oath to keep the faith.

The canon put it shortly. The lords spiritual had found the practice to be heresy. That finding was theirs and was not before the council. The king had taken the practice on the Crown, in this hall, in his own voice; it was in the roll. A king who takes heresy upon the Crown has not kept the faith. That was the whole of it, and the canon sat down, and the brevity of it was its argument: there was nothing in it to take hold of, because there was nothing in it at all but the roll.

Keller stood.

He had prepared for three weeks. He had not slept well for any of them. He had gone through every roll in the chancery that touched the king's person or the lords spiritual or the oath, back to the founding, and he had found what he had expected to find, which was that the law was entirely on his side and had never been tested because nobody had ever been fool enough to test it. He laid it out now, in order, one pin at a time, as he had laid out the duke's file, and he did not raise his voice.

"The first point," he said. "Jurisdiction. The lords spiritual have no jurisdiction over the person of the sovereign. None. They have never claimed it. The council has only such jurisdiction over the king's person as the oldest rule in the rolls gives it, which is to act by two parts in three where the king cannot rule, or will not keep his oath. It has done so twice in four hundred years. Both times the king's incapacity or his breach was a thing any man in the realm could see." He did not look at the dais. "No man in this hall has seen the king do anything."

The benches were quiet.

"The second point. The oath. And keep the faith. The Crown's table has searched the rolls from the founding for any finding, by any court, of what that clause requires of a king, or of a duke, since the duke's oath is the same words. There is none. There is no finding that any king has ever kept it, and none that any king has ever broken it. It has never been construed. A clause that has never been given any content cannot be found to have been breached, because there is nothing in it to breach. It is not justiciable. It is a prayer, and the council does not try prayers."

A murmur on the east bench. The canon did not move.

"The third point. There is no statute. There is no law of this realm that makes any act of a king, or any leave he gives, heresy. Heresy is a finding of the lords spiritual on doctrine, and it binds the faithful in conscience, and the realm has always been content to let it. It has never been a thing the Crown can be tried for, because the Crown is not a communicant. The Crown is an office."

He turned a page he did not need to turn, and squared it.

"The fourth point, which I ask the council to attend to, because it is the whole of the matter. The king took upon the Crown the practice at Kaldo. He said so, and it is in the roll. The Crown's table asks the council: what is the practice?" He let that sit. "The lords spiritual found it. The cardinal laid nine papers on this table in the winter, and the Crown's table read every one of them, and so may any lord in this hall. There are rolls, and they show men with parishes. There is a register, and it shows a boy with a stone. There is a prayer with a misprint. There is a licence for a chapel, granted by the close itself. There is a school list. And there is a parish book." He laid his hand flat on the table, where the paper had lain in the winter and did not lie now. "The parish book proves that fewer people died in a parish in the south than die in other parishes. It does not prove that anyone did anything to make them not die. It does not name an act. Nothing on this table names an act. The lords spiritual have found a practice to be heresy without the practice ever once being said aloud, in this hall or in any paper put before it, and the king has taken upon the Crown a thing that nobody has ever described. The council is asked to find that the king broke his oath by giving leave for something that no man here can name."

"The fifth point, and the last." He did not raise his voice for it. He never raised his voice. "Fewer burials is not, on its face, a harm. The Crown's table knows of no law, and no doctrine, and no council, that has ever held that it is a sin for children not to die."

He sat down.

It was the best argument he had ever made. He knew it as he made it, the way a mason knows a true course while the mortar is still wet. Every point was sound. Every point was the law as it stood and had always stood. On the record, point by point, the Crown's law had answered the question and the answer was no.

And he felt the hall not care.

He had felt it before, in the same hall, in the same chair, at the duke's matter. The benches shifted. The stewards leaned to murmur. Nobody on the floor looked at the Crown's table. He had won, cleanly and on every count, and it had not moved the room an inch, because the room had not been listening to the law. It had been listening, three weeks ago, to a level voice say Aubrey, second son of the lord of Esterby, and it was still listening to that. Every man on those benches had heard the fifth point and heard, under it, the thing Keller had not said and could not: that fewer burials in the south meant a rite in the north that cost a year's rents and was not the only road. That was the harm. It was not a harm any law had a name for. It was their sons.

The canon did not answer him. He did not need to. He rose, and bowed to the Crown's table, courteously, and said, "The lords spiritual do not ask the council to try a practice. They ask it to answer a question. The Crown's table has argued very ably that the question cannot be asked. It has been asked," and sat down again.

Augustine, at the head of the east bench, did not speak at all.

The steward called the voices.

Keller counted. He did not want to and could not help it, and he knew, as he began, that the lord of Kaldo had sat on a stool below this dais eighteen months ago and done exactly the same, with his face arranged into boredom, and kept two columns in his head. Keller kept two columns. He did not arrange his face. There was nobody looking at it.

Sixty-four voices sat. Two parts in three of sixty-four was forty-three. Keller had known the number for three weeks. He had known, too, near enough, what the natural lean of the hall would give, because he had gone along the benches every night in his head the way he had gone along them this morning, seat by seat: the east bench whole, and the fathers of knighted sons, and the old blood behind Aldane, and the men who owed Aldane, and the men who would not be seen to shield a heretic. Against them the king's own household lords, and the men who owed the Crown their land and knew it, and the old earls who did not like the Church in the council on principle, and the handful who had come down from their manors for no reason but that the law was the law. He had it at forty-two. He had had it at forty-two on the first night and on the last, and he had not let himself believe it on any of them.

The lords spiritual gave their voices first. Augustine's, one word, pleasantly, as a man agrees about the weather. His bench followed him down to the end, all of them, and the old abbot at the far end said it too, and looked at nobody.

The old blood. A duke, aye. An old earl, aye. Another, no, loudly. Aldane, aye, in the voice that expected to be listened to, and he did not look at the dais as he said it. The man beside him, aye. The man beside that, who had looked at Aldane and at Corbin at the elevation before he voted, did not look at anybody this time, and said no.

The columns went on filling. Keller kept them. The benches under the windows gave their voices by rank, and most of them gave them as their seats had already given them, and he ticked them off as they came, and they came as he had counted them, one after another, with a very few surprises that cancelled each other out.

By the end of the first bench he had the ayes at thirty-one. By the middle of the second, at thirty-nine.

Grier was on the second bench.

At the elevation Grier had stood when he was called, and swayed, and put his cup down, and looked left and then right along the bench as if to see which way the wind was going, and said aye for the lord of Kaldo, loudly, and somebody had laughed. Keller had seen it. Everybody had seen it. Saban of Kaldo had gone round every tavern in the chancery lanes that night asking why, and had heard Grier's name in four of them, and every time it was somebody he owed.

This morning Grier stood when he was called and did not sway. He had no cup to put down. He did not look left or right. He looked at the floor in front of his own feet, pale, and said, "Aye," before the steward had finished his name, so quickly that the clerk at the Crown's table looked up and asked him to say it again, and he said it again, more quietly, and sat down, and put his hands between his knees.

Nobody laughed.

Keller entered it. It was the fortieth. By the end of the benches it was forty-three.

He added the columns twice, as he did everything, and then the clerk added them and handed the sheet up to the steward, and the steward read it to the dais: forty-three to twenty-one. Two parts in three, and not one voice over.

One voice.

The chancellor read the finding. He read it in his soft voice without inflection, as he read everything, and it was very short, because there was nothing to put in it.

That the king, having taken upon the Crown the practice at Kaldo which the lords spiritual have found to be heresy, has not kept his oath to keep the faith. That the king is committed to the keeping of the lords spiritual, for examination, at such place as they shall appoint. That the council, as the custom of the realm gives it, names the king's heir lieutenant of the realm, to act in the king's name until the king is restored.

That was all. It did not say what the practice was. It did not say what the king had done. It did not answer one of Keller's five points, or pretend to, because it did not need to: the count had answered them. Keller had read a great many findings in his life, and some of them had been thin, and some of them had been wrong. He had never read one that so plainly contradicted the record it was written under and made so little effort to hide it. The record said nobody had named an act. The finding said the act had been done. It was a finding that knew exactly what it was, and said so, and did not care, and there was nobody in the hall who could make it care.

The clerk wrote it into the roll. Keller watched the pen go across.

At the foot of the gallery stair, the sheet in the princess Maeve's hand was folded. Keller had not seen her fold it. It had been flat at the start of the voices. It was folded now, twice, very small, and held in her closed hand against her grey skirt, and her face was the face she wore for a sitting, and told him nothing.

The king stood.

He did not speak. There was nothing left that he could say that would not be carried away under the doors and made into something. He stood with his hands at his sides and looked down the length of the hall to the south door, as he had looked at it all morning, for as long as it takes a man to read a short line on a page. Then he turned and went out by the door behind the dais, as he always went, and the two priests at the foot of the steps went up them and out after him, one on either side, and the door closed.

The chair stood empty.

The prince Corbin did not sit in it.

Keller watched him not sit in it. He had half expected him to, and had half expected, if he did, that the benches would see it and not like it. Corbin had seen that too. He stood where he had always stood, a pace behind the empty chair at its right hand, with one hand resting on the back of it, lightly, the way a man rests his hand on the back of his father's chair at table when his father has gone out of the room for a moment and will be back. He looked down at the hall with his grave face, and when the steward turned to him and bowed, and said, "My lord lieutenant," he inclined his head as though the words were a weight he had not asked for and would carry because somebody had to.

"I'll keep it for him," Corbin said. It was not loud. It carried anyway. "Until he's home."

It was very well done. The benches thought so. Keller heard them think so. He had watched the prince Corbin say generous things in that hall for twenty years and had never once heard him say one that cost him anything, and he did not hear one now; but he could not have put his finger on what was wrong with it, and nor could anyone else on the floor, and that, Keller thought, was the whole of the prince's art.

He went back to his room over the chancery with the roll under his arm and did not light the lamp.

Forty-three. He sat in the dark and went through it again, seat by seat, not because there was anything to be done with it, but because he could not stop. Forty-two had been the natural lean of the hall, and he had been right about forty-two; every voice had gone where he had put it, give or take a pair that cancelled. Every voice but one.

Grier had voted for the lord of Kaldo at the elevation, and against the king today, and had been pale, and had not drunk, and had said aye before his name was finished.

Keller knew Grier's debts. Not all of them; nobody knew all of Grier's debts, and Saban of Kaldo had said at the elevation that he owed everyone, which was nearly true. But Keller knew the ones that had come before the Crown's table. Two of the three lending houses Grier owed had brought actions against him in the last five years, and won judgment, and never been paid, because a lord's land could not be taken for a debt and Grier had nothing else. A third house held paper on him that had not come to court yet and would, by the spring, at the rate he was going. His steward had a suit pending for four years' wages. The vintners' company had given up.

A man who owes everyone votes with whoever he owes most that week. Saban had said that too. Keller had thought it was a clever thing to say, and true of Grier, and not true of anybody who mattered.

This week Grier had not been red. He had been pale, and sober, and quick, and he had not looked left or right. He had voted the way a man votes who does not owe anybody anything any more, and is frightened of the reason why.

Keller sat with that for a long time. He turned it over, and over, the way he turned a page he did not need to turn. He could not see the whole of it. He could see the shape of the hole where the whole of it would go, and he did not like the shape. A debt does not vanish. It is paid, or it is forgiven, or it is bought, and each of those leaves a line in somebody's book. There would be a line somewhere. There was always a line somewhere.

But it would not be in the close's book. He was sure of that before he had finished thinking it, and he did not know why he was sure. The Church had laid nine papers on the Crown's table, and not one of them had been in the Church's own hand. It had asked for the rolls by its clerk. It had let a canon argue the question and a cardinal give one word. It had found, and let the council act. Whatever had been done for Grier had been done, Keller would have bet his office on it, by somebody else, with somebody else's money, for somebody else's reasons, and the Church's name would not be on any line of it anywhere.

He did not like the sum. He added it again, in the dark, and it came out the same, and he still did not like it.

At some point he lit the lamp and took a sheet and squared it, and wrote at the head of it, in his own small dry hand that leaned backward, Grier, and nothing under it. He looked at it for a while. Then he folded it once and put it in the drawer with the other things he noticed and did not know what to do with, and shut the drawer, and sat with his hand on it.

Across the square, in the palace, a king was in his own rooms with two priests at the door, waiting for the lords spiritual to appoint a place. And in the room where the council's business was done, a man who had never paid for anything that cost him was keeping the realm for his father until he was home.
