Chapter 246 - By Night, North

He did not tell himself it was anything but what it was.

He sat in his rooms in the east range on the last night before, with the lamp out and the shutters open on the court and the cold coming in, and said it to himself plainly, the way Ward said a road. The king had told him in the day room, in the loud voice, in front of the door, that he would not come up this road again unless he was sent for. The king had not sent for him. The king could not send for him, because the king was in the Church's keeping, in his own rooms, with two priests at the door, and had gone there of his own motion so that nobody need be sent for. And tomorrow night the lord of Kaldo was going north anyway, by a road his father had never forbidden him because his father had never known it was there.

It was breaking the king's order. There was no other name for it. If it was ever known, it would not stay the breaking of an order. It would be a duke, found in the heresy of the Crown he held of, in the capital without a writ, in the dark, with armed men, in the same month the council had put the king in the Church's keeping. There was a word for that too. Keller would know the form of it. Emeric did not need the form. He knew the word.

He sat with it until the lamps in the court went out one by one and the dome was dark over the tree. Then he got up and shut the shutters, because there was nothing more to sit with, and went down to the lake.

The Discovery went up from the lake an hour after full dark, with Osric at the levers and her lamps out, and climbed through the cloud over the flat into the band.

It was bitter up there. It always was, in winter; the band was colder than the ground by as much as a man could stand, and in winter the ground was cold already, and the frost came in round the window frames of the long cabin and grew in feathers on the glass. They sat in their coats, the eight of them: Emeric; Ward and Rylan in their suits, with the capes furled and the helms on their knees; Saban, wrapped to the eyes in a borrowed fur, with Vantic's letter inside his coat under all of it; and four of Rylan's men who had been at the Staple, in plain coats with nothing under them but knives. Nobody talked much. Osric flew her north in the band at her full pace, which was a pace no horse had ever gone, and the frost thickened on the glass, and somewhere under them in the dark the Wayford road went by, and the junction, and the long straight run up through the market gardens that had taken Emeric twelve days in a hired carriage the last time he went to be judged, and took the Discovery a little over two hours.

She did not go near the city.

He had settled that at the table, before anything else, and nobody had argued. Not over it, not near it, not in sight of any wall. The capital had walls and towers and men on them, and men on towers look up. A shape between a watchman and the stars over the king's own city, the month the king went into the Church's keeping, would be a third article to lay beside the scout's and Dray's, and this one would not be laughed at in any hall. So she held in the band well to the south and west of the capital, over the empty water-meadows along the river where nobody lived in winter, and came down there, slowly, out of the cloud, in the dark, onto her legs on frozen ground, three miles from the nearest gate.

Three miles was his own rule, and he did not examine it. He had never delved inside the capital's walls since he was a boy; not the delve, not even the long slow reach of the sense. He had told himself at the elevation that it was a habit and not a rule, and that the house was a house where nothing was private, and he had kept his door shut there the way he kept his voice down in the mews. He had never quite asked himself where the habit came from. He did not ask now. He only knew that whatever he was going to do tonight he would do out here, in a mill by the river three miles from any wall, and not one step nearer, and that when he thought of doing it in the city something in him turned away from the thought the way a hand turns away from a stove before the mind has said hot.

The mill was Maeve's.

Not hers in any way that a roll would show. It belonged to the widow of a miller who had been Mistress Oake's uncle, and it had not turned since the miller died, and its wheel was frozen into its race and its millstones were under sacking, and it stood by itself on a bend of the river with willows round it and no other house in sight. Wald had come down to it on the first night to see that it was empty, and it was, and he had left a fire laid in the miller's room upstairs and a lantern with its shutter down and a jug of water, and gone back to the city before dawn without having been seen by anybody. That was the whole of what Maeve had sent: a mill, and a fire laid, and a man in grey who could come and go.

And Grier.

They brought him in a little after midnight, two of Saban's men and Wald, in a closed cart with a tired horse. Emeric never learned the whole of how it had been done, and did not ask. He knew the shape of it from Saban, who had set it going from the ship's cabin with a pencil and a list and three days' letters sent ahead by the ordinary post to men who drank in the chancery lanes. Lord Grier was no longer drinking in the chancery lanes. He had stopped the week of the vote, the lanes said; he sat in his lodging in the upper town and did not go out, and did not see anyone. But a man who has not been out for three weeks will go out for a cousin's funeral, if there is a cousin and a funeral, and there was a funeral that night in a church by the river gate for a vintner Grier had owed money to, and the vintners' company had sent Lord Grier a black-edged card, as they sent one to everyone who had owed the dead man anything. It was the first card anyone had sent him since the vote. He went. He came out of the church into the dark, alone, a little unsteady, the first cup he had drunk in three weeks inside him, and a young man in grey with no badge fell into step beside him and said that the lady Maeve's compliments, and would his lordship step into the cart, and his lordship, who had been in the lady Maeve's hall at every sitting for years and never once been spoken to by any of her people except to be shown his seat and have his cup filled, stood in the road in the snow and looked at the young man for a long moment, and got in.

Nobody had touched him. Nobody had needed to. Emeric thought about that afterwards, a good deal.

He was sitting on the miller's bench by the fire when they brought him up the stair.

Emeric had seen him twice. Once from a stool on the floor of the hall, a red face above a soft coat looking left and right along the bench before he said aye, and somebody laughing. Once, in Keller's letter and Maeve's, as one voice. The man who came up the mill stair now was not red. He was grey under the lantern, and heavier than he had looked on the bench, and older, and he had snow on his shoulders and on the black band round his hat, and his hands were shaking, and he saw Emeric by the fire and stopped on the top step as if he had walked into a wall.

"Your grace," he said.

It came out of him in a whisper. Emeric did not know whether it was meant for the duke of the council he had sat on, or for the son of the king he had voted into the Church's keeping, and he thought Grier did not know either.

"Sit down, my lord," Emeric said. "You're cold."

Grier sat. He sat on the other bench, across the hearth, because Ward put him there with a hand under his elbow that was not quite a grip, and he held his hands out to the fire without seeming to know he was doing it. Ward went back to the wall by the window. Saban sat on a sack in the corner with his fur round him. Rylan stood at the door, at the top of the stair, in his black plate, with the lions at his shoulders catching the fire, and did not move, and did not look at anybody. He had stood at a door like it once before, in a cellar under the Staple, with a man face down in the ash.

Emeric did not say anything for a while. He let the fire do some of it.

"You had a quittance," he said at last. "In the first week of the new year. From the House of Ducatis, in full, by the House's own rider. For your paper at three houses, which the House had bought at its face the week before. And your saddler was paid, and your steward, and the vintners."

Grier's hands stopped moving in front of the fire.

"And the week after that you stood up in the council and said aye before the steward had finished your name."

Grier did not say anything. He looked at the fire.

"I'm not going to hurt you, my lord," Emeric said. "Nobody here is. When we've done you'll go back to your lodging in the same cart, and nobody will know you were out of it but the vintners' company, who'll think you went home drunk from a funeral. I don't want anything from you that you'd ever have to say aloud. I don't want you in any court. I want to know who."

"I can't," Grier said.

"Can't, or won't."

"Can't." It was barely a word. "I don't know who. I never knew who." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and red in the grey face. "A note came. That's all. A note, and then the quittance, and then nobody ever said anything to me again, and I went to the council, and I." He stopped. He put his hands over his face. "I don't know who."

Emeric believed him. That was the worst of it, and he had half expected it on the ship, going up through the cloud. A man who owes everyone does not ask who is paying when the paying starts. He holds out his cup before the jug reaches him. Grier had been handed a note, and then a quittance, and had not asked anything of either, and had voted, and had not drunk since, and did not know.

But he had seen the note.

He took the stone out of his coat.

It was his mother's, the one he had carried in his coat since he was a boy, the one he delved on; not the chipped one, which was a finger deep in the middle bed under the dome, and not the bead, which was on its cord at his wrist and never came off. He held it in his left hand, closed, and leaned forward across the hearth, and Grier took his hands down from his face and looked at the closed hand and did not understand it.

"My lord," Emeric said. "The note. You read it. You'll have read it more than once. You'll have burned it after, I expect, or you'll have meant to, and you'll have read it again before you did."

Grier's face moved.

"I can see it," Emeric said, "if you'll let me. Not the words you'd tell me. The note, the way you saw it. I'd need to put my hand on the back of your neck, and you'd need to let me in, and you'd need to not fight me while I'm there. It won't hurt, if you don't fight. You'll sit on that bench and it'll feel like a moment, and it'll be a moment. And then I'll know who, and you'll never have told me."

He did not say the rest. He did not say that he could do it the other way. He had done it the other way once, to Marrow, on the floor of the Staple's strongroom, through the man's fear, with a hand on his bare neck and the fear turned up on purpose, and had found afterwards, when he looked for it, that he minded less than he ought to have. He did not want to find that again. Marrow had gone out like a lamp rather than give up the one page, and woken on the second evening asking for water, and given nothing since but one true sentence at a time. That was what happened to a man who was pulled. Emeric had no wish to do it to Grier, and no need to, and he thought, looking at the grey wet face across the hearth, that Grier would not have survived it the way Marrow had.

He waited.

At the door Rylan did not move. Emeric could feel him not moving, the way he had felt him not move in the strongroom, with his gauntlet an inch off the back of Marrow's neck. Rylan had been in that room. He knew what the other way looked like. He stood at the top of the stair tonight and looked at nothing, and Emeric knew, without turning, that if he did it the other way tonight Rylan would stand there and let him, and would never once afterwards say anything about it, and would go down to the east tower once a week for the rest of his life to look at another man through a grille.

Grier was looking at the stone in his hand.

"They'll say I sold him," he said. He said it very quietly. "The king. They say it already, in the lanes. There's a song." He was not looking at Emeric. "I didn't. I didn't sell him. I was never asked to. Nobody asked me anything. They just took it all away, all of it, everything I owed, years of it, and I woke up and it was gone, and I went to the council, and I thought, if I say no, it'll come back. That's all I thought. That it would all come back." His voice cracked. "He knighted my father. The king. At Carrow, in the old war. My father used to say so every Candlemas, at table, till he died. And I sat on that bench and I thought about my saddler."

He put his hands between his knees and bent over them, and his shoulders shook, and nobody in the room said anything at all.

Emeric had seen a great many men afraid, and a few ashamed, and he had learned to tell the two apart long before he had any sense to tell them with. A frightened man holds on. A man who is ashamed wants, more than anything in the world, to put the thing down.

After a while Grier lifted his head.

"Do it," he said.

Emeric put his right hand on the back of Grier's neck, under the collar, on the bare skin, and felt the man flinch and then, slowly, not flinch; and closed his left hand on his mother's stone, and went down.

He did not pull. There was nothing to pull against. It was like the first time with Bram, years ago, at the table in the old keep with Bram's big hand on his shoulder, when Bram had come in on wanting alone: the door was simply open, and a man stood in it who had decided to be there, and Emeric reached out and brought him through.

The office. It was always the office, soft at the edges and sharp where it mattered, the desk and the window and the grey carpet from another life. Grier stood in the middle of it in his soft coat with the snow gone off his shoulders, and looked round him at the walls, slowly, with his mouth a little open, and did not ask where he was. He looked at the window for a long time. Emeric let him.

His shelf was a poor one. Emeric had seen Marrow's, bound ledgers one to a season, square and orderly as a counting house; and Saban's, which was a counting house; and Ferra's, which he was not allowed to see and had only ever felt from the door. Grier's was a sideboard in a dining room, heaped. Cups, and cards, and bills, a great many bills, some of them opened and most not, in drifts, in the hands of every tradesman in the upper market; and a letter from his father with a black edge; and a sword with a plain hilt that had been given at Carrow; and on top of all of it, where a man puts the thing he has just put down and not yet put away, a single sheet of paper, folded twice, with the crease worn nearly through.

Emeric did not touch it. He did not need to. He looked at it the way Grier had looked at it, as many times as Grier had looked at it, and it opened under his eyes.

It was short. It was not signed. It was in a clerk's hand, a good one, a secretary's, on plain paper without a mark. Your lordship's paper at Hendry's, Cowle's and Pellam Brothers is bought. A quittance in full will come to you from the House within the week. At the council's next sitting touching the king, his lordship will give his voice with the lords spiritual. This is the whole of what is asked. It will not be asked again, and it will not be spoken of.

That was all. No name. No seal.

But the note was not only the note. Grier had not only read it. He had had it put into his hand, at his own door, on a raw evening in the week after the council's first sitting, by a man who had waited on his step in the dark and would not come in; and that was on the shelf too, because a man remembers the hand that gives him the thing that changes his life, even if he does not know he is remembering it. Emeric turned to it. It came up the way the ledger had come up in the Staple's cellar, but without any pressure in his head at all, easily, because nobody was holding it shut.

A step, a lamp over a door, snow. A man on the step in a dark good coat, young, clean-shaven, with a face that wanted to be forgotten and a household's quiet in the way he stood; a man used to waiting on other men's steps. He held out the note. Grier took it. And as the man turned to go, Grier, who had drink in him and was frightened, had said, whose are you?, and the man had stopped on the bottom step and looked back up at him, and not answered, and gone. But Grier had seen his coat as he turned under the lamp. Under the cloak, on the breast of the dark coat, where a household man wears his badge when he is not trying to hide it and forgets once to turn his cloak over it: a small device in silver thread. Grier had seen it for a heartbeat and had known it at once, because he had sat on the benches for years and seen it every sitting on the men who stood behind the dais, and he had put the knowing on his shelf and never taken it down, because he did not want to.

The prince Corbin's household badge.

And the man's face, which Grier did not know, and which Emeric looked at until he would know it anywhere; and, a week later, at the upper-market fountain, the same face again in the crowd, and a vintner's boy beside Grier saying, that's Master Rudd, my lord, the prince's under-purser, and Grier turning away as though he had been struck.

Emeric took it all. The note, word for word. The date, which was on the quittance lying under it on the heap: the fourth day of the new year. The step, the lamp, the badge, the face. The name at the fountain.

Then he brought Grier out, gently, the way you bring a man back across a room with a hand at his elbow, and let go.

In the miller's room it had been a moment. The fire had not moved. Grier sat on the bench with Emeric's hand on the back of his neck and his eyes open, and blinked, and put his own hand up to his face, and took it away, and looked at it.

"Is that it?" he said.

"That's it."

"I thought." Grier stopped. He sat very still, as if he were listening to something inside himself, the way a man does who has been told he will feel a thing and is waiting to feel it. There was nothing to feel. There was no pressure behind his eyes, no cold in his arms, no blood at his nose. He had let a man into the one room he owned, and the man had looked at what was on the shelf and gone out again, and closed the door, and left everything where it had been.

"It doesn't hurt," Grier said, wondering.

"No." Emeric took his hand away. "It doesn't, if you let it."

At the door, Rylan let his breath out through his nose. It was the only sound he made all night.

They sent Grier back to the city in the cart before the first grey, with Wald on the box and a flask of the miller's brandy that Saban found in a cupboard. At the foot of the stair Grier stopped, and turned round, and looked back up at Emeric on the top step with the lantern behind him.

"Will they know?" he said.

"Not from us."

Grier looked at him a moment longer. Then he nodded, and went out to the cart, and they heard it go away along the river toward the gate, slowly, the tired horse picking its way on the frozen ruts.

Emeric came back to the fire. Saban was sitting up on his sack in the corner with the fur off, awake, waiting.

"The fourth day of the new year," Emeric said. "The note came to his door two nights before. Hendry's, Cowle's and Pellam Brothers. The man who carried it wears Corbin's badge under his cloak, and his name is Rudd, and he's the under-purser of the prince's household."

Saban did not move for a moment. He sat on his sack with his eyes on the fire, and Emeric watched him add it, the way he added a cargo in his head before the ship was unloaded.

"Rudd," Saban said. "Corbin's household draws on the high desk. It always has. The heir's household is Crown paper." He opened his eyes. "An under-purser draws for the household. In his own name. On the household's account."

"Can you see it?"

"Wednesday," said Saban. "If it's there, I'll see it Wednesday."

Outside, the sky was going grey over the water-meadows. Ward came away from the window where he had stood all night and went down to the Discovery on her legs in the frozen field, to tell Osric to take her up before it was light. Rylan stayed at the door until the last of them had gone down the stair, and then he followed, and did not look back at the bench where Grier had sat.

Emeric banked the fire, because Wald had laid it and somebody ought to, and put the lantern out, and stood for a moment in the dark miller's room three miles from the city where he had been born, where his father was sitting in his own rooms with two priests at the door.

He did not go nearer. He did not know why he did not, and he did not ask.
