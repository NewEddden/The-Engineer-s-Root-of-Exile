Chapter 327 - Low and Slow

The first dead-zone stone came over the roofs at a quarter past nine on the third of December, and landed in a cabbage field.

Emeric felt it go up. He was on the K Amara's tower at Band height with his sense out over the market gardens behind the south suburbs, where hull four was putting down the day's first shore team a mile outside the wall and a long way outside the reach of any tower on it; and out of the lower town, from somewhere behind the wall among the tanneries, something rose that was not a note, only a cold small thing held very tight, going up in a long slow curve over the roofs and turning over as it went.

"Hull four," he said into the line. "Something thrown. From inside the wall, south-west. Coming down on the gardens."

Hull four's master did not ask what. He put her up on her matrix with the ramp still down and the last six men hanging off its edge by their hands, and the cold thing came down two hundred yards short of where she had been, among the cabbages, and burst. A hole went into Emeric's sense the size of a village green: a glasshouse, a gardener's hut, and eleven of Kaldo's regulars walking up a cart track with their rifles slung, who stopped all together because the suits on the two knights with them had gone heavy as coffins and both knights had said the same word at once.

"Trebuchet," said Ward, at the rail beside him, with Ivo's long glass. "Over the tanners' yards. There's the arm." He handed Emeric the glass. "They've had it in a yard since the summer with sacking over it. Saban's walkers thought it was a crane."

A beam in a yard, very small, going back up on its rope with men round it winding; at its foot a box with a gravity stone in it, told to be heavy. He had seen one at fifteen on a proving field with his father beside him.

"It can't see what it's throwing at."

"It doesn't need to. It needs to know where we can come down." Ward took the glass back. "Anywhere a ship can set a ramp outside the towers. Three fields and the tenter grounds and the gardens. They'll have every one paced." He looked again. "They're winding."

The eleven walked out of the dead ground in eight minutes on their own legs, with the two knights clanking behind them in plate that did nothing now but weigh, and lay in a ditch until hull four came back low from the east and took them up her ramp in a scramble. Nobody was hurt. The second stone burst on the empty track eleven minutes after the first.

The capital had been waiting for them all summer, under sacking.



The leaflet had emptied a cathedral. It had not emptied a city.

Corbin's men were still in it: in the barracks behind the drill yard, the guardhouses at the gates, the market squares with their crossbows and copied fire-tubes, the old gate towers, and the lanes, among a hundred and twenty thousand people who had come back into their houses because there was nowhere else to go. A man who could drop a rod on the barracks with the bakers round it would have been done by dinner on the third. Emeric was not that man, and so it would be done in the lanes, on foot, by men who could be killed.

Ward drew it on the K Amara's chart table with three fingers: the towers first, one at a time, because a tower with a chest and two Venn's was a ring nothing of Kaldo's could fly into; then the gates; then the squares. He worked it every night after, with his two cracked ribs creaking under Bettany's strapping whenever he leaned over the board.

"Breathe," said Bettany, going past with a tray.

"I am."

"Like a man stealing apples. Breathe properly or I'll strap you to the table."

Ward breathed properly, once, for her, went grey round the mouth, and went back to the board.

The shore teams went down in thirties and forties onto whatever ground the trebuchets had not paced, and walked in with knights among them, who flew low in the open and walked in the lanes. Every one had his grip boots on, the gravity stones in the soles and palms, and a coil of rope and a hook at his belt, because Ward's rule was that a man went up a wall no higher than his rope would cover. And every sergeant had a set.

Ambrose and Tess had built the sets small in November, a box the size of a cigar case behind the ear and a cup of a mouthpiece inside the chin; and on the crown of every helm that took one, two short brass spikes stood up like a hare's ears, to put the voice out of the iron and up four miles. Abloh had drawn those spikes on the MK3's first sheet, for no reason he could give anybody, because they looked right.

"Antennas," Tess had said, fitting the first to Mira's helm on the bench. "Aerials, if you like. They're what it talks with." She had looked up at him. "You can stop looking at them. They're staying."

"I know they're staying," said Abloh. "I drew them."



On the fourth Emeric went down.

Ward said no once, and then said what he would have instead. From four miles up a family under a table and six crossbowmen at a window were the same note; from a roof two streets off they were not. So Emeric went down in his suit off hull four's ramp onto the tenter ground east of the south suburbs, between the long empty frames where cloth was stretched in summer, with four knights round him and Kember's team in front.

Kember was a sergeant of the ward companies, a lean bald man from the brickfields who whistled through his teeth when he was thinking and did not know he did it. His road on Tess's tracing was short: up the potters' lane to the corner of the horse market, along the market's south side to the gate in the second wall, where there was a guardhouse with twenty of the Lieutenant's men in it and a tower over the gate with a chest.

Emeric went along the roofs on his boots, the knights slower behind him with their ropes hooked off to chimney stacks, and read the lane under him. A woman and three children under a stair. An old man in bed. A cat on a sill that Kember's front man nearly shot. A cellar full of apples and one boy.

"Corner's clear," he said into his helm. "The market's not. Twenty-two. North side, upstairs, and the saddler's roof. Crossbows. Four on the gatehouse leads." He read them again. "Two with burst stones in their belts."

Kember whistled through his teeth. The horse market was a long square of beaten earth with rails along it and nothing in it today but the rails. The tracing went along the south side under the eaves, where the north windows could not see down; but to get under the eaves the team had to come out of the lane's mouth and round the corner, ten yards of open ground, forty men in two files, with the north side's windows looking straight down the length of it.

"We'll do the turn quick," said Kember, on his knee in the lane's mouth. "It's only the turn."

"I'll give you a wall," said Emeric. "Across the corner. Wait for it."

He went down the last house front on his boots with his rope paid out above him, stood in the lane's mouth behind the corner, and put his hands flat on the street.

Setts: old granite cobbles on a bed of sand and gravel, on clay. Earth was not his. It came at three times the cost of fire, every time, slow, the way a man's left hand writes; and granite barely moved for anybody. But the sand under it moved, and the gravel, and the clay would if he leaned on it. The glyph came out of his hand broad and brown on the cobbles, the ring and the core and the long heavy strokes Paskin had made him draw a thousand times on a slate, and the men behind him looked at it and then at him; and he leaned.

The street came up the way bread rises, a long low hump across the corner, the setts tipping and grinding on it like a cart going over a weir. Waist high, the grey clay heaving up between the cobbles; he felt it in his arms, then his back, then behind his eyes. Chest high. Ten yards of it, leaning toward the market like a sea wall built by a drunk.

The first bolts came across the market and went into it like hail on a cart roof. Two went over it into the house front behind and stood there quivering, a hand's breadth into the timber.

"Go," said Emeric.

Kember's people went along the back of the hump bent double, and the bolts threw clay in their faces. A burst stone came over from the saddler's roof, turning, and dumped its heat all at once against the front of the wall, so that the clay cooked and steamed and a smell came off it like a kiln door opening; the men behind it felt the heat through two feet of wet earth and kept going. The second fell short in the open and set fire to a rail. Then the self-loaders went from under the eaves on the south side, twenty to a pull, up into the north windows, and the north windows stopped.

Emeric took his hands off the street. The hump stayed, as earth does, a grey scar across the corner with bolts standing out of it like pins out of a cushion. He sat down on the bottom step of the last house, because his knees had decided it, and found that one of his knights was standing over him with a shield up and had been for some time.

"That's three times fire, isn't it," said the knight. "Earth. For you."

"About that."

The knight thought about it. He was very young and had a smear of clay across his visor. "I'd have run it," he said.

"So would they," said Emeric. "That's why."

The guardhouse came out at a quarter to one, when the self-loaders had been at its windows for an hour and Kember had sent a man across under a white rag with the note Ferra had written for every team in October, saying what Kaldo was and what it was not and that every man who came out would be fed. Eighteen came out. Two had been killed at the windows. The tower over the gate they did not take that day: its crew had the stair, and its breaker was awake.

At noon one of Kember's knights went up off the market to look at the tower's leads, and a wind crossbow on the gatehouse put a bolt through his breastplate at sixty paces. Emeric felt it go in under the right collarbone and through him and stop against the backplate from the inside, and the knight came down on the market's earth from twenty feet with his stones still lit. He lived. Imani had the bolt out by four, and told him that a hand's breadth to the left he would have been talking to God instead of to her, and that he was not to take it as a sign God was on his side.

That night Ward chalked a new rule on the chart table under the old one. Never above the eaves. Never more than a count of three.



The Isle came up on the north set at nine that evening, in Rylan's voice.

Since the boats Rylan had said the same four lines every night, until Ferra told him from Kaldo that she could have written them for him and saved the battery.

Tonight there were more than four.

"The high mage is off his tower." A line and then a gap. "Came down this morning. He doesn't walk. He hangs. Sixty feet up, inside a skin, and he's over the wells. Since noon." The gap. "Rounds into the skin slow and drop. Every one costs him. He doesn't care. He threw lightning at the drum at two. The rock held. We're in the gully and the levy's in the pipe trench, and he can't get at the trench without coming down to forty, and he won't come to forty." A breath. "We can't come out of the gully without him seeing. He can't come into it without us seeing. So we're sitting."

"Hurt?" said Emeric.

"Four. Burned. Nobody bad." Then, in the same voice: "Kesrath's levy came back up the beach behind him at four. Not to us. To the village. They're sitting in it."

"You've got the wells."

"We've got the wells," said Rylan. "He's got the sky over them. I'll call at nine."

The set clicked. Ward stood in the cupboard's door with his hand on the frame. He had said at the light sand table on the twenty-fifth of November that it was not enough, and been told no; and he looked at Emeric now for exactly as long as it took Emeric to see that he was not going to say it again, and went back to the chart table.



The open apron at Band height had no iron in its walls and no scrubber in its air, nothing between a man and the Band but his skin; and a man who had not been going up on the Hod's racks since he was twenty bled from the nose out there inside the hour, then from the ears, then lay down. Osric bled in forty minutes. Arthur did not bleed at all. Mira and Bren bled a little after an hour and a half, and wiped it, and did not mention it.

So at one in the morning on the sixth the three went out onto the after apron into the dark and the wind in their MK2s and sat down on the steel with their backs to the coaming and their self-loaders across their knees, and Osric sat inside the hatch on the warm side of the iron with a mug of Maren's tea going cold in his hands, and watched them through the glass, and talked.

Tess said he talked the way other men chewed. He told the stone-keeper's mate, who had not asked, about the tower by the old wool hall on the second wall, that had swung its frame onto the K Discovery on the second: its two Venn's on the leads, its chest on the floor below, and its breaker asleep beside the chest on a straw tick, who could break it in his two hands if anybody woke him, and then every knight over that tower would come down like a sack of flour off a cart.

"Unless he's asleep," said the mate.

"He's asleep. He's been asleep every night for a week. Snores, the duke says. You can feel a man snore if you're the duke."

He told the mate why it was the MK2s. The MK3 flew on stones, and if the breaker woke on the way down it would be a very fine black coffin with a chute on it. The MK2 had Sorrel's cape-wing, batten and canvas, that flew on nothing but air; if the tower went dead under them they would still be gliding, and Abloh's chutes were the floor under that.

"And you, sir?"

"Me too." Osric looked at his own MK2 on its frame by the hatch. "I've not flown a cape-wing since the year Tess went to sleep. I told her. She said it's like a bicycle." He drank some cold tea. "I've never ridden a bicycle. Neither has she. She was very pleased with herself."

At a quarter to three the wind came round from the north-west, as Linus's sheet had said it would, and the K Amara, which had been creeping on her matrix for an hour with no light on her anywhere, hung where the sheet said a cape-wing let go off her after apron would come down on the wool hall tower with the wind behind it. Ward's voice said so in the hatch's horn. Then: "Osric."

The cold came in when the hatch opened, and the thinness, and the Band behind the eyes like being held under water by somebody kind. The three stood up off the coaming: Arthur very tall in the dark with his blade at his hip; Mira with her plait tucked down the back of her collar, which she did when it mattered; Bren, square, with a dark line on his upper lip.

"You're bleeding," said Osric.

"So are you," said Bren. "Sir."

Osric put his gauntlet to his face and it came away dark. He had been out for under a minute.

"Well," he said. "Let's not stand about, then."

They went off the apron one after another on Ward's count, Osric last, with their wings furled and their stones lit no brighter than a coal's last red.



Four miles took eleven minutes.

The first three they came down on their stones in a line, like leaves, the cold easing and the bleeding stopping as they came out of the Band, and the city coming up under them with almost no lamps lit, the river paler round three sides of it. At the third mile, on Arthur's hand, they put their stones out. The violet went off their suits like four candles pinched, and the cape-wings came open on their battens with a crack each that Osric felt in his teeth.

It was not like a bicycle. He would tell Tess so.

He hung under the wing by his harness with the dark city turning slowly under him and nothing holding him up but a sheet of canvas. He could hear the wind in the battens, and Bren breathing in the set, and Mira counting under her breath. Arthur was ahead and below, going down in long slow turns toward a round squat shape with two thin dark fingers standing up on its top. No crew on the frames; it was December, and nobody in the capital had ever seen anything come down out of the sky without a sound.

Arthur landed on the leads as if stepping off a stair. Mira after him in two strides, her wing coming down behind her in a long sigh of canvas. Bren harder, a thump Osric heard from thirty feet up that made him shut his eyes and wait for the tower to wake.

It did not. Osric came in too fast and too high, remembered the island and the flare just in time, caught the parapet with his knee, and went over it onto the leads among the others in a heap of canvas and steel; and lay there on his back with the Venn's over him against the stars, and nobody moved, and in his ear Bren said, very quietly, "Like a bicycle."

"Shut up," said Osric.

The hatch to the room below was by the far frame, oak with an iron ring. Mira went down the ladder first, because Mira was quietest; then Arthur; then Osric with his blade out and unlit, because a lit blade was a light; Bren on the hatch with his rifle down the hole.

The room was warm and smelled of men asleep in their clothes. Eight on pallets round the walls. In the middle of the floor the chest, iron-banded, the size of a child's cradle, the cold of the stones in it so still and held that even Osric, who was no delver, felt it in his skin like standing by a well. Beside it a straw tick, and on the tick a man in grey on his back with his mouth open.

Snoring.

Mira knelt beside him and put her gauntlet over his mouth, gently, as a woman puts her hand over a child's, and her other hand on both his wrists together. His eyes came open over her glove and looked up into a black helm, and he did not try to break anything. Osric did not think he could have. Whatever it was in the three of them, that Imani had looked at and had no word for, the breaker lay under Mira's one hand like a man under a fallen beam and wept quietly into her glove.

They woke the crew one at a time with the muzzle of a rifle and sat them along the wall with their hands on their heads. Nobody shouted. An old man with a grey moustache looked at Arthur's bare breastplate in the crack of the shuttered lamp, no crest on it, no device, and then up at the open hatch and the stars in it.

"From up there," he said.

"From up there," said Arthur.

"I told the sergeant. I said one night they'll come down out of it and we'll be in our beds." He looked at the chest. "He said that's what the box is for."

"Box is ours now," said Osric. "Sorry."

He went up onto the leads with the set and said it into four miles of dark. "Wool hall tower. Taken. Eight and the breaker. The chest's asleep. Nobody hurt." And because he could not help it: "Bren landed like a sack of coal and they slept through it. Tell the duke."

"He heard," said Ward's voice. And after a little: "Good."

The tower fell at dawn without a shot. Hull four's anchor teams carried the chest down off the leads between six of them like a coffin; and Osric sat on the parapet with a handkerchief to his nose and worked out how he would tell it at supper. He would leave the parapet out. Bren would put it back in.



The next one cost men.

It was the tower at the east gate on the outer wall, over the barracks, with the drill yard behind it where the proof rod had gone into the earth five days before; and they did not drop on it, because its breaker did not sleep. Emeric read it every hour through the night of the sixth: a man in grey on a stool beside the chest with his hands on its lid, wide awake, and every few hours another coming up the stair to take the stool. One on, one off. Somebody in that tower had heard about the wool hall's morning.

So it was the ground. At first light on the seventh, sixty of the shore teams up the drill yard's long edge under the barracks' blank back wall, where the windows could not see down, with Osric's twelve knights in front of them on foot and the three among the twelve; breach stones on the tower's door at the yard's north-east corner; and up the stair inside the wall's thickness to the chest before the man on the stool knew they were there.

"He'll break it when he hears the door," said Osric, at the chart table.

"Then you're in a dead zone on a stair with rifles," said Ward, without looking up. "Your knights keep their strength. Theirs keep theirs. You've done that."

"I have," said Osric. "I'd just sooner do it with the lights on."

They went up the yard at a quarter to seven in silence. Halfway along there was a hole in the beaten earth the size of a cart, with a little water in its bottom, and they went round it.

At three hundred yards Emeric's voice said in Osric's ear, from four miles up, "They've seen you. Two on the gatehouse leads. One's going down the stair. Quick."

The door was oak, iron-strapped, in a deep arch at the tower's foot. Arthur had the breach stone against the lock before they reached it, and they went flat either side of the arch, and he woke it with his thumb and threw himself back; the stone dumped its charge in one shove, and the door went in off its hinges into the dark like a kicked plank.

Something came out of the dark, low, spinning, and spread as it came: a net, weighted at its corners and strung all over with flat dark stones the size of a thumbnail, sewn in a grid like the scales on a fish. Osric had his hand up with the fire coming down his arm into it, the thing he had learned late and hard and still could not do without thinking first; and the net came over his hand and his helm and his shoulders, and the fire went.

Not out. Away. It went into the net the way water goes into a dry sponge, out of his hand and his arm and the place in his chest where it lived, and when he reached for it there was nothing, as if he had put his hand in his pocket for his knife and found the pocket sewn shut. He could feel the little stones on the net warming against his plate. A hundred small warm things, drinking.

A drain net. Armand had told it at the long table as a story about a mage of the tower forty years ago: the temple guard took him under a net, and he kept his strength and lost his casting, and Armand never saw him again.

His strength he still had. Two men came out of the arch behind the net in the Church's grey with short blades, temple guard, the kind that took mages alive for the drain cellars; and the first laid hold of the net's edge to haul him in like a fish, and Osric put his fist through the cords into the man's chest and the man went back into the arch and sat down. But the net went with the fist and wrapped his arm, the weights swinging round, and the second man came in low for the gap under the arm where the plate opened.

Mira's spear took the blade out of his hand in passing. Bren took the man.

And Arthur had his short knife out, the plain steel one, and was cutting. Not the charged blade; a charged edge was a cast, and the net would have drunk it. Plain steel and his own hands, which did not care about the net at all, cord after cord, quickly, the way a man cuts a sheep out of a fence, while Mira held the arch with her spear and Bren sat on the first man, who was trying to get up.

"Hold still," said Arthur. "Sir."

"I am holding still."

"You're not." The net came off Osric's helm in a sag of warm stones, and off his shoulder, and Arthur pulled the last of it off his arm and threw it behind him onto the yard, where it lay on the beaten earth with its little stones glowing faintly, full of Osric.

Osric reached for his fire. It came up his arm small and slow, like a man getting out of bed on a cold morning; but it came.

Then the ground dropped half an inch under his boots and came back, and his stones went still, and the fire he had just found was gone like a lamp out of oil, and on the yard the net's stones went dark. Somebody at the top of the stair had his hands on a lid.

"Dead," said Osric into the helm, which still worked. "Powder. In we go."

The stair went up inside the wall, steep and narrow and turning to the right, so a man coming up had his sword arm to the wall and a man coming down had his free; and it was dark, and there were men on it, and that was all the stair was for an hour. Afterward Osric could not have said what order it came in. Rifles in a stair are louder than anything in the world. The self-loaders went up the turns in the hands of the shore team's front four with the brass coming back down over Osric's boots in a stream, and men came down at them out of the smoke with crossbows and blades and the copied fire-tubes Saban had sent drawings of, the kind that burst on the fifth shot; and one did, two turns up, and Osric heard it. The knights went up on their own bodies, without stones, which was all a knight had ever had. The temple guard came down on theirs, and they were older and better than any of Osric's twelve but one, and that one was Arthur.

They had the top room a little after eight. The chest stood open on the floor with its stones gone to grey dust in their straw, and the breaker against the wall beside it as limp as a dropped glove, because he had forced it with everything in him and there was nothing left. Two of the Lieutenant's men put their hands up when they saw black plate in the door. The rest were on the stair.

Osric went back down it with a lamp.

Two of the shore team were dead on the stair. Thaddeus Wyatt, from the coal quay, in Ward's levy since the year of the dead field, who had carried the first turn with his self-loader at his shoulder; and Joby Dowd from Milford, nineteen, who had learned to swim on the island in October and been very proud of it, and had been third up the turn when the fire-tube burst. Five more were hurt. One of the knights had a blade through his thigh. Bren had a crossbow bolt's graze along the meat of his left arm above the elbow, where it had come off the plate's edge, and sat on the third step looking at it with deep resentment, and would let nobody dress it but Imani. And the Lieutenant's men who had come down the stair at them in the dark because they had been told to hold a tower: eleven, of whom six were alive. Osric counted them because Ward would ask.

He sat on the step below Bren with the lamp between his feet and found that he did not want to talk, for long enough that Bren looked down at him.

"Sir. Are you all right?"

"Ask me at supper." And then, because the boy was still looking: "I'll tell it at supper. Dowd and Wyatt. Properly. So it's said."

Bren nodded, and looked at his arm again.

On his way out to the ship Osric stopped by the drain net on the yard, dark in the dead air, and stood looking at it. Then he gathered it up in his two arms, cords and weights and the little flat stones with his fire in them, and carried it up hull four's ramp himself, because Tess would want to see it, and because he did not want anybody else to carry it.



Imani dressed Bren's graze at half past ten, and put a cup of Hester's broth in Bren's hand to stop his mouth, and went to look at the knight in the end bay who walked wrong.

She had seen him on the fifth, carrying a man down the hold's ladder: his left foot going up the rungs turned a little out, like a duck's, the knee over it not quite where a knee should be. She had not had a minute until now.

Abram Coyle was one of the hundred off the K Amara's aprons, twenty-six, a tall fair man from the Wayford road with a long jaw and a habit of saying nothing. He sat on the cot with his boot off and his trouser rolled to the knee because she had told him to, and looked at the bulkhead.

"When did you break it?"

He did not ask how she knew. Nobody on the K Endeavor asked her that any more.

"The first. In the cloud." He moved his shoulders. "Caught a brother in white going over. Hit the escort's rail with him. With my shin. Strapped it under the plate. It was all right by the third."

"It was not all right by the third."

"It held me up."

"That's not the same thing." She put her hand flat on the shin below the knee, and asked him, as she asked everyone now, to stop holding. Knights never knew how at first. Then he did, a little, the way a man lets out a held breath, and the push went out of him, and she was through.

The shinbone had broken a third of the way down, clean, a little slanting. And it had mended. That was what knights did: their own mana went to a hurt at twice the body's rate without anybody setting anything, so that in six days the bone had grown a thick grey collar round the break, still soft in its middle like a weld not yet cooled, and joined the ends where they had lain under his strapping. Not where they belonged. The lower half had turned on the upper as he walked on it, a quarter of the way round, and slid back along the slant the width of a finger, and knit there. Fast and strong and crooked. He would walk on it like that for the rest of his life with his foot turned out and his knee taking all the wrong of it, until the knee was finished at forty; and he would fly and fight and never say a word about it.

She sat back on her stool.

"It's knit," she said. "Your body did it, faster than mine would. But it knit it where it was, not where it goes." She held up her two hands, one twisted on the other. "That's your leg. In ten years your knee's done. In twenty you're on a stick." She put her hands down. "I can put it right. I have to break it again first, on the old line, while the new bone's soft in the middle, which it is today and won't be in a week. Then set it straight, and mend it straight, slowly, through my hand. You'll be under the ether for the break. Then three days on your back with me in your leg six times a day, and you're off the aprons for the rest of this." She held his eyes. "You can say no."

He was quiet for a long time.

"Is it the Light?" he said at last; not as a question, the way a man repeats a thing he has heard said on a ward.

"No," said Imani. "It's me."

"Then yes," said Coyle, and looked at the bulkhead.

She did it at noon in the forward theatre, a corner of the hold with a canvas screen round it and three of Bram's lamps on a bar overhead, with Bettany across from the K Amara to give the ether and two orderlies on the leg. No saw. A padded bar, a wedge of oak under the shin at the old break, and the orderlies' weight; because a bone that has knit in six days has a soft heart, and a soft heart can be found. She put her left hand on the shin and read for the softest line through the grey collar, and put the bar over that line and nowhere else, and said, "Now."

It went with a deep wet crack like green wood. One orderly went pale. Bettany did not look up from the mask.

Then the long pull on the foot, both orderlies leaning back slowly, until the ends came apart and round and back, a quarter turn and a finger's width, to where they had been before he hit the rail; and her hand on the shin the whole way, so that she knew where the ends were by feel and not by guessing. Under Bram's lamps it struck her for the first time that no surgeon in the world had ever stood at a table and watched two ends of a bone meet inside a man, line to line, the way Ferra watched a column come out. She nearly laughed. Then the splint and the strapping, and Bettany taking the mask away.

Then the part that was hers. She found his room first, because she did nothing to anybody now without finding it first: a knight's, deep, plenty. Then the two raw ends set true, the torn remains of the crooked collar round them, the blood gathering, the body already beginning in its slow way. She did not pour. She fed: a thread down her arm and through her palm along the road her reading had made, to the new break's edges and nowhere else, a trickle, like water into dry ground. She held it for a slow count of a hundred.

A knight cost her more than anybody. She paid it behind the eyes, a great ache like the morning after wine, and sat with her head between her knees under the lamps until it passed.

"Did it do anything?" said Bettany, cleaning the mask.

Imani went back in for a breath, lightly. Along both edges of the break there was a faint grey haze where the knit would come. Straight, this time, because she had put it there.

"Yes," she said.

She wrote it in her book that evening against the cot rail while Coyle slept with his leg on a pillow and his mouth open. TIBIA, LEFT. KNIT 6 DAYS, TURNED A QUARTER, SHORT A FINGER. HIS OWN MENDING, NOT SET. REBROKEN ON THE SOFT LINE. SET BY READING. FED 100. And under it, smaller, a line for her class's board when she was home, under ROOM: A knight mends fast. Fast is not right. Read first.

The hold had been full since the second: Kaldo's own, burned and cut and bolted; the knight with the hole under his collarbone, who had asked her twice whether God was on his side and been told twice to drink his broth; the six from the east gate's stair, who would not look at her and took the broth anyway; and the brothers from the white hulls, who had watched her for five days asking people first, and argued about it in low voices after she had gone past. Cuts went bad here as everywhere, and Hester's broth stopped them, and it was keeping half the hold out of the ground.

The Hod had taken fourteen of Kaldo's worst home that morning. Ferra said on the home set at six that Kaldo's hospital had been full since the South Gate's night, and the overflow was going up the line on the people's train to the Grayrock clinic and the nurses there; and that the halts at Grayrock and Milford were held by the holdings' watch, drilled by Ward's yard teams, who had stood on the platforms with rifles since the raid and let nothing through that was not on a card.

"Edda Rowe's at Grayrock," said Ferra. "Taking them off the train. She sent a line. She said to tell you she's reading every one first."

"Tell her," said Imani, and could not think for a moment what she wanted Edda Rowe told. "Tell her yes."



On the eighth, over the crown wall, Ward and Osric did a thing nobody had done before, and Emeric was the only one who saw the whole of it, because the only way to see it was from inside.

The crown wall's towers were the worst of the ring. The hill under them gave the trebuchets in the lower town nowhere to throw that was not their own streets, so it had to be from the air. At nine Ward sent twenty knights off the K Amara's aprons down over the hill's north flank, in pairs, never above the eaves, never more than a count of three, to draw the crews of the palace wall's north-west tower onto that side, while a shore team came up the steep lane under the wall's face behind them. Ward went with the twenty. Bettany told him not to on the apron in front of everybody, and he looked at her and said, "I know," and went. Osric went with him, with his fire back since the morning after the net, using it as if he expected somebody to take it away again.

There was a mage on that tower. Emeric had felt him all morning on its leads: not a breaker in grey, but a mage of the Circle in the tower's blue, with a great deal of his own mana in him and two notes in it that went together like two hands. Earth and fire. A two-handed mage was a rare thing, and the Circle kept them close, and the Lieutenant had kept this one on the wall over his father's house.

"Good," Ward had said at six, at the chart table. "Then I'll know where he is."

The mage waited until the shore team was in the lane, forty men between two walls with nowhere to go but on or back. Then he cast, and Emeric felt it begin: two glyphs on the leads side by side, an earth ring and a fire ring with their strokes going through each other like the fingers of two hands. He had seen that core once, in a book of Armand's, drawn in red ink with a warning beside it. Magma. A combined glyph was big and slow to close, and this one was very big, and it would come down into that lane like a ladle of iron into a mould with forty men in the mould.

"Ward."

"I've got it." Ward was short of breath. "Osric. You see the fire half?"

"I see it."

"The earth's mine. You take the fire. The other way round. Not the mirror; the other way round." A breath that caught on something. "On my count. Three."

Emeric had his hand on the rail hard enough to feel the rivets. A reversal was a spell read and its exact opposite cast, so the two met and were nothing; and under pressure the eye flipped a glyph instead of turning it, and a flipped glyph was no counter at all. One mage in five at the top did it reliably, alone. Nobody had ever done it two men to one spell, each his own half, from two places in the air. Nobody had ever had the helms to count it by.

"Two."

The mage's rings were closing. Emeric felt Ward's earth come up in him, slow and even, a wall; Ward, who had drawn his first glyph in the yard three years ago and raised a footing stone a hand's width and dropped it, with the whole garrison too careful to laugh. And Osric's fire, spilling, the way his note always spilled, too much and in a hurry and somehow in the right place.

"Three."

The mage's glyph closed on the leads in a great red and brown double ring with its core bright as a smith's fire, and for one breath the whole spell hung over the lane, heavy and hot, so that Emeric could feel the heat of it in his face through four miles of air; and two smaller things met it from two sides at once, an earth and a fire turned exactly the other way.

And there was nothing. No flash, no crack. The two rings went out together like a candle and its reflection pinched at once, and over the lane there was only a little warm air, drifting. On the leads the mage stood with his hands up, looking at them.

"Gods," said Osric's voice, softly.

"He'll go again," said Ward.

He did not get the chance. Osric went for him, down off the flank in a dive straight at the tower with his stones pushing and his blade lit, like a hawk off a hill; and the crew on the leads saw him and the wind crossbows came round. The first bolt went past him. The second. He was going faster than the island had ever let them go, because the faster he went the less time they had; and a man's height over the leads, with the mage throwing himself flat and the crew after him, he pulled out.

The nodes lift the plate, and the man rides in it on his straps and feels every turn; and at the bottom of a dive that fast, turning up that hard, the blood goes out of a man's head into his boots like water out of a tipped bucket. Emeric felt Osric's note go grey from the top down, all at once, like a wick turned. His stones stayed lit; they did not care whether the man in them was awake. The blade fell out of his hand. He went on up out of the dive limp in his straps, his head lolling in the helm, slowing, over the top of the wall and out above the lane, turning over onto his back with nothing in him steering at all.

"Ward," said Emeric. "He's out. He's..."

Ward was already there. He came across the top of the lane at the height of the eaves, flat out, with his ribs strapped under his plate, and did a thing with his body that nobody with two cracked ribs should have done, a long hard reach out and up as Osric came over above him; and his gauntlet closed on the harness strap at the back of Osric's neck, where the chute pack rode, and held.

They went down into the lane together a good deal faster than either would have liked, Ward's stones taking two men in plate and not liking it, and came down on the cobbles among the shore team with a noise like a dropped stove. Ward on his back, Osric on top of him.

After a while the shore team's set said, in the sergeant's voice, carefully, "They're both breathing."

Osric came round in a quarter of a minute with forty men standing over him, said he was fine, tried to get up, and sat down again. Ward did not try to get up at all. He lay on the cobbles with his helm off and his eyes shut and one arm over his chest, and told the sergeant he was thinking.

The tower was taken from the lane before noon with breach stones on its door. Its breaker had gone down the stair with the crew when Osric came at the leads, and nobody had gone back up. The mage was in the K Endeavor's cells by two in a pair of cuffs, looking at them. Nobody on that tower died.

That night Emeric wrote both in the fleet's log in his own hand: the first joint cancel, on a count by radio; and a knight blacked out at the bottom of a pull with nobody steering, who lived because another knight was there. He looked at the second line a long time. Then he drew a man at the bottom of a dive on a clean sheet, with the blood going the wrong way, and put it in the back of his book with the things that were not yet.

Bettany restrapped Ward's ribs at nine. She did not say anything to him at all, which the after deck agreed was the worst thing they had ever seen her do to anybody.



The ninth and the tenth were rain, and towers.

The South Gate's two went on the ninth from the ground, and their breakers came out with their hands up, because they had heard about the east gate and did not want to sit on a stool with their hands on a lid while men came up a stair at them. The old gate tower over the tanners' yards went on the tenth, and the trebuchet below it with it, cut off its frame, its gravity stone carried away in a cart; its crew said there were more in the city and would not say where. Two more on the palace wall went by night on the ninth, the three and Osric off the K Amara's after apron again on their cape-wings, without a shot, because their breakers slept. Osric said he had landed better. Bren, with a bandage under his sleeve, said he had not.

Nobody of Kaldo's died after the seventh. Eleven more were hurt. Wyatt and Dowd went home on the Hod on the eighth in two boxes Fletcher's carpenter made on the after deck, with a flag of Abloh's over each; and Ferra met them on the quay herself, at night, and wrote them in the death book in her own hand, and sent the line up afterward in plain words and not in cipher, so that anybody listening could hear it. Thaddeus Wyatt, ward three, the levy, the coal quay. Joby Dowd, Milford, the shore teams, nineteen. The east gate. Emeric heard it in the cupboard under the tower, and neither clerk wrote it down, because it was not for writing down.

At nine on the north set Rylan said four lines. The gully. The mage over the wells. Nobody new hurt. He would call at nine.

On the evening of the tenth Emeric stood on the tower's walk in the wet and counted what was left.

On the twenty-first of November the ring had been a necklace in his sense, cold and still and held wherever a pair of Venn's stood. It was a broken one now, with most of its beads gone. Three were left, cold and asleep across the lit wet roofs: one on the second wall above Tanner's Row, uphill, in the old town's thickest lanes; one on the crown wall under the empty palace, on the hill's east shoulder above the gardens; and one on the gatehouse at the town end of the long bridge, over the river, where nobody from Kaldo went. Ward had them on the chart table in chalk, under the rules, with nothing chalked beside them yet.

Saban came out onto the walk with his hat in his hands. On the hill above the falls, he said, there was smoke from four chimneys, and the gate was shut, and a baker's boy went up at seven every morning with a basket and came down without it. Nobody else had gone in or out since the second.

"He's in there," said Saban.

"I know."

"He can't stay in there."

"I know," said Emeric. He had not let his sense go up the falls road once since the second, and he did not let it go now. He stood at the rail with the rain on his face and the three cold beads across the roofs and the dark hill beyond them, and the K Amara's deck humming under his boots, and did not go anywhere.
