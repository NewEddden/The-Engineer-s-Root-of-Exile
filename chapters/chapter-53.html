Chapter 53 - Harder, Not Softer

Emeric had bought ore.

That was the whole of it, as he understood it. A fixed price. A standing weight. Grain up the hill and stone down it, exclusive both ways, and a foundry margin that would stop moving every time a factor two hundred miles east changed his mind about barley. He had ridden home with a dead man delivered and a contract folded in his coat, and for eleven days he had believed he had done a clean piece of arithmetic.

Saban came in off the autumn circuit on the twelfth.

He did not go to the keep first. He went to the yard, and had the carts unloaded, and checked the seals on two crates that had been opened and closed again somewhere north of the fork, and only then came up, and by then Emeric had been told twice that he was back and looking grim about it.

"How bad," Emeric said.

"Not bad. Loud." Saban sat down without being asked, which he had earned three years ago. "You want it the way I heard it or the way it's true?"

"The way you heard it."

"Grayrock's gone over to Kaldo." Saban said it flatly, the way he said prices. "The hill that raised the war has signed itself to the town that killed its baron, inside a year of putting him in the ground, and the widow did it, and the widow was in that town for six hours before she did it."

"It's an ore contract."

"It's an ore contract," Saban agreed. "Nobody outside this barony thinks so. I had it three times before I got as far as Wayford and I had it from a man in Milford who likes you."

Emeric worked at it for a moment. He could see the shape they were seeing. He simply could not make it fit over the shape he knew, which was six hours at a table with a woman who had haggled him down on carriage and up on weight and had not once mentioned her husband.

"There's no clause in it about men," he said. "There's no clause in it about anything but stone and grain. Anybody can read it. I'd show it to them."

"You'd show it to them," Saban said. "And they'd read it. And then they'd put it down and say, yes, and what else did he give her." He shrugged. "A hall doesn't read contracts. A hall reads who is standing next to whom. You and the hill are standing next to each other now and there's a dead baron on the ground between you, and the three halls that can see it have been waiting a year for somebody to tell them what the war meant. You've just told them."

There were three. Wayford, which had lost forty men and its baron's dignity. Milford, which had lost nothing and been blamed for it. And a third, east of Wayford, whose name Emeric had to ask for and then had to be told twice, because he had never in four years had cause to think about it, and which had subscribed nothing to the coalition and had watched the whole business from a comfortable distance and now found that the distance had shortened.

"The third one doesn't matter," Emeric said.

"The third one has a cousin married into Wayford," Saban said. "They all matter. That's what a region is."

What Saban had come back with about Sethe was worse, and he gave it second on purpose, because he had learned that Emeric absorbed bad news better when it arrived after something he could argue with.

Sethe was home. He had been home since the spring, ransomed at a rate Ferra had set and Kaldo had banked and spent on pipe. He had gone back to Wayford a man who had lost a battle, lost sixty of his own and forty of somebody else's, and been kept for eleven days in a tower room with a fire in it and fed what the keep ate, which was the part that had cost him most and which Emeric had never understood at the time and understood perfectly now.

And he was buying.

Saban laid it out the way he laid out a market. Money moving out of Wayford toward men who fight for wages, in three payments that were not large and did not want to be. Inquiries put out through a factor in the capital, not for hedge-mages and not for backcountry work but for men with lineage, men who could name a master and a tower, which was expensive and slow and meant somebody had decided last time's mages had been the wrong purchase rather than the wrong idea. A levy discussed in the Wayford hall in the autumn, which was not the season for it, in front of men who were not normally in the room when levies were discussed.

"Numbers," Emeric said.

"None yet. He's not raising a war. He's building the ability to raise one and then leaving it built." Saban spread his hands. "It's what I'd do. It costs him a little every season and it costs him nothing to stop."

Ward had come in halfway through and stood by the door with his arms folded, and had not said anything, and now said, "How long."

"Before he uses it? A year. Two. Whenever it's cheap."

"Before we can do anything about it."

Saban looked at Emeric.

Emeric took the question seriously, because it deserved to be taken seriously, and he sat with it for a long time, and what he arrived at was nothing.

Hiring men was not a crime. Asking after mages was not a crime. Discussing a levy in your own hall in the wrong season was not a crime, and none of the three together was a crime, and the fourth thing, which was the reason all of them were happening, was not written down anywhere and never would be. A baron was permitted to arm. That was most of what a baron was for. It stopped being permitted at the moment he marched, and at that moment it also stopped being preventable, and the whole of the distance between those two points was a distance Emeric could watch and could not enter.

"We can see all of it," he said, eventually. "And there's not one piece of it I can put a hand on."

"That's about the size of it," Saban said.

"Keep seeing it. Everything. Names of companies, sizes of payments, who carries the money. I want it dated." He was already reaching for the notebook. "If I can't stop it I want to be able to describe it afterward."

It did not occur to him, that afternoon or for a long time after, that he had just given the correct order for entirely the wrong reason, and that a description of a thing that has already happened is the least useful object in the world to a man being marched on and the most useful object in the world to a court.

He wrote north about it, because it was the obvious thing.

Not a complaint. He had learned that much. A report, three pages, in his own hand, with Saban's dates in it and no conclusions drawn, stating that a baron who had marched on a prince of the blood the year before and had been returned to his holding was hiring companies out of season and inquiring after trained mages, and that the crown might wish to know it. He put the ransom figure in, because the crown had never asked for it and it looked better volunteered than found. He put nothing in it about the hill.

He sealed it and sent it up the road with the quarter's accounts, and it went out of the Western Gate on a grey morning in a satchel, on a good horse, with a rider who had done the run four times.

Nothing came back. That was not remarkable. Nothing had come back from the capital in two years, on anything, and he had a perfectly serviceable explanation for it, which was that a third son in a southern barony was not somebody the crown answered, and it had fitted every fact he had ever had and he had never once tested it. He noted the silence the way a man notes weather in a season where the weather is always the same, and put the copy in the box with the others, and thought about it no further, and went down to the foundry.

Paxton came in the first week of winter, which nobody expected, on business nobody could argue with.

The soap works had restarted in the autumn and had eaten through Kaldo's own tallow inside a month, which Ferra had predicted and Emeric had not believed until he saw the empty rack. Milford ran cattle. Kaldo wanted fat, at weight, standing, for three years. It was a small contract by the standards of what the barony now moved, and it was exactly the sort of thing a factor handles by letter.

Paxton came himself, with four men and no banner, and stayed two days.

He was older than Emeric had remembered from the field, which was reasonable, since on the field Emeric had seen him at half a mile through smoke and had been thinking about something else. Up close he was a wide, unhurried, weathered man in a good coat that had been mended, with the hands of somebody who still put them on animals, and he looked at Kaldo the way a buyer looks at a herd, which is to say he looked at the parts nobody shows you.

He asked to see the drainage. Not the foundry. The drainage. Ferra took him round it and he asked her four questions and did not ask Emeric any.

They did the contract in an hour on the second morning. Ferra had it drawn before he arrived and Paxton changed two lines in it, both of them in Kaldo's favour, on the grounds that the delivery schedule as written would have had his drovers on the road in the week the river came up and somebody would have drowned a cart. Emeric signed it. Vantic read it and initialled it without comment, in the corner of the room, and Paxton watched her do it and said nothing about her at all, which Emeric noticed.

There was one thing Paxton asked for that was not in the contract and it was small. His drovers would come in on the evening before the weighing, and the beasts would stand overnight in Kaldo's pens, and he asked whether the barony would stand the fodder for that one night. Across a year it came to a few crowns. He asked it the way men ask for that sort of thing, half over his shoulder, already reaching for the pen.

"No," Vantic said.

She did not look up while she said it. Barony fodder was pledged stock under the security schedule, and there was no line in the schedule for the keep of another holding's animals, and she gave both halves of that in one sentence and went back to the ledger.

Paxton said that was fair enough and signed. Ward, by the door, looked at her for slightly longer than the moment was worth, and did not say anything about it then or afterward.

The rest of it happened afterward, in the yard, with the horses being brought.

"You'll want to know why I came," Paxton said.

"I assumed the contract."

"The contract's worth forty crowns a year to me. I have a man who does forty crowns." He pulled his glove on. "I came because I've been carrying something for a year and I'd rather say it standing up than write it."

Emeric waited.

"I watched you win a fight you had no business winning," Paxton said. "I was at the back of it with a hundred men, and I did not move them, and I have been called a coward for that in three halls and I don't much mind. Then I watched you take the field and let my hundred walk off it. No ransom. No terms. Nothing asked for, then or since." He looked up. "I spent the winter deciding whether that was mercy or arithmetic."

"And?"

"I got about halfway and stopped, because I worked out it doesn't signify." He said it without any warmth in it at all. "If it was mercy, you're a man I can stand next to. If it was arithmetic, you're a man who does arithmetic well enough to have got that one right, and I can stand next to him too. The outcome's the same and I've got a barony to think about. So that's where I am. I'm not fond of you. I'm not going to write you letters. But I'm in, and I'd rather you knew it than guessed it."

It was, Emeric thought, the least pleasant declaration of loyalty he had ever received and by a considerable margin the most reliable, and he found he did not want to say anything that would spoil it.

"All right," he said.

"There's a second part and it's the useful one." Paxton put his hand on the saddle and did not get up. "Sethe thinks Milford's still his."

"After the field?"

"Because of the field. He thinks I held the hundred back out of cowardice, or out of hedging, or because I'd been got at in some ordinary way he can price. He has never once considered that I might have sat there doing nothing because I'd looked at your line and decided you were going to win. Men like Sethe don't think other men can read." Paxton shrugged. "He writes to me. Not often. He wrote in the autumn."

"What did you answer?"

"Politely. Nothing in it. Which is what I answered him for fifteen years before you ever came south, so it doesn't read as anything." He got up onto the horse. "I intend to let him go on being wrong. It's the only thing I own that he hasn't priced, and once I spend it I don't get it back, so I'd thank you not to spend it for me."

"Ward and Ferra and Saban. Nobody else."

"Then we're square," Paxton said, and rode out.

The other thing about that visit surfaced three weeks later, and Emeric was not looking for it.

Saban mentioned, in the middle of a list of entirely other things, that Milford's wool had gone at a poor rate that season. Not ruinously. A shade under. It had gone through a different house than usual, because the house Paxton had sold through for twenty years had discovered a difficulty with its schedule in the one week that mattered and had been very sorry about it, and had taken the clip the following spring at the ordinary rate as though nothing had happened.

"Is that a thing that happens," Emeric said.

"It's a thing that happens."

"Is it a thing that happened?"

Saban thought about the question properly, which was one of the reasons Emeric kept him. "There's no way to know," he said at last. "That's what makes it good. A scheduling difficulty is a scheduling difficulty. You can't take it to anybody. You can't even be rude about it at a market, because the man was sorry, and he was probably sorry, and next year he'll buy."

"Paxton didn't mention it."

"Paxton wouldn't. He's a cattleman. He'd have counted it and put it in the column marked cost of doing business and gone to bed."

Emeric wrote it in the notebook that night, on its own line, with the date. He did not know yet what he was going to do about it, and there was in fact nothing to do about it, and he wrote it down anyway, and the line sat there for a long time afterward with nothing underneath it.

A letter came down from Grayrock in the middle of winter, in Bryda's own hand, and it was about schedules and weights. The upper workings would be shut for six weeks by frost and the tonnage would come off the lower seam instead, which was slightly poorer and which she proposed to make up on volume at the contract rate rather than renegotiate, since renegotiating in the first winter of a three-year agreement was a bad habit to start. The grain carts should come up on the ninth and the twenty-third rather than weekly, because of the road. There was one line at the end thanking Kaldo for the return of her husband's body, phrased exactly as such a line is phrased.

Emeric read it twice. The second time he read it for what was not in it, and found nothing at all that was not in it, and sat back and thought that a woman who had something to hold back would write precisely this letter, and that this was consistent, and filed it.

Ferra showed him the curve on the shortest day of the year, in the long room over the threshing floor, with the shutters closed and two lamps going.

She had drawn it herself on a sheet the width of the table, which was not something she normally did, and she had drawn it because she had worked out that he understood pictures faster than he understood columns and had decided this was worth the paper.

"The ore contract did what you said it would do," she said. "I want to show you what it did, because it isn't what you said."

"Go on."

"Before, we made our money on the gap between what the harvest cost us and what Duvrenne paid for it. That's a harvest business. It's one number a year and the number belongs to the weather and to a man in another province." She put a finger on the left of the sheet. "Now the input is fixed. High grade, known price, known weight, three years. Which means the foundry's margin isn't in the stone any more. It's in what we do to the stone."

"We sell goods."

"We sell goods," Ferra said. "Bar, plate, tools, the fittings, the etched work when Vesk's floor can keep up. Every one of those has a margin we control, because it's made of our labour and our method and not of anybody's rainfall. It is a much better business than the one we had." She paused. "It is also a considerably slower one, because goods have to be made one at a time and a harvest arrives all at once."

She moved her finger along the line, which climbed, shallowly, without any drama in it whatsoever, and crossed a second line that ran flat across the sheet.

"There. Eighteen months."

"To clear?"

"To turn. Debt service paid out of what the barony earns, with nothing drawn down and nothing borrowed, and something left at the end of the year that is ours." She let him look at it. "On current terms."

"On current terms."

"The stone stays at that price. The road stays passable. The foundry runs. Nobody burns anything. The house doesn't call anything. The hill doesn't change its mind." Ferra squared the sheet with the edge of the table, which was what she did instead of sighing. "Every one of those is a thing that has never once gone wrong in this barony, except that four of them have."

Emeric looked at the curve for a while. It was a good curve. It was the first document anybody had put in front of him since the loan that had a future in it rather than a hole.

"Eighteen months," he said.

"If nothing happens."

The winter was hard and ordinary. The upper workings froze and the lower seam came down as promised. Armand's corrections went on arriving in the school in a hand nobody in Kaldo could read at speed, and Orrin worked through them in the evenings with a patience that Emeric found harder to watch than anger would have been. Hollis's arm was no better. Two of the four capital men wanted to go home and did not say so and Ward knew anyway.

And somewhere six days north, at a hall Emeric had never seen, a man he had fed and housed and released for money was paying three companies a small retainer to remain available, and asking after mages with proper lineage, and waiting for the season when it would be cheap.

Emeric knew all of it. He had it dated, by carrier, with names. He read it every month the way a man reads a barometer, and there was not one thing in any of it that a magistrate would have called a crime, and there was not one thing in any of it he could touch.
