Chapter 53 - Carbons

The first carbons came out of the potter's kiln as ash.

Emeric had expected that, more or less, and had said so beforehand, which did not make the potter feel any better about it. They had made a dozen rods for the first firing, as long as a man's forearm and as thick as a thumb, out of charcoal ground to a black flour and bound with hot pitch, and laid them on a shelf in the kiln among the potter's pots, and in the morning when the kiln was opened there was a row of grey smudges on the shelf, each one the exact shape of a rod, which fell in on itself when the potter breathed on it.

"Charcoal burns," the potter said, to the sky. "That's what it's for."

"It burns if the air can get to it." Emeric picked up a pinch of the ash. "So we keep the air off."

The charcoal was the barony's, out of the store behind the granary, ground in the dyehouse on the old dyer's stone, where the madder had been ground in better years, until Tess's hands and face and the whole of the inside of the dyehouse were the same soft black. The pitch was the dead port's. There had been a shed at the head of the moorings where the caulkers kept their barrels, and the barrels were still in it, a dozen of them, with the pitch inside gone as hard as rock. Bram broke it out with a chisel and melted it down in his iron pot over the forge, and the smell of it hung over the forge yard for a week like the smell of a ship.

The second batch went into the kiln inside clay pipes, the potter's, packed round with sand and stopped at both ends with clay, so that no air could reach them. Those came out whole, and when Emeric broke one open the inside was a black honeycomb, light as bread, that crumbled between his fingers into grit. The pitch had boiled inside the pipe before it could set, and the steam had blown the whole rod hollow.

"You brought it up too fast," the potter said.

Emeric looked at him.

"That kiln's got a hot back and a cold door. You put your pipes at the back, and the back was boiling while the door was still warm to the hand." He pointed, as if the kiln were standing between them. "Anything with water or tar in it goes by the door the first night, and comes up slow, two days, and moves back on the second. A day just to warm her, before you so much as think about heat." The potter looked at the grit in Emeric's palm with a craftsman's contempt. "You were in a hurry."

So the third batch went in on a Monday and did not come out until Thursday, and the potter sat up two nights with his kiln and brought it up so slowly that on the first night the clay pipes were barely too hot to touch. On Thursday he broke the clay off the first pipe himself, with a little hammer, and drew out a rod, and held it up.

It was dull grey-black, hard, and straight, and when he tapped it with his fingernail it rang, a small clean note, like a good pot.

The potter tapped it again, and listened, and his face did something it plainly did not want to do.

"Hm," he said, which he had learned from Bram.



They struck the first arc in the powerhouse on the third night of February.

It had a lamp's worth of light in it, and no lamp yet to hold it. What it had was two carbon rods, each one clamped in an iron holder on the end of a long handle of ash, and a pair of wires from the machine to the two holders, and in the circuit between them the great loose coil of line wire from Tess's test board, to stand in for the seven lamps that were not there.

Harl was on his stool by the door, between his buckets. Tess was at the lever. Rylan had brought Armand down from the keep in a cart, at Armand's request, and against Imani's advice, which Armand had received from her personally at her side step that afternoon and set aside with great courtesy, and he was sitting on a sack of carbons by the wall with a rug over his knees, getting his breath back from the yard.

Emeric handed round the glass.

It was window glass, squares of it out of a broken casement in the keep, held over a candle until they were black. Everybody looked at it, and then at him.

"Don't look at it without that," he said. "Not once. Not for a heartbeat. It'll burn your eyes the way the sun off snow does, and you won't know it until morning."

Harl held his square up between two floury fingers, and looked through it at the lamp on its hook, which was invisible through it, and lowered it again, and said nothing.

Tess pulled the lever. The machine came up to its hum. Emeric took a holder in each hand, and held the two carbons point to point, and touched them together.

A crack, small and sharp. A spit of sparks. He drew them apart, the width of a fingernail, no more.

The powerhouse went white.

It was like the sun coming in through the roof, all at once, and then going on coming in. Every stone in the walls stood out sharp. Every shadow in the room was suddenly black and hard-edged and thrown flat against the far wall, the machine's shadow, Harl's, the buckets'. Through the black glass the gap between the two carbon points was a blaze too bright to look at even so, a small fierce bridge of light, blue-white at its heart and violet at its edges, with a hiss coming off it like fat in a pan. The light went out through the slot in the wall and through the cracks round the river door, and outside on the black water a long bright bar lay across the river from bank to bank.

Nobody said anything.

The carbons burned. Emeric could feel them burning, could see the tips eating away in the glare, and the gap between them widening by a hair at a time, so that the light changed its note and began to flicker. He pushed the holders together, a hair, and the hiss steadied. Then again. And again. He was doing it every few breaths, and his arms were beginning to ache from holding the long handles out, and he understood with great precision, standing there in the white light with his arms out, why nobody on the walls of Kaldo was going to do this all night eight times over.

"Enough," he said, and drew the carbons apart, and the light went out with a snap, and the room was full of red afterimages and the smell of burning and the machine's hum coming up as it ran on nothing until Tess pulled the lever back.

Armand was the first to speak. He had taken the glass down from his eyes and was looking at the place in the dark where the light had been.

"There was no stone in it," he said.

"No."

"Brighter than any lamp stone the tower ever cut. Brighter than ten." He was quiet. His breath was still short. "Charcoal and pitch, and the river."

"And the potter."

Armand did not smile at that. He sat on his sack of carbons with the rug over his knees and his old hands folded on top of it.

"In September I told you they would laugh," he said. "When they hear about this, they will stop."



Tess had timed the burning. She had marked both carbons before Emeric struck them, with a scratch at every quarter of a finger, and in the morning she brought the two stubs up to the dyehouse and laid them on the bench and measured what was left against her own finger.

Ferra did the rest at her table.

"A finger an hour, the top one," she said. "Half a finger, the bottom. Twelve hours a night in winter, less in summer. Eight lamps." She wrote. She did not look up. "So many rods a night. So many a month. So much charcoal and so much pitch to make them, so many days of the potter's kiln, so many days of somebody grinding." She turned the page round for him. "Two sacks of charcoal a month. A barrel of pitch a season, and the port's got twelve, and then we'll be buying it. And the kiln."

"Is that a lot?"

"No." She sounded faintly annoyed about it. "It's the cheapest thing you've built."



The lamps would hang on the walls, and they would need something to hang from and something over them to keep off the rain.

Hale knew where both were. The keep had torch brackets on every face of it, old iron, rusted, set into the stone for a garrison that had once walked its own walls at night with fire, and nobody had put a torch in any of them since before the plague, because torches cost money. Hale had them listed, every one, in a book of the keep's fittings that nobody but Hale had opened since he became steward. He brought it down to the gate yard and read out where each bracket was, and the gate crew took eight of them down off the keep in a morning, and Bram carried them over the east bridge to bay three.

He made them into housings. Each bracket got an iron hood over its arm, like a small roof, to keep the rain off the carbons, with a short chimney in the crown of it for the fumes, and a pair of clamps under the hood to hold the lamp's works. They were ugly, and strong, and Bram would not let anybody paint them.

Ward chose where they went. He walked the whole circuit of the wall with Emeric on a grey afternoon and stopped eight times, and every time he stopped he looked outward.

"Not on the gate," he said, at the South Gate, where Emeric had assumed the first lamp would go. "On the wall beside it. High, and to the side. The men in the gate stay dark. The road is bright."

At the river tower he wanted the light on the water. At the corner of the wall above the east bridge he stood longer than anywhere else, looking out across the river to the moorings and the roofs of the dock quarter behind them, and said nothing at all, and then said: "Here."

Emeric had meant the lamps to show the town its own wall. Ward meant them to show the wall everything that came at it. By the end of the circuit they were Ward's eight places, and Emeric did not argue with one of them.

The lamp's works were Emeric's.

He had designed the regulator in the room, where he could hold every piece of a thing in front of him at once and turn it over. It was a good design. It was the kind of design that had been used, in the other life, in the first years of the lamps, before anybody had thought of anything simpler. The upper carbon hung on a toothed rod. The rod was driven down by a little train of wheels and a spring. In the lamp's own circuit was a magnet that held a catch against the train while the current through it was strong, and let the catch go when the current weakened. The same magnet, when the current first came on, drew the lower carbon down a fingernail's width to strike the light. So when the carbons burned back and the gap widened and the current fell, the train turned a tooth or two and brought the carbons closer, and the current came up, and the catch fell back into the wheel.

The wheels were the candlesticks. The brass ones, out of the dead houses, that had gone into a box under the bench in October because brass would not do for wire. Bram melted them down and cast them into a plate, and Tess and Bram cut the wheels out of the plate and filed the teeth, by hand, one tooth at a time, for nine days. Tess's fingers bled into the brass filings. She did not mention it. Emeric found out from the stains on the bench.

They hung the first lamp in the powerhouse on the thirteenth of February, on a beam, in its housing, with the regulator inside and its wires down to the machine.

It struck by itself. The magnet pulled the carbons apart as the current came on, a fingernail's width, and the white blaze filled the powerhouse again, and everybody looked at it through their black glass. And it held. It flickered when the gap widened, and the catch clicked, and the wheels turned, and the upper carbon came down a hair, and the light steadied. Click. Steady. Click. Steady. Tess counted the clicks aloud and Harl watched the light through his glass from his stool by the door.

It ran for a quarter of an hour.

Then the flicker did not steady. The catch clicked, and nothing happened. The gap went on widening. The light went from white to blue to a shivering violet, and the hiss became a rasping roar, and the arc stretched out between the two points like a thread of something alive, and snapped. Dark. The machine's note climbed as it ran on nothing, and Tess pulled the lever.

When the regulator had cooled enough to take apart, it was full of soot. A fine black dust off the burning carbons had come up through the works and settled in the teeth of every wheel, and the heat of the arc, a hand's length below, had swollen the brass until the smallest wheel bound against its neighbor and would not turn. Emeric cleaned it with a feather and a pin, and put it back together, and they struck it again.

It jammed in less than ten minutes.

He sat on the floor of the powerhouse with the regulator in his lap, in the light of an ordinary lamp, picking soot out of its teeth with the pin. It was a design for a workshop with a lathe in it that could cut a wheel true to a hair, and air that did not have carbon in it, and he had built it in a place where the only lathe was Bram's eye and the air was mostly soot. He knew that. He had known it in the room and built it anyway, because it was the design he knew.

Tess was sitting across from him with her bench book open on her knees. She had drawn the regulator in it, all of it, every wheel, and then, on the facing page, she had drawn just the carbons, one above the other, and the gap between them, and an arrow pointing down beside the upper one.

"Why does it need teeth?" she said.

He looked up.

"It's going down anyway," Tess said. She put her finger on the arrow. "It wants to fall. You don't have to push it down. You only have to stop it falling too far."

Emeric opened his mouth to answer that, and did not have an answer, and closed it again.
