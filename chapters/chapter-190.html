Chapter 190 - The Iron Hat

Bram would not run anything up the Wayford line until he had walked it.

He had said so in the winter, at the table, when the iron was still going down through the frost toward the junction, and he said it again in the first week of the thaw, when the ground began to give. Then he went and did it. He walked it with a gang of eight and a handcart of keys and chairs and a long iron gauge, from the works to the Milford siding and on north up the leg to the junction below Wayford, every joint of it, with the frost coming out of the bank under the sleepers and the ballast sinking round them as it went. Where a rail had lifted, or a chair had cracked, or the bank had slumped in the wet, he had it out and set it right and walked back over it before he went on. It took him eleven days. He came home on the twelfth with mud to his thighs and a list in his coat, and gave the list to Ferra, and said the line would take the engine.

It went up two mornings later, in the dark.

Emeric rode the footplate. He had not asked whether he might and nobody had told him not to, and he stood in the corner behind the watcher with his back to the tender and his hands in his coat and tried to take up less room than a prince. The driver was a rail works man with forearms like hawsers who had driven the Grayrock line since it reached the hill. The watcher was younger, and stood at the gauge with the water cocks at his hand, and looked at nothing else. Between the two of them, bolted to the back of the boiler where both could see it and neither could miss it, was a brass plate that Bram had had cast at the foundry the week the rail works fired again. The words were Bram's own, off the back of the first engine's rule, cut deep in letters an inch high.

The man who watches is not the man who drives.

Nobody on the footplate read it. Nobody needed to. It was there in the same way the gauge was there.

They went west across the flat in the first grey, with the ridge black on their left hand and the lamps of the quarry going out one by one on its face as the night gang knocked off, and ran on into the open country beyond the barony's old fields. By full light they were at the Milford siding, where the grain wagons stood in a row with their doors shut and three of Paxton's men came out of the siding hut to watch them pass without stopping. Then north, up the western leg of the river, with the water brown and bank-full below them in its cut and the flats going away on the other side under last year's grass. Twice the driver eased her through a stretch Bram had marked on his list, and twice the watcher said nothing, and twice they came out the other end of it at the same pace. Toward noon the country began to rise, and there were hedges again, and sheep, and a church tower Emeric knew, and then a new platform of stone beside a new shed at the foot of the town, with a turntable let into the ground in front of it and forty people on the bank above to see what would come.

Pennock was on the platform with his watch in his hand. He had been Wayford's clerk since the week after the square, and he had never in all that time been seen to hurry, and he did not hurry now. He closed the watch and put it away and came down the platform and said, "Six hours and a quarter, Your Highness. I've written it," and that was the whole of the ceremony.

The road from Kaldo to Wayford was five days, when the road was good.

They turned her on the table by hand, eight Wayford men on the bars and the town's boys hanging off the rail to watch, and took water from a tank on a trestle, and went back the way they had come. The light went on the way home. It was an hour past dark when they came in off the flat, and the last of the sky was a thin green line in the west behind them, and ahead of them, across the flat, was the city.

He had not meant to look at it. He looked at it every day. But he had never come at it from the west in the dark on an engine, and he found that he was holding the rail beside him harder than the ride needed.

The wall stood all round. It had stood all round since the autumn, full height, the last courses laid by Tarle's four and the gangs in the week before the frost, and from the flat in the dusk it was a long black line against a sky a little less black, with the sixteen towers standing up out of it like the teeth of a comb. The north gate had its leaves. The east gate would have its own by the end of the week; the sheerlegs were up over its arch, and he could see them against the sky, three thin poles leaning together. Inside the line, off to the north-east beyond the dark rise where the palace's first storey stood, the first quarter was lit. Two streets of windows, yellow, and the market, and the tower standing up out of the roofs with the last of the light on its tank, and the little bell-cote of the school. Near two thousand people behind those windows, eating their suppers. The rest of the ground inside the wall was dark, and in the dark, where the engine's lamp swung across it for a moment as the line curved, there were white lines on the grass. Streets, pegged in lime. Three quarters of a city that was a drawing on the ground and nothing else.

One quarter of the drawing, standing and lived in.

Ferra was at the works with the book open on a trestle under the lamp, waiting, as she had waited the day the Grayrock line reached the hill. She did not ask how it had gone. She looked at Bram, and Bram nodded, and she turned to the page. The works book had three headings on it, ruled in her hand the year the rail works was founded: the Grayrock line, and under it Milford, and under that Wayford, with a space left under each for a figure that had not come in. Two of the spaces were full. She wrote the date under the third, and Pennock's six hours and a quarter beside it, and then she laid her rule across the page under all three and drew a line, the way she had drawn one under Grayrock.

She did not say anything. There was nothing on the page left to say it about. Kaldo, Grayrock, Milford, Wayford. Four baronies on the iron, and a man could go from any one of them to any other between a breakfast and a supper.

The second tower went back up that week.

Weekes had taken the frost's course out as soon as the ground would let him and laid it again in the first dry days, and by the time Emeric came out to see it the base was in and the first lift of the shaft was rising off it, round, in the grey stone off the ridge, with the scaffold poles lashed round it and a gang on the planks laying the second lift over the first. Weekes met him at the foot of it with his level on his shoulder and his staff under his arm, and nodded, and did not say anything, which was his way of saying good morning.

It stood on the tower ring beside the first, on its downhill side, where the ring road bent south. The first tower was forty paces off, tall and finished, with its slate cone on its head and its tank full. Emeric had walked past the place where the second would stand a hundred times in the winter and had thought of it as a hole in the ground with a problem in it, the problem being that the frost had got into it. He had not thought about what it would stand in once it stood.

Weekes showed him.

He did it without words, as he did most things. He set the level on its legs on the new course and sighted up the ring toward the first tower, and had his boy carry the staff up and hold it at the lip of the channel where the aqueduct's water came in at the first tower's head. Then he stepped back from the level and let Emeric look.

Emeric looked. He looked twice.

"Four feet," Weekes said, "and a hand. Below the lip." He took the level back. "The first one's fed from the top. It's nearest the inlet. The water comes in off the aqueduct and falls into it. This one's down the slope." He tapped the course with his boot. "Water won't climb four feet to get to it. Not for anybody."

That was in the drawing. Emeric had drawn it. The first quarter's first tower had been fed from the top because it stood nearest the inlet, and every tower after it, down the fall of the ground, would have to lift its water off the ring canal. He had written it on the sheet himself in the summer the wall went up without the mages. He had known it for two years. He had not, until he stood on the new course with the level beside him, understood that this was the one it would first be true of.

The pump house was already built at the tower's foot, as every pump house was, because Weekes built the house before the thing it housed so that nobody could say afterward there had been no room. It was a squat stone room with a slate roof and a door, and a mast beside it with a wind pump's vane on its top and the sails furled, and inside, bolted to the floor on an oak bed, a hand pump with a long iron handle, both of them drawing from the same pipe off the ring. In the floor, beside the pump, was the mouth of a clay pipe coming up out of the ground, with a wooden plug hammered into it.

"Same as the first," Weekes said. "Wind, and a hand to it when the wind drops."

"And when the wind drops for a week?"

Weekes did not answer that. He was not a man who answered questions he knew the asker had already answered for himself.

Imani answered it. She had come up the ring road while they stood at the level, on her way back to the ward from the first quarter's dispensary, with her bag and her sleeves rolled, and she stood in the pump house door and looked at the hand pump for some time before she said anything.

"A still week," she said. "In summer. The vane stands. This handle wants a man on it every hour of the day and night, and he can't do an hour. Four men to a shift, turn and turn about, three shifts round the clock. Twelve men a day." She looked at Emeric. "At two Copper the shift, if you can get them at two, which in the harvest you can't. For every tower on this side of the ring, when there's a tower. And the fourth draw's households have their keys to the houses this tower serves, and they're waiting on its taps to move in."

"They'll have taps."

"They'll have taps when the wind blows." She shifted the bag on her shoulder. "When it doesn't, they'll carry. From the ring, in buckets, in summer, into houses with children in them. You know what carried water does in summer. You were there."

He had been there. He had been there the winter of the fever, on the gravel shelf, capping wells with her in the rain, and she had not spoken to him the whole morning except about the wells.

"I know," he said.

"Good," said Imani, and went on down the ring road to the ward.

He walked the line that afternoon.

He had drawn it in the fever winter, the week after the wells were shut, on one of the nights when he had not been able to sleep and had walked every line he had ever drawn to its end, to make sure it ended somewhere. The ducts beside the mains, the empty clay pipes the Tarrants had laid a hand wider for him in every trench, came up in a pump house at every tower. He had drawn their ends that night. From every pump house, along the ring road, out under the south gate and down the fall of the flat to the river, to the sawmill. To the wheel. Not a stone anywhere on it. A wheel that turned because the river turned it, and would go on turning whether he was alive or not.

He walked it with Ward, from the pump house door to the sawmill, and it was a mile and a quarter, and at the end of it he stood in the sawmill yard in the noise of the saws and knew it would not work, and knew exactly why, and was annoyed that he had needed to walk a mile and a quarter to find out a thing he could have found out in a chair.

The wheel was busy. That was the first thing. It drove the saws from first light to last, a great undershot wheel in its race with the river going white under it, and the whole yard shook with it, and the timber for three quarters of a city came off its benches stacked and squared. At dusk the saws stopped and the sawyers threw the belt off the saw shaft and onto the pulley of the shed at the end of the race, where the dynamo stood on its bed, and the dynamo turned all night and sent its current up the wire on its poles to the quarry, and the arc lamps on the quarry face came up white, and the night gang cut stone by them until dawn. By day it sawed. By night it lit the quarry. There was no third shift in a wheel.

The wire was the second thing. He looked at it going away up the poles toward the ridge, a single line of it, and knew its length to the yard, because there was not much of it to know. Half a mile of copper in the whole barony. The dynamo's windings had taken most of what the boy's pots had given in a year and all that Saban could buy on the coast without anybody asking what for, and the line to the quarry had taken the rest. There was not a yard over. There had not been a yard over the day the quarry lamps first came up, and when Ward had asked Bram the spring before why the market street had gas lamps and not the white ones, Bram had said there was no copper for the other kind.

The head sawyer gave him the third thing without being asked. He was a thick old man called Aske, with sawdust in his eyebrows and a voice that had shouted over saws for forty years, and he came across the yard to see what the prince was looking at, and saw that it was the wheel, and stood and looked at it with him.

"Good water," Aske said. "Thaw water. She'll go like this to midsummer." He spat into the race. "Back end of the summer, she drops. Every year. Wheel goes round like a funeral. Last year the quarry lamps went yellow on us the whole of the last month, and the quarry men came down here and called us names." He seemed pleased by it. "Nothing to be done. River's the river."

Emeric thanked him and walked back up the flat with Ward, and did not say anything for the first half mile.

"It doesn't reach," Ward said at last.

"It reaches. There's nothing at the end of it." He counted them off, because it was the only way he knew to be done with a thing. "A wheel with no hours left in its day. No copper to carry it if it had. And a river that drops in the one season the towers need it most." He looked at the second tower on its ring, rising grey above the empty ground, with the gang on the planks against the sky. "I drew a line to the only thing in the barony that turned without me, and I drew it in a winter when I needed very badly for something to."

Ward did not answer that. After a while he said, "Copper, then."

"Copper first," said Emeric. "Then what turns it."

He had known where the copper was since the spring before last, standing on a heap of broken blue stone half a mile under Grayrock with three brassy grains in his handkerchief. He had not said it then. The powder had been spoken for, every charge the beds would give, and the old diggings had kept for a long time and would keep a little longer.

They were Kaldo's own diggings, two days out on the old ore road: a long red hill scabbed with pits and grassed-over spoil, where Kaldo had dug its iron for longer than anybody could remember, and where the iron had got poorer every season the deeper the men went, until the last loads had been the worst the foundry ever took and the diggings had been left to the sheep once Grayrock's ore began coming down the river. The leach heap behind the foundry was made of those last loads. The blue water came out of it and out of nothing else. The boy had been scraping red mud off plate trimmings in the blue end of the trough every morning for three years, a pot a month, and the pots stood in a row on Ferra's shelf, and every one of them was a little of the hill, washed down.

Iron on top. Something else under it.

He asked Ward for powder at the table that night.

Ward did not give it at once. He never gave powder at once. Since the field below the stream the armoury had claimed every charge the beds had made, for the guns and the store behind the guns, and he had signed every one of them in himself and knew where each keg sat. But the beds had come in fuller over the winter than anybody had reckoned, the second year's heaps giving what the first had promised, and the spring making was the first since the war that the armoury's store could do without.

"Forty charges," Ward said. "Out of the spring making. Not one more. The rest stays in the house."

"Forty will do."

"Forty will have to." Ward wrote it on his own slip and passed it to Ferra, who wrote it in the book. "Who's firing them?"

That was the question. Nobody in Kaldo had ever driven a heading in hard rock. Kaldo's miners had dug for iron in soft red ground with picks and shovels, and gone down and not in, and not one of them had ever driven a hole into a hill on the level, round after round, and fired it, and gone in after the smoke.

He wrote to Bryda. The letter went up the Grayrock line on the morning train in the guard's pouch and the answer came down on the evening one, which was a thing that still surprised him every time it happened, and it was two lines long in her own hand.

Hask comes down on Thursday with two of his men and his own steels. His rate is his rate; Ferra has it. Feed them properly. B.R.

Ferra had it. She paid it without a word, at Grayrock's rate to the Copper, and entered it under a new heading that she wrote at the top of a clean page and underlined once: The old diggings.

Hask walked the hill for a whole morning before he would put a steel to it.

He was a short broad man with grey in his beard and a way of standing with his head a little on one side, as though he were listening to the ground. His father had driven headings under Grayrock before him, and Hask had grown up in them, and he looked at Kaldo's old pits the way a mason looks at a wall somebody else has built: politely, and with growing pity. He went down two of the old shafts on a rope and came up red to the elbows. He walked along the foot of the deepest spoil heap and turned stones over with his boot. At noon he came back to where Emeric and Ward sat on the tail of the cart, and sat down on a rock, and took a heel of bread out of his coat.

"They went down," he said. "Every one of them. Straight down, like a well, and when the iron went thin at the bottom they stopped and dug another hole beside it." He ate some bread. "Never once went in. Never once went under." He pointed with the bread at the flank of the hill, below the deepest of the old pits, where the ground fell away toward a stream. "In there. Low. Under the lot of them. Drive in on the level till you're under where they stopped, and keep going."

"How far?"

Hask shrugged. "Till it changes."

They started the heading that afternoon. Hask marked the face on the hillside with a chalk cross and his two men set the first steel against it, one holding and turning, one swinging the hammer, and the ring of it went out over the empty hill and came back off the far side of the valley, and the sheep on the spoil heaps lifted their heads at it and went back to the grass. Emeric stayed two days, long enough to see the first round fired and the smoke come rolling out of the hole and Hask go in after it with a lamp before it had cleared, which Ward told him not to do and he did anyway. Then he left Hask to it, with Ward's four men to keep the powder and a boy with a pony to bring word, and went back to Kaldo.

The boy came on the eighteenth day.

The heading ran fifty yards into the hill by then, low and square and just high enough for a man to walk in bent, with a timber set every few paces and the floor wet, and the air in it still sharp with the last round's smoke. Hask took him in with a lamp and did not say anything on the way, which Emeric took for a good sign.

For the first thirty yards the walls were red. The red of the old diggings, the rust-red of the whole hill, poor and crumbling, with the timber sets holding it back. Then, between one set and the next, it changed. The red ran out in streaks and patches, and under it the rock was stained green, a bright soft green like old bronze on a church door, and blue in places, a deep sudden blue that Emeric had not seen in stone before in this world. It was soft. He put his thumb to the wall and the green came away on it.

Hask did not stop there. He went on to the face, and held the lamp up.

At the face, low down, running across the heading from wall to wall at about the height of a man's knee, there was a band of something dark. Not red, not green. A dull grey going to black, like soot on a hearth stone, a hand thick at one wall and more than a foot at the other. Hask set the lamp on a timber and picked up a piece of it off the floor from the last round and put it in Emeric's hand without a word.

It was heavy. It pulled his hand down the way the grey lump at Grayrock had pulled it, the first day he found the lead in the old spoil. He turned it over under the lamp. It was soft enough to cut with a knife, and where he scratched it the scratch shone.

He knew what it was, and he knew how it had got there, though he did not say it and had nobody to say it to. The rain had got into the top of the hill, a very long time ago, and gone down through it for longer than there had been anybody in the south to watch it, and taken the copper out of the rock as it went and left the iron behind as rust. And where it came down onto the rock underneath, the rock the rain had never reached, it had put the copper down again, all of it, from all the way up, in one thin rich layer, like silt at the bottom of a bucket. The hat was the rust. This was what the hat had been made of, and more of it than had ever been in any one place in the hill before the rain got at it.

"The old men," Hask said, "would've been rich if they'd gone ten more yards."

Emeric weighed the lump in his hand.

"They'd have been rich," he said, "if anybody had wanted copper."

Hask thought about that, with his head on one side, and then laughed once, short, the way a man laughs at a thing that is true and no use to him, and picked up the lamp.

The first carts came into the foundry yard four days later, and the foundry's small furnace took the dark ore with coke far more easily than Emeric had feared. It ran in the first heat. It came out of the tap red, and when it had cooled in the mould and Bram broke the first bar out and turned it over, it was full of holes, a sponge of little bubbles right through, and it broke in his hands like bad bread.

Bram looked at the broken ends for a while.

"Gassy," he said. "Same as bad brass." He put the pieces back in the pot. "My father used to stir it with a green stick."

He melted it again the next morning, and when it was down he took a pole of green ash off the wood stack and thrust it into the melt, and the melt boiled up round it and spat and roared, and he held it there with his face turned away until the roaring died. Then he poured. The second bar came out of the mould close and heavy and red all through, and rang when he dropped it on the floor, and he picked it up and handed it to Emeric without saying anything, which was the most Bram ever said about anything he was pleased with.

Ferra weighed the first week's copper on the big scale in the yard and wrote it in the book under The old diggings, by the hundredweight. Then she went up to the counting room and wrote it again on the shelf's page, beside the boy's pots. The boy's pots were on the page by the pound. Three years of them, a pot a month, every one scraped over a crock in the cold at the end of the trough, and the whole row of them came to less than one cart off the hill.

She did not take the boy's pots off the page. She left them where they were, at the top, where they had been first.

He told her what the copper was for that evening, in the long room, after the others had gone, with the lamp between them and her book open in front of her.

He told her the whole of it, which he did not often do before a thing was built, because it was the kind of thing that would be easier to build if she had heard it first. A wheel that did not saw, turning a dynamo that did nothing else. A shed that ran all day and all night, not by the river, where the river could not starve it. Wire from the shed to the tower on its ring, in the empty duct the Tarrants had laid for it two years ago, and a little machine in the pump house at the tower's foot that would turn the pump when the wire was put to it, the way the dynamo turned when the wheel was put to it, only the other way about. A little machine in every pump house, then, one at each tower, down the whole of the ring. And one day, when there was copper enough and something to turn it, lamps. In the quarters. In the streets. White ones.

Ferra listened to all of it without writing anything. When he stopped, she asked the only question she ever asked.

"What turns it?"

"Coal," he said. "The rail's at the hill."

She did not answer. She turned the book round on the table so that it faced him, and turned back through it to a page he had not looked at since the winter, and put her finger on a line.

It was the coal. All of it. What the Grayrock line brought down off the hill in a day, on a good day and on a bad one, and under that, in her small clear hand, where every ton of it went before it was off the wagon. The converter. The rail works. The lung, and the gas it made for the market street's lamps. The fireboxes in the keep and in the ward, which had burned coal since the rail reached the hill. The back boilers in every house in the first quarter, one to a kitchen, that gave the quarter its hot water. He read down the column and it came to the figure at the top, less a little, and the little was written in at the foot.

"The rail works came off its rush at the thaw," Ferra said. "The third stand went quiet the week the engine went to Wayford. It gave back a little over three tons a day." She moved her finger to the foot of the column. "That's the only coal in the barony that nobody's burning. I've written it against the pumps. A coal engine can turn the pumps on three tons, Bram says, every tower in the first quarter and the next, with some over."

He looked at the three tons. Under them she had written a second figure, larger, with no line under it, and a question mark after it, which was a thing he had seen in her book perhaps four times in all the years he had been reading it.

"That's the lamps," she said. "The quarters, the works at night. I don't know it exactly. I know it's four times the three, or near it, and I know there isn't four times the three, and won't be this year or the next." She did not take her finger off the page. "A coal engine can turn the pumps. It cannot light the city."

She did not say no. She never said no to him. She wrote the number, and turned the book round, and waited.

He looked at it and said nothing.

After a while she closed the book, and put her pen away, and said good night, and went. In the morning she went down to the ward before her clerks came up, and found Imani at the dispensary table, and told her that she would have her taps.

He did not go to bed. He went down to the pattern store instead, because it was the place he went when he had looked at a figure and not had an answer for it, and because there was a light in its high window across the yard.

Bram was there. He was at the forge.

The forge stood where it had stood since the summer Bram built it, against the back wall of the pattern store: a chamber of firebrick the size of a baker's oven on its stone footing, with its floor a hand deep in dry sand, and the flue going up beside the high window with the air stone in its throat to draw the smoke away, and at the front the port, with its iron shutter down in its groove and the smoked pane behind the little inner shutter glowing faintly with whatever was going on inside. On the shelf beside it sat the rack that fed it, a long iron frame of charged stones with its feed cup to the port, humming, as racks hummed, like a wet finger on the rim of a glass.

It was not humming.

Emeric noticed that before he noticed anything else, because the pattern store was never quiet when the forge was running, and it was quiet now. Through the smoked pane he could see the plate hanging in the chamber on nothing, level, correcting itself against nothing, and a stone cell on the plate taking its shape, slowly, the way cells took their shape. The forge was working. The rack beside it was silent.

Bram was sitting on his stool in front of the port with his hands on his knees, counting under his breath. He did not stop counting when Emeric came in. He held up one finger, and went on, and Emeric stood by the bench and waited, and after a while the rack beside the forge began to hum again, faintly, and then in its usual voice, and Bram stopped counting and said a number.

"Nine hundred and forty."

"What was?"

Bram told him. He told it the way he told a coal count, flat, in order, with nothing in it that was not a fact, and Emeric listened to it the way he listened to a coal count, and understood about halfway through that he was not going to sleep that night at all.

The forge ate a rack a day when it ran. It always had, and racks were Ferra's column, and Bram did not like to draw on Ferra's column for anything he could get some other way. He never had. He had been stingy with racks since the day the forge was lit.

And Ned had come up from the lake that afternoon with the month's flasks for the Hod's helm. Four iron quarts, pitched shut, filled at the top of the band, to sit in their rack at the helm and be opened if she ever lost her racks at height again. The four that were in the rack now had been filled a month ago, near enough, and Bram's pitch held a month and not much more, and after that the seal went soft and what was inside began to go with it. So the old four came down, and the new four went up, and the old four went back up in the hold on the next clear night to be let go over the side in the band and filled again. Every month. It was the rule.

"Two of them," Bram said, "I took off him."

"To let go over the side."

"To let go over the side," Bram agreed. "Which is a flask of the band, carried down two miles and carried up two miles, and thrown away." He did not say anything about that. He did not need to. Bram had never in his life thrown away a thing that could still be used, and had once walked back a mile to the works for a dropped rivet.

So he had put one in the forge.

He had done it properly, as far as there was a proper way to do a thing nobody had ever done. He had lifted the air stone out of its collar in the flue's throat, so that nothing would be drawing on the chamber from above. He had laid an iron plate over the throat and weighted it with a brick. He had cut the pitch round the flask's lid with his knife, and backed the lid off to its last thread with his fingers, and stood the flask upright in the sand at the back of the chamber with the tongs. Then he had put the long iron key he used for the forge's inner fittings through the port, and turned the lid off, and pulled the key out and brought the shutter down in the same movement, as fast as he could move.

Not fast enough. A little of it had come out of the port over his hands before the shutter was down.

"Warm," he said, when Emeric looked at his hands. "Like putting them to an oven door. Up the arms a way." He turned them over, front and back, on his knees. "Then it stopped."

Emeric took the lamp off the bench and looked at them anyway. The palms, the backs, the wrists. There was nothing on them but Bram's own scars, old and white, from thirty years of hot iron. No lines. No frost.

"Don't do that again with your hands at the port," he said.

"Then you'll want a door," said Bram, "that doesn't open onto a man." He said it as a thing to be built, and put it away, and went on.

The forge had been working when he opened the flask. It was pressing a cell for the spare bank: a slow job, a night's job, with the plate hanging level in the chamber and its four stones under the corners holding it there against everything, pressing heavy at one corner and easing at another as the work shifted on the plate, and drawing what they spent from the rack through the feed. The rack hummed while it gave. It always hummed. Bram had heard it hum every night the forge ran since the forge was lit, and tonight he had heard it stop.

"Stopped when the shutter came down," Bram said. "Forge kept on. Plate never moved. Rack sat there with nothing to do." He looked at the rack. "I counted till it started again."

Nine hundred and forty. A quarter of an hour.

On the Hod's deck, in the open, a flask gave a count of ninety or so, and then it was the sky's. Emeric had counted it himself the first time, on his feet in a dead patch he had made in the forge yard, with a lamp stone on a post: sixty, and the light wavering; eighty, guttering; ninety-two, and nothing. Ninety, in the open. And here, in a firebrick box with the flue stopped, the same flask had run the forge for a quarter of an hour without the forge taking one breath from anything else.

"The other one," he said.

Bram looked at him.

"The second flask. Do it again. I'll open it." He was already taking his coat off. "My hands have been in worse."

They did it again, the same way, with the plate hanging in the chamber and a fresh cell blank set on it so the forge had something to work, and Bram on the stool with his count ready. Emeric put the key through the port and turned the lid off and had the shutter down before the lid had finished falling into the sand. He felt it go past his wrists anyway, warm, going in, the way Bram had said. He did not look at his hands. He put the right one flat against the firebrick at the side of the chamber, and shut his eyes, and let the sense out through his palm into the brick and past it.

He had had the band in a room on the ground a hundred times, at the handcart on the flat and in the forge yard and wherever a mage turned a lid: three breaths of it, thick as the sky, and then gone through the walls. He had had it in iron, in a flask, a hard tight knot of it that he could feel through the metal like a banked fire through a stove door. He had had it round him in the band itself, three miles up, with the cold coming in round the window frames. He had never had it stay. Not here, on the floor of his own pattern store, with the lamp on the bench and the smell of the day's castings in the air, long enough to watch what it did.

The chamber was full of it. Full to the brick. It lay in the chamber as thick as it lay over the sea at the top of the band, and in the middle of it the plate hung on its four stones, and the stones were working.

He watched them work.

The corner nearest the port was the one pressing, because the blank on the plate sat a little toward it and the plate wanted to dip there. So the stone under that corner was being told heavy, and it was heavy, and it pressed. And a stone that pressed spent itself pressing. That was the oldest thing Armand knew, written on his own bench years ago with a lead sheet over the pan: Set below weight. Presses. A stone told heavy pressed until it was empty, and then it was a dim grey thing and somebody had to take it away and fill it. That was why nobody in the world ran anything on heavy for long. Nobody had ever been able to fill a stone as fast as heavy emptied it.

The stone under the port corner was pressing, and spending, and it was not going dim.

It was drinking. As fast as it spent, it drank, out of the thick air round it in the chamber, the way the ship's cells drank through their necks in the band. It pressed, and spent, and filled, and pressed, and the level of it in the sense did not move at all. It was as though a man had been set to bail a boat and somebody had taken the boat away and put him in the sea.

And the chamber was going.

He felt that too, under his palm. The band did not stay in the chamber. It went out of it the way water goes out of a sieve, through the brick, everywhere, all over the brick at once and slowly, between every grain of it, past his hand and into the pattern store and on out through the walls and the roof and away. Not in three breaths, the way it went out of an open room. In a quarter of an hour. But it went. The firebrick held it back the way a sieve holds water back, for as long as a man goes on pouring.

At some point Bram said, "Nine hundred," and a little after that the rack on the shelf began to hum again, and the stone under the port corner began, very slightly, to go dim.

Emeric took his hand off the brick.

Bram went home a little after midnight. He had a heat in the morning and he was not a man who went to a heat without his sleep. At the door he stopped and looked back at the forge, and at the rack humming on its shelf, and at Emeric sitting on the stool in front of the port with his hands between his knees.

"Worth it?" Bram said. "The two flasks."

"Yes."

Bram nodded, as though that were a figure he could enter, and went out.

Emeric sat on for a long time after he had gone.

A room full of the band, on the ground. That was what he had had under his hand for a quarter of an hour. Not a stone, and not a flask, which was only the band folded small and shut in iron. A room of it. And in the room, a stone told heavy, pressing, and spending itself pressing, and filling back up as fast as it spent.

Heavy was a push. It had always been a push. It was what held the forge's plate level against a billet that wanted to tip it. It had never been anything but a push that ran out. A stone that pushed and did not run out was a different thing. A weight, told heavy, would fall. The same weight, told light, would rise. Heavy, light, heavy, light. Down and up and down again, as long as the room stayed full, and every time it went down, it pushed. And a thing that pushed down, over and over, could be made to push round, if what it pushed on was the right shape. Every engine he had ever seen in his old world was a thing that went up and down made to go round.

He did not let himself go any further than that. He had gone further than that before, on nights like this one, and built things in his head that did not stand up in the morning. There was one question, and it was the only one worth taking out of the pattern store. The brick had held a quarter of an hour. A room of the band that was going to turn anything worth turning would have to hold it all night, and the next night, and every night. The brick had not held it. The brick had never been going to hold it. It was a sieve.

Iron held. He knew iron held. Iron was all he knew that did.

He got up and went to the long bench under the high window and took a fresh sheet off the pile, and set it square, and weighted its corners with four of Jorrin's gauge blocks. He took up a pencil. He did not write anything. There was no word yet for what he wanted to write, and he did not want to put one down that would be wrong in the morning.

He drew a ring.

Then he sat and looked at it for a while, a single pencil circle in the middle of a clean sheet, and put the pencil down, and left it there on the bench under its weights, and put the lamp out, and went up to the keep through the dark yard, where the only sound was the rack in the pattern store behind him, humming.
