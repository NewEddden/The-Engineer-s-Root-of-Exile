Chapter 190 - Your Grace

Fletcher took her off her foils two miles below the harbor and brought her the rest of the way in on the motor.

It was Emeric's order and Fletcher did not like it. A ship that could do forty-five knots did not creep into a strange port at a walking pace with her stones dark like a barge with a sick horse, Fletcher said; it made her look poor. But the order stood, because everybody on that quay would be looking at her, and what they would see was a long bronze boat with a dome on her back and no mast and no funnel and no oars, coming up the last reach of the inlet with no sound at all except the water at her bow, and no stone lit anywhere in her that a mage on the harbor wall could feel. A boat. A strange one, and a rich one, and a boat. Whatever else the duchy wrote down about her that day, it would write down that.

The bell in the harbor tower had been going for a quarter of an hour.

They could hear it across the water long before they could see the tower, a single bell rung over and over without a pause, and then the harbor opened in front of them round the last bend: a long stone mole with a light at its end, piers inside it, a forest of masts, coasters and fishing boats and two long grey galleys with their oars shipped, and behind all of it the white city going up the mountain in terraces with the smoke from its chimneys lying flat in the warm air. Every boat in the harbor had people on its deck, looking. Every window on the waterfront had somebody in it.

And the quay at the foot of the city, where a broad flight of steps came down to the water, was full of knights.

Forty, Ward said afterward; Emeric did not count. They stood in two ranks along the edge of the quay in plate that was not black and not plain: bright steel, engraved at the breast and the shoulders with patterns of leaves and waves, and blue half-cloaks off the left shoulder, every one the same blue. Behind them a crowd. In front of them, alone, at the top of the steps, a woman.

He knew her before Fletcher had the motor stopped.

He did not know how. He had last seen her when he was twelve, from the mud of a practice yard, looking up, and she had been seventeen years younger and a queen of Randillia in a borrowed court; and the woman on the quay was a duchess on her own stones with grey in her hair and plate on her back. But she stood the way she had stood over him in the mud. Her weight a little forward. Her hands loose. Black hair going grey, in one long tail down her back bound every hand's width with a ring of steel, and two swords at her hip, a long and a short, and a blue cloak like her knights' but longer; and down the left side of her face, from the cheekbone to the corner of the jaw, a scar, white and old and very straight.

He did not remember the scar. He must have seen it at twelve; it was twenty years old if it was a day. He remembered a scar across the back of her left hand, from the yard, because at twelve he had been watching her hands and the sword in them, and from the mud he had kept nothing of her face but the height of it.

His sense was open. It had been open since the island, out of habit, the way a man keeps his eyes open in a strange street. He felt her across forty yards of water: dense, and still, a note like a struck bell held in a hand so that it would not ring.

And he felt her feel him.

He saw it happen. The K Discovery's side came against the quay's fenders and Cade went over with the lines, and the woman at the top of the steps went still in a different way; and then she took one step back, and her right hand went to the hilt of the long sword, and rested there, not drawing it.

"Well," she said, across the gap. Her voice carried without being raised. "I didn't know you'd got so skilled, Emeric."

He went up the side of the ship and onto the quay.

The knights did not move. The woman looked at him. She looked at his face for a long time, as if she were reading something in it that had been written there a long while ago in another hand; at his eyes, which were his father's, gold; at his mouth, which was not. Then, without taking her eyes off him, she spoke to the grey square knight at her elbow, the one with a captain's knot on his shoulder.

"He's a prince of Randillia," she said. "Harrold's third son. Amara's boy." Her hand came off the hilt. "They're mine."



They were hers in the harbor house for the rest of the day.

It was a courtesy, and everybody knew what it was. The harbor house stood at the head of the quay, an old square building of white stone with the customs hall underneath and a floor of rooms above for the ships' masters who came in from the coast, with a long balcony across its front over the water; and the ten of them were taken up its stair with great politeness by the grey knight, whose name was not given, and shown the rooms, and the balcony, and a dining room with a table already laid, and a pair of knights at the foot of the stair who were very sorry, but the Sovereign would send for them when the Sovereign was ready. The ship was left at the quay with Cade and Tobe aboard her and four of the duchy's knights on the quay beside her, not touching her, looking at her very hard.

Fletcher stood at the window of the dining room and looked at the knights looking at his ship.

"If one of them puts a hand on her," he said.

"Nobody will put a hand on her," said Emeric.

"If one of them does."

"Then Cade will ask him politely not to," said Ward, "and he'll stop, because Cade is six foot three and whistles."

The rooms were good rooms. Somebody had lit fires in them that nobody needed; the air off the harbor through the shutters was as warm as an evening in May at Kaldo. Saban went down the stair within the hour and talked to the knights at the foot of it, and within another hour the harbor house's clerk had come up the stair with a jug of wine and a book of the port's dues, which he was proud of, and was sitting at the long table with Saban going through it line by line; and Saban was asking him, with the warm, slow interest of a man who had never in his life wanted anything but to understand a harbor, what the dues had been in his father's time.

Emeric sat in the room he had been given, at the end of the balcony, on the edge of the bed, and opened the door.

Not the room's. The other one. The width of a hair, out of himself, thin, the way his sense went out with no glyph; across the passage, through two walls, to the landing at the top of the stair, where Ward had taken a chair from the dining room and put it with its back to the wall and sat in it with his arms folded, a few yards from the duchy's two knights at the foot. He found Ward's note. It was the easiest note in the world to find. He touched it.

He felt Ward feel the door open, a breath before the first word, the small quiet that came first; and Ward did not move in his chair.

Forty, said Ward, in the room's way, which was not quite a voice. On the quay. Twelve of them are real. The rest are good-looking.

And the two on the stair?

Real. Both. The left one's watching me. He's been watching me since we came up. Ward's note did something Emeric knew for amusement. I think he knows who I am.

Does he?

He was at the capital. With her, when she had her own knights in your father's yard. Before your time. I think I put him on the ground once. The door was very quiet. He's polite about it.

He let Ward go, gently, and went the other way along the passage, through the wall of his own room and the next, to Ferra's.

Ferra wore a council band. It made it easier; the grey bead on her wrist was a bead of the same stone as his own, cut by the same forge, and it met him half way, the way a door that has been oiled meets a hand. She was at the window of her room with the harbor house clerk's second book, the arrivals register, which she had asked for and been given, and she put her finger on the page when she felt the door, and kept it there.

They've written us down, she said. Today's date. A Kaldo boat. No sail, no oars, no smoke. Twelve souls. Master Fletcher. She moved her finger. The Holder of Kaldo, and a party. And then, under it, in a different ink, later: held at the Sovereign's pleasure.

That's honest of them.

It's correct, said Ferra. It's not the same thing. A pause. They've counted us twice. Once on the quay and once on the stair. They got thirteen the first time. Somebody counted Cade's shadow.

He laughed, on the bed, out loud, alone in his room.

Ferra's note did not laugh. But it warmed. Go and lie down, it said. You were up all night. I know you were. Imani said you would be.

He shut the door from his side, and lay down on the bed in his coat, and did not sleep, and found that he did not mind.



The Sovereign sent for them at dusk.

The grey knight came up the stair with a lamp and said so, and the twelve of them went down into the warm evening, Cade and Tobe brought off the ship for it and four of the duchy's knights left on the quay in their place, and up into the city.

It was all stairs. Nobody in Emberfell walked on the flat if there was any way to avoid it, because there was no flat; the city went up the mountain's knees in terraces, and the streets on each terrace ran along it, level, and the stairs between them went straight up, steep and narrow, worn hollow in the middle of every tread by five hundred years of feet. Vines on every wall, black and bare now in winter, with the knots of last year's growth on them like knuckles. Lamps at every turn of the stair, on iron arms out of the walls; some of them light stones in glass, white and steady, and some of them little copper bowls with a wick in them and a flame, burning something that was not tallow and not olive oil, with a smell under it like the boatyard on a hot day. Water ran beside the stairs in stone channels, warm; steam came up off it into the lamplight.

The dome was on its crag at the top, over the harbor. The hall was under it. To reach it they crossed a terrace that was a practice yard.

He knew it was a practice yard before the grey knight said so. It was a long level square of beaten sand, walled on three sides and open on the fourth to the drop and the harbor below, with a rack of wooden swords under a roof at the far end and posts along one wall for tying horses; and the sand had the look a yard gets that is fought on every day, raked smooth at dusk and scuffed again by noon.

He crossed it. He let his sense go down into it as he walked, the way he let it go into every floor he walked on now, without deciding to: down through his boots into the sand, and under the sand. A hand's depth of good sharp sand, and under it clay, red and dense and holding the day's warmth. A drain under the clay, running corner to corner from the north-west to the south-east, an old one, stone-lined, that had settled in the middle so that the sand over it lay a finger lower in a long faint trough nobody would see in daylight. And by the east wall, where the yard met the cliff, a patch the size of a cart's bed where the clay was wetter, and softer, and gave; an old cistern under it, he thought, filled in a long time ago, and the water still finding it.

He filed it. All of it, the way he filed everything, without meaning to. And he went on across the yard after the grey knight and up the last stair to the hall.



The herald at the hall door had a staff and a voice, and used both.

"The Sovereign of Emberfell," he said, to the hall, which was full, and struck the staff on the floor, and the hall stood.

Sovereign. Not duchess, which was what Randillia called her when it called her anything. Not queen, which was what she had been at Harrold's court and was still on paper, as far as Emeric knew; Harrold had never put her aside. Sovereign. A word that belonged to nobody's court but her own, and meant that this was not one.

She came in from a side door and walked the length of the hall to the high table, in a gown of dark blue with no jewels on it, with the long sword still at her hip and the short one gone, and her hair in its ringed tail down her back. She did not look at the guests from Kaldo. She took her chair and the hall sat, and she looked down the table to where the grey knight had seated him, at her right hand two places down; and he stood up again.

"Your Grace," said Emeric.

A small thing. It went down the high table like a stone dropped in a well. He saw the herald look at him. He saw the grey knight look at the herald. He saw two of the men further down the table, older men in good dark coats with chains of office on them, look at each other.

The Sovereign of Emberfell looked at him for a breath.

"Your Highness," she said. "You're welcome in my hall." Her voice was cold and perfectly correct, and gave nothing at all. "Sit down."

He sat down.

It was a long dinner and a good one. Fish he did not know the name of, baked in salt; a kid roasted with something green and bitter; bread with oil; the wine of the terraces, which was black and heavy and tasted of the mountain. She spoke to him three times in the course of it. Once to ask whether the voyage had been calm, and once whether Kaldo's harvest had been good, and once whether his father was well, to which he said he did not know, and she said, "No," and went back to her plate. Each time in the same voice. Each time as if she were speaking to the coast's ambassador from a country she had no quarrel with and no interest in.

But she watched.

He felt it all through dinner, not on himself so much; on the table round him. The grey knight had seated the Kaldo party with care, by rank as the duchy reckoned it, which put Ward on his right and Ferra, as chancellor of four baronies, on his left, and Imani across the table from Ferra beside Saban, and Vantic further down beside the harbor master; and the Sovereign of Emberfell ate her fish and drank her wine and said nothing, and her eyes went, once in a while, from his face to Ferra's, and from Ferra's across the table to Imani's, and back to his.

She saw Ferra pass him the salt without being asked, because he had been looking for it, and put it down by his hand without looking at him. She saw Imani, across the table, glance at his plate when the kid came and then at Ferra, and saw Ferra, without looking up, move the dish of green stuff an inch toward him, because Imani had told him in October that he ate too little green and Ferra had written it down. She saw Imani laugh at something Saban said, the short dry laugh through her nose, and saw Emeric turn his head at it before he knew he had, and saw Ferra see him do it and not mind.

She saw all of that. He watched her see it. By the time the kid was cleared she had stopped looking at them; and he understood, with a feeling in the back of his neck like being read over the shoulder, that she had stopped because she was done.

He drank the wine. He drank it the way he had drunk at his father's table for seven years before Kaldo, which was the one thing he could do without thinking: a cup raised often and lowered not much emptier, a hand loose on its stem, the wine going down in it slowly enough that nobody watching would see the level, and a face that looked as if it had been at the jug since noon. He did not decide to. It was the habit of a court, and he was at a high table.

Halfway down the second cup he looked up and found her looking at the cup.

Not at him. At the cup in his hand. At the level of the wine in it, which had not changed since the fish.

She looked away.



When the hall rose she spoke to the grey knight, low, and the grey knight came round the table to Emeric and said that the Sovereign would be glad of a word with His Highness in her solar before he went down.

"With his knight," said the grey knight. He looked along the Kaldo party with no expression whatever. "And the two ladies. The chancellor. And the healer." He paused. "The Sovereign said, the two ladies."

The solar was a small round room off the hall, under the curve of the dome, with a fire, and a window over the harbor black with night and pricked with the ships' lamps, and four chairs and a bench. The grey knight stood inside the door with his back to it. The Sovereign of Emberfell stood at the fire with one hand on the mantel and did not sit down until they had come in and the door was shut; and then she sat, in the chair nearest the fire, and unbuckled the long sword and laid it across her knees, and looked at the five of them, and said, in an entirely different voice:

"Sit down, for God's sake. You look like a deputation."

They sat. Ward stayed on his feet a breath longer than the rest, and then sat too, on the bench under the window, where he could see the door and the grey knight and her.

"Ward," she said.

"Your Grace."

"You've got old."

"So have you."

"I had help." She touched the scar with one finger, not as if it hurt; as if it were a fact she was putting on the table with the others. "You didn't. You did it yourself, on that boy's account." She looked at Emeric. "Rowena," she said. "Rowena will do, in here. It did for your mother."

"Rowena," he said.

"You weren't drinking." She said it without any change in her voice, in the same tone she had used for the voyage and the harvest. "At my table. Were you."

He did not answer at once.

"I've heard you were a drunk," said Rowena. "For years. Everybody on this coast with a cousin at court has heard it. Harrold's third son, Amara's boy, at the jug since he was fifteen. I heard it in this room and I believed it and I was sorry, because I had known you at twelve and I didn't think you'd have the stomach for it." She looked at the fire. "And tonight you sat at my table for two hours with a cup in your hand that never got any emptier, and a face on you like a man who'd been at the jug since noon." She looked back at him. "Nobody drinks like that by accident. That was work."

"Not as much as it looked," said Emeric.

"No." She turned the sword on her knees a little, so that the firelight ran down the scabbard's chape. "Don't tell me who it was for."

He looked at her.

"I can think of one man in Randillia it might have been for," said Rowena, "and if it was him, I'd have to decide what I think of him, and I'm too old and too far away to start that now." She did not look at Ferra or Imani or Ward. "Keep it. It's yours. I only wanted you to know I'd seen it."

Ferra, on Emeric's left, had not moved. But he felt her, close, the way he felt her at the long table when somebody had said a figure she had been waiting for; and he knew, without looking, that she had written it in her head, the date and the hour and the room, and who had seen it.

Rowena's eyes had gone to his coat.

To the left breast of it. To the silver star, and the grey stone in its collar in the middle of the star, that did not light and did not do anything. She looked at it for a long time.

"Where did you get that?" she said.

"It was hers," said Emeric. "My father sent it. With Ward. Two of them." He touched the brooch, and felt the chip on the back of it under his thumb. "The other one's in my band."

"She wore one like that." Rowena's voice had not changed, quite. "On a cord. Under her collar, against her skin, where nobody could see it but her maids and her husband and me." She looked at the stone. "She used to put her hand on it when she was thinking, without knowing she did. I never asked her what it was. I thought it was a keepsake. From her people. She never once spoke of her people." She was quiet. "I never asked."

Ward, on the bench, said nothing. Rowena glanced at him, and let it go.

"You hum," she said to Emeric, after a while.

"I'm sorry?"

"You hum. When you work. You were humming on my stair, coming up to the hall. Under your breath. You didn't know you were." She tipped her head toward the door, toward the yard below it, the long dark square of beaten sand. "Going across my yard. You were doing something to my yard, and you hummed while you did it." She looked at him. "Amara did that. Every time she was working something out. In the stillroom. At her desk with the household books. Two notes, up and down, over and over. It used to drive Harrold out of the room."

Ferra made a very small sound.

Emeric turned his head. She was sitting very upright in her chair, looking at the fire, with her hands folded on her book in her lap, and her face had gone faintly pink across the freckles; and she was not looking at him at all.

"He does it at the counting room table," said Ferra, to the fire. "He's done it since the first winter. Two notes. When he's doing a sum he hasn't told anyone about yet." She paused. "I've always known when he's planning something. It's never been anything clever. He hums."

Rowena looked at Ferra. Something moved in her face, quickly, and was gone, and might, on another face, have been the start of a smile.

"You'd have liked her," she said.

"I'm told so," said Ferra. "By everyone." She did not look away from the fire. "It's very annoying."

Imani laughed. She tried not to, and failed, and put her hand over her mouth; and Rowena looked across at her, and then at the two of them, and then at Emeric between them, and he saw her see it again, the thing she had seen at dinner, and file it; and then she touched her face.

"You'll have heard about this," she said. "Everybody has. They tell it differently in every port." She ran her finger down the scar from the cheekbone to the jaw. "I'll tell it to you once, the right way, and then you can tell it to whoever asks."

She told it in the same voice she had used for the harvest.

Emberfell had an old law, older than the duchy, older than the dome: that the land went to the strongest of the blood, and that any of the blood could call another into the arena for it. Nobody had used it in a hundred years. Her father had left the chair to her brother, as was proper, while she was in Randillia as a treaty; and when the treaty no longer needed her in a room, and she came home, her brother could not bear it. He had not wanted her back. He had thought she had come for the chair. She had not. She had come home because her friend was dead and there was nothing for her in Harrold's court any more but Sabina, and she did not want to grow old in a room with Sabina.

"So he called me out," said Rowena. "In the arena below the works. Under the old law. In front of the whole city." She looked at the fire. "I beat him. It wasn't hard. He'd never been much with a sword and he was frightened, and frightened men fight badly. I put him on his back and took his sword, and I told him to get up, and gave him back his sword, and his chair, in front of everybody, and said I'd only wanted to come home. And I turned round and walked out." She touched the scar. "He had a knife in his boot. I heard him get up. I turned in time for my cheek and not my throat." She looked at the scar's reflection in the dark window, or seemed to. "So he's dead. And I have his chair. And I've had it twenty years."

Nobody said anything.

"Three healers closed it," said Rowena. "In a day. I made them. I had to stand on the steps of the dome the next morning in front of the whole city and be the Sovereign, and I wasn't going to do it with a hole in my face." She shrugged. "They did it well enough. It doesn't bleed."

"It pulls," said Imani.

Rowena looked at her.

"When you talk," said Imani. She had leaned forward a little in her chair, and her face had gone into its other shape, the one Emeric knew from the ward: interested, exact, without any pity in it at all. "At the corner. When you say anything with an M in it. I watched it at dinner. And you can't close that eye all the way. You blink with the other one first." She looked at the scar the way she looked at a wound. "They pulled it together too fast and too tight. A day's healing for a month's wound. It's held. It'll always pull." She sat back. "I'm sorry. I don't mean to be rude. It's my trade."

"It's not rude," said Rowena slowly. "Nobody's told me that in twenty years." She looked at Imani a moment longer. "The eye's right. I'd stopped noticing."

"I could fix it," said Emeric.

They both looked at him.

"Not now," he said. "Not quickly. Someday." He looked at the scar. He could feel it from where he sat, if he let his sense go, the way he could feel a bad weld in a seam: the skin grown together too fast, too much of it pulled into too small a line, the little nerves under it gone wrong and sullen. A month's work done in a day, by people who had been frightened of their Sovereign. "It would have to be slow. Slower than they did it. Weeks." He paused. "And you'd have to let me."

Rowena was quiet for a long time.

She did not say yes. She did not say no. She looked at him across the fire with her hand on the sword across her knees and the scar white in the firelight, and he could not read her at all; and then she looked at Imani, and Imani, who had heard him say it and had seen nothing in the room that told her how, looked back at her with an expression that said she would be very interested to watch.

"Maeve's at the capital," said Rowena.

It was so sudden that he took a breath before he understood it.

"She went up at the end of the autumn. To her father. She goes every winter and comes home in the spring." She turned the sword on her knees. "She'll be sorry she missed you. She was here when they sent you to Kaldo."

He had known that. He had known it for seven years, in the way he knew most things about his family: as a fact he had filed and never gone back for. Maeve had been at Emberfell the night he was sentenced in his father's hall. And now he had come to Emberfell, and she was in his father's hall. Twice.

"Tell her I'm sorry too," he said.

"I will." Rowena stood up. She stood up all at once, the way a knight does, with the sword in her hand, and buckled it on again, and the solar was a Sovereign's room again round her. "You read my yard," she said.

He looked at her.

"Coming up to dinner. Going across it." She did up the last strap. "You let your sense go down into my sand like a man putting his hand in another man's purse, and you hummed while you did it. I felt you from the top of the stair." She looked at him. "So you'll fight on it. In the morning. At dawn. Steel only. No casting, no sense, no knight's tricks, nothing you can't do with your two hands and a blade." She looked at Ward. "He'll want to watch. Let him."

"I'll watch," said Ward.

"Good." She went to the door, and the grey knight opened it for her; and in the doorway she stopped, and looked back past Emeric at the two women in their chairs by the fire.

"Tomorrow evening," she said to them, "the two of you will bathe with me. In the springs, under the dome. My women will come for you at the harbor house after the lamps are lit." She looked at Emeric, briefly, and then back at them. "Not him. He can amuse himself."

Ferra stood up.

"Thank you, Your Grace," she said.

Imani stood up too. She looked at Rowena with her head a little on one side.

"Is it hot?" she said.

"It's a volcano," said Rowena, and went out.



Vantic was on the balcony when he came in.

It was very late. The harbor house was quiet; Saban had gone to bed, and the clerk with his book had gone home, and the two knights at the foot of the stair had become two different knights, who had never been in Harrold's yard and did not look at Ward. Ward was in his chair on the landing. Ferra's door was shut, and so was Imani's, and there was a line of light under one of them and a murmur behind it that he did not let his sense near.

The balcony ran the length of the house over the water, and she was at the far end of it, by herself, with her elbows on the stone rail and her hands round a cup that had nothing in it. The harbor below her was black and still, full of the lamps of ships at anchor laid on the water in long wavering lines, and beyond it the inlet went away down the dark between its hills toward the sea. Above the city the mountain stood up against the stars with its plume of steam faintly white, going up and leaning east.

He did not mean to stop. He stopped.

"I'm not asleep," said Vantic, without turning round. "You needn't creep."

He came along the balcony and stood at the rail a few feet from her, and looked at the harbor.

"I filled," she said, after a while.

He did not say anything.

"In the Band. You felt it. I felt you feel it." She turned the empty cup in her hands. "I have been a mage since I was a girl. Nobody in my House knows. My father doesn't know. My brothers don't know. I have carried it all my life in a body that was told every day of its life to hold a ledger and nothing else, and I have never once in all that time been full." She looked at the water. "Not once. You can't, at the bottom of the world. There isn't enough. You take a little in and you spend it on nothing, a lamp in the night when nobody's looking, a draught in a cellar, and you're never more than half. I thought that was what it was. I thought that was what everybody felt like." She was quiet. "And then the floor came, and I was full in the time it takes to drink a cup of tea, and I didn't know there was that much of me."

"Vesk said the same," said Emeric.

"I heard him." Vantic almost smiled. "I was very glad somebody else said it, so I didn't have to."

The ships' lamps moved on the water.

"I can't tell anybody," said Vantic. "You know that. Not my father. Not the House. I'll go back to Kaldo and write my month's letter to the House, and the House will read that the voyage to Emberfell was uneventful and the harbor dues are high and the Holder offered the Sovereign grain, and whatever she says to it, and that will be true, all of it." She set the cup on the rail. "That's the House's whole life. In a sentence. Everything true and nothing said."

"You could stop," said Emeric.

"I could." She looked at him for the first time. "I won't. It's a good House. It's mine. It was there before I was and it will be there after." She looked back at the harbor. "And it's useful to you to have somebody in it who knows what she's not writing down."

He did not argue. It was true.

She nodded. She stood a while longer at the rail with the cup, looking at the inlet going away into the dark, as if she could see down the length of it to the sea and the coast and the long road home to the House, where an old man sat at a desk reading letters that said nothing.

"Go to bed, Highness," she said at last. "She'll have you on your back in the sand at dawn, and Ward will enjoy it, and I'd like to see that, and I'd like you to be awake for it." She went past him along the balcony to her door, and went in.

He stood on the balcony a while. Then he went to bed.
