Chapter 318 - By the Cartridge

The first lot of the new rifles jammed on the second of November, at the butts behind the cart shed, on the fourth round, every one.

The self-loading rifle was Emeric's name for it, from the drawing; the yard had shortened it to the self-loader inside a day. There were forty of them in the first lot, out of the three hundred Bram's floor had promised by the end of the month, and they lay on the trestle by the butts in a row on their sacking with their magazines beside them, and they were the best thing that Emeric had ever seen come off a bench in Kaldo, and he could not look at them for long. They were short. That was the first thing anybody noticed. Half the length of the long bolt rifles the knights had carried since the spring, with a curved box under the action that held twenty rounds, and a wooden stock, and a grip; and the hard parts in them, the bolt and its carrier and the trigger's little world of springs and catches, had come out of the Forge, because nothing else in Kaldo could make a part that size to that exactness three hundred times; and everything else in them had come off Bram's floor on gauges, which was harder, and slower, and was the part that would still be there if the Forge were ever gone.

A man pulled the trigger and one went. He pulled it again and another went. He did not touch the bolt. The bolt did that itself, off the gas of the round before, back and forward, faster than a man could see, throwing the spent case out of the side and stripping the next round up out of the box and into the breech; so that a man with one of these in his hands could fire twenty rounds in the time it took a man with a bolt rifle to fire four.

Ward had fired the first one himself, on the first of November, at the butts, five rounds into a straw man at a hundred yards, as fast as he could pull; and then lowered it and stood looking at the straw man without a word, while the smoke that was not smoke, the faint blue of Fennick's smokeless, drifted away off the end of the barrel.

On the second, they all jammed.

Not at once. Each one fired three, as sweet as Ward's had, and on the fourth round the bolt came forward and stopped short with a round half in the breech and half out, nose up, cocked against the barrel's lip like a man who has tripped on a stair; and the rifle would not fire, and would not clear, until somebody put a thumb in and pulled the round out by hand. Forty rifles. The fourth round. Every one.

Bram's people took them apart on the trestle. They rewound the magazine springs, in case it was the springs, and tried again on the third; and on the third they all jammed again, on the fourth round, every one.

Emeric stood at the end of the trestle and watched Bram's fitters take them apart the second time, and found, to his own great surprise, that he was glad.

He did not know he was glad until he noticed that he was not trying to work out why they jammed. He could have. He could see the magazine in his head, the box and the spring and the follower and the two little curled lips at the top that held the rounds down until the bolt stripped them, and he knew what the fault was likely to be, as he would have known it on any machine; and he was standing at the end of the trestle not thinking about it, with his hands in his pockets, and the thing he was thinking instead was that forty rifles that jammed on the fourth round were forty rifles that would not go north.

He had felt it in August. Saban's man's drawing on the back of a smith's price list, a rolled barrel with the seam drawn in, and his hand pressing flat on the table beside it. That had been a bad gun, a copy of a copy, that would burst in a boy's hands on the fifth shot; and he had felt the whole of it in his hand on the table, that it was his and was not his and would go on being what it was in anybody's hands. This was the other thing. This was a good gun. This was the best gun anybody in the world had ever made, by eighty years at least, and it was his, every line of it, drawn in this town on his own bench; and when it worked, it would work for whoever held it, and go on working, twenty rounds at a pull, long after he had stopped being in any room where anybody could ask him whether it should.

And for two days it had not worked, and he had been glad.

He took his hands out of his pockets.

"Ottoline," he said.

Ottoline had been at the butts all morning, at the far end of the trestle, on a stool, with her case of gauges open on her knee and her sleeves rolled up, not saying anything. She had made every gauge Bram's floor used for the new rifles, out of the Forge's masters, and she had come down to watch them fail because she wanted to know whether it was her fault.

"I know," she said. "I've been doing it."

She had been. She had the magazines off the trestle in a row in front of her, forty of them, and she was putting a gauge to the lips of each one, a little flat steel plate with a slot in it that should have slid over the lips with a hair to spare; and she had a pencil behind her ear and a card on her knee, and on the card two columns.

"Nineteen go," she said. "Twenty-one don't. The twenty-one don't by a hair. The lips are pinched. A hair at each lip, inward; so the top round's held a hair too low, so the bolt catches it a hair too low, so it noses up." She held up one of the twenty-one, and slid the gauge at it, and it stopped. "And the nineteen that go, jam anyway, on the fourth, because the round under the top one in a pinched box comes up wrong too when the spring's pushing four up behind it." She put it down. "All twenty-one were pressed on the number two press. The number one press is right. Number two's die is worn, or set wrong, or both. It's taking a hair off the lips on the closing stroke."

Bram looked at the card. He did not say anything. He went off to the works at a walk that became something faster than a walk at the gate, and by noon the number two press was in pieces on the floor, and by evening it had a new die off the Forge, gauged by Ottoline's own hand twice; and on the fifth of November the first lot fired twenty rounds a magazine, every one, a hundred magazines, without a jam.

Emeric was at the butts. He watched them do it. He stood there through all of it, until the last magazine was empty and the last straw man had nothing left above its waist, and did not put his hands in his pockets once.



The cartridges were the war. The rifles were not.

That was Ferra's line, and she said it at the long table on the sixth, and then wrote it down, and then read it out again so that nobody could pretend afterward not to have heard. Three hundred rifles were three hundred rifles. Bram's floor would have them all by the end of the month, and every one would fire as long as anybody wanted to pull its trigger. But every pull was a round; every round was a drawn brass case and a primer and a pinch of Fennick's smokeless and a bullet, and every one of those had to be made by hand by somebody in a shop, and the shops could make only so many a day; and a man with a rifle that fired twenty in a breath would fire twenty in a breath, every time, because that was what it was for, and that was what men did.

"So we count them," said Ferra.

Ottoline's tabulator counted them. Every round off the loading benches went into a box of fifty with a card on it, and every card went through the tabulator at the end of the day, and every morning Ferra had a number on her desk: rounds made, rounds in store, rounds issued, by company, by man. The issue was by the cartridge and not by the rifle. A man who had a rifle had what the card said he had and no more, and when it was gone he came back to the store with his empty magazines and his card and said where they had gone.

"Like a ration," said Ward.

"It is a ration," said Ferra.

And at night, at her table, she read him the count.

It started as work. He had come up at midnight from the airship yard on the seventh with the yard's day still on him, to look at the next day's figures before he slept, and she had them, and she read them to him from her chair while he sat in the other one by the dead grate with his boots off; the rounds made that day, by shop, by bench, by the hour. And she read them in her flat square voice, as she read any column, a number and a number and a number; and somewhere in the second thousand he found that he had his eyes shut, and that her voice reading numbers in the lamplight was the most restful sound he had heard in a month, and that he was going to sleep in the chair and could not do anything at all about it.

He woke at two with a blanket over him. She was still reading. Not to him; she was reading the count to herself, under her breath, in the same voice, as she went down the column checking it, a number and a number; and when she saw his eyes open she did not stop. She went on to the end of the page, every figure, in the same voice, looking at him over the book while she read it, until the last number at the foot of the page, and then she shut the book.

"Two thousand and eleven," she said. "Today. Bench four's slow. I'll speak to them." She put the book on the table. "You went at the second thousand. I kept going. I thought you might be listening."

"I was listening."

"You were snoring."

"I was listening while I snored."

Ferra looked at him in the lamplight for a while, with her hair coming out of its pin at the nape where it always came out by midnight, and her face doing the thing he could not quite read, the thing from the map room in September.

"I'll read it to you every night, if you like," she said. "Till you go. Then I'll read it to you over the set." She stood up. "Imani says she wants to do the hospital's stores the same way. She's worked out that it's what makes you sleep." She held out her hand to him in the chair. "She says it's cheaper than anything she's got in a bottle."



The cartridge train ran into the dead zone on the ninth, at the foot of Fallow Bank, at four in the afternoon, with sixty thousand rounds in four closed wagons behind the engine, bound for the depot at Wayford where Ward's northern companies had their stores.

Nobody was hurt. That was the first thing the driver said when he got to a telegraph, and the first thing the line room took down, and the first thing Ferra read out at the table that night; and it was true, and Emeric held onto it for the rest of the evening.

Fallow Bank was the long grade where the Wayford line came up off the river flats and climbed into the ridge country on Lord Lisle's side of the border; the one ridge on the line too broad to cut, that the crews had named for the plough-land along its foot, and that every heavy train on the line had climbed for three years with a banking engine at its back, or with the stones in its bogies. That was the layer. Every wagon on Kaldo's lines rode on a bogie with a gravity stone in it set to lighten, a little, on the grades and the curves and nowhere else, so that a train that would have needed a banker up Fallow Bank could go up it on its own, and get to Wayford an hour sooner. It was a good layer. It saved coal. And the rule under it, the rule that every rule in Kaldo had under it, was that the train could go up without it. Slower. Heavier. With the sand on and a banking engine whistled up from the siding at the foot. But up.

The cartridge train came onto the foot of the grade at four, in the layer, lightened, at a good pace; and crossed the culvert over the brook at the bottom; and somebody under the culvert had put a stone.

The driver felt it as the train felt it: all at once, the whole weight of four loaded wagons coming back onto the engine through the couplings as if somebody had hooked a hand into the back of the last one and pulled. The bogies were dead. Every stone in every wagon had gone out at once, at the same breath, and there was no wind to feel and nothing to see and the engine was dragging four wagons of brass and lead and powder up the first of the grade at their full weight, on rails that had been laid for a train that weighed two wagons less.

He did what the book said.

Ward's book, that every crew on Kaldo's lines had been made to learn in the winter of the first through train, and say back, at the shed, with their eyes shut: if she goes heavy on a grade, sand on, throttle off, whistle for the banker. Not throttle up; not a driver's pride, not trying to take her over by main force and slipping the wheels and losing the grade and running back down it with four wagons of powder behind. Sand on, so the wheels would hold where they were. Throttle off, so they would not spin. Brakes on, all down the train. And the whistle, the long two and the short one, that every banking crew on the line knew meant come.

The banker came. It was in the siding at the foot of the bank where it always was, with its fire banked, because Kaldo's lines had never once taken a banker off a bank since the layer went in, though Ferra had asked twice what it cost to keep it there; and its crew heard the whistle and had steam up in twenty minutes and came up behind the cartridge train and buffered up to the last wagon and pushed. And the two engines took the four wagons up Fallow Bank at a walk, at their full dead weight, a mile and a half, with the sand going down under every wheel and the smoke going straight up into the still November air; and at the top, past the reach of whatever had been put under the culvert, the bogies came back on, one wagon at a time, as the trains passed out of the dead ground, and the driver felt her go light again under him like a horse that has had its pack taken off.

They reached Wayford at half past three in the morning. Half a day late.

"Somebody overloaded a stone," said Ward, at the table, that night. "Under the culvert. Forced it till it went. Knew the time the train was due and where the layer was and that a dead zone would take it."

"Knew the book?" said Imani.

"Didn't know the book." Ward looked at the telegraph slip. "Or didn't think we kept it. Thought she'd go heavy and slip and run back down onto the flats with sixty thousand rounds behind her. That's what he wanted." He put the slip down. "She'd have gone off the rails at the curve at the foot. Into the brook."

Nobody said anything for a while.

Saban's people found the stone by morning, or what was left of it. It was dust, in a little grey drift under the culvert's arch where the brook ran, a stone that had been forced until it emptied and fell to nothing; and beside it, in the mud of the brook's edge, the prints of two men's boots, and a third set of a horse's, going off north toward Lisle's woods. And in the dust, when Saban's man sieved it, the cord: a twist of the dark waxed cord that the Lieutenant's men in the capital tied their dispatch packets with, a hand's length of it, with a knot in one end.

"They tied it to the stone to let it down," said Saban, at the table, holding the cord up. "Into the culvert. Off the parapet. So they wouldn't have to climb down." He put it on the table. "They left the cord. Everybody leaves something."

"Corbin's," said Ferra.

"Somebody who gets his cord from Corbin's stores. Which is a great many men." Saban turned his hat. "But not a great many on Lisle's side of the Wayford line, with a forcing stone and a timetable. I'll have a name by the end of the month." He looked at Ward. "He'll not have been told about the book either, I expect."

"Nobody outside the four baronies knows the book," said Ward. "Nobody outside the four baronies thinks we'd write down what to do when the magic goes." He stood up. "That's what it's for."



On the tenth Emeric doubled the escorts' shell order.

He did it at Ferra's table, over her column, in pencil, while she watched him: the line that said what the four escorts would carry for their twin turrets, in rounds, which Ferra had worked out in October from what Ward thought an escort would fire over the capital and what Fletcher thought she could hold; and he put a line through the figure and wrote twice the figure beside it.

Ferra looked at it.

"What for?" she said.

"The yard at Ostrel."

She looked at the line in her book where roof had been, with its one straight line through it; and at the doubled figure; and at him.

"You think they'll fly," she said.

"I think they've been building for nine years and hurrying since the spring." He put the pencil down. "I think they'll fly when we fly. I'd rather have the rounds and not need them."

Ferra nodded slowly. She took the pencil back. She did not say anything else about it; she went down her column to the powder line, which was Fennick's, two lines below the shells, and put a line through Fennick's figure, and wrote twice that beside it too, without being asked, without looking up.

"Shells without powder are paperweights," she said. "Go away. I've got to find the money for both."



Kaldo plate went out to the companies on the twelfth, and Ward rated it the hard way.

It was dust, layered: the island's mill's sorted dust, poured and pressed and fired in Bram's kilns into plates the size of a man's hand and the thickness of a finger, and laid up on a canvas backing in overlapping rows like the scales on a fish, and sewn into a coat. Each plate was four things, a layer of each. A face of shard dust, outermost, to slow what hit it over the last inch. A core of gravity dust behind it, keyed to push outward from the body, hard, so that a blow that got through the face met a push going the other way. A skin of fire enamel on the back, so that a man in a winter coat of it would not freeze to death inside it at height. And through all of them, round the coat's hem and down its seams, an earthing strap of copper braid, so that if a man in Kaldo plate were ever hit by a grey mage's lightning, the lightning would go through the braid and into the ground or the deck and not through him.

Each plate had a keystone, set in a dimple in one corner, and every keystone in a coat was set by a delver, with the wearer's hand on the coat, to one instruction and one man.

Ward pulled them.

He did it at the butts behind the cart shed, with a coat on a post and the company it was for standing in a row to watch: he went over the coat with a pair of pliers and pulled every keystone out of every plate, slowly, one at a time, and dropped them in a tin, until there was not one left and the coat was what it would be in a dead zone, or after a grey mage had broken a stone near it, or in the middle of a capital that had been made a stream ground on purpose: dust in a kiln, nothing more. Fired dust. Clay, very nearly, with a copper braid in it.

Then he fired at it.

He fired the old bolt rifle at it, at fifty yards, five rounds; and walked up to the post with the company behind him and looked at what the rounds had done. Two of them had gone through. Three had not. The three were in the plates, deep, flattened, the plates round them starred and cracked, but in them; and behind them, in the straw man on the post, nothing.

"That's dead," said Ward, to the company. "That's what it does dead. Three in five." He put the keystones back, one at a time, out of the tin, while they watched, every one of them into its dimple. Then he fired five more. None of them went through. One of them did not even crack the face; it hit, and slowed over the last inch, and dropped onto the ground at the foot of the post as if it had been thrown underhand, and lay there.

"That's what it does alive," said Ward. "Five in five." He turned round. "You'll wear it as if it's dead. Every day. You'll stand behind things as if it's dead. And when it's alive, you'll thank God for it, and you'll still stand behind things." He handed the rifle to the company's sergeant. "Issue it."

The MK3s had theirs that week too. Not plate; the stealth suits were wool, black, for moving without a sound, and plate clattered. Abloh's people sewed shard-dust panels into them instead, inside, between the wool and the lining, over the chest and the back and the belly, thin, flexible, keyed: not enough to stop a crossbow bolt at close range, Ward said, testing it, but enough to slow one so that it went into a man and stopped, instead of going through him. Arthur looked at the test bolt where it had gone into the straw man behind the panel and stopped, three inches in, and then at Ward.

"Wounds," said Ward. "Not kills. That's the difference. A man who's wounded can be carried home."

And the canister sleeves.

Every flask of liquid mana the fleet would carry, and it would carry a great many, went into a double case of steel, an inner and an outer, because Bram's rule was that the case must be stronger than the thing it held; and a flask of liquid mana, cracked, in a hold, in the air, would come out of its flask and become a cloud of mana hundreds of times its size in a breath, and blow out whatever was round it. So the outer case of every one had been catch-plated, inside, by Tess's tanks, with a skin of shard dust: so that a ball or a splinter that hit the outer case would slow over the last inch, and the inner case would have a chance.

Emeric stood in the K Endeavor's hold on the thirteenth and watched the plated sleeves go into their racks, in rows, with Kaldo plate stacked beside them in bales for the shore teams, and thought about the golem's armour on South Sentinel.

The old races had done this. He had read it in the armour in the summer, in the plates Bram had brought back from the island's long hall on a handcart: dust, keyed in seams, holding mana in, not out, so that a golem's store did not bleed away. Sealed stores. They had kept their mana behind plated walls the way Kaldo was keeping its flasks behind plated sleeves. He had not invented it. Tess had not invented it. They had found it in the island's dark, in the armour of a thing that had been made by people who had known how to do it long before anybody in Kaldo was born; and Kaldo had taken it, and made it smaller, and put it round a flask.

It was a strange feeling, and he had it more and more that autumn. That they were not going forward. That they were going back to somewhere.



Bram and Tess built the cloud seeder in five days, at the K Amara's condenser outlet, from one sentence.

The sentence was Emeric's. He said it at the long table on the twelfth, when Ward asked how a fleet of ten black ships was going to hang over a city in daylight without every soul in it seeing exactly where to aim; and it was the only thing he said about it, then or after.

"A cloud forms on salt," he said. "Put enough salt in wet air and you make one."

That was all. He did not draw anything. He sat back and let them look at him, and let them look at each other, and Bram's eyes went to Tess's, and Tess's went to the K Amara's drawings on the wall, to the condenser at her stern that took the water out of the Band air for her crew and dumped what it did not want overboard as a fine wet mist; and Emeric watched her see it.

She saw it in a breath. Bram took longer, and took it further. By the seventeenth there was a thing on the K Amara's stern that had not been there before: a hopper, a long iron box behind the condenser's outlet, full of salt ground finer than flour, with a feed screw in its throat that blew the salt into the condenser's mist as it went out; and beside it, a small sealed tank of liquid mana on a needle valve, that put a thin breath of it into the mist with the salt, so thin that it would not do anything at all to anybody, except that it would blur, a little, a sense put out into it, like breath on a pane.

They tried it on the eighteenth, off the island, over the sea, on a grey wet morning. The K Amara hung at a thousand feet in the wet air with her seeder going, and in a quarter of an hour she was in a cloud. Her own. It came off her stern like a slow white smoke and spread, and thickened, and wrapped her, until from the K Discovery a mile off she was a long soft white shape in the grey, and then not even that; and Emeric, on the K Discovery's deck, put his sense out toward where she was and felt her, faintly, as if through a wet wool blanket, and lost her, and found her again.

"Salt," said Ferra, when they came home, at the table. She had the seeder's figures in front of her. "She'll want two tons a day of it, running." She looked up. "Salt's cheap. It's the only thing in this whole war that's cheap. I want that written down somewhere, so I can look at it."

"It won't work everywhere," said Emeric.

They looked at him.

"Dry air," he said. "If there's no water in the air, there's nothing to make a cloud out of, and all you'll do is put salt on the city." He said it plainly, because it was the thing they had to know. "It works in the wet. Over the sea, in a fog, in a low sky. It won't work on a clear day inland in a frost. It'll make a little haze and blow away."

"The capital in November," said Ward slowly, "sits in the river's fog every morning till ten."

"Then it'll work in the capital in November before ten."

Bram had said nothing all evening about the seeder, which had been mostly his. He was sitting with his arms folded and a frown, and at last he unfolded them and put his finger on the K Amara's drawing on the table, on the binnacle in front of the helm on her tower.

"In a cloud," he said, "you can't see where you're going."

"No."

"So you steer by the compass."

"Yes."

"Three hundred men on her deck," said Bram, "in three hundred pairs of iron boots with a magnet in the heel of each one, clicking on and off every step." He took his finger off the binnacle. "Tess's boots. I tried it this morning. I stood her helmsman's compass on the deck and walked past it in a pair, and it followed me round like a dog."

There was a silence. Tess, at the end of the table, put her hand over her eyes.

"Move the compass," said Emeric. "Up the mast. Away from the deck. Box it, in brass, with a mirror down to the helm. And swing it, with the crew aboard and booted, so you know what they do to it, and write the error on a card by the wheel."

"That's a day's work," said Bram.

"Then it's a day."

"I'm only saying," said Bram, with great dignity, "that every time anybody in this town fixes anything, it breaks something else," and folded his arms again, and Tess took her hand off her eyes and was laughing.

And the held stones, which were Fletcher's and had been since the K Amara was laid down: a second set of gravity stones in steadied frames behind her air louvres, anchored, that could push her sideways at half the strength of her stones' up and down, silently, without turning a single blade. She had had them from the keel. Now every hull in the fleet had them, or would by the end of the month; because in a cloud, Fletcher said, a ship that turned her propellers churned the cloud round her into a hole with herself in the middle of it, and a ship that hung on held stones and crept, sideways, at half push, between the volleys, kept her cloud round her like a coat.



The three hundredth flight suit came off Bram's floor on the seventeenth of November, at a quarter past six in the evening, and Ferra closed the line.

She was there to do it. She had been at the end of Bram's floor every evening that week with her book, because Bram had said the hundred and fortieth in September, and fifteen a week since, which came to three hundred at the end of November; and Bram had beaten his own figure by a fortnight, with the yard's men off the towers, and she wanted to see it. She stood at the foot of the last rack with her book open, beside the girls at the node benches, and watched the three hundredth come off the fitting stand: matte, grey-black, with its forty nodes dark under their violet glass and its two hearts in their housings, breast and back, and its flat pack, and its helmet on the hook above it; and Bram's fitter checked the last strap, and stepped back, and looked at Bram.

"That's it," said Bram.

Ferra wrote it. Three hundred. The date. Then she ruled a line across the page under it, very straight, with the ruler, and wrote under the line, in the same hand: Closed.

The girls at the node benches clapped. Somebody at the back of the floor banged a spanner on a rack, and then everybody was banging something; and Bram stood at the end of his floor with his arms folded, looking at three hundred suits hanging in their rows down the whole length of it like a company of men standing in the dark, and kept his mouth shut. Emeric, by the door, felt his note. It was the note of a man who has done the thing he said he would do, on the day he said, and is too tired to be pleased about it until tomorrow.

The suits had been the bottleneck all autumn. Not the men. There had been three hundred knights in September; there had been a hundred and forty suits. Every knight Ward had sent to the island had flown in a suit that had been flown in by somebody else the day before and would be flown in by somebody else the day after, and Imani had complained about the smell. Now there was one each.

"Go home," said Bram, to his floor. "All of you. Tomorrow off. Anybody I see in here before Monday I'll put on the chain."



Three things in passing, that month, that nobody at the table remarked on and Emeric noticed.

The first was the line. The grid's line along the rails had reached Wayford in October, poles and copper all the way up the line beside the track from Kaldo through Milford, and over Fallow Bank, and into Wayford's yard, and on the twelfth of November Wayford's main street was lit for the first time from end to end with Kaldo's lamps on Kaldo's poles: forty lamps, in two rows, from the market cross where Mother Tennick had cut down the proclamation to the station. And on the fifteenth, Saban said, a column of Lord Aldane's men had gone up the capital road through Wayford at dusk, north, a company of them, mounted, in Aldane's colours, going to join the Lieutenant's army; and had come down the main street between the two rows of lamps, under the lights, at a walk, and every man in the column had looked up.

The second was the Assembly. It had argued Ferra's rule for the yellow cards for four weeks, as she had said it would: that a visitor with a card in date got the same hearing as a man on the register, and could not be carried off or held without an open court. On the eleventh of November a lighterman from ward four had stood up at the back of the hall and said that if he were ever in Ostrel with a card in his pocket he would want the same; and it had passed that night, by a long way, with Emeric nowhere near the hall, as Ferra had arranged.

The third was Abloh's trousers.

The clothing works had been making a thing since the summer that it called joggers, after Emeric, who had drawn them for the knights to train in and called them that without thinking: soft trousers of knitted cotton, loose in the leg, with a band of the Isle's rubber thread knitted into the waist and the ankles so that they held without a belt. Abloh had refused to sew them at first. He had made sails for twenty years before he made anything else, and he said that a garment held up by stretch was a garment that had given up; and he had made the knights' pairs under protest, and said so to anybody who would listen.

By November half the lower ward was wearing them. Not to train in. To work in, to sleep in, to sit by the fire in, the dye works and the mill and the tram sheds and the kitchens; and the clothing works could not make them fast enough, and the elastic thread off the Isle sap was the thing Ferra had to find more of after the salt.

Emeric saw Abloh in the square on the eighteenth, crossing to the records hall, in a pair.

Abloh saw him see. He stopped in the middle of the square. He looked down at his own legs, in grey knitted cotton held up by stretch, and then at Emeric, with great dignity, with the expression of a man who has been found out in a thing he has denied in public for six months.

"My knees," said Abloh. "In the winter. They are better in these."

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to," said Abloh, and walked on.



And on the nineteenth of November, at night, from the secret dock, the Well went up.

Not all of it. The Well as Bram had drawn it, as Ferra had it in her tally for the far side of the war, was a station of three floors and two wings and fifty souls, hanging over the sea four miles up with ships coming and going at its dock like boats at a quay. That was years off. What went up on the nineteenth was the first floor of it: the platform, the deep sealed hull with its flat top that Fletcher's men had built round the column on the hard; and on top of the platform the house, one floor of it, plated and sealed, with its airlock and its suit room and its berths for twenty-four, and the engine room and the board; and under it, through the middle of it, the spire, the column and the compressors and the heat exchangers and the band engines Tess had built for them, and below all of it, hanging, the tank, and the ballast at the very tip. The dock arm on one side, folded. The fill boom beside it. The stone banks in their pods underneath, four of them, each separate, each able to hold the whole thing up by itself if the other three went; and at the corners of the house, four light guns on rail mounts, because Ward had said so, and Fletcher had not argued.

And in the keel of the platform, in a padded cradle in a sealed steel box, the stone from the cove. Tess's stone. She had finished it on the fifteenth, at four in the morning, at the secret dock, in the pit under the platform, with the lamp, and come up out of the delve and sat in her chair with her hands over her face for a long time; and then she had gone home and slept for a day and a half and nobody had woken her. The register: every soul in the first crew and the two knights and the six and the K Endeavor's people who would come to fill, so that the shell would know them. The lattice, faceted round the whole of the platform's hull and the house on it. And the pin, at the stern, the anchor facet: so that the Well would not drift in the Band's winds on its engines, burning fuel to hold its place, but hang there, pinned, on the still mana of the sky, the way the K Amara had hung over the sea and fired.

All of it in one hand. Tess's, from end to end.

The crew went aboard at ten. Nineteen of them, in their Band suits, with their kit, and the two knights, Tamsin Reeve and Lucan Ashby, in their flight suits; and Orla Garran last, up the ladder from the hard, without looking back. Emeric watched them go up. Imani had read every one of them that afternoon, their numbers and their rooms, on the scanner, and written them on their cards, and set the station's cabin mark in its brass bypass herself, for the weakest aboard, and locked it with her key; and she stood beside him on the hard now with her arms folded and watched them go up the ladder one by one, and counted them as they went, under her breath.

Tess sat in her chair by the slip, with her stick across her knees.

The Well lifted at a quarter to eleven.

Emeric was on the K Discovery, a quarter of a mile off over the lake, with Fletcher at her helm; he wanted to see it from outside. He saw it. The platform came up off the hard on its four banks, slowly, level, with the house lit on its top and the spire going down under it into the dark, and the tank coming up last out of its brick pit, swinging a little on the end of the spire, and then steady; and the whole of it rose, quite silently, out of the reeds and the black water of the lake's far end, a lit house hanging on a long iron stalk in the night, going up.

It went up for an hour. The K Discovery went up beside it. Two miles. Three, with the cold coming and the air going thin and the stars coming out hard and close above the cloud; and then the Band, the thick sweet mana of it closing round them both like warm water, and four miles, and Orla Garran's voice on the K Discovery's set, low and rough, in the line room's cipher, saying the station was at its height.

"Pin," said Emeric, into the mouthpiece.

He felt it go. He was a quarter of a mile off, and he felt it: the thump, low and long, the great door shut in the great house, going out from the station's stern into the Band, and the Well stopping, all at once, the way the K Amara had stopped over the sea, as if somebody had driven a nail through it into the sky.

And then Hold.

That was the station's own, and Emeric had never seen it work except on a bench. A control stone in the board room, the station's one, out of the year's crop: the decider, that Tess had written to read the pin's load and the wind on the station's flanks, every moment, and trim the air louvres on the spire and the held stones behind them to keep the station on its mark, so that the pin did not have to fight the wind alone and spend itself. The deck watch could switch it on. The deck watch could switch it off. Only the captain could move the station.

Orla Garran switched it on.

A gust came across the Band from the west, a hard one; Emeric felt it hit the K Discovery and Fletcher take it on the wheel. It hit the Well a breath later. And the Well did not move. He watched its louvres on the spire turn, all together, a fraction, like the gills of a fish, and the held stones behind them take the gust; and on the set Orla Garran's voice came again, a little rougher than before.

"Felt that," she said. "In the floor. A shiver." A pause. "That's all."

Then, after a longer pause, in a different voice, low, not in cipher, not to anybody:

"There's a brass plate on the wall of my engine room," she said. "Over the board. Somebody put it there that I didn't ask to." Another pause. "What must not stop goes on what cannot stop." She cleared her throat. "Station's on Hold. Station's breathing. The plant's cold. We'll light it in the morning." And then, in cipher again, the number, the station's first, the reading off its board: tank empty, air good, the house warm, twenty-two souls.

Emeric stood on the K Discovery's deck four miles up in the Band, in the dark, with the Well hanging a quarter of a mile off in the starlight like a lantern on a nail, and looked at it for a long time.

The Church did not know. Nobody in the Church, or in the capital, or in the tower, looked up at the Band. Why would they? Nobody but the Church had ever been able to get up there, until Kaldo, and the Church did not know that Kaldo could. If it ever found out, the station would move; it could move, slowly, on its louvres, a house that crept across the sky. But for now it hung, over the Mere, four miles up, where nobody would think to look; and in the morning it would light its plant, and start to make what the fleet would need on the way north, out of the sky itself.

Fletcher, at the helm, had been quiet since the pin.

"It's a house," he said at last. "In the sky. With a woman in it who's going to put the kettle on." He shook his head slowly. "Forty years I've built boats. I've built them for the river and the sea and the Band. I never once built one with a kettle in it that stays where you leave it."

"You didn't build that one."

"No. I built the box it sits on." Fletcher put the K Discovery's head round, gently, for home. "I'll be telling people I built it by spring."
