Chapter 39 - The Break

He had two stones left and he took one of them out of the satchel.

It took about four seconds to work and it was the only genuine decision he made all day.

The zone over the lane was going to die in a few minutes. He knew roughly how long it lasted because he had timed it three times on empty ground, and that number, at least, had been right. When it died, Renner was reachable again, and so were the two grey-robes still standing behind him, and there were two grey-robes still standing behind him.

So: throw the second one now, onto the same ground, and stack a new zone under the old one before it lapsed. Renner stays unreachable but so do the mages, and the mages were the thing that had nearly taken the flank off the hill.

And it would roll over his own line again. He knew that now.

He did not know how far. That was the whole problem and he turned it over and could not get round it. The number in his book was wrong by an amount he could not calculate standing on a hill with people screaming below him, and the only measurement he had was the one he had just taken by accident, which was that it had gone over Declan at a distance he had believed was safe.

Ward was in the lane. Osric was in the lane. Rylan was at the mouth of it with eleven men. Three sets of high-grade crystal in a barony that had four and no way on earth to get a fifth.

Bram's breakers would fire. Probably. They had fired once, on one suit, under conditions nobody had tested for.

He put the stone back in the satchel.

Ten seconds later he took it out again, because the front had come forward another six feet and the arithmetic had changed, and then he put it back a second time and left his hand on the satchel for a while and then took his hand off the satchel.

It was the right decision. He was fairly confident of that within the hour and entirely confident of it within a week.

It felt like nothing at all. It felt like standing on a hill not doing anything, which is exactly what it looked like from six feet away, and nobody who saw him do it knew that anything had happened.

He was looking at the wrong part of the field when the war ended.

There was a crew on the left of the flank that had stopped shooting, and he was watching them and trying to work out whether they had stopped because they were out of bolts or because they were finished, and the answer mattered, and he spent perhaps forty seconds on it.

Osric told him afterward that the distant figure had gone sideways off the horse rather than backward, which is apparently how you can tell.

What Emeric saw, when he turned back, was a horse with nobody on it and a knot of men doing something at ground level, three hundred yards away, in the middle of a field with two hundred people in it.

There was no sound. There was no flash. There was nothing to learn.

He watched it for some seconds without understanding any part of it and then went back to the crew on the left, who had in fact run out of bolts and who got a runner sent to Abloh inside the minute.

Somebody told him about a quarter of an hour later.

He did not know who had fired it until that evening and he had assumed, without thinking about it, that it had been Ward or one of the four.

It had been Enno, who was nineteen, who had been the worst man in the garrison two years earlier by a distance that had been a joke in the yard, and who had been put in the gate tower with the air-crystal rifle for the specific reason that it was a slow weapon fired from a fixed position and required patience rather than anything he was bad at.

He had fired it four times all morning. The first three had hit nothing. He called out the range off the marked stake at the bridge foot the way he had been taught to, and he had not moved from the tower, and afterward he did not understand why people kept coming up to look at him.

Emeric heard the name and it did not mean very much at the time and meant a great deal more about four days later.

Grayrock broke first and broke hardest.

Renner had put nearly everything he had into that lane, personally, at the front, in his own colours, and there had been no second decision available to anybody once he was down, because he had not left anybody the authority to make one.

Sixty men went into that ground. Most of them did not come out of it.

Wayford filled in and then did not.

Sethe's forty came forward on the right in reasonable order, into ground that was already full of dead men, and discovered the thing that nobody outside the lane had been able to see all morning, which was that the lane did not widen.

They stopped.

Nobody announced it. No horn went. No officer made a decision and no officer was asked to. Forty men individually and privately declined to be next, over the course of perhaps ninety seconds, and the formation was simply not there any more, and that is what a rout is when you are standing close enough to watch one.

Milford never moved at all.

Paxton sat at the back of the road with a hundred men and watched the front of the coalition dissolve. He watched Renner go down, at three hundred yards, from a tower, by something nobody could see. He understood inside a minute that everything he had been told about the southern barony was eighteen months stale and that the man who had told him it was lying face down in a wheat field.

He pulled off the road with his strength whole and went home.

A hundred men walked away from a battle without having been in it, and fifty-odd of those men were drovers and herdsmen who had been on open pasture the week before, and they told the story for thirty years, and the story got better.

Nobody in Kaldo cheered, because there was no moment to cheer at.

It ended in pieces, over a long time, untidily. There was no point at which anybody declared anything. Men were still being pulled out of the ditch an hour after the last bolt went out.

The first indication Emeric had that it was over was that the noise had changed, and he could not say when, and then a boy came up the back of the flank asking whether there was water anywhere because the crews had drunk all of theirs, and that was it. That was the end of the war. Somebody asking about water.

The cost to Kaldo made no sense and everybody said so.

There were heavy wounded and there were a great many of them. There were burns off the mages that Imani would be dressing for six weeks. There were crush injuries and broken forearms and one man with a shoulder nobody could put back for two days.

The harnessed men came out of it beaten rather than cut. That is what good plate does. It does not make a blow disappear, it converts it into something else, and what it converts it into is bruising and cracked ribs and a man who cannot lift his arm above his head for a month.

The volunteers were the ones who bled, and the volunteers were on the hill.

And nobody died.

Not one. Not on the flank, not in the lane, not in the ditch, not that night and not in the six weeks after, and Ferra said it out loud in the small room two days later with the list in front of her in her own handwriting and then said she did not believe it and read the list again.

Emeric said the same thing, separately, on the wall, to nobody, and had the same reaction to it.

Ward was the only person who declined to be amazed.

"It was the ground," he said. "It was the ground and it was ten minutes and it was that they never got onto the hill. If any one of those three had gone the other way you would be burying people for a week."

"I know."

"Then do not let anybody in this town start believing it means something about them," Ward said. "Because it will not happen twice and they will not know that."

Hollis took a bad arm and kept it.

Something came off the bank and took him across the forearm and he finished the morning anyway with Marit's crew and did not mention it until the crews came down. It never worked properly again. He was in the fields the following spring with it, doing most of what he had done before and doing it slower, and he never once raised it with anybody in any context.

The count took two days and Ferra did it properly.

Sixty-one dead in the lane and on the ground around it.

Twenty-seven wounded carried up into a town that had nowhere to put them.

Twelve unhurt prisoners, one of whom was the Baron of Wayford.

Two mages alive.

Emeric stood on ground that he had personally arranged and did the arithmetic he had been avoiding since the middle of the morning.

Two hundred had come. Roughly a hundred had actually fought, because Milford's hundred had never left the road and Wayford's forty had stopped after ninety seconds. Forty-four harnessed men and a hundred and thirty people with crossbows had fought a hundred men in a ditch of their own making.

He had been calling it five to one for a month. He had said it to Ward on the bank and written it in the book twice and worked every decision off it.

It had been closer to two to one at the point of contact and he had never once considered that not all of them would fight, which was the single most obvious thing about a coalition and which he had known perfectly well about coalitions in another life and had failed entirely to apply to this one.

He wrote that down, in the flat language, and did not soften it.

The granary went up that afternoon and it took eleven minutes.

A handful of men broke away from the Wayford ground rather than be taken and went south along the bank instead of west, which made no sense to anybody watching until it made sense, because they went straight to the old granary in its tidy yard and they did not have to look for it.

They knew exactly where Kaldo kept its grain. They had carried it, some of them. They had stood on that wall.

It was empty.

It had been empty for four months and there had not been so much as a sack in it since the eleven days of hauling, and they fired it anyway and stood off and watched it burn, and four of them were ridden down at the corner of the yard and the rest scattered into the woods and were never accounted for.

None of them ever found out it was empty.

Nobody in Kaldo celebrated it, and Emeric made sure of that in the only way available, which was by not saying one word about it himself.

A man on the garrison detail that went down to the yard came back up and told Ossen that he had recognised one of the bodies at the corner, and gave the name, and Ossen said he knew, and that was the whole of the conversation and neither of them ever repeated it.

Then they fired the field, and that one worked.

There was nothing clever about it and nothing personal in it. A retreating column with torches went along the river road and put fire into standing green grain on both sides of it for a mile and a half, and green grain does not burn well, which is why it took two days instead of two hours.

Kaldo could not spare one hand to fight it. Every able person in the barony was carrying a wounded man up a hill, or boiling water, or holding somebody down, or digging, and there was no version of the afternoon in which forty people went out with beaters into a field while twenty-seven men were bleeding in a church.

So it burned, and they watched it burn, and it took the whole of two days.

Emeric was on the wall for a good part of both of them. He had known since the autumn that this was exactly what would happen. He had stood up in the long room over the threshing floor and said it out loud to the men who owned that crop, and priced it in front of them, and refused to soften the number.

Knowing it in advance helped considerably less than he had expected it to. He had assumed there would be some benefit to having been right. There was not any. It burned exactly as much as it would have burned if he had never thought about it at all.

Farris came up on the second morning and stood next to him.

He did not say anything for a long while. The smoke was coming across the water and getting into the town, and everything in Kaldo smelled of it for a fortnight afterward, and people were still finding the smell in wool at midwinter.

"Eleven hundred bushels," Farris said. "That is the nine divisions at what they were carrying. It will be less because some of the fourth will come through, the low corner is too wet to take properly. Call it nine hundred and fifty."

"You have counted it."

"I counted it in the spring. I have counted it every spring for nineteen years. I could tell you what the sixth division was carrying in the year of the flood."

He was not angry at Emeric. That was the part Emeric noticed and went on noticing.

There was no accusation anywhere in the man and there never was, then or later, and Emeric had been braced for one and had prepared two answers for it and used neither.

Farris was angry at nothing available. He stood on a wall and looked at four years of his own work going up in a field he had laid out himself with a rope, burned by men who had already lost and were going home, for no purpose that would appear in any account of the day.

That is worse. There is nowhere for it to go.

"The seed is in," Emeric said, eventually, because it was the only true thing he had.

"I know where the seed is," Farris said. "I put it there."

They stood there a while longer.

Two days of smoke over the water. A church full of other men's sons on the floor between the pews. A baron in a locked room in the keep being brought his supper. Nine hundred and fifty bushels in the air.

And a barony that had just won a war it could not remotely afford to have had.
