Chapter 39 - After Winter

Ward brought the list up to the keep on the first Monday in June, written in his square plain hand on a single sheet, and laid it on the table in front of Emeric without a word.

Forty-one names. Ward had drawn a line under the thirty-second.

"The nine below the line go," he said.

Emeric read them. He knew some of the faces that went with them, and most of the reasons. Four were the men who had stood on the keep doors in the spring, the ones who answered to somebody else. Two had kept the South Gate for Wexler for years, and Ward did not trust them to keep it for anybody else. The last three he did not know anything about at all.

"And these three?"

"I will not stand next to them," Ward said.

Emeric waited for more. There was no more. He had known Ward as long as he could remember anything, and in all of it he had never once heard him say a thing he could not have stood behind with his hand on a book, and he did not ask again.

"In the yard," he said. "In front of everybody. With their pay to the day."

"Yes."

"And the rest?"

"The rest are soldiers," Ward said, "or will be," and took the list back.



The garrison yard behind the guardhouse was the size of a small field, beaten earth with a well in one corner and a row of lime trees along the back wall that somebody had planted a long time ago and nobody had pruned since. The whole garrison stood in it on the Tuesday, in two ragged lines, in whatever they owned. Half the castle had found reasons to be on the walls above.

Emeric read the nine names himself, because they had been the barony's men and the barony was dismissing them, and it would not do for them to hear it from Ward and tell themselves Ward had done it.

He gave no reasons. He had decided that the night before. Reasons were for arguing with, and he did not want an argument in that yard, and the nine knew their reasons better than he did. He said that their service was ended from that hour, that their pay to the day was waiting at the table by the gate, that their kit would be handed in at the armory door before they reached the table, and that nobody would stop them taking anything that was their own.

One of the four from the keep doors laughed, short. Nobody joined him.

They went. They handed in what was the barony's at the armory door, under Rylan's eye, mail and helmets and a sword that turned out to be the barony's although its owner had sworn for nine years that it was his father's. Then they took their pay at the table by the gate, and Hale counted it out to each of them in Silver and Copper and wrote the sum in the daybook, and they walked out under the arch.

The last of them carried something rolled under his arm. It was the old map of Kaldo off the guardroom wall, drawn by some baron's clerk before the plague and hung on a nail there ever since, gone brown, with the wall and the gates and the bridges inked in and half the street names faded past reading. The man had taken it down on his way out, and nobody had stopped him, and he looked back once from under the arch to see whether anybody would.

Emeric saw it go. It was older than the plague and the frame was riddled with worm, and there was nothing on it that anybody standing on the road outside the South Gate could not see for himself. He let it go, because the alternative was a scene in the arch with half the castle watching, over a piece of old paper.

Ward, beside him, watched it go as well, and said nothing.



Then Emeric called Corliss.

He had thought about doing it privately, a second time, and had decided against it for the same reason he had read the nine names himself. A garrison that knew one of its knights had been taking from the garrison's own share for nine years, and saw nothing done, would draw its own conclusions about what could be taken next. He had held the confirmations since last summer: the garrison's book, the quartermaster's slips, the coin against the box, the signature against the muster mark. He had held the man's own yes, in that same yard, over a buckle. He had held off only because he had not held the garrison. Now he held it.

Corliss came out of the line and stood in front of him. He was broad and quiet, as he had been last summer, in the same coat mended at both elbows. His hands hung at his sides. The palms were still blistered.

Emeric said it without cruelty, as plainly as he could, loud enough for the walls. Draws on the garrison's share, small and regular, for nine years, in Corliss's name and in Corliss's hand. Four separate records agreeing. Corliss's own word, given when he was asked.

"You can answer it," Emeric said. "Here, now, in front of all of them. Whatever you want to say."

Corliss looked at him. It was the same look Emeric had seen twice before, outside Petram's door and over the buckle: a man waiting to be asked something, and ready for it.

"The numbers are correct," Corliss said.

That was all he said.

The yard heard it. Emeric watched the yard hear it, the way the two lines shifted and a man near the end looked at his feet, and the walls above went quiet from one end to the other, and he knew exactly what every one of them had decided it meant.

"You'll repay it," he said, "from your pay, a little each quarter, until it's paid. You keep your post. If it happens again, you don't."

Corliss inclined his head, exactly as far as a knight does to a lord who has given an order, and went back to his place in the line.



The training changed the next morning.

Ward did not announce it. He measured them.

Every man in the garrison, one at a time, in the yard, with Bram's big scale brought up from the forge on a handcart and a sand-glass borrowed from Imani's ward. How much each man weighed. How tall he stood. How many times he could lift a sack of sand from the ground to his shoulder before he could not. How long he took to run the wall walk from the South Gate tower to the river tower and back. How far he could carry a man his own weight. Ward wrote every number on a board, against every name, and when the board was full he nailed it to the guardhouse wall where the old map had hung. The sand-glass went back to the ward by noon, because Imani had said noon twice.

Half the garrison could not read it.

They stood in front of it anyway, the first evening, in a little crowd, looking at the marks against their own names, and the men who could read called out the others' numbers to them, and some of the numbers got laughed at and some did not.

At the bottom of the board, under the garrison, was one more name.

Ward had measured him too, on the first morning, before anybody else was up, without asking whether he minded. Emeric had lifted the sack to his shoulder a number of times that he had been privately rather pleased with, and run the wall walk in a time he had thought respectable, and carried Osric thirty-odd paces before his legs gave out, and Ward had written all of it down without expression and nailed it up with the rest.

By the second evening the men who could read had found it. Emeric heard them from the guardhouse door: a little silence, and then somebody reading his numbers out to the ones who could not, slowly, and then the comparisons, man by man, from the bottom of the board to the top. He was not at the bottom. He was a long way from the top. The river crew's cook, a man of fifty with a bad knee, had carried his weight further.

Nobody laughed while he was in earshot. He was fairly sure that a good deal of laughing went on when he was not, and minded it less than he would have a year ago; and the men who had read his numbers out looked at him a little differently in the yard afterward, as though he had told them something about himself that they had not expected him to tell.

The four from the capital trained apart.

Emeric had not thought about why until he watched it. On the second morning, in the heat, Rylan rolled his sleeves to the elbow to lift the sand-sack and did it forty times without a change in his breathing, and the bands on his forearms caught the sun: the rite's tracery, ring over ring of it from wrist to elbow, pale and raised under the skin, like wire set flush in wood. Every man in that yard had seen a knight's arms before. They watched anyway. Declan and Osric had the same, under their sleeves. Ward kept his covered.

"They are not a measure," Ward said, when Emeric asked. "A man who has been through the rite does not tell you what a man can do. He tells you what the rite did." He watched the garrison sweating at the sacks. "These are the ones who will hold this wall. I measure them against themselves. Next week a little more than this week. Every week."

"And the four?"

"The four train with me."

That was the first rule. The others came over the following days, one at a time, and Ward said each one once and then enforced it.

A little more each week, and never a great deal more. Every number written down, every week, against the name. A rest day in seven that was a rest day, and any man found drilling on it sent to bed. When a big wall-crew man from the river tower complained that rest was for women and priests, Ward looked at him until he stopped, and said, "Rest is training," and the man went and rested.

The garrison split into crews, gate and wall and river, and each crew trained only at its own work, the gate crew at the gate, the wall crew on the wall walk with the sacks, the river crew on the bank below the east bridge in a borrowed boat. Ward said a man who trained at everything learned nothing in particular.

And the food changed. Ward came up to the keep with a second list, this one of what each crew ate, and what each crew did, and asked Emeric to put the two lists side by side. The wall crew, carrying sacks of sand up and down the wall walk all morning, ate exactly what the gate crew ate standing in an arch. Emeric looked at the lists and took them to Hale, and Hale took them to the kitchen, and from the next week the wall crew got meat four days in seven and the gate crew got it two, and the gate crew, who could read numbers well enough when the numbers were about meat, complained, and were told why, and went on complaining, and did not stop the wall crew eating.

He signed another order in Hale's office the same afternoon. The pouch would be empty at the end of the month, and from the first of July the four from the capital went on the barony's roll at the garrison's rate, paid out of what was left over in the sixteen struck lines once the households had been paid. Hale entered it without remark. Ward, when he heard, said only that he had been paid by worse.

Bram came up on the Thursday to look at the kit.

It was laid out on trestles in the armory, everything the garrison owned, and Bram walked down the trestles slowly with his hands behind his back, as he had walked the old exchange looking at the trusses. Mail shirts from Wexler's time, most of them rusted at every ring that touched another, some rusted solid, a few cut and mended with wire. Helmets, of several patterns and several ages, enough to go round about half the men. Swords, which Bram picked up one by one and sighted along and put down without comment. Two crossbows with the strings perished.

"Well," Bram said, at the end of the trestles.

He picked up the nearest mail shirt by its shoulders and shook it, and a fine brown dust came out of it onto the stone, and several rings fell out after the dust and rolled away under the trestle.

"Rust you can scour," he said. "That's a boy and a barrel of sand and a week. It's the rings." He held up the shirt so the light came through it, and there were holes in it a hand could go through. "Every ring's a rivet. A shirt's a year of a man's life. There's twenty years on this table, and half of it's rust."



The evening class started at the exchange the week after, two nights in seven, after supper, when the light was still good through the high windows.

It was not ordered. Ward put it to the garrison in one sentence, in the yard: there would be letters with the priest and figures with a teacher from the town, at the exchange, two nights a week, for any man who wanted them, and when it came to choosing crew leaders he would choose from the men who could keep their crew's count. Then he dismissed them and did not mention it again.

Doran took the letters, at the front of the room, with the alphabet sheets from the cheese press pinned up on the wall behind him and a stick to point with. Ferra took the figures, at the back, on her own terms, which were that nobody from the keep asked her name and nobody carried it back up the hill. Nobody did. The men called her the smith's girl, and she did not correct them.

Seven came the first night. They sat on benches meant for children with their knees up near their chins, embarrassed, and stayed embarrassed for about a quarter of an hour, and then forgot to be. One of them was a wall-crew man who had carried a man his own weight further than anybody on the board and could not tell which mark on it was his.

Ferra did not teach them anything that first night that a child on the same benches in the morning would not have learned. She asked the wall-crew man for his number, and he did not know it. The next evening a copy of Ward's board came down to the exchange in Ward's own square hand, which nobody had asked him for, and she found the man's name on it and put his finger on the mark beside it.

"That's how far you carried," she said. "In paces. That's a four and that's a seven. Forty-seven."

He looked at it with his finger on it. Then he took his finger away and looked at it again, without the finger, to see whether he could still find it.

He could. He said it out loud, to the paper, the way a man tries a word in a foreign language, and two of the gate crew on the next bench heard him and did not laugh, which in that yard was very nearly a kindness.

Corliss did not come.

Rylan did not say anything about Corliss either, which was stranger. Rylan said something about everything. Corliss lived in the quarter where Rylan's family lived, and Rylan had been beside Emeric the day they went to find him in the guardhouse yard, and Emeric had expected some remark, sharp or sorry. Rylan ate his supper that night at the end of the guardhouse table and talked to Declan about horses, and when Emeric came in he looked up, and looked down again at his plate, and went on talking about horses.



Ward found him that night on the wall walk above the South Gate, where Emeric had gone because it was the one place in Kaldo where nobody brought him a petition after dark.

Ward stood beside him and looked out at the road, the pale line of it going away north between the fields toward the woods, empty now, with the nine somewhere up it in the dark and the old map with them.

Then he said, flatly, as though he were reporting the state of the gate:

"You asked the ledger. Then you asked him to agree with the ledger." He did not look round. "That is the same question twice."

Emeric turned to ask him what he meant by it.

Ward had already gone down the stair.
