Chapter 39 - The Standing Order

He came up into the hangers a little after noon with the whole of it composed, because eleven miles is a long time to be alone with a thing.

He had the three sentences he was going to say to the foreman and the two he was going to say to the men, and he had the shape of the fix, which was trenches below and the draw fenced above and a man on the water, and he had the harder sentence after that, which was that it was going to cost them nine days they did not have and that they were going to do it anyway.

He came round the shoulder of the hill and the first thing he saw was a line of whitewashed stones.

They ran across the slope below the huts, a hand's length apart, cut off square at both ends where the ground broke, and they were the same stones and the same whitewash as the hundred and twenty paces below the fence line on the eastern approach, because there had been a barrel of it left over from the spring before and it had been sitting in the joiner's shed ever since.

Above the line, the huts, the cook fire, two fires, one of them under a copper with a boy sitting at it doing nothing but that. Below the line, downslope and out along the fall, a trench, cut narrow, with a heap of earth beside it and a spade stood in the heap. Further down, a second trench, closed, with a post at each end of it and a board on the post.

The stream came off the hill above the whole camp, and eighty paces of it were fenced on both banks with cut brush, and there was a gate in the brush and a bucket stand at the gate, and a man of about fifty sitting on a stool beside it with a sack over his shoulders against the drip off the trees.

Emeric sat on his horse and looked at it for a while.

"You'll be here about the water," the man at the gate said.

"I'll be here about the water."

"It's done." He got up, on a bad hip, and did not hurry about it. "Third of May. Took four days to shift the trench and another two to run the brush. Healer's up at the felling."

"Who are you?"

"Osgood." He looked mildly surprised at being asked. "I'm the water."

The board was nailed to the end wall of the second hut where a man walked past it to get to the fire.

It was a plank, planed, and the writing on it was in charcoal gone over in ink so it would take the wet, in a hand Emeric had seen on the side of eleven jars in a vestry.

ORDER FOR ANY CAMP OF WORK.

For any lodging of more than ten persons standing more than eight days. Any site. Any season. This barony or any ground it holds.

1. Find the water first and stand at it before one hut is set. Everything else is placed from the water.

2. Sleeping, cooking and eating above the draw. All fouling below it, by three hundred paces on the fall of the ground and not on a map.

3. The draw is fenced. One man is named to it. His name is written on this board.

4. No fouling within a hundred paces of any spring, seep, wet hollow or dry ditch, whether water stands in it that week or not.

5. Trenches cut narrow and deep, covered with the day's earth every evening, closed and posted when full, a new one cut, none reopened.

6. All water for drinking or cooking boiled hard while a man counts three hundred. A fire kept for it and not shared with the cooking fire.

7. Hands washed before food is handled, by the cook and by any man come from the trench, at a bucket kept for it and emptied below the draw.

8. Any man with a flux, or a fever with a flux, comes off the work the same hour, is lodged apart and below, does not go to the water or the food, and the foreman sends for me that day.

9. If any of the above cannot be done at a site, the site is wrong. Move the camp.

This is not for this camp. It is for any camp. Copy it to the next one and to the one after that.

Imani. Third of May.

Under it, in a different hand and much worse writing: Osgood is the water.

He found her at the felling with her sleeves rolled and both hands on a man's forearm, doing something to it that the man was not enjoying, and she said "wait" without looking round, and he waited for nine minutes.

"Three of them," he said, when she was done.

"Three." She washed her hands in a bucket that was standing there for it. "Bern Ostry on the eleventh, here. Wick on the fourteenth, here. Tulley at the road camp on the nineteenth, and he is the one I would take back if I could have one of them."

"Why him."

"Because he died sixteen days after I moved the trench, and men do not understand that." She dried her hands on the cloth at her belt. "A stream that has had a trench above it since March is not clean on the fourth of May because you moved the trench on the third. It is clean in three or four weeks, and in the meantime you are boiling, and the boiling is the whole of what stands between you and it, and on about the fifth day men stop believing in the boiling because the trench has been moved and they can see that it has been moved." She looked at him. "That is what killed Tulley. Not the ground. The improvement."

"When did you get here."

"The stone camp on the fourteenth of April, for a fever that turned out to be a fever. I saw the trench while I was there and I did not like it and I moved it and I thought that was the end of it." She started walking and he went with her. "Then I rode down to the lime camp on the twenty-second because it was a mile and I had an afternoon, and it was the same camp. The same camp. Different ground, different men, a spring instead of a stream, and the same three things in the same three places."

"That's when you wrote it."

"That is when I understood I was going to have to write something, because I could not be in four places and I was not going to spend the summer riding between them shouting." She stopped and let a man past with a load. "I wrote it on the third of May and I wrote it badly twice before that. The version I burned had eleven clauses and was correct and no foreman would ever have read it."

"Imani." He had had this composed too, on the road, and it was the only part of the composed material that had survived contact. "You did the thing I didn't. I solved a town and I stopped. You took a solved thing and got the rule out of it, and clause nine on that board is the sentence I have owed this barony for fourteen months and it isn't in my hand, it's in yours."

She listened to all of it.

"I wrote a version of that in the summer of the plague year," she said. "Not as good. Nine clauses instead of nine, near enough the same nine. I gave it to Wexler's steward on a Tuesday and I know it was a Tuesday because it was the market. He said he would put it in front of the baron." She started walking again. "Eleven people died on Corrow Street that August. I do not know that the paper would have saved any of them. I know that it was never read, because I asked for it back two years later and he had no idea what I was talking about and was not lying."

"I didn't know that."

"No." She said it without any weight at all. "So do not tell me I have extracted a principle, Your Highness, because I extracted it five years ago and it did not matter in the slightest. What is different this year is that I can nail a plank to a wall and a foreman does what is on it and a man called Osgood sits at a gate all day and gets paid for it out of a wage book. That is not my cleverness. That is a thing you did, and it is the only part of this you have any business being pleased about, and you may have it."

He walked beside her for about thirty paces working out whether he had been given something or told something, and concluded, correctly, that it had been both and that she had not thought about it for a second.

"Then the congratulating was wrong," he said.

"It was not wrong. It was late."

Vantic came in at four, having ridden out separately with a boy from the mill and a measuring line, because the frame was the critical path and a covenant schedule is not a thing a man takes on report.

He walked the camp. He read the board twice. He asked Osgood three questions and wrote the answers down and asked what Osgood was paid, and wrote that down too.

They put it in front of him in the foreman's hut with the light going.

"I am entering the order in the barony's book as a general order," Emeric said. "Dated the third of May, which is when it was made, and not today, which is when I found out about it. Under her name. Ferra will copy it to the four camps and to anything we ever open after this, and it hangs where the men eat and not in the long room."

"Noted."

"And the second thing." He put it on the table because he had written it on the ride and it was better written than said. "Any camp, any site, any works of this barony. She can stop it. Not report it, not ask for it, stop it, with the men standing there and the day paid, and it stays stopped until she says otherwise or I overrule her in writing with a reason that goes in the book beside her name so that anybody can read the two of them together in ten years."

Vantic read it.

"You understand what that costs," he said.

"Say it, since you'll write it anyway."

"A hundred and forty-three men at four sites. A day's stop at all four is a hundred and forty-three days of wage against a schedule with a covenant date on it, and there is a fortnight of float in that schedule and no more, and she will not spend it carelessly but she will spend it, because she is the sort of person who will, which is why you are giving it to her." He put the paper down. "I will write it as a material change in the works and I will write that it was made at the borrower's own instance and not on the factor's requirement."

"That's a longer sentence than it needs to be."

"It is exactly as long as it needs to be," Vantic said. "My house reads it. The distinction between a thing a lender made a borrower do and a thing a borrower did with a lender in the room costing him money is the only distinction in my trade that is worth anything at all." He capped the ink and looked at the plank on the wall through the doorway, some way off in the dusk. "I have seen nine building schedules in this kingdom. I have never seen one of them stop for a bucket."

They had the three of them at the edge of the felling, in ground that had been cleared for it, because eleven miles is a long way to carry a man in May and nobody had asked the castle.

Emeric stood there for a while with the foreman and about thirty men who had come off the work when they saw him go down.

He said it once.

"I fixed the water in the town fourteen months ago and I never wrote down why it worked, so it stayed a story about the town and it never came out here. Four foremen built four good camps out of ordinary sense and every one of them came out the same wrong shape, and it was the same shape that killed people on Corrow Street, and it was mine to see and I didn't, because I had it filed as something I'd already won." He did not look at anything but the ground. "That's the whole of it. I'm not going to say it again and I'd rather nobody told me it wasn't so."

Nobody did.

He rode back the next morning and got in at noon, and the seventh lid was on the bench where he had left it on the second of June with about four hours of work in it.

He sat down and finished it, because it needed finishing and because the granary took the harvest in six weeks whatever else was true, and worked until dark, and it came out the best of the seven.
