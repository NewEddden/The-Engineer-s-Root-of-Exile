Chapter 333 - Cold and Very Large

He moved across to the K Endeavor on the twenty-third, because that was where she was, and nobody argued with him about it, which was how he knew everybody had expected it.

Imani found him a cabin. It was not much of one. It had been the hospital hull's linen store until the war, a slot of a room off the forward end of the hold with a shelf bunk, a hook, a lamp in a cage and a smell of carbolic that had got into the wood and was never coming out, and it was eleven steps from the curtain of the set room and forty-one from the space past the last cot where the cradle lay lashed under its canvas. He had counted the forty-one the first evening without meaning to, and did not tell anybody.

"You'll walk the length of the hold twice a morning and twice an afternoon," Imani said, in the door of it with her arms folded, "and if you want to walk it a third time you'll ask me, and I'll say no. And you'll not sit up at night by her. Armand does that. He's better at it."

That was true. Armand had taken a camp stool from the stores on the first night and set it at the cradle's foot, and he sat there every evening from the time the ward lamps were turned down until somewhere after two, with the oak box on his knee and a book open on top of it that he did not read. He did not talk to the canvas. He only sat, with his spectacles pushed up into the white curls, and listened to the two notes in the hold that were hers: the small warm one in the box, and the one that was no note at all, under the canvas, that he listened to anyway.

The K Endeavor sat over the outer ring with her lamps lit along her belly, and the city went about under her in the cold as if she had always been there, the way a town gets used to a weathercock. Somebody in the drapers' street had started selling small wooden hulls on a string for a window, three for a penny, with the K painted on the side the wrong way round; Saban brought one back on the twenty-fourth and hung it on the hook over Emeric's coat, and said he would like it understood he had paid a penny and not three, because the man had not had change.

The hand knitted. Imani read it every morning with her thumb on the strap and said "Straight," and once, on the twenty-fifth, "Straighter," as though it had been showing off. He could close it now round a cup, if the cup was light, and if he did not mind what it said about it afterward.

The mana came back the way it had been coming, an inch a day, from the bottom. By the twenty-sixth he could not feel the bottom unless he went and looked for it. He went and looked for it a good deal. It was still there.

The cardinal's watcher was in a cabin aft with an apron knight on the door and a slop bucket and opinions. On the twenty-fifth the door was open while the knight took the tray in, and Brey's voice came out of it complaining about the porridge with the unhurried thoroughness of a man going through a household inventory.

"There's salt in it," said Brey. "I'm not against salt. I'm against salt instead of anything. This is what you give a horse you've stopped liking."

"It's what everybody gets," said the knight.

"Then I'm sorry for everybody." A spoon went down. "Has he sent? His Eminence. For me." Brey waited. "No. He won't. He never sends for a thing he's put down." The spoon came up again. "I'd still like it written somewhere that I asked. Nine years. You'd think it'd be worth a letter."

The knight came out and shut the door, and saw Emeric in the passage, and did not know whether to salute with a tray in his hands; and Emeric shook his head at him and walked on, and thought about the last thing Brey had said all the way to the end of the hold.

All that time the thread did not move.

He had laid it on the fifteenth, in the steam, and it ran out of him still, up and north, over the long bridge and the woods and along the river to the white falls, a measuring line with nothing tied to the end, lying along the outside of a man without touching him. He had done what Armand said. But it was there when he woke and when he lay down, and at the end of it, in a house he had never seen, the great cold note sat where it had sat since the night of the fifteenth.

It was not quiet, exactly. A man that size did not go quiet; he was too big to. But he had gone still, the way a man goes still in a chair at night with the lamp out and his hands on the arms of it, waiting for a step on a stair he has heard coming for a long time. Twice on the twenty-sixth, in the afternoon, the note went thin all through, as it had gone in the square under the garden wall, ice under a boot; and each time it came back, slowly, and was still again.

Saban's people said the house above the falls had smoke from four chimneys and its gate shut, and that a canon went down to the village for bread at seven every morning and came back, and that nobody else had gone in or out. Saban brought it across every evening and said it in the same words, because he said things in order. On the twenty-sixth he said it, and then put the slip down, and looked at Emeric over it, and did not say anything else, which was not like him.

"What?" said Emeric.

"The bread," said Saban. "He sent for bread for four, every morning since the sixteenth. This morning it was bread for three." He picked the slip up again. "I'd like to know who's stopped eating."



It happened a little before four in the morning of the twenty-seventh, and it woke him.

He did not know at first what had. He lay on the shelf in the dark with his strapped hand on his chest and listened to the hospital hull breathe round him, the plates ticking with the cold, a man on the ward coughing and stopping, the duty nurse's shoes going down between the cots. Armand's stool, at the far end of the hold, was empty; he had gone to his own bunk at two. Imani's note was in her room behind the ward, asleep, which it so seldom was that he noticed it.

Then he understood that it was the thread.

The cold note at the end of it had opened.

He could not have said it better than that afterward, even to Imani. It opened, the whole of it, from inside, the way a man opens his own door: not forced, not broken, a bar lifted from the room side by a hand that knew where the bar was. He felt it do it all along the line, a long slow lifting, as of a very large thing going down on its knees; and he lay on the bunk with his eyes open in the dark and did not breathe.

And then, a breath after, the note flinched.

It went thin, the way it had in the square, all through at once; and it pulled back from its own open door, the way a man's hand comes back from a stove; and it stopped where it was, half open, the way the clocks in his father's gallery had stopped, each at a different minute, all their works there and nothing going.

Somewhere at the back of him a question he had asked himself months ago and put down because it was hot moved a little where it lay. He did not pick it up.

Then the cold came.

It came up from underneath, as it had come in the reeds on the bank in the summer: up through the floor of things, out of the dark under every room, a cellar door opening in a house that had always had a cellar. He knew it at once. He had felt it once and he would have known it in his grave. Larger than a room had any business being, and older than anything, and cold the way the bottom of the sea is cold, not as a temperature but as a fact about how far down you are. It came up the stair under the house above the falls, and into the stopped note, and filled it.

At the end of his thread, where the cardinal of Randillia had been since the fifteenth, there was something cold and very large; and it was settling, slowly, the way a big man settles into a chair somebody else has warmed, feeling for the arms of it.

He was sitting up. He did not remember sitting up.

Don't go down the stair yet. Armand in the chair by his bunk on the K Amara with Ferra's column on his knee, and his young face not wry at all. Not after him. He'll keep. Down a line you laid yourself, after a man who's had ninety years to furnish his own cellar, with your cup empty to the bottom.

The man was not keeping. That was the whole of it. Whatever had been in the house above the falls for ninety-three years was going out at the end of his line while he sat here with the lamp off, and in a few breaths there would be nothing in it at all but the large cold thing, wearing it. He could feel the bottom of his cup. He could feel exactly how far down it was.

He went down the stair anyway.



His own room first, because you always go out through your own room. He knew it in the dark: the long low workshop-shaped place it had always been, the benches, the drawings on the walls, the window at the end that did not look out on anything in this world; and the floor of it, and under the floor the stair Armand had shown him on the night of his stone, going down.

He took it with the thread in his hand.

Down there it was as it had been. Every room in the world stood on one black water, the way the trees of a wood stand on their roots, and between them nothing at all; no distance, only the water, and the rooms up out of it as far as there was anything. The capital was all round him, ten thousand small lit rooms like windows on a hill at night. He did not look at any of them. He went along the thread, which was easy, because he had laid it; north, if north meant anything down here, and up out of the water into the cellar of a house that was not his, by a stair that was not his, with the lamp of his own sense held low in front of him.

It was a big house. Ninety years of a man's life, furnished, room on room, long passages going back further than he could see; like walking into a cathedral at night with a candle and knowing from the sound of your boots how far the roof goes up.

And it was going out.

It went the way a great house goes out at the end of a night when the servants have been sent to bed: a room at a time, from the far end inward. The lamps along the passages were dying in order, each one down to a red line and then nothing, and behind them the rooms went dark and stayed dark and were not rooms any more, only cold, and the shape of where they had been. He could see it coming toward him down the long passages, not hurrying, the way the tide comes over a flat. Here and there, as he went, something flared in a room and went out, and he understood that these were the last of the man: sparks, things the man had held hardest, catching for a breath on their way out the way a fire's last coals catch when you open the door of the stove.

And over all of it, filling the upper floors, the thing.

He did not look up. He could feel it the way you feel weather. It was busy upstairs, in the big rooms at the front of the house, settling into them one at a time, trying the chairs. It had all the time in the world, and knew it, and was not looking down.

A few breaths. He had a few breaths.

He held the lamp up and took what was there.



A door at the top of a stair. That came first, and it came very clear, because the man had been up that stair a great many times and had held it hard: a narrow stone stair in a very old building, worn in the middle of every step, going up and up with a rope on rings for a rail, and a small door at the top of it, and a lamp burning beside the door in a niche, always burning, day and night, as long as the man had known it.

And a man at the door. Old, small, bald, in grey, with soft hands. Not a priest's face; a steward's, or a clerk's, the face of a man who had spent his whole life between one room and the next and was perfectly content to. He had no name. That was what came with him: the cardinal had never known it, in twenty years, and had never once been fool enough to ask. He answered to one person in Ostrel and one only, and not to any cardinal, and not to any council, and a cardinal who went up that stair went up it with his hands where the old man could see them. He kept the door. He was not what was behind it.

The chamberlain. The cardinal had thought of him by that word and no other.

A letter. Many letters, the same letter, the man's left hand writing it again and again across twenty years in a hundred rooms: folded small, in four, and sealed. Not with the Eye. Not with any device at all. A plain round of grey wax with nothing in it, pressed down flat with the side of a thumb. Every letter in the Church went under a cardinal's seal, or a bishop's, or an abbot's; a letter under plain grey went past every one of them unopened, courier to courier, up the pilgrim road and the passes into Ostrel, the way a stone goes through water, because every clerk in the Church had been taught as a boy what plain grey meant and what happened to a clerk who broke it. It went to the old man at the door at the top of the stair. It went to nobody else.

And on the outside, the words. He saw them written in the man's hand, small and exact, a hundred times over, as if he were looking down at a desk over the man's shoulder.

For the lamp, and no other hand.

The spark that held it caught, and burned very bright for a breath; and the room it was in went dark around it, and it went out.

He had it. He held it against himself, the way you hold a thing you have caught that is trying to get out of your hands.

The dark was nearer. He could feel the cold on the side of his face, the way you feel an open door in winter.

There was one more. He did not go looking for it. It came to him, because it was the biggest thing left in the house, and the brightest, and the last; because the man had held it harder than he had held anything, for twenty years, and had never once let himself look at it.

It was fear.

It was enormous, and very old, and it had been folded and put away in the deepest room the man had so many times over that it had worn the shape of the folds into itself, like a letter carried a long time in a pocket. And now the room was opening and it was coming unfolded, all at once, as the lamps went out; and it was not fear of Emeric.

It had never once been fear of Emeric.

A high room in Ostrel, lamplit, late. A desk with papers on it. The holy father at the desk, alone, with his head bent over his work, humming under his breath: a few notes that climbed and came to rest, and stopped there, comfortably, as if that were where they had always meant to stop; and then broke off, because the door had opened and the man was standing in it. And the holy father looking up, and smiling at him, kindly, and speaking of something ordinary, a bishop in the south who wanted a new roof; and while he spoke his hand going up, by itself, to his breastbone, to the place where something on a cord would hang, and closing there on nothing at all, because there was nothing there, only the cord, and coming down again. And later, the same night or another, a hundred other nights, the holy father with his two hands laid on the desk under the lamp, turning them over, slowly, palm and back and palm, and looking at them; not the way an old man looks at his hands, to see how old they have got, but the way a man looks at a pair of gloves somebody has lent him, to see whether they will do.

That was all. A tune. A habit with a cord. The holy father looking at his hands. Nothing. Twenty years of nothing, seen out of the side of the eye in a hundred rooms, and not one of them anything a man could put in a letter. The cardinal had made himself stop seeing them in his first year at Ostrel, so thoroughly that he had not known, until the square, sitting against a garden wall with somebody else's stone in his hand, that he had been seeing them every day since, and filing them, and adding them up in the dark like a clerk who cannot leave a column alone; and what the column came to was not a holy father at all, nor any one old man at any one desk. It came to a man who had been many men. Who had worn them one after another, the way you wear coats, until he had forgotten some of them and not all; and who sat under the lamp, some nights, and looked at the newest one to see how it fitted.

And the man had known, for twenty years, in the room he never walked, which coat came next. He had known it at every desk and on every stair. He had knelt at the end in the dark of the house above the falls and said yes, because a cardinal says yes; and the whole of every one of those twenty years he had been more afraid of going home, up that narrow stair to the lamp in the niche, than of anything any prince or any war had ever done to him in the world outside. More afraid of going home than of dying. Far more than of Emeric. Emeric had only beaten him.

It came unfolded, all of it, the whole of the terror at once, so large and so cold and so long carried that for the space of a breath Emeric could not tell whether it was the man's or the thing upstairs or his own; and then the room went dark around it, and it went out.

There was nothing left after that. He knew it the way you know a house is empty. Down every passage the lamps were out, and the dark had come right up to the cellar stair, to the foot of it, to his boots.

Upstairs, the thing stopped moving.

It had not turned. He was nearly sure, afterward, that it had not turned. But it had stopped, in the big front room of the house where it had been trying the chairs, the way a man in a strange house at night stops with his hand on a door, because he thinks he heard something below him, a step, a board, and wants to hear it again.

He did not let it hear it again.

He let go of the thread. He did not cut it; there was nothing to cut it with that would not have made a sound. He opened his hand and let it lie on the floor of the dead man's cellar, as fine as a hair, with nothing on either end, and went back down the stair he had come up by into the black water, and along it, and up his own stair, two steps at a time, the way a boy goes up a dark stair with something behind him, into his own room; and shut the floor.

Nothing came after him.

Nothing could have. He knew why, though the knowing came slow and stupid, like arithmetic done with a fever. A man you read against his will pushes back, and takes a corner of your room for every corner of his; both sides pay. But there had been nobody left in that house to push. The man had been gone before Emeric came down the stair. A dead room cannot reach back, and cannot keep anything it is handed, and so whatever the thing upstairs had inherited of the cardinal of Randillia, it had inherited nothing of Emeric. Not his door. Not his face. Not one board of his floor.

He was nearly sure.

And under his wrist the root bead lay in its band, warm, where it had lain since the night of his stone, his own door's latch on his own arm; and the thing in the house above the falls could not lift a latch it was not holding. He had thought it a great many times since the summer. He thought it again now, on purpose, as a man puts his hand on a rail.



He came up on the deck plates of the linen store, on his side, with his face against the cold iron and something warm running over his mouth.

He put his good hand to his face and it came away dark in the light from the passage, and he looked at it with interest for some time before he understood that the door was open, and somebody was standing in it.

Two people.

Imani came down onto her knees on the plates beside him without a word and took his face in both hands and turned it to the light; looked at it, read it, with her thumbs on his cheekbones and her eyes half shut, the way she read a hand; and let it go. Then she took his hands.

They were cold. He had not known they were until hers closed on them. They were colder than the iron, cold to the bone and past it, the way a man's hands are who has had them in the winter sea; and hers were warm from sleep, and she held them both in both of hers, the strapped one and the good one, and folded them in against herself, under her chin, and held them there.

"Give me them," she said.

That was all she said. She did not ask him anything. She did not tell him what he looked like, or how stupid it had been, or what she would take off him next time. She knelt on the cold plates in her nightdress with a shawl half off one shoulder and held his hands under her chin, and breathed on them, once, and went on holding them; and the blood ran down off his lip onto her wrist, and she let it.

Armand was in the door.

He was in his shirt and trousers, barefoot on the iron, his curls flat on one side from the pillow and no spectacles; and in his left hand, held against his chest, the little oak box. He had not stopped for his coat. He had stopped for the box.

"I felt it go," said Armand.

His voice was quite quiet. Emeric looked up at him over Imani's shoulder.

"I was asleep, and I felt it go. Your line. It went down out of the top of your head like a bucket rope with nobody on the winch. I heard it run." He came one step into the store, which was all the store had room for. "And then I felt what was on the other end of it."

"Armand."

"No." In all the years Emeric had known him he had never once said it like that, without anything round it. "No. You'll listen to me. I asked you. I sat in that chair and asked you as plainly as I know how, and you said you weren't going to, and I knew you were lying, and let you." The box was held so tightly against his chest that his knuckles stood up white along the lid. "You went down a stair with your cup empty to the bottom. After that. Into a house that thing was standing in. You went in at its back door while it was taking its boots off in the hall."

"It didn't see me."

"You don't know that."

Emeric did not answer, because he didn't.

"You don't know that," said Armand again, louder, and on the ward beyond the curtain somebody woke and asked what it was, and the duty nurse hushed them. Armand did not lower his voice. Emeric had never heard him raise it. The wry deliberate voice that laid every sentence down in front of you like a card was gone, and under it was a plain hard one, the voice of a big young man who was very frightened and did not know where to put it. "Seventy years I've known what lives under that floor. Seventy years, and I have never once gone down to look at it on purpose, and I am not a coward. I had a stone in my hand the whole time your mother was in the tower, and I never went down. Do you know why?"

"Because it reads you."

"Because it reads you. Because if it ever once turns round and looks, it knows what you are, and where you sleep, and who you'd cross a kingdom for, and it has ever such a long time to think about what to do with that." He took a breath. "She was in somebody's fist for twenty-eight years. Twenty-eight. She's been out of it twelve days. She's in this box, and her body's at the end of this hold with straw coming out of the canvas, and I've not slept three hours together since I came aboard; and you. You. Would go down that stair the same week. You're all of her there is that walks about." His voice cracked on it, quite badly, and he did not stop for it. "Do you have any idea what she'd have said to me? If I'd let you? Do you know what she'd have written to the tower?"

Nobody said anything.

Imani had not moved. She knelt with Emeric's hands under her chin and did not look round, and did not tell Armand to be quiet, and did not take Emeric's side; and Emeric understood, somewhere under the cold, that she agreed with every word and had decided to let somebody else say them.

Armand stood in the door with the box against his chest, and breathed, and looked down at the two of them on the floor; at the blood on Imani's wrist, and on the plates, and at Emeric's face. Something went out of him, not the anger, but the noise of it.

"Is he dead?" he said.

"Yes."

"And was it worth it? Whatever you went in for?"

"I don't know yet."

"No. Of course you don't." Armand looked at him a while longer. "Are you going to tell me what you saw?"

"Not now."

"Not now," said Armand. He nodded slowly, as if Emeric had confirmed a figure he had already worked out and disliked. "Not now. No." He turned in the doorway, and stopped, with his back to them, and stood like that, a big barefoot young man in his shirt with his head bowed and an oak box against his chest.

"I've not been as angry as this," he said to the passage, "since I was sixty. And that was at a woman." And went away up the hold, barefoot, between the cots, toward the far end, where the canvas was; and Emeric heard the camp stool creak as he sat down on it.

Imani held his hands.

She held them until the bleeding stopped, which was a long while, and she held them after that, kneeling on the plates, until her own knees must have been as cold as his fingers; and then she got him back onto the shelf bunk and sat on the edge of it with both his hands in her lap in the shawl. She did not go in. He could feel her wanting to, and not; and he lay and looked at the lamp in its cage and let her not.

A little after six, when the first grey was coming in at the hold's high ports, Armand came back down the hold with a mug of tea, and set it on the shelf by Emeric's head, and did not say anything, and went away again.

The cold did not go out of his hands. Imani said, at seven, that she did not think it was in the hands at all. It stayed the whole of that day in the bones of his fingers, so that everything he touched, the mug, the rail, the edge of a table, felt warm, as everything feels warm to a man who has just come in out of the sea.



Sefton had been hedging the same forty yards of the north road since the sixteenth, and it was by now the best-laid forty yards of hedge in the north of the kingdom, and he was a little sorry it was going to have to stop.

It was a good place for a hedge, which was why Master Saban had put him there. Eight miles out of the capital the falls lane came down off the hill on the west through a cut in the beechwoods and met the north road at a little stone bridge over a stream, and anybody coming down from the house above the falls who wanted to go anywhere but back to the city had to come over that bridge, and turn north, and pass forty yards of very fine hawthorn, laid and staked and bound along the top with hazel, with a man in a leather apron and a billhook at the end of it, working.

He was a quiet man of fifty with very large hands, and he had found in sixteen years of Master Saban's work that nobody in the world looks at a hedger, any more than at a milestone; and that if a hedger stops to straighten his back and look at the road, that is only what hedgers do.

He heard them before he saw them, on the frozen lane under the beeches, a little after nine in the morning of the twenty-seventh: four horses, walking, and a fifth on a lead.

He laid a pleacher down, and bent it, and staked it, and straightened his back.

The young cardinal came first out of the cut. Master Saban's sheet had him: tall, fair, close-cut, a face like something made on purpose, in red under a dark riding cloak, on a grey. Then, beside him, so close that their stirrups nearly touched on the bridge, the old one.

Sefton had never seen him close. He had read the sheet so often in eleven days that he could have said it with his eyes shut. Six feet and two. Pale as a peeled stick. Looks about fifty; is nearer a hundred. White and gold under a grey cloak, a red cap, the gold Eye on the chain. The right arm no good since some old fight, and the right hand always shut, always, at his side or on his thigh or under his cloak, and never once seen open in the city by anybody of ours. Blesses with the left. Rides with the left. Holds a cup with the left.

The two canons came behind with the led horse and the baggage, black and plain and not looking at anything.

Sefton found that he had taken his cap off. He had not meant to. He was Church-raised, like everybody, and his hand had done it the way it had every time a priest rode by since he was four. He did not put it back on; a hedger who snatches his cap back on is a man with something to hide.

They came over the bridge at a walk and turned north, and passed along the forty yards; and the old one, as he came level, turned his head and looked at the hedge.

Not at Sefton. At the hedge. At the long run of it, laid and bound, as a man looks at a piece of work, along its whole length and back, with what looked very much like pleasure.

"That's well done," said the cardinal. A low warm voice, a teacher's voice, the voice in the sheet. "Who taught you to lay a hedge like that?"

"My father, Your Eminence."

"He taught you well." The pale eyes came off the hedge and onto Sefton, and rested there, quite kindly, for the time it takes to lay one pleacher; and went on up the road. "Good morning."

"Good morning, Your Eminence."

The young cardinal had not looked at Sefton or the hedge at all. He had been looking at the old one, all the way along the forty yards, the way a man watches somebody walk who has been ill, to see if he will need an arm.

Sefton watched the four of them go on up the north road at a walk until the rise past the milestone took them. Then he put his cap on and laid one more pleacher, because a hedger who stops the moment the gentry have gone over the hill looks like a man who was waiting for them.

It was the hands he wrote down, an hour later, in the back room of the Plough at Wendle with the door shut and Master Saban's man on the set waiting. He wrote the time, and the four, and north, and the young one stirrup to stirrup; and then he sat looking at the paper, because Master Saban had told him years ago that it was always the small thing, and that he would know it when he saw it and wish he hadn't.

He had seen the old cardinal's hands for the whole of the forty yards. The left on the reins, as it should be. And the right on the reins too, beside it. Open. The long pale fingers wrapped round the leather, not well, stiff, the way a man holds a rein who has not held one in that hand for a long time and has forgotten the knack; but open, on the rein, in the daylight, where anybody at a hedge could see it.

The cardinal rode north with both hands on the reins, Sefton wrote. The right hand open.

He looked at it a while longer, and then, because Master Saban liked everything, he wrote under it: He liked the hedge.



Saban brought the slip across himself, on the noon boat, and laid it on the little table in the K Endeavor's set room, which was where Emeric was sitting because it was warm, with the cold still in his fingers and a blanket Imani had put round his shoulders that he had stopped arguing with.

Saban read it out, in order. At the last lines he stopped and read them twice in his head before he read them aloud, and Emeric watched him do it.

"Both hands on the reins," said Saban. "The right one open." He put the slip down. "Sefton's careful. If he says open, it was open. He liked the hedge, apparently." He did not say anything about that. "I've two hulls' worth of glass on the north road from here to the Cocan passes, and a rider at the Plough with a fresh horse. Do you want them stopped?"

"No."

Saban waited.

"Watch them to the passes," said Emeric. "Nobody goes near. Nobody on the road, nobody low in the air, nobody speaks to them at an inn. If they stop at Sallow, they stop at Sallow. Let him ride."

"Him." Saban looked at Emeric's face, which he had been looking at since he came in and had not mentioned. "He's held the reins left-handed for twenty-eight years. I've a sheet on it. Every man I've got in the city has a sheet on it." He folded the slip once. "Is he coming back?"

"No."

"Then who's riding his horse?"

Emeric did not answer, and Saban did not ask again. He put the slip in his pocket and got up.

"Bread for three," said Saban, at the curtain. "I did wonder." And went.

His father came across at four, and sat down on the other side of the little table with his collar up, and did not ask Emeric anything at all.

"Gone north," said Harrold.

"Yes."

"I'd have liked to see him go." He was quiet a while. "Thirty years he stood at my elbow in that cathedral. I never once knew what he was thinking." His father got up again. "Your hands are cold," he said, which he could not have known by looking; and put one of his own over Emeric's good one on the table, for a breath, warm, a big dry old hand, and took it away, and went back to his boat.



He told Imani that night, in her room behind the ward, with the door shut and the ward lamps down and her two hands round his again, because she said she was not having him tell anybody anything with fingers like that.

Her room was smaller than the linen store and warmer, with a jar of honey on the shelf and a spoon standing up in it. He sat on the bunk. She sat on the stool in front of him, knee to knee, with his hands in her lap, and listened, and did not take her eyes off his face, and did not go in. She had not gone in all day.

He told her all of it. The door at the top of the stair, and the lamp in the niche, and the bald doorkeeper with soft hands who had no name. The plain grey wax, pressed with a thumb, that went past every cardinal's desk in the Church as a stone goes through water. The words on the outside. He said them to her twice, so that two people would have them, and she said them back, once, under her breath.

"For the lamp, and no other hand."

"Yes."

And then the rest, which was harder, because most of it had gone, the way a dream goes at the washstand. He had held it for a few breaths; by the time he was on the plates it had come apart into a room, and a desk, and a lamp, and the holy father doing something ordinary that he could not now remember. What stayed was the fear. That stayed perfectly clear, the whole cold enormous folded-up shape of it, as if somebody had pressed it into him like a seal.

"He wasn't afraid of me," he said. "Not in the square. Not ever. I thought he was, at the end, under the wall. He wasn't. There was something in Ostrel he'd been afraid of for twenty years, so afraid he'd stopped looking at it; and it wasn't a man. Or it was, and it had been a great many of them. That was what he thought. A man who's been many men." He looked down at their hands. "And he knew it was coming for him. He'd always known. And he said yes anyway, at the end, in the dark, because that's what they're made to say. And then he tried to take it back."

Imani said nothing.

"Augustine died more afraid of going home than of me," he said.

She was quiet a long while. Her thumbs moved, once, over the backs of his fingers.

"Is it going to come for you?" she said.

"I don't think it can." His good hand turned in hers, and his wrist with it, and she looked down at the band there and the bead in it, and back up. "Not while that's on me. I think it can only take you if it's holding your latch. His was in Ostrel. Mine's on my arm."

"You think."

"I think."

"Hm," said Imani, which was what she said to a fever that had come down in the night when she had not expected it to, and did not trust it.

"It's got everything he had, though." He had been working it out all afternoon in the set room with the blanket round him. "Whatever he knew, it knows now. It's got the square. It's got me casting like a knight in front of half the hill. It's got Kaldo's delvers, as many as he'd counted, and how we go in, and how fine a line I can lay." He stopped. "Not the dome. He never knew what goes on under the dome. So it doesn't."

"And you."

"It didn't get anything of mine. He was gone before I got there. There was nobody left to take anything." He heard himself say it a little too firmly, and so did she. "I'm nearly sure."

Imani looked at him for a long time, over their hands. Then she lifted his two hands up out of her lap, the strapped one and the good one together, and held them against her mouth, and breathed on them, warm, the way you breathe on a child's hands coming in from snow; and put them down again, and did not let go.

"They're warmer," she said. "Not much."



At eleven he sat in the set room with the clerk asleep on her arms on the far end of the table, because he had told her he would wake her when he wanted the horn, and wrote it out himself, left-handed, slowly, in the private table Ferra had made him learn by heart in his second winter in Kaldo, when the first letters from the capital began coming opened and resealed. Nobody else had the key. Nobody else had ever asked whether there was one.

A IS DEAD. NOT BY ME. SOMETHING CAME UP UNDER HIM AND PUT HIM ON LIKE A COAT. I WENT DOWN AFTER. I HAVE THE ROAD TO THE DOOR AT THE TOP OF HIS HOUSE. A DIED MORE AFRAID OF GOING HOME THAN OF ME. NOBODY ELSE. E.

He coded it into groups that meant nothing in the chained book on the desk, and woke the clerk, and she read them into the horn in the flat voice, five letters and a pause, without the least idea what any of them said; and at the far end of seven hundred miles of dark the line room's set clicked twice, which was Kaldo, and the voice that was not Ferra's read them back, steady, all the way to the end, without stopping once.

Then a long wait. Long enough that the clerk looked at him.

Then the set clicked once, and the precise dry voice came on, and read a handful of groups, and stopped; and they were not in the chained book either, and the clerk wrote them down and handed them over without trying, and went back to sleep on her arms.

He worked them out by the lamp.

RECEIVED. NOBODY. WAS YOUR CUP EMPTY. DON'T ANSWER. F.

He did not answer.



Afterward he lay on the shelf bunk in the linen store with the carbolic in his nose and the little wooden hull swinging on its string over his coat on the hook, and the lamp out, and the plates ticking.

At the far end of the hold the camp stool creaked as Armand shifted on it. Tomorrow he would be wry again, probably, and formal, and pleased with himself about something, and would never once refer to this morning; and Emeric would let him.

His hands were warmer. Not much.

North, up the north road, past a stone bridge and forty yards of very good hedge, there was something cold and very large riding toward the passes at a walk with a beautiful young man beside it watching it in case it needed an arm, and he could not feel it from here, and he did not try. He had put the thread down on a dead man's floor. He was not going to pick up another.

It knew what Kaldo could do in a square now, and a prince who cast like a knight. It could not read what it could not reach. It had all the time in the world, and it had spent a very long time of it hiding; and a thing that hides that long has something it cannot afford to have found.

He lay in the dark and looked at that, the way he looked at a part on the bench to see where it would take a load.

On another world, in a school in a town on the far side of everything, a teacher with chalk on her sleeve had drawn two circles on a board and an arrow from each to the other, and told a class of bored children that this was how half a world had kept from burning for forty years: nobody had to be good, or wise, or kind; each side only had to be sure, past any doubt at all, that the first step it took would be its last. Marcus had thought it the most depressing thing he had ever heard. He had been fourteen. He had not thought about it since.

He thought about it now, for a long while, with his cold hands folded on his chest and Imani's warmth not quite gone out of them, and the address on a letter he had not written yet going round and round in him like a sum that would not come out.

One word, in the end, in the dark of the linen store, heard by nobody.

Deterrence.
