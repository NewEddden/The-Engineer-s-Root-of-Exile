Chapter 250 - Side Dish

"You could see inside her," said Imani. "I couldn't."

"But I can show you."

"Yes, but what happens when you're not here to see for the both of us?"

"True."

He had heard the question before. He had asked it himself, at Bram's bench and Tess's and the long table, for nine years: what happens on the day the stones stop, or the day the man who knows how is at sea, or in the ground. He had built Kaldo's whole road on it, and she had picked it up off the road and handed it back to him across her own bench.

Imani waited, with the jar turning slowly on its thread between them, and let him get there by himself.

"It won't last anyway," he said at last. "What I did last night. It only went through her because she's thin. A child, a commoner's child, with hardly anything in her. Every month they're all a little thicker, with the water. In a year it'll come back off Wren's skin the way it comes back off Ward's." He looked at the jar. "In two years I'll not see through anybody in this town."

"Then you'll want a way more than I do," said Imani.

"I'll want a way you can work with nobody in the room but you and a nurse."

"And the patient."

"And the patient."

She nodded, as if he had agreed to a dose, and stood up, because the ward bell had gone twice in the passage while they talked.

"Not now," she said, before he could reach for the pencil on the bench. "I've a ward. I've a girl in the end room who'll want to know why her belly hurts, and her mother, who'll want to know the same thing six times." She looked at him. "And you've been asleep in my chair for an hour with your face on my gloves. Go up the hill. Eat something Maren didn't watch you eat." She picked the register up off the bench and held it against her chest. "Tonight. After I close. Bring a pencil you don't mind losing."



He came down at nine, after the lamps were lit along the wall, with three pencils.

The ward at night was quieter than the ward by day, and not much: Bettany moving between the beds with a shaded lamp, a man coughing at the far end, Wren asleep in the end room with Nessa asleep in the chair beside her. Rylan was not there. Imani had sent him home at six to sleep, and he had gone, which said more about Imani than it did about Rylan.

The dispensary was lit. Imani was at her bench with the register open in front of her and a pen, writing up the day, and she did not look up when he came in. He sat down on the stool across from her and waited, as she had, and put his three pencils on the bench in a row.

She finished the line she was on. She blotted it. Then she turned the register round and pushed it across to him, open, not at the day's page but at the back, where the last leaves were still blank, thick good Kaldo paper ruled faint blue, and nothing on them.

"There," she said. "Where I'll see it every day."

He looked at the blank page. Then he picked up a pencil and began.

He drew a bath first, because the bath was the easy part: a long glass trough, longer than a man, the kind Ivo could pour if the big oven was big enough; water to the brim; and in it, on its back, a figure, a few lines, because he had never been able to draw a body. Imani, watching upside down, said nothing about the figure, which was kind of her.

"Warm," he said. "The water. Blood warm."

"Why water at all?"

"Because the thing I want to put round them reads better through water than through air. Skin into water is an easy step for it. Skin into air is a cliff." He drew a line across the bath above the figure, and then below it, and joined the ends: a long flat rectangle, like a picture frame laid on the water. "And because water holds them up, so a man with a broken back can lie still and not be moved."

"What's that?"

"The frame." He shaded it lightly. "Brass. Flat. Set all round with small stones, close together, like the teeth of a comb. The wall's sensor stones, the ones that feel who's near, pointed inward and cut finer." He drew it again, end on. "It goes over them slowly, the whole length, under the water. And every stone in it feels the field bend as it passes over them; and what bends it hardest is bone. Then the meat. Then the soft parts. Air in the chest hardly at all. A stone in a kidney, sharply, like a nail in a plank." He drew a wire from one end of the frame, and ran it off the edge of the bath. "And every stone sends what it feels down a wire. Like the wall sends to the board."

"To where?"

"To a print head." He drew it: a box, and a roller, and a sheet of paper going under it. "Over a sheet of paper. Like the printer. As the frame goes over her, the head goes over the paper, at the same pace, and every stone that feels bone puts down a little ink, and every stone that feels nothing puts down none." He put the pencil down. "And at the end of it, there's a sheet of paper, life size, with her on it. Not the outside. The inside. The bones pale. Everything else in greys. Straight through, front to back, as if you'd held her up to a lamp."

Imani did not say anything for a while. She was looking at the page; and then she was not looking at the page, she was looking at him, and he saw her look and did not know why.

"You haven't drawn me anything," she said, "in a long time."

He opened his mouth.

"The gloves were Tess's," said Imani. "The stethoscope, you made with your hands, in a night, and you didn't draw it, you just put it on my bench. The scalpels came out of the cellar on Bram's list. The needles, the pans, the still. You stopped drawing for me years ago. You draw for Tess. You draw for Bram." She turned the register a little toward herself, to see the frame the right way up. "I'd forgotten what your face did."

"What does it do?"

"It goes young," said Imani, and took the pencil out of his hand.

She did not go back round the bench to do it. She came round to his side of it, and looked at the stool he was sitting on, and at the page, and at the narrow space between his knees and the bench's edge, and evidently decided that the page was the important thing; and sat down sideways in his lap, with her back against his arm and her feet on the rung of her own stool, and pulled the register toward her, and began to correct his drawing from there.

"You've drawn the head under," she said.

He looked. He had. The figure in the bath lay with the water over its face, because he had drawn the water to the brim and the figure flat on the bottom.

"They'd drown."

"A sling," said Imani. She drew it, under the figure's neck, a strap from side to side of the bath, quick and certain. "Canvas. Under the back of the head and the neck, so the face is out and the ears are under. And something under the knees, or the back aches in ten minutes and they move, and you'll want them still." She drew that too. "How long does your frame take to go over them?"

"For a commoner, a few minutes. Longer for a knight." He thought about it. "The field has to sink in at their own rate, the same as the sense. A thick body pushes back. Rush it and you'll get fog."

"Then it's a commoner's tool," said Imani, not at all sorry. "Good. That's most of mine. How warm, and for how long?"

"Blood warm."

"Twenty minutes at the most," said Imani, and wrote it beside the bath in her own small hand: 20 MIN. NO MORE. "After that they chill, and a chilled body shakes, and a shaking body won't lie still, and your stones will draw me a ghost." She tapped the bath. "A clock on the wall where the nurse can see it from the frame. And a towel warming on a stone at the end."

"A towel."

"You'd have forgotten the towel," said Imani. "You always forget the towel."

He would have. He had his arm round her, under her ribs, because there was nowhere else for it to go, and he could feel the quick tap of her heart where his hand lay on her side, which she would have known he could feel; and she went on drawing as if she were alone at her bench.

"Two pictures," she said. "Not one."

"Why two?"

"Because one picture from the front tells me there's something there. It doesn't tell me how far in." She drew the figure again, small, on its side. "Front. Then turn them on their side, and the frame goes over again, and that's how deep. Like you had to look at the girl from the side in your head before you knew it lay behind the gut."

He looked at the second figure, on its side in her bath. It was what Earth's radiographers did, and had done for a hundred years: a front film and a side film. He had not drawn it. She had got there from one night in an end room.

"What do you call it?" said Imani.

"A mana scanner." He had had the name since the end room and had not said it to anybody.

Imani looked at the long glass trough with the figure lying in it. "It's a tank," she said. "Who works it? When you're not in the room."

"A nurse. I cut the stones once. Inside. Then they do one thing for the rest of their lives, and a nurse turns a handle."

"Bettany."

"Bettany, if Bettany wants it."

"Bettany wants everything with a handle," said Imani. She put the pencil down on the register, and looked at the page: the bath, the sling, the frame, the wire, the head, the paper; the side view; 20 MIN. NO MORE. Then she turned her head and looked at him from about four inches away.

"A hundred and two," she said.

"You're counting."

"I'm always counting." She put two fingers flat on the side of his neck, over the place, as if to make certain. "A hundred and four. You like drawing."

"I like drawing for you."

"Then you should have done it more often," said Imani, and kissed him.

It was a long kiss and she was not careful about it. She turned in his lap to do it properly, which put her knee against the bench's edge and knocked one of his pencils onto the floor, and neither of them looked for it. She had both hands in his hair, in his locs, pulling his head back, and he had both arms round her now and her whole weight against his chest, warm through her plain ward dress, and the stool under them creaked in a way that suggested it had not been built for this; and somewhere in the middle of it she laughed against his mouth, once, at nothing he could tell, and did not stop.

He felt the boy before he heard him: a thin quick note coming back up the passage at a trot, much too late to do anything about with Imani's hands where they were. The outer door of the dispensary opened.

It was Imani's ward boy, a long thin lad of sixteen with ears like a jug's handles, come back, he said afterward to anybody who would listen, for the copper basin he had left on the shelf at six, because the night nurse would want it at five and he would get the blame. He stood in the doorway with his hand on the latch.

Imani did not get up. She did not even take her hands out of Emeric's hair. She turned her head and looked at the boy over her shoulder.

"Second shelf," she said. "On the left. Take it. Shut the door."

The boy walked to the second shelf on the left with his eyes on the floor, and took down the basin, and held it in front of him in both hands like a shield, and went out, and shut the door with enormous care, so that it made no sound at all.

They listened to him walk away down the passage. He was walking very fast.

"He'll tell the whole ward by morning," said Emeric.

"He'll tell his mother," said Imani. "His mother does Maren's washing." She thought about it. "So Maren will know by noon. Ferra will know by breakfast, from me, before Maren gets the chance." She looked down at the register, still open on the bench beside them at the frame and the bath, and the pencil lying across the side view. "Draw me the head again," she said. "Properly. I can't tell what that box is."

He drew the head again.



The post came up with the tray on the Tuesday, at seven, as it always did, and Ferra read it out.

They were in Ferra's room at the top of the east stair, with the shutter open on a white sky and the fire not lit because none of them could be bothered; Ferra in her shift and his old grey coat, which she had taken off the chair in the night and not given back, sitting up at the head of the bed with the letters on her knees; Imani at the other end in one of Ferra's nightgowns, too long for her, with her back against the bedpost and her feet in Emeric's lap; and Emeric in his shirt in the middle, eating bread and butter, because it was Ferra's post and the rule in Ferra's room was that she read it to them and nobody touched it until she had.

Nobody needed anybody to do anything; it was before the second bell, and before the second bell nothing in Kaldo was allowed to need them, by a rule Ferra had written on the inside of her book's cover in the spring and which they all three kept better than they kept most rules.

"Paxton," said Ferra, opening one. "The coaches. He'd like one with a door that shuts, because the grain wagon's didn't, and his nephews were sick out of it all the way down in April." She put it on the pile. "Bryda. Eleven more for the summer term, three of them girls." She looked up. "One taught herself to read off the pay notices at the pithead. Bryda says she'll have Ilsa's chair in five years."

"Good," said Imani, with her eyes shut.

Ferra picked up the last one, and turned it over, and looked at the seal.

Red wax, a small crown over a shield, on thick cream paper nobody in the south could afford; addressed in a clerk's beautiful hand to the Chancellor of the Duchy of Kaldo, by her own name, correctly spelled.

Ferra broke it, and read it, and turned it over, and read the back.

"Well?" said Emeric.

"A lady of the capital," said Ferra, "whose name I'll not say at breakfast, because it's three lines long and two of them are places, presents her compliments to the Chancellor of Kaldo, and recalls with pleasure the Chancellor's gown at the Duke's investiture, and in particular its left sleeve, and wonders whether the Chancellor would consider sending the pattern for it to her seamstress in the capital, who has tried four times from memory and cannot get the band to sit." She turned the page over. "The lady will of course pay whatever the Chancellor thinks fair."

Imani opened her eyes.

"One of the three," said Emeric.

"One of the three duchesses," said Ferra. "And the cardinal's niece, I expect, by the next post."

He held out his hand for it, with great formality. Ferra looked at the hand, and then at him, and gave it to him; and he turned it over and read the direction on the back, and then held it out to her again across Imani's feet, with a small bow from the waist, sitting down, which was hard to do.

"Correspondence," he said, "for the Duchess."

Ferra looked at him over the letter for a long moment.

Then she took it back, and folded it, and put it on her knee, and picked up her tea.

"What's a duchess paid?" she said.

"Nothing. Board and lodging." He thought about it. "And a duke."

"Then it's a poor post," said Ferra, "and I'll keep the one I've got." She drank her tea. "I'll sell her the pattern. Abloh cut it, so Abloh gets half. Twenty Silver."

"Twenty?"

"She said fair," said Ferra. "She didn't say cheap. She has four failed sleeves in a press somewhere already. She knows what it's cost her not to have it." She wrote it down on the back of the lady's letter: 20 S. ABLOH HALF. "And the next one will be twenty-five, because by then three of them will want it, and the sleeve will be in fashion, and it'll be Kaldo's sleeve."

At the other end of the bed, Imani had pulled the nightgown's hem down over her knees and was looking at both of them with her chin on her chest and enormous disapproval.

"I spent a whole feast," she said, "avoiding duchesses. I went round the room twice to keep out of the way of one of them. I'll not have one at breakfast."

Emeric put his hand round her nearer foot, which had been in his lap the whole time, and was cold, as Imani's feet always were before eight in the morning in any weather.

"Duchess Imani," he said, "your feet are cold."

Imani looked at him.

"Then warm them," she said. "That's what you're for." And shut her eyes again, and left her foot where it was; and he held it in both hands and let a little warmth come up into his palms, the inward thing, a knight's trick, the way he had learned to on the road to court with Ferra cold behind him, until her toes stopped being stones and she made a small sound of no particular meaning and went back to sleep against the bedpost.

Ferra watched him do it over the rim of her cup. Then she turned to a fresh page in her book and wrote the date at the top of it, and under the date, small, a word he did not lean over to read.



The seed came up on the twenty-seventh of April, and Ferra was the one who saw it.

She went into the winter garden every evening at nine, not because she was a gardener, which she said she was not, often, but because the day's cartridge went in at eight, at the line, and the gauge had to be read an hour after, and nobody else in Kaldo read a gauge the way Ferra read one.

On the twenty-seventh she came out again a little after nine, and stood at the outer door of the pass-box, and did not shut it. Emeric was crossing the ward from the stair to the gate with Ward. She did not call out. She lifted the lantern, once, which was enough.

He went in after her, one at a time.

The dome at night was close and warm, and smelled of earth and Imani's comfrey; the screens were drawn round it outside, so that there was no light but Ferra's lantern. The air was thick on his skin, the way the Band's foot was thick: a hand, gently. Imani's beds round him were growing a little too well, and she had begun to keep a list.

Ferra stood at the edge of the ring of white stones with her lantern held low, and did not say anything.

In the middle of the black earth, where he had firmed it with his palm eleven days before, something had come up.

It was a finger tall. Not green: slate, the colour of a wet roof, with a sheen on it like satin where the lantern touched it. It came up not as one stem but as three or four, very thin, already twisting round one another as a rope twists, as if they had been at it in the dark for days and had not thought it worth mentioning. And on the top of it, riding up on the stem like a hat a size too small, were the two grey halves of the seed's coat, split clean apart, cupped open, with the fine raised net still on their outsides; and between them, very small, folded in on itself, something that might have been a leaf.

"There," said Ferra.

He knelt down on the stones.

He did not touch it. He felt it with his sense, and it was there as a stone was there, small and dense and quiet, and not a stone: warm the way a sleeping animal is warm. He had thought, he realised, that he would remember something. He did not, and he had known he would not, and he knelt there anyway.

"Put the lantern out," he said.

"Why?"

"I want to see something."

Ferra looked at him. Then she shut the lantern's shutter, all the way, and the dome went black.

For a breath there was nothing. Then, as his eyes went wide, there was: down in the grooves where the strands lay against each other, very faint, a blue. Not a light that lit anything; a light that was only there, the way the last of a coal is there in a dead grate at midnight, brightest halfway up the stem and fainter toward the grey coat at the top.

She had seen it already. He understood that afterward: she read the gauge with the lantern shuttered to a slit, as she always did, so as not to dazzle the needle, and in the slit's dark she had seen the blue, and had stood there alone with it, for how long he never asked, before she came out and lifted the lantern for him.

They stayed in the dark a while, long enough for his knees to go stiff on the stones. Ferra stood behind him in the dark, and after a while she put her hand on his shoulder, and left it there, and did not say anything, which was the thing she did that nobody else could do.

Then she opened the lantern a crack, and opened her book in the crack of light, and wrote.

27 APRIL, he read over her arm. 9 IN THE EVENING. UP. And a mark beside it, small: not a ring and not a box, a little tick, which he had never seen her use before. He asked her about it in the autumn. "It came up when it was told to," said Ferra, and that was all she ever said about it.

He stood up off the stones, and felt the rack of cartridges outside the pass-box, seven of them, full and bright, and thought about it for the whole length of one breath, plainly, the way Armand had said he would.

Ferra did not lift her book. She did not need to. She stood in the crack of the lantern's light with the book open in her hand at the gauge page, and the red line across it, and the word, and looked at him, and he looked back.

"At the line," he said.

"At the line," said Ferra, and went out through the pass-box first.



The bread was Maren's, and Maren hated it.

She did not say so. Maren did not say anything she could avoid saying. But she baked the first loaves on the last day of April with her mouth set like a seam, and set them on the scrubbed table to cool, and stood back from them with her arms folded over her scarred forearms. They looked like bread. That was the trouble. Brown, split along the top, a good crust; flour and water and salt and her leaven, which was older than Ferra.

But the flour had been to the Band.

Since Imani's oatcakes last summer there had been sacks in the Hod's hold on the up runs, under the empty racks: a sack a run, then four, because the class ate a weighed corner of Band oatcake on the yard days and Maren had said, to nobody, that if they were going to send her baking into the sky they might as well send her flour and let her make something a person could eat without breaking a tooth. The sacks came down tasting of nothing but flour, and Emeric could feel them across the yard when Tobe carried them in, a hundred pounds of it, warm and bright as a stone.

And the water in Kaldo's pipes had been dosed since November, so that anything baked with it carried a little of the Band already, the way the town's people did. Maren cut the flour, on nobody's say but her own: one part Band to three of plain, sifted together twice, because she did not trust a thing she could not see to mix itself.

Imani came up to taste it at eleven, with her book and her scales.

She weighed a slice. She ate it at the end of the long table, standing up, with her eyes on the clock over the kitchen door. She did not say anything for a while. Then she weighed a second slice, and looked at it, and did not eat it.

"Half," she said. "I'd eaten my breakfast at seven and I'm full again. From one." She wrote it. "A loaf of this fills a grown woman before she's had half of it."

"It's a side dish," said Maren.

Everybody looked at her. Maren did not, as a rule, say anything in her own kitchen while the six were in it.

"It's a side dish," she said again, to the bread, as if it had asked. "Not a meal. You don't sit down to a plate of it. You put a slice of it by the plate, like the pickle, and you eat your dinner, and you leave the pickle if you're full." She unfolded her arms. "Anybody who sits down to a plate of this," she said, "I'll not feed again."

Imani wrote that down too, word for word, and underlined it.

Bram ate three slices.

He did it that evening at the long table, in front of all of them, because Imani wanted to know where the line was for a man, and Bram said that if she wanted a big man she had one; and nobody could talk him out of it, least of all his daughter, who sat at the table's head with her pen not moving.

He ate the first slice the way he ate everything, steadily, with his elbows on the table. He ate the second slice more slowly. He stopped in the middle of the third and looked at it for some time, and then finished it, because he had said three; and then he put his big hands flat on the table either side of his plate and sat there.

Imani had her fingers on his wrist.

"How is it," she said.

"Heavy," said Bram.

"Where?"

"Here." He put his hand on his stomach, at the top. "Like I've eaten a stone." He thought about it. "Like I've eaten a good dinner and then somebody sat on me."

"Anything else?"

He did not answer at once. Then he put the back of his wrist to his nose, slowly, and took it away, and looked at it; and there was a bead of blood on it, bright, the size of a lentil.

Nobody at the table moved.

"That's it," said Imani. She did not raise her voice. She took a clean cloth out of her apron pocket and gave it to him and put his hand up to his face with it, and held his head forward with her other hand on the back of his neck, the way she would have done for a child at the ward door. "That's the line. Stomach first. Then the nose." She looked down the table at the rest of them, at the six and the knights, over the top of Bram's bent head. "That's Bram's line. And Bram's the biggest man in Kaldo."

"Heavy bread," said Bram, into the cloth, to nobody, a little thickly.

It stopped in a minute. He went to his bed at nine, which nobody had ever seen him do, and was perfectly well in the morning. Imani wrote all of it, on the page where the oatcakes were and the class's corners, every one weighed and dated; and on the opposite page, beside it, the other thing she had brought up the hill under her arm.

The rats.

Two years of them, in Bettany's back room that smelled of cedar shavings: a colony on plain feed and a colony on Band feed, and blood moved between them by the same small needles Imani used on people. She read the cards out that night after Bram had gone up, with Ferra checking the sums, and it took an hour, and Emeric did not interrupt her once.

Blood skipped the stomach. That was the whole of it, and the danger. Bread was safe, or as safe as anything, because a full stomach said stop; Maren's side dish was only the stomach's own rule written on a card. But blood went straight in. Fresh blood from a dense rat into a thin one did in a thimble what a loaf could not do: the thin rat bled at the nose and the gums inside an hour, and if the dose was big, it marked, faint lines under the fur on the forelegs; and if it was bigger, it died.

But blood that had sat a day in the cold room, in an open cup, carried nothing much. Nothing more than any matter carried. Rested blood was safe for anyone, the type matched; and plain blood into a dense rat did it no harm at all.

"So arm to arm only downhill," said Imani, and drew it in the register's margin: an arrow, pointing down. "A thin body can give to a thick one. A thick one never gives fresh to a thin one."

"A knight never gives to a commoner," said Ferra. "Not straight."

"Not straight. Not ever." Imani looked at him across the table. "Six months of the water in the town, and your knights are all the thickest people in Kaldo after you and Armand. If there's ever a field with a bleeding man on it and a knight beside him with his sleeve rolled up, I want every knight in this barony to know that, before the day comes, in words he can't get wrong."

"They'll know."

"I'll tell them," said Imani. "Not you." She shut the register. "You'll make it sound interesting."

And then Ferra read the other count.

It was the ward's sick days, month by month from the November the water went into the towers, set beside the same months of the year before so that nobody could say it was the season; and she read the two columns out side by side in her flat voice, without comment.

The sick days were down by more than a third.

Cuts closed in days that had taken weeks. There was a line in Imani's hand in the margin of February: the old men in the almshouse had not coughed through the winter, any of them, for the first time she could remember, and one of them had complained about it, because he had nothing to do at night now but sleep.

"Six months," said Ferra, and closed her book.

Nobody said anything for a while.

"So," said Saban, at last, from the far end of the table, where he had been sitting with his chin on his hand the whole of the hour, listening to rats. "When do we sell it?"

Imani looked at him.

"The bread," said Saban. "A loaf of it feeds like two, if Maren's right and people stop when they're full. The flour rides up on runs the Hod's making anyway. We've a hundred trainees down the line and more coming, and a town that grows every year." He spread his hands. "Half the flour for the same bellies. And every slice is a month of what you've just read us out of the register. When?"

"When they've been told," said Imani.

"Told what?"

"All of it." She put her hand flat on the register. "What it is. What it does. What it does past full. Before one corner of it goes over a counter in this town." She looked round the table, at all of them, and stopped at Ward. "And not by me alone. They've heard me say boil the water for nine years. They boil it because their children stopped dying, not because I said so." She looked at Ward a moment longer. "They'll need to see it."

Ward did not say anything.

But he turned his head, after a moment, and looked down the table at Rylan; and Rylan, who had been eating an apple, stopped eating it.



They said it on the market day, the sixth of May, a Thursday, from the ward's front steps at the foot of the square.

It was cold. It had rained in the night and the wind had come round into the north behind the rain, and it went through the square at nine like a blade through cloth; the crowd that came, came in coats. Sela had brought the whole school down in a double line and stood them along the bottom of the steps. Ward had brought the class, sixty-one of them, Noll at the end of the line on his toes. Maren stood by the bakery door with her arms folded, because it was her bread.

On the ward's door, at the top of the steps, a sheet of paper was pinned. It was a drawing, and Emeric had drawn it, plainly, in ink, in three pictures from left to right with no words under them: a plate with a dinner on it and a single slice of bread beside it, and a man eating, smiling; then the same man with a whole loaf in front of him, eating, holding his stomach, with blood running from his nose; then the same man lying on his back on a floor with lines drawn down his arms, and his eyes shut.

Some of the people who came up to look at it laughed. He had not drawn it well.

Imani spoke first, and not for long.

She told them what it was: Maren's bread, made with flour that had been up where the ships went, and Kaldo's own water; that it filled a person before they'd eaten half, and that was all the good of it and the danger too; that it would be at the bakery from tomorrow, a corner a person a day, the side of the plate and never the plate. Past full: the stomach first, an ache like a stone; then the nose and the gums; then lines; then, sometimes, worse. She said it once, in short words, as she said everything in the ward. Then she said, "Rylan," and stepped back.

Rylan came up the steps.

He stood at the top of them a moment with his back to the crowd, looking at the door, at the drawing. Then he turned round, and began unbuttoning his shirt.

The square went quiet from the front backward, the way a field goes quiet when a hawk goes over it. He gave his coat to Osric and pulled the shirt over his head in one movement, the way a man does who has stripped in a yard every morning of his life, and stood at the top of the ward steps in the north wind, bare to the waist.

And he began to steam.

It came off him as the cold hit him, off his shoulders first and then his back and arms and chest, thick and grey, going up and sideways in the wind in long rags the way it went off a horse run hard in winter; so that he stood at the top of the steps in front of half of Kaldo smoking like a pudding.

Somebody in the front of the school line laughed out loud, a high delighted little shriek, and was hushed.

"Rylan," said Osric, under his breath.

"What?"

"You're smoking."

Rylan looked down at himself. He looked at his own arms, and at the steam pouring up off them, and swore, quite loudly, in front of the whole school.

"Sorry," he said, to the square. "Sorry. It does it. I don't tell it to." He shut his eyes, and his face went tight, and the steam thinned, and wavered, and came back as strong as before. "It's the cold. It feels the cold and it does it, before I know. I can't..." He opened his eyes. "Give me a breath."

"Take your time," said Declan, beside Osric, kindly. "We've all morning."

The square laughed, the whole of it at once; and Rylan stood in the middle of it with the steam going off him and grinned, the old wide grin from the road south before the fire, and the steam thinned again and this time stayed thin.

Then he held his arms out, palms up, so that the people at the front could see.

The town knew the lines on his forearms: the rings from wrist to elbow, pale and raised, ring over ring, like wire set flush in wood. Every child in the school line had seen them on the wall walk. What the town had not seen was the rest. Above the elbow the rings went on, finer, branching, over the shoulder, and in one single fine line up the side of his neck that his collar had hidden every day the town had known him; across the chest and down the ribs; and when he turned round, slowly, they were all over his back as well, a pale net from the neck to the belt, silver where the cold light caught it, like the veins in a leaf held up to the sun.

He turned back round.

"That's what it looks like," he said, "when it works."

He did not raise his voice. He did not need to. Nobody in the square was talking.

"I was put forward for the rite when I was twenty," said Rylan. "There was no test. There was a captain with a list. They took us to the capital, to a stone room. No windows. A drain in the floor." He looked at the school, at the front of the line, and then away from them, over their heads. "Nineteen of us. On the floor, on our backs, in our shirts, in rows. Mages at our heads in the dark, taking turns. They put it into you from outside, in waves, and you lie there, and it comes again. Nine hours."

He stopped.

"I don't know how many came out," he said. "Nobody told us. Nobody counted where we could hear. I think one in ten walked out of that room. I think it was two of us. Maybe three." He looked down at his own hands. "The night before, the four of us from my unit, we did what you do. Each man tells the man beside him what to say, and who to, if he doesn't come out. I couldn't write. So I held three of them in my head." He was quiet. "I walked out with all three. I carried one, the next year, on foot, to a woman at her door two days out of the capital. The other two I've had so long I don't trust the words any more, so I've never taken them."

Ferra spoke from the side of the steps with her book open, in her flat voice, which carried in the cold better than anybody's.

"One in ten is his figure," she said. "Not mine. I can't check it. Nobody's ever let anybody count." She looked up then, at the four of them: Rylan smoking faintly at the top of the steps, and Osric with his coat over his arm, and Declan, and Ward. "But if it's right, then there are four men on these steps who walked out of rooms like that. And for every one who walked out, nine didn't." She shut the book on her finger. "That's thirty-six. For these four."

Nobody said anything.

"That was a stone room with a drain in the floor," said Ferra, "and priests who'd done it a hundred times. And it still went nine in ten." She looked out over the square, at the young men at the back of it by the stalls, the ones who had laughed at the drawing. "You'd have a loft."

Then Rylan told them how the others died.

He told it plainly, as he had told it once at a fire by a well, to four men, years before; as a man gives evidence. He did not tell them the men's names. He told them what happened to a body when more went into it than it could take: the ache first, deep in the belly, like a fist closing, so that men drew their knees up on the stone. Then the bleeding: the nose, and then the gums, and then the eyes, at the corners, a little, like tears. Then the lines. "They start," he said. "Like these. Here." He touched the inside of his own wrist. "They start, and on a man who's going to live they go on, and close up, and go pale, in a day or two. On a man who isn't, they start and they don't close. They stay open. Red. And they bleed, under the skin, along where they were going, so his arm goes dark along the lines, like somebody's drawn on him with a stick dipped in a bruise." He looked at his own arm. "And the heat. They burn. You can feel it off them a yard away, lying beside them. It goes on two days. Three. Some of them they carried out still breathing and they died after, somewhere I never saw."

He was quiet a moment.

"What I remember is their hands," he said. "Men pulled at their arms. They tore their shirts off to get at them, because they itched under the skin where the lines were, and they couldn't stop. They pressed their hands flat on the stone, because the stone was cold. The man on my left held my hand." He looked at his right hand, and opened it, and closed it. "Nine hours. I couldn't feel it by the end. In the morning, when they came to take him out, I still had hold of it, and they had to take my fingers off him one at a time."

A woman near the front of the crowd had her hand over her mouth.

Somebody at the back called out, a young man's voice, half laughing, what everybody under twenty in the square was thinking: then how come the yard lot eat it?

"Because they eat a corner," said Imani, "under me. On the days I say. Weighed, on a scale, by Bettany, with their names on it." She held up one finger. "And the same day, before they sleep, they put their hands on a stone and drain off what they've taken, into the stone, till the needle says stop. Every one of them. Every time." A second finger. "And they go in the river at six in the morning in February, which you've all seen them do and laughed at." A third. "And when I say stop, they stop. And if they don't, they go home, and they don't come back to the yard, ever." She lowered her hand. "Eating it without the draining isn't the yard's road. It's the rite without the priests."

"Arthur's a knight!" called the voice at the back. "Arthur never went to no stone room!"

Heads turned. Arthur was in the class's line at the bottom of the steps, at its far end beside Mira, in his yard coat; and he stood very still, the way he had stood at eleven when Ward first looked at him, and did not move.

"Show 'em, Arthur!" somebody else called. "Show 'em your arms!"

Arthur looked at Ward. Ward did not do anything at all.

"No," said Arthur.

He said it to the square. He did not shout it. He had Sela's voice for that, the voice that left a person nowhere to stand.

"There's nothing on them," he said. "That's the point. Half my life in the yard, and the river all winter, and the stones, and there's nothing on my arms, and there never will be. The slow road doesn't leave anything." He looked at the young men at the back, by the stalls. "That's how you know which road you're on. If you're getting lines, you're on his." He nodded up the steps at Rylan. "And he's the one who walked out."

He looked down at his boots afterward. Mira, beside him, put her shoulder against his and left it there.

Somebody near the stalls asked Ward, then, the question everybody had wanted to ask Ward for nine years and nobody ever had: and you, sir? How many on your floor?

Ward looked at the man. He looked at him for so long that the man put his hands in his pockets.

"One in ten," said Ward.

That was all he said, the whole morning.

Emeric said the last of it, beside Rylan, who was buttoning his shirt with the steam still going up faintly off his collar; and the rules were short. A corner a day. At the bakery, from tomorrow, against your name, in the book. One hand may fetch for a house, as it fetches the water, and signs for every name it takes. Nobody under fourteen at all. And anybody who felt the ache, or bled at the nose, was to come to the ward that hour and say so, and nobody would be in trouble for it, ever, for any reason.

The square broke up slowly. At the back of it, by the cooper's stall, a knot of young men stood looking up at the drawing on the ward door, at the third picture. One of them said something. The others laughed.

Emeric saw them laugh. So did Rylan, at his side.

"They'll do it anyway," said Rylan. "Some of them."

"Then we'll tell them again."

"You can tell a boy a thing a hundred times." Rylan finished his top button. "He hears it the hundred and first, from somebody he likes better." He looked at the knot of young men a moment longer. "I'd have laughed," he said. "At their age. At a drawing."



They told them again.

The drawing went to the press. Not the cellar's press; the plain one in the town's print shop, with anybody's hands on the lever, because there was nothing in it that needed hiding. The three pictures across the top, and under them in big plain letters for those who could read, the rules, and Rylan's count, and Ferra's sum, thirty-six. Two thousand sheets the first week. Sela read it out at the school on the Friday and the Monday and the Friday after, to every class from the smallest, and made the older ones read it back to her aloud. Doran read it from his pulpit on the Sunday without being asked, and so did the holdings' priests when Ferra's sheets reached them. The Stewards' Council took it: Bryda posted it at Grayrock's pithead, Paxton at Milford's mill and inns, and Aster at Wayford's posting house, though the bread would not reach Wayford for months, because Aster said a thing worth telling was worth telling before it was needed. Maren had the drawing printed small on the paper the bread was wrapped in, so that nobody in Kaldo could buy a corner without carrying the warning home round it. The bakery kept a book, ruled by Ferra: the day, the name, the house, the corners, and the hand that took them; a corner a day against each name, and no more.

Ferra wrote all of it down. Every reading, every posting, every parish and inn and pithead, with the date and the place and who had said it, in a column of her own on a page headed THE WARNING. She did not explain why. Nobody asked her.



The first blood went into a body in Kaldo on the twelfth of May, at a little after two in the morning, and it went in by hand.

The woman was a cooper's wife from the river lane, her fourth child born at midnight, a girl, alive and loud; and after the afterbirth the blood would not stop. Imani had seen women bleed white in an hour on that bed while she kneaded and packed and pressed, and some of them lived and some did not. This one was going white. The bleeding was slowing under Imani's hand, and not fast enough, and the woman's lips were grey.

"Bettany," said Imani. "The dish. And the book."

The white dish was on the bench in a quarter of a minute: the clumping test, the woman's drop and the book's drops, side by side. B. The register gave four names in Kaldo with the letter B who lived within a quarter of an hour of the ward and were on Imani's card as willing; and the nearest was in the dock quarter.

She was in the register because of her sister, who bled in childbed most years; she had come in alone in March to be typed, past the grandmothers and their cup of blood that was a year off your life, because Imani had said the families of those on the card would be first in line. She came up the hill at half past two in her shawl, frightened and furious about being frightened, and lay down on the next bed, and gave Imani her arm.

A needle left in each arm. A rack of the glass syringes, the slim ones off the Forge's plate, standing in a pan of warm salt wash. Imani filled one from the dock woman's arm. Bettany carried it the four feet between the beds in both hands, fast, without looking at it, and gave it to the second nurse, who emptied it into the cooper's wife's arm, slowly, steadily, with her thumb; and by the time she had, Imani had filled the next, and the empty barrel went straight back into the wash before what was left in it could set. Blood set in glass in minutes. So it had to be fast, and it had to be steady, and it had to be counted; and Imani counted the syringes out loud, under her breath, one, two, three, the way she counted a pulse, and did not look up.

Emeric stood at the end of the beds and watched the cooper's wife's lips.

It was the slowest thing he had ever watched. What she needed even more than blood in that first hour was salt water in her veins, enough to give her heart something to push; and Imani had it warm in a jug beside the rack, and was giving it the same way, a syringe at a time, between the blood.

He could have done it in a minute. He could feel the water in the jug and the woman's thin veins; he could draw the one into the other along the needle's line by hand, a steady stream, faster than any nurse's thumb.

"I could keep the salt water going," he said. "Along the line. Steady. While you do the blood."

Imani did not look up from the syringe she was filling.

"My patient," she said. "Not your trick."

He did not move.

"Seven," said Imani, to the syringe. "Bettany. Eight." And then, still without looking at him, in the same voice: "If you do it tonight, I'll need you tomorrow night, and the night after, and every night a woman bleeds in this town, and you'll not be here. Bettany's thumb will be. Eight."

He stood where he was and kept his hands at his sides, and watched the nurses do it.

They stopped at twenty-two. Imani's number. By then the cooper's wife's lips had colour in them, and her pulse under Imani's fingers had come down out of the gallop it had been in, and the bleeding had stopped under Imani's hand; and at four she opened her eyes and asked for the baby.

The dock woman ate her dinner in the ward.

Imani gave it to her at noon at a table by the main ward's door, where everybody who came in and went out could see: Maren's stew, a heel of plain bread, small beer, an apple. She ate all of it, sitting up straight with the bandage on her arm, and talked to the nurses about the price of fish. Imani did not say anything to her about it. She sent her home with the day's wage from Ferra's fund.

The dock quarter's column in the register had four names in it in April. By the end of May it had thirty-one. Ferra noticed it, not Imani. She noticed it in the count, and said so at the table, and Imani went on eating her dinner and did not look up, and did not need to.



Bram's mill was finished on the twentieth, and Bram said it was the first thing out of the cellar he could keep.

It stood in the long shed beside bay three on a bed of cut stone sunk five feet into the ground: a horizontal boring mill, Emeric had called it, and the works called it the borer. The cellar had made its heart. In March and April the big room's tube had built, one at a time, the parts no shop in the world could make true: the spindle and its bearings, ground round to a hair; an eight-foot straightedge in iron, flat from end to end; the lead screw, in short sections, each one checked against the last; and the masters for a heavy lathe, whose frame still lay in pieces on the shop floor; and the boring bar, a long round bar of steel with a slot for the cutter, straight as a plumb line. They had come out of the cellar one by one in Bram's cart under sacking, and Bram's shop had built the rest of the machine round them.

Now the bar went through a cylinder casting the size of a barrel, and the cutter went round slowly and took a curl of grey iron off the inside, and the carriage crept along the bed at a pace you could see only by a chalk mark; and when it came out at the far end the bore was true, and Bram's gauge read the same at the front and the middle and the back.

Bram looked at the gauge a long while.

"Every engine I've built," he said, "I've waited on the cellar for its cylinder. Every one. One at a time. You downstairs in the dark with your hands in the tube, and me upstairs waiting." He took the gauge out. "Now I don't."

"You'll still want the cellar for the masters."

"For the masters, once." Bram put his hand flat on the mill's bed, on the cold oiled iron, the way he put his hand on a horse. "This makes the rest. Any shop with this and a man who knows it can bore a cylinder. Any shop in four baronies." He looked at it. "If the cellar fell in tonight, I'd still have this in the morning."

On Earth an ironmaster's cannon borer had once bored the cylinders of an engine nobody else could make tight, and everything had come after it. Emeric did not say so.

"It's the first thing out of the cellar," said Bram, "that I can keep." And went off down the shed to see about the next casting, and did not look back at it, which was how Emeric knew how much he had meant it.



The table met on the twenty-second, a Saturday, and ruled the printing.

It was Saban's idea. He had sold grain half his life; you never sold this year's seed until next year's was in the barn. "We sell last year's," he said. "Whatever we're past, we sell. Whatever we're still on, we keep. And when we move up, the one we've just left goes on the shelf for sale." He spread his hands on the table. "One step behind ourselves. Always. They're buying the stair we've already climbed."

Ferra gave it its rungs. Three: what went for sale, to anybody, anywhere, at the price of the paper; what went to the holdings and no farther, the trade books, bricks and lime and kilns and pumps and rail work and bookkeeping on her own forms; and what was never printed, which was anything with a stone in it, and steam, and steel, and the lightning, and everything that had ever come out of the room. The table argued over every line for an hour; Armand argued about the lightning and lost, and Imani wanted her wound book on the first rung and got it.

On the Monday the presses started on the primers. Letters first, with a picture for every letter, and then numbers; for sale, in every market in four baronies, at the cost of the paper and a copper over.

That week the first courtyard block in the first new ward was lived in, eight families round a yard with a pump in it and a privy block that drained to the new sewer; the second rotary kiln was lit at the lime works, and burned for a day and a night before anybody let it cool; and on the old stone piers of the river bridge, which had stood empty since before the plague, Bram's men began to bolt down the first steel of the new one.



The bakery on the corner of the lower ward sold its last corners at six on the twenty-fifth of May, as it did every day: a corner a name, in the book, and the paper round it.

The baker had baked for the lower ward for thirty years and knew most of the names. He knew the four lads who came in together just before six, every evening, for near three weeks now: two carters' sons, the chandler's boy from the river lane, and a quiet one who'd been on the school's books and wanted, his mother said, to be a knight. A corner each against their own names in the book, and a corner against each name in their houses, a mother's, a sister's, as the rule allowed, every one signed for. They paid their half-copper and took their corners wrapped in the paper with the drawing on it, and put them inside their shirts, and did not eat them at the counter, as most people did at the warm end of the day.

"Saving it for your suppers?" he said, as he said most evenings.

"That's right," said the chandler's boy, and grinned at him, and they went out together into the lane.

He thought they were sensible lads, to keep it for their suppers and not wolf it in the lane like the carters. He thought so for two more days.
