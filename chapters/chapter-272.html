Chapter 272 - Zale Sees the Ships

There was no wind on the morning Zale came back, and his sail was full.

Emeric saw it from the end of the long jetty on the Mere, at a little after five, with his coat buttoned against the dawn chill and his hands in his pockets, waiting for the Hod. She was due down at half past from her night at the Band, and he liked to be on the jetty when she came, though there was nothing for him to do there; Old Edrin brought her down as gently as a leaf, and Ned's crew had the hoses on her before she had stopped swinging, and nobody needed a duke standing about. He came anyway. It was the one half hour of the day nobody asked him anything.

The Mere was like a sheet of tin. Not a ripple on it, end to end. Over the town behind him the smoke from the first chimneys went straight up into a sky that was going from grey to white, and stood there in long thin threads, not leaning at all; and down at the far end of the Mere, where it opened into the river, the reeds stood up out of their own reflections as still as if they had been drawn.

And coming up the river, into the Mere, against the current, was a coaster.

He knew her. Everybody in Kaldo knew her by now: a broad-beamed old trader out of Emberfell with a black hull and a patched brown mainsail and a figurehead of a woman holding a fish, who came up the river three or four times a year with spices and seeds and things nobody in Kaldo had a name for. She was coming up under sail, slowly, with a little white water at her bow; and her sail was full. Not drawing a little, the way a sail does in a breath of air. Full, round, bellied out from the yard like a pillow, as if there were a good steady wind behind her, in a dawn so still that the smoke stood up off the chimneys like masts.

Emeric looked at it for a while.

Then he opened his sense, a little, toward the river, the way he would have turned his head toward a sound.

At her stern, by the tiller, there was a deep slow note. Steady. The deepest note on that river. He had felt it first across a table at the decaday, the winter before last, and had wondered at it then, and had not asked, because Zale had not said.

The coaster came on up the river into the Mere with her sail full.

Behind Emeric, over the castle, a long dark shape came down out of the white sky.

The Hod came down the way she always came, slowly, out of the high air above the town with the sun not yet on her, so that she was a long grey shadow against the white for most of the way and only turned to iron and brass and glass in the last few hundred feet; with her two hulls side by side and her fins out and the faint violet glow along her keel that you could see at dawn if you knew to look. She came down over the castle and over the station and out over the Mere, very low, turning, her shadow going across the tin-flat water toward the jetty; and she came to rest a few feet over the water at the jetty's end, as she always did, swinging very gently on nothing, while Ned's men ran out along the boards with their hoses.

Emeric did not watch her. He was watching the coaster.

Out in the middle of the Mere, two hundred yards off, the coaster's sail had gone slack.

It went all at once, as if somebody had cut a rope. The great brown belly of it fell in and the canvas hung straight down from its yard in long folds, and flapped once, and was still; and the coaster, with nothing driving her any longer, slowed, and slowed, and began, very slowly, to turn on the current, broadside to it, drifting back down toward the river's mouth with her bow swinging round toward the reeds.

Nobody on her deck was doing anything about it.

There were five men on her deck. Emeric could feel them, their small bright notes, and see them, black against the white water: four of them standing in a line along her rail with their faces turned up, and one at her stern, by the tiller, with his hand still on it and his face turned up too, not steering. All five of them were looking at the Hod.

The coaster drifted. Her bow came round to the reeds and nosed into them, softly, with a long rustling sound that came across the water, and stopped.

The man at the stern let go of the tiller.

He sat down on the deck, all at once, the way a man sits who has been standing on a ladder and finds the ladder gone; and from two hundred yards off, across the flat water, in the quiet of the dawn, Emeric heard him begin to laugh.

He laughed for a long time.



They had to row out to him. The coaster's crew could not stop looking up long enough to get her off the reeds, and their master was no use to them: he was sitting on his own deck with his back against the tiller post and his arms round his knees, and every time he looked at the Hod he started again. Ned sent two men in the jetty's skiff, and they got a line on her bow and towed her off the reeds and in to the jetty's far side; and all the way in, Zale sat on his deck and looked up at the Hod swinging over the water a few feet off the jetty's end, with her two hulls and her fins and her brass, and laughed until the tears ran down into his beard.

He came up the ladder onto the jetty at last, wiping his face with both hands. He was as Emeric remembered him: a big man, broad and long, tall enough that he had to duck under the Hod's hose where it ran across the jetty at head height; a sailor's face, burned dark by every sun between Emberfell and the end of the world, and a sailor's eyes, very pale in the dark of it, creased at their corners into a hundred small white lines from looking into the light; and handsome, still, after all of it, in a way that had made Emeric wonder at their first meeting how anybody kept their looks at sea for twenty years. He stopped at the top of the ladder and stood there, swaying a little, as men do for the first minutes off a deck; and looked at the Hod again, close now, a few yards off, her iron flank hanging over the water at the height of his chest.

He put out his hand and touched her.

She swung a little, away from his hand, on nothing, and came back.

"She's not tied," said Zale.

"No."

"She's not on anything."

"No."

"She came down out of the sky." Zale looked at Emeric. His pale eyes were very wide and very wet. "I saw her. Over the castle. She came out of the sky like a gull off a cliff." He looked back at the Hod. "How long?"

"How long what?"

"How long have you had her? How long have you been doing this?" Zale had his hand flat on the iron again. "You had me at your table at midwinter with my maize and my spices and my bloody chronometer, telling you how far west I'd been. Telling you about the edge of the sea. And you had this." He started to laugh again, and stopped himself, and it came out of him as a sort of cough. "How long?"

Emeric told him.

Zale stood with his hand on the Hod's flank and looked at him, and then up along her, to her bow, where Old Edrin had come out of the casting seat's hatch onto her foredeck in his sheepskin to see what all the noise was about, and stood there looking down at the big sailor on the jetty with his pipe in his teeth.

"Years," said Zale.

"Years."

"And nobody told me."

"Nobody told anybody."

Zale took his hand off the Hod. He turned round on the jetty and looked at Emeric, and the laughter was gone off his face, all of it, as fast as a squall goes off the water; and Emeric thought, for a moment, that he was angry. He would have had a right to be.

"She knew," said Zale. "Didn't she?"

He did not say who. He did not need to.

Ward had come down the jetty behind Emeric, from the town, because Ward came to the jetty when the Hod came in too, and did not admit it. He stopped beside Emeric. He looked at Zale, and Zale looked at him, and something went between them that Emeric could not read; the look of two men who had known each other's mother, one way and another, a long time.

"She has a suit," said Ward.

Zale looked at him.

Then he put his head back, and laughed again, harder than before, so hard that he had to put his hand on the Hod's flank to hold himself up, and she swung away from his hand and he nearly went into the Mere; and Ward caught him by the back of his coat with one hand and held him on the jetty, without any expression at all, while Zale laughed.

"A suit," said Zale, when he could. "She has a suit. Of course she has a bloody suit." He wiped his face. "Oh, she'll pay for that. She sat at my table at midwinter and let me tell her about the edge of the world, and she'd been up there." He looked up at the sky over the castle, white now, with the sun coming. "She'd been up there. Over my head."

"She was told not to say," said Emeric.

"She was told not to say," said Zale, "and she didn't, which is the first time in her life she's ever done as she was told by anybody, and it had to be this." He let go of the Hod. He was still grinning; but under the grin, Emeric thought, there was something that did not hold it against her at all, and that was proud of her, and that would not have said so for a ship. "Can I go up?"



He went up. Old Edrin took him round the Hod for an hour, from the bow to the stern and down into the hold and up into the casting seat, while Ned's men filled her; and he came down the gangway at the end of it white in the face under the sunburn and very quiet, and sat on a bollard at the jetty's end with his hands between his knees for a long while, looking at the water.

Then he came up to the castle to breakfast.

He brought his sea chest. He had always brought it, every time; it was what he came up the river for, in the end, more than the trading. It went up to the hall on two men's shoulders and was set down at the end of the long table, where it had been set at midwinter and in the spring; and after breakfast, with the table cleared and the six round it, and Maren in the doorway with her arms folded because she wanted to see what he had brought this time, Zale opened it.

He took out the book first.

It was his own book, his fair copy of the first half of Vey's notebook, that he had been working on since the spring: a ship's logbook bound in oilcloth, with Vey's words copied into it line by line in Zale's big slanting hand, and under each line, in pencil, smaller, the words they made when you said them aloud in the language Zale had heard in a ship's fo'c'sle three winters running and had not known was a language. He had taken it away in March with half of it done. He laid it on the table now, open at the last page.

"The way down," he said.

They looked.

It was a drawing, copied from Vey's: a long low shape that was the Isle of Shards as Vey had seen it from the sea, with its cliffs on the west and its desert behind; and a dark round place in the desert near its middle, which was the pitch lake, that Emeric had seen with his own eyes from the K Endeavor's deck on four night runs and never set foot near. And beside the pitch lake, a mark, small, like a keyhole.

Under the drawing, in Vey's hand and then in Zale's, three lines.

"Where the black water breathes," read Zale, "go down by the old stair that the sand has eaten. The stair goes to the sea's door. The sea's door is open at the low water, and shut at the high, and there is no third time." He put his finger on the keyhole. "It's a sinkhole. On the lake's east shore. With a stair cut in it, gone under the sand. It goes down to water. The water goes through, under the island, to the sea. You can get in at low tide, and out at low tide, and not otherwise." He looked up. "That's the way to his cave. That's the way he went in. He drew it so somebody could go after him."

Nobody said anything.

"And the second half of his book," said Zale, "the half I've not got, is in there. With him. That's what the first half says." He shut it. "I've read the first half nine times. There's nothing else in it worth a fish. He's telling you how to get to the second."

Ferra wrote something in her book.

Ward was looking at the drawing. He had stopped looking at it as a drawing some time ago; Emeric could see him looking at it the way he looked at a map before a march.

"The sinkhole's on the lake's east shore," said Ward. "That's a mile and a half from the Kesrath tower on the ridge."

"About that."

"And the patrol boats."

"Twice a night, round the west cliffs. Duvrenne's. Nothing on the east side but the sand and the lake and the lamp on the tower." Zale looked at him. "You've been there."

Ward did not say anything.

Zale looked round the table at all of them; at Ferra writing, and Bram with his pipe cold in his fist, and Saban who had not moved, and Imani, and the old man in the fur at the end, and Emeric. He looked at them for a long time.

"You've been there," he said again, slowly, "in that," and he tipped his head toward the window, toward the Mere, where the Hod was swinging at the jetty's end in the morning sun. "On the Isle. In the night. Where nobody can see you." He was quiet a moment. "That's why you wanted the book."

"Yes," said Emeric.

"What for?"

Emeric looked at Ward, and at Ferra, and at the others round the table; and he decided, as he had decided in the spring, how much.

"For what's in the cave," he said. "I don't know what's in it. Nobody does. Vey went down there a thousand years ago and didn't come up, and his first half says the second's with him, and his drawings say there's more than a book." He met Zale's eyes. "I want to go and see. I'd like you to come."

"Why me?"

"Because you read him," said Emeric. "Nobody else can. And because you've been to sea for twenty years, and none of us have, and the way in is through the sea's door at low water."

Zale looked at him a long time.

Then he looked down at his book, his oilcloth logbook with Vey's words in it and his own under them; and something went across his face that Emeric had seen on Tess's, the night she found the rail in her room.

"I'd have gone if you'd asked me at midwinter," he said. "I'd have gone in a rowing boat."

"I know."

"You didn't ask."

"I didn't have a way to get you there you could live through."

Zale looked out of the window at the Hod.

"No," he said slowly. "No, I suppose you didn't." He shut the book. "And now you do."



He had more in the chest. He always had more in the chest; it was the joke of the table, by now, and Maren in the doorway had been waiting for it.

There was a billet of wood, first. Short, the length of his forearm, as thick as his wrist, dark and close-grained and heavy, with a sheen on it as if it had been oiled; and when Zale set it down on the table it went down with a knock like a stone. He told them about the tree it came from: on the last islands, where the charts stopped, a low twisted tree as hard as iron, that greased your hands when you cut it, as though it sweated; with small blue flowers, pale, all over it in the spring. The islanders made mallets of it, and clubs, and the pins for their rudders, and nothing else would do for a rudder pin, they said, because it never wore and never wanted grease even in salt water.

Emeric picked it up.

It was very heavy. It was heavier than any wood had a right to be, and it was greasy to the touch, faintly, all over, as if it had been handled by a cook. He turned it in his hands.

He had thought he knew what it would be. He had thought so in the spring, when Zale first described the tree, and he had not said the name then, because there was no saying it to Zale; it was a name from the other side of everything, in a language nobody in this world had ever heard. He did not say it now. But he asked Maren, in the doorway, for a bucket of water; and when she brought it, he dropped the billet in.

It sank. Straight to the bottom, with a soft knock on the bucket's floor, and lay there under the water, dark, with a few small bubbles coming off it.

Zale was staring at it.

"Wood floats," he said.

"Most wood."

"All wood floats. That's what wood's for." Zale looked at the bucket. "I've carried that in my chest for a month. I never put it in the water. Why would I put it in the water?"

Emeric looked at the billet lying on the bottom of the bucket.

He was thinking about a shaft. A long iron shaft, coming out through the bottom of a ship's hull at the stern, turning, all day, all night, in seawater, with a screw on the end of it; and the bearing it turned in, where it went out through the hull, that had to be tight enough not to let the sea in and loose enough to let the shaft turn. A bearing in salt water that could not be greased, because the sea washed the grease out. Every steamer on Earth had had one, for a hundred years, until somebody thought of something better. And what they had made it of, for a hundred years, was this. A wood that sank. A wood that greased itself.

He did not say any of that.

"How much of it is there?" he said.

"On the islands? Whole hillsides." Zale was still looking at the bucket. "They burn it."

"Bring me a ton of it. Next time."

Zale looked at him.

"A ton," he said. "Of wood that sinks."

"Billets like that one. As long as you can get them. And don't let them burn any more of it than they have to."

Zale looked at him a moment longer, with his pale eyes narrow in the brown of his face, the way he looked, Emeric thought, at a sky he did not like. Then he shrugged, the big easy shrug of a man who has carried stranger cargoes for stranger men; and reached into the chest again, and brought out a plank.

It was a plank of yellow-brown wood, rough-sawn, as long as the table was wide; and Zale said it was from another tree on the same islands that the worm would not touch and the sea would not rot, and that the islanders built their boats of it and the boats lasted longer than the men; and Bram took it out of his hands before he had finished talking and turned it over and smelled it and put his thumbnail into its end grain, and put it down again, and looked at Emeric with an expression of naked greed.

There was a second sack of the dark bitter beans he had brought at midwinter, that Maren had made into the thick sweet drink; bigger than the first, because Maren had told him to bring more or not come back, and he had believed her. She took it out of his hands at the door before it reached the table.

And then, at the very bottom of the chest, a sack.

It was a small sack, of coarse grey cloth, tied at the neck with a knot of the same stuff, and it was nearly full of something light; and Zale took it out and put it on the table without much interest and said it was nothing, it was ballast money. The last islands paid him in it sometimes when they had nothing else, for a bale of needles or a pot of salt, because they did not use coin; they spun the stuff in it into thread and wove the thread into cloth, and the cloth was the best thing on the islands, and they would not part with that, so they paid him in what the cloth was made of. He took it because it was light and filled a corner. He had not looked in this one.

He untied the knot and turned the sack's mouth down on the table.

What came out was a soft grey cloud.

It was like wool, but finer than any wool Emeric had ever seen in Kaldo, and whiter under its dust; tufts of it, a handful each, each one a tangle of short fine soft fibres clinging round a hard brown pip at its heart, the size of a pea. They lay on the long table among the cups like a little fallen fog. Maren, in the doorway, came forward a step without meaning to.

"The wool that grows on a bush," said Zale. "That's what they call it. It grows in a pod on a little bush and the pod splits and that's in it." He picked up a tuft and pulled it out between his fingers, and the fibres drew out long and fine and white. "Spins lovely. Their women spin it with a stick, sitting on the beach. You never saw cloth like it."

Emeric looked at the tufts on the table.

He did not pick one up. He did not need to. He had known what it was from the first grey edge of it coming out of the sack's mouth, the way you know a face in a crowd before you have seen it properly; and his chest had gone very still.

He said, "How much of this did they give you?"

"That sack," said Zale. "Once. They give me a sack when they've nothing else."

"The seeds. In it. Are they all like that? With a pip?"

Zale looked at the tuft in his hand, and picked the brown pip out of it with his thumbnail, and looked at that.

"I suppose so. I never looked." He dropped it on the table. "Why?"

"Can I have it?" said Emeric. "The sack. As it is. I'll give you whatever you were going to get for it."

Zale looked at him for a long moment, the way he had looked at him over the sinking wood.

"You want to grow it," he said.

"I want to try."

"It won't grow here. It's hot on those islands. Hot all year. There's no winter." Zale shrugged. "Take it. It's ballast."

"Bring me more," said Emeric. "Next time. As much as they'll give you. And ask them how they grow it."

Zale was already putting things back in his chest.

"I'll ask," he said, over his shoulder; and Emeric knew from the way he said it, easily, the way a man says he will ask about something that is not on his mind, that he would forget.

The sack went to Milford with the maize, on the next morning's train, to Hollis, with a note in Emeric's hand that said: Keep it. Keep it dry. Don't plant it. And at the bottom, because Hollis would want to know: I'll tell you why in a year or two.



Finn found Zale on the quay that evening, at the coaster's side, where Zale was sitting on a bollard watching his crew stow the empty water casks; and stood beside him for a while saying nothing, which for Finn, at seventeen, took a good deal.

Then he said, "Can you teach me knots?"

Zale did not look round.

"What knots?"

"All of them. The ones your men tie." Finn had been watching them for an hour, from the quay's end, while they made the coaster fast and unbent her sail and stowed it; the way their hands moved in the rope without their eyes on it, fast, the line going over and under and through and pulling tight in one movement, every time, as if it had been a single thing they did and not a dozen. "I can tie a reef knot. And the hitch for a horse. That's all."

Zale looked at him then: a long look, up and down, the way he looked at a sky.

"Why?"

Finn opened his mouth and shut it again.

"I don't know," he said honestly. "I've never been on a boat. Not a proper one. Not out there." He looked past the coaster, down the river, toward where the river went, past the reeds and the Mere and the long reaches below, to the coast and the sea that he had never seen. "I just want to know."

Zale looked at him for a while longer.

Then he reached down by the bollard's foot, where a coil of old line lay that his men had cut off a frayed sheet, and picked up an end of it, a fathom long, and held it out.

Finn took it.

"Bowline," said Zale. He took the other end of the coil and tied it, once, in front of Finn's face, slowly, so that he could see: a loop, and the end up through it, and round behind the standing part, and back down through the loop; and pulled it tight. "That. Behind your back. With your eyes shut." He undid it. "When you can tie that behind your back with your eyes shut, in the time it takes me to say it, come and find me, and I'll show you the next one."

"When are you going?"

"Your duke's hole in the sea first. Saturday, he says, at dark." Zale looked down the river. "Then the sea, when I'm back from it, if I'm back from it."

"I'll have it by the autumn."

"You'll have it by Saturday," said Zale, "or you'll not have it at all," and he went back to watching his casks.

Finn stood there a moment longer with the fathom of old line in his hands. Then he went off along the quay with it, and sat down on the end of it with his back to the coaster and his hands behind him, and began; and at eleven that night, when Emeric came down to the quay to see the Hod's crew off on their second run, Finn was still there in the dark under the quay's lamp, with the line behind his back and his eyes shut and his lips moving.



They packed the bag on the long table on the Thursday, in the morning, with the hall's doors shut.

Everything that would go down into the Isle with them came up from the stores and the works and Ivo's shop and Bram's, and was laid out on the long table in rows, item by item, and Emeric named every one of them and what it was for. He did it like a rota. He had decided that the night before, in his room, with the list; that if he did it like a lecture, the way he wanted to, nobody would remember it, and if he did it like the duty rota Ward read out at six, everybody would. So he read it like the rota.

"Flasks," he said. "Two each. On the sides, not the back. The way in's narrow."

"Why two?" said Ward.

"Because one fails."

He went down the table. The helms, each man's own: the flight helms off the suits, with the water edit, closed in front with a round glass and the demand valve inside, so the whole face breathed and nothing had to be held in the teeth. A spare mask each, because a cracked glass forty feet down is a drowned man unless he has something else to put on his face. The fins, stiff, short. The lights, three each: a big one on the hand, a small one on the strap, a smaller one on the belt. "Why three?" said Ward. "Because one floods and one breaks and you still have to get out." The reels, the line on them that would go down from the sinkhole's mouth to wherever they went and be their only road home. Arrows to clip on the line, pointing out. "Why arrows?" "So that if a man finds the line in the dark and doesn't know which way is out, the line tells him." Two knives each, one on the chest and one on the leg. "Why two?" "Because a man tangled in an old rope with one hand trapped can reach one of them." The gauges. The watches. The long hose on one flask in each pair, seven feet of it, coiled under a band, so that a man out of air could breathe off his partner's flask while they swam out side by side. The lift bags, canvas and rubber, for anything heavy that needed bringing up. And at the end of the row, by itself, a brass tube as long as a man's forearm, with a grip either side and a grating over each end, and a water stone set in a ring in its throat.

"The pump-jet," said Emeric. There was no wheel in it and no blades. The stone pushed water in at the front grating and out of the back one, hard, and the water going out of the back pushed the tube, and anybody holding the grips, the other way. Tess had built it from his sheet in nine days. Her apprentices had been calling it the sea scooter since it towed Finn the length of the boat shed's tank on his belly, shouting, and the name had stuck to it the way names stuck in the Works, whether Emeric liked them or not.

"Why one?" said Ward.

"Because I don't trust it yet." Emeric turned it over. "It throws the water out behind it like a hose. In a passage with silt on the floor, it blinds whoever's behind me. So it doesn't go in. It comes out, last, with nobody behind it."

"And if the stone stops?"

"Then I'm a man holding a brass tube, and I swim."

Ward looked at it, and nodded. The dry bags, tarred, sealed. The ropes for the dry cave. Chalk. A short rope ladder. Bram's safety lamp in its brass gauze, that would tell them by its flame whether the air past the sump would kill them.

"Why do we need that?" said Ward. "Why can't you tell us?"

"Because I can't feel gas," said Emeric. "I can feel stone, and water, and anything alive, and anything with mana in it. I can't feel air. If there's bad air in the dry cave, I'll walk into it with the rest of you." He tapped the lamp. "That can."

Ward looked at the lamp.

Then, quietly, he stopped asking why.

Emeric noticed it about two-thirds of the way down the table: that the questions had stopped. He looked up. Ward was standing at the table's end with his arms folded, looking at the things laid out in their rows, every one of them doubled, some of them tripled; and his face had the look it had on the night before a march, when he went round the camp after dark to every fire.

"You've done this before," said Zale, from the window.

He had been standing there all morning, listening, with his back to the light.

"No," said Emeric.

"No?" Zale looked at the table. "Then somebody has. That's somebody's kit. That's somebody who's been down a hole in the sea and come back and written it down." He looked at Emeric. "Who?"

Emeric did not answer that. There was nothing he could say that was true and that Zale could be told.

"People who died," said Ward, before the silence could go on, "finding out what they'd need." He unfolded his arms. "That's who writes a list like that. Every time." He looked at the table. "Read the rest."



They took Zale to the Mere that afternoon, in a borrowed rubber suit and a pair of the flasks, to see whether he could swim.

He could swim. He was a sailor; he had been in the sea since before he could walk, off every beach between Emberfell and the last islands, and he went off the end of the jetty in his suit and his flasks like a seal off a rock and came up laughing and blew water out of his nose. Then Rylan made him go down to the bottom and swim along it, ten feet down, along the line they had laid there, the way he would have to swim in the cave.

He swam like a sailor. That was the trouble. He kicked like a man swimming across a harbour to a boat: long and hard and fast, his legs going up and down from the hip, his fins thrashing; and the Mere's bottom came up behind him in a great brown cloud, every kick of it, so that by the time he was halfway along the line nobody on the jetty could see him at all, or the line, or anything, and the water at that end of the Mere was the colour of tea.

He came up at the end of the line in the middle of his own cloud, looking pleased with himself.

"Again," said Rylan, from the jetty.

"What was wrong with that?"

"Look behind you."

Zale looked behind him, at the great brown cloud hanging in the water where he had swum, slowly spreading.

"That's the cave," said Rylan. "With you in it. And the line in it. And the four of us behind you in it." He sat down on the jetty's edge with his feet in the water. "Come here. Not like that. Like a frog."

Rylan taught him the frog kick. It took the rest of the afternoon. Rylan had learned it in the spring, at the same Mere, from Emeric, for the Isle; it was the kick a cave diver used, short and flat, the knees bent and the fins kept high and turned out, pushing back with their flat faces like a frog's feet and then gliding, so that the push went backward along the diver's own line and never down at the silt. It was slow. It was dull. It was nothing like swimming. Zale hated it.

"It's not swimming," he said, for the fifth time, hanging on the jetty's ladder with his mask up on his forehead. "It's crawling. It's a crab's way of going about. I've swum in the sea for thirty years and I've never in my life done anything so slow."

"You've never in your life swum in a cave," said Rylan.

"I've swum under ships. Under the keel. In the dark."

"With silt under you?"

Zale did not answer that.

"Again," said Rylan.

He did it again. And by the end of the afternoon he could swim the length of the line along the Mere's bottom without lifting a cloud behind him, slowly, frogging, his long body flat and level in the water and his fins turning out and pushing and gliding, while Rylan swam above him and watched; and when he came up at the end, he hung on the ladder and pushed his mask up and said, to nobody, in a voice of great disgust, "It's easier," and pulled himself up onto the jetty and lay there on the boards in his wet suit with his arms out.



Emeric asked Rylan for the door while Zale was lying on the boards, at the far end of the jetty, out of hearing.

He had asked Ward in the winter, and Ward had said yes before he had finished asking; Ward had had the door for years, and lived with it as he lived with everything Emeric had ever asked him to carry, without comment. Rylan had not had it. Rylan had been read, once, in the delve, with his leave, years ago, so that Emeric knew his note; but he had never been asked to let Emeric into his sight.

He asked him now, aloud, in plain words, standing on the jetty in the afternoon sun with the Mere's water still running out of Rylan's hair. What it was. That it would be faint, like lines drawn on a window, with the water still there behind them. That it would show the walls past the lamps' reach and the line and the four of them and the way out. That they would be able to talk, the three of them, in it, with no breath. That it would be in Rylan's sight for as long as the dive lasted, and that Rylan could shut it at any moment by deciding to, and it would shut.

"And if I don't want it?" said Rylan.

"Then you don't have it. And you're on the line with Zale, on the touch signals, like anybody."

Rylan looked down the jetty at Zale, lying on the boards with his arms out, complaining to the sky.

"He'll feel it," said Rylan. "He's got a sense. Like hers. Like his mother's. Close in." He looked back at Emeric. "Ward says. He'll feel something going between us, in the water, and not know what."

"Yes."

"And he'll not ask."

"I don't think he will."

Rylan was quiet a moment.

"Ward has it?" he said.

"For years."

Rylan looked at the Mere for a while, at the place out in the middle where the water was still a little brown from Zale's first try.

"Then yes," he said. "To the door."



The hose fitting cracked at four.

It was Declan's: a brass fitting where his long hose went into his flask's valve, a thing the size of a thumb joint, and it had a hair crack in it that nobody had seen, that opened when the flask was turned on full at the bottom of the Mere and let out a long thin hissing stream of bubbles; and Declan came up the ladder fast, with his flask hissing on his back like a kettle and his face set, and said, "It's the fitting," and Rylan swore.

They needed the fitting. There was no spare on the jetty. The spares were in the Motion Shop, two miles off, in Tess's drawer.

"Wren," said Emeric.

Wren had been sitting on the jetty's end all afternoon with her shoes off and her feet in the water, watching Zale, because Finn was watching Zale and wherever Finn went that summer Wren went too, to see what was so interesting. She was on her feet before he said anything else.

"The long hose fitting," said Emeric. "The brass. Tess's second drawer down, on the left. Tell her it's for Declan's flask and it's cracked at the shoulder."

Wren was gone up the jetty in her bare feet with her shoes in her hand before he had finished.

Declan had the flask off. The hiss went on. It was full; every minute it hissed was air they would have to make again, and the plant was a day behind already with the Hod's runs. Emeric took the flask from him.

He put his hand round the fitting. Brass, wet, cold from the Mere; and under his palm the thin hard stream of air coming out of the crack, against the skin, like a draught under a door.

He took the heat out of it.

Not the air. The brass, and the water on it, and the thin film of the Mere still wet in the crack: he pulled the heat out of all of it, through his palm and away past his fingertips, the way he had learned on a Lantern Night years ago, the way he had frozen the plate on the Mere in April; slowly, not much, a little. The wet in the crack went white. Then the brass round it. A rime of frost grew on the fitting under his hand, and on his fingers, and the thin hard draught against his palm stopped, as the frozen water in the crack locked it shut.

The hiss stopped.

Declan was looking at the fitting.

"Hold it like that till she's back," said Emeric, and gave it to him: the flask, with its fitting white with frost, cold as January in the July sun. "Don't put it down in the sun. It'll go in a quarter of an hour."

"It'll hold?"

"It'll hold till Wren's back."

It held till Wren was back. She came down the jetty at a run at twenty to five, red in the face, with the new fitting in her fist and her shoes still in her other hand, and Tess's note round it, which said only, She ran the whole way. I'd give her the job for that. T.



The line fouled on the last run, at six, with the sun going low.

It was the long line they had laid along the Mere's bottom, the practice line, from the jetty's foot out to the deep water and back. Somewhere out in the middle of it the weed had got into it: a great floating mat of the long brown ribbon weed that grew in the Mere's south end in summer and broke loose in the afternoons and drifted, and had drifted over the line and sunk on it, and wrapped it. Osric found it, swimming the line on the last run. He came up and hung on the ladder and said there was a mat of weed on the line forty yards out that he could not get through, and that he could cut it, but that it would take him half an hour with a knife in the water, because the stuff was tough as wet leather, and the light was going.

Emeric went down himself.

He went down the ladder in his own suit, without flasks, with his breath held, the way he had gone into the Mere in April; and swam out along the line, in the brown water, with his eyes no use and his sense open, until he felt the weed. It was a great dense haze in the water ahead, faintly alive, faintly green to the sense, wrapped round the line where it lay on the bottom in a tangle as big as a cart.

He had had four lessons in water from Hedda, in the spring of the year before, and had never been any good at it; it was not his element, it cost him as dearly as air did. But he did not need to be good. He did not need to cut the weed or burn it. He only had to tell the water round it to move.

He told it. All of it, at once, the water in the mat and round it, in one long slow pull, upward and away from the line, toward the surface; the way you lift a sheet off a bed by its middle. The water went. It took the weed with it, every ribbon, slowly unwinding off the line in long brown spirals as the water drew it up, and up, into the light; and the line lay on the bottom under it, clean, as if it had never been touched.

He came up beside the weed. It lay on the surface of the Mere in a great brown floating mat, the size of a cart, unwound, drifting.

On the jetty, Zale was standing at the edge with his mask in his hand, looking at it.

"Water stone," he said, when Emeric came up the ladder. "Isn't it? You had one on you." He was still looking at the weed. "There's a crown galley on the coast road runs on water stones. No sail, no oars. I've seen her. Nobody knows how." He shook his head, slowly. "Is there anything you don't have a stone for?"

Emeric did not say anything to that.

Zale did not seem to need him to. He stood on the jetty with his mask in his hand and looked at the weed drifting away on the darkening water, and then up at the Hod, swinging at the jetty's end with her hoses off now and her lamps lit for the night run; and then back at Emeric, standing there in his wet suit, dripping, with no stone in his hands.

He looked at Emeric's empty hands for a moment.

Then he looked away again, at the water, and did not ask; and Emeric saw that he had decided not to, and that he would go on deciding not to, for the whole of the dive, and probably for a long while after, because he was Rowena's son and had grown up in a house where a great many questions were not asked; and that he would remember every one of them.

"I'll want a ship," said Zale.

He said it to the water. Quite casually, the way he had said he would ask about the seed.

"One of those." He nodded at the Hod. "Not that one. One of my own. One that I can take out past the last islands, and over the edge, and come back from." He looked at Emeric. "When there's time. When you've got one to spare. I know you've not got one to spare."

"No."

"But when you have."

Emeric looked at him, at the big sunburned sailor standing on his jetty in a borrowed rubber suit with a mask in his hand and the Mere's water still running out of his beard, asking for the sky.

"When there's time," he said. "Yes."

Zale looked at him a moment longer, and nodded, once, as a man nods at a price.

"I'll hold you to it," he said; and then, as if he had said nothing at all, "That frog kick's a crab's way of going about," and went off down the jetty to find his boots.



Finn found him on the quay at dawn on the Saturday, the day they were to fly for the Isle at dark, and tied it.

Emeric was not there; he heard about it from Wren, who was. She told it at breakfast, to the whole table, in order, with her elbows on it, because Imani had told her once that that was the way to tell a thing, and she had remembered.

Finn had walked up to Zale on the quay, where Zale was standing at the coaster's side with a cup of tea, and said, "Say it."

"Say what?"

"Bowline. Say it. Like you said."

Zale had looked at him. Then he had put down his cup on the bollard, slowly, and said, "Bowline."

And Finn, with his hands behind his back and his eyes shut, standing on the quay in the grey light, had tied it, in the fathom of old line, in the time it took Zale to say the word; and pulled it tight; and brought it round in front of him, and held it out.

Zale had looked at it a long time.

Then he had taken it, and turned it over in his hands, and pulled on the loop, hard, to see if it would slip; and it had not.

"Where'd you learn that?" Zale had said.

"Here," Finn had said. "On the quay. Since Tuesday."

"All night?"

"Most nights."

Zale had looked at the bowline in his hands, and then at Finn, a long look, up and down, the way he looked at the sky; and then he had reached into his coat, Wren said, and taken out another length of line, a short one, finer, tarred, and held it out to Finn without saying anything.

"Sheet bend," he had said. "When I'm back."

Then he had picked up his tea, and gone up his own gangway onto his own deck, and had not looked round again.

"And Finn just sat down," said Wren, with her elbows on the table, very satisfied. "On the bollard. With the new rope. And started." She looked round the table. "He's still there. He didn't come up for breakfast. I brought him bread and he didn't even look." She considered. "I don't think Mother knows."

Rylan, at the far end of the table, put down his cup, and looked at the window, toward the quay; and did not get up.
