Chapter 49 - Field Coils

He drew the machine again in the last week of November, smaller.

The four sheets had been drawn in August for forty bars of copper, at a time when forty had seemed like a number somebody on the river would sell him. There were thirty. Ferra had them on the copper page, twenty-nine bars out of the dead houses and what was left of Grayrock's after the still and the night wire, and against the total, in small letters, she had written: and a half, if he stops drawing it into wire at night.

So he stopped, and drew the machine again on three sheets, and took copper out of everything that could live without it.

There was one choice in the three sheets that he did not have to make. Every wire in the machine was thick enough to come through Harl's wheel without breaking. Fine wire would have put more turns on the ring for the same copper, and more push at the end of the wire. It would also have been a machine whose wire only one man could draw, at night, behind sacking, with his nose bleeding, and he had spent the third week of November finding out what a thing like that was worth when the man went away.

On the corner of the third sheet he worked out what it would light. Then he took the sheets down the hill.

Ferra read all three. She did not ask what the machine was. She had asked that when he first showed her the four sheets, and he had told her, and she had written it down.

"Eight lamps," she said, at the corner.

"Eight."

"Out of thirty bars." She looked at him then, the way she looked at a page to see whether it had been added in a hurry, and then back down. "Where?"

"The walls."

She wrote it on the copper page, eight, with the date, and under it the name of the only place on the river where there was iron enough for the frame and nobody left to sell it.



The iron was lying in the mud below the dock quarter.

Three moorings in four had stood empty since the port silted, and in the empty ones, where their owners had left them, lay the boats that nobody had come back for. Some were still afloat, just, low and grey and full of rainwater. Most had settled at the stern into the mud and stayed there, with their ribs showing through the planking like the ribs of a starved horse, and weed on everything below the line where the river came up in winter. Gulls stood on the gunwales. Nothing else did.

Rylan went down with him, because Rylan had grown up on those piers and knew which boat was which.

"Coaster," he said, walking the soft boards ahead of Emeric with his hands in his armpits against the cold. "She went down to the coast and back twice a month when I was nine. That one fished. That one fished and smuggled, and everybody knew, and nobody said, because he gave the priest a fish every week." He stopped by a broad black hull sunk to its deck. "I learned to swim off that one."

"Off it?"

"Off it, into the river, in March, because a bigger boy pushed me." Rylan considered the hull. "So I learned to swim off it in the sense that I didn't drown."

Hale had found the harbor book in the vaulted room. Every mooring had paid the barony a due each summer for as far back as the harbor book went, until the port silted and there was nobody on the piers to pay it. Under the harbor custom, Hale said, a hull with nobody to answer for its dues was the barony's to clear. Then he added that the custom said nothing about whether the barony ought to, which was not his to say. So Rylan stood in the market two mornings running and named the boats, every one by what the dock quarter called it, and two old men came over from the dock quarter, and were paid for their iron by the weight on Bram's big beam, as the plum pan had been.

Then the gate crew went into the river.

It was ugly, cold work, and it took the whole of a week. They went in to the thigh with crowbars and ropes, cursing in turn, and came out blue to the knee and stood by a fire on the bank until they could feel their feet, and went in again. The anchors came up out of the mud one at a time, on a rope over the old crane at the end of the pier, which nobody had turned since the port died and which screamed when it did. The chain came up after them in rusted heaps, link by link. The iron in the hulls, the bolts and straps and the pins the rudders had hung on, had to be prised out of oak that was soft on the outside and hard as horn an inch in, and the gate crew learned to hate oak.

The timber they left lying on the piers, because somebody would want it.

Emeric broke a link off the first chain with a cold chisel and looked at the broken end.

It had broken like green wood, in fibers, grey and silky, with the grain running along the link. Somebody had made it by hand, a long time ago, out of iron that had been hammered and folded and hammered again until there was almost nothing in it but iron. Soft. Nearly pure. It would take a magnet as easily as anything he could have asked for, and let it go again as easily, which was the other thing he wanted it for.

"Is it good?" Rylan said, from the fire.

"It's the best iron in Kaldo."

Rylan looked at the mud, and the gulls, and the gate crew coming up out of the river with a length of chain over their shoulders like a dead snake, and laughed until he had to sit down.



Bram moved across the river in the first week of December. Half of him.

He kept the old forge, the one his kitchen shared a wall with, with its own fire and its own anvil, for nails and hinges and the small work of the town, and he said so to anybody who looked as though they were going to ask. The heavy work went over the east bridge to the old works, into the third bay, the one with the clean footing and the surveyor's pegs still standing round it, where he had stood in August of last year and said that he supposed he had better find somebody to teach.

He had found three. Tess, who came with him as a matter of course and was not asked. And two big boys off the school benches, both about fourteen, both from the lower ward, who wanted to be smiths, which Bram told them on their first morning was a stupid thing to want, and then put a hammer in each of their hands to see how they held it.

The works' wheel was the reason for all of it. In August it had stood with its lower paddles in the mud. Now the river was up with the autumn rains, brown and fast, and the wheel turned all day on the river side of the building with a long wet groaning that could be heard from the east bridge.

The carpenter had been in the third bay since the end of November, and he put two things on its shaft.

The first was the bellows. A pair of them, each as big as a door, made of oak boards and leather Bram had been keeping against a day when he would want a great deal of leather at once, and never told anybody what day that was. Pegs on the wheel's shaft pressed each board down in turn and let it rise, so that the air went into the hearth in long slow breaths, one after the other, steady as a sleeping man. Bram stood beside it on the first morning with his hand flat on the leather, feeling it fill and empty, and did not say anything, and the carpenter began to look worried.

The second was the hammer.

It was a beam of oak as long as two men, pivoted on an iron pin a little way from one end, with a head on the long end made out of a cracked anvil from the back of his own forge, as heavy as a full sack of grain. Pegs on the shaft caught the short end and pressed it down, and the long end rose, and the peg slipped off, and the head fell onto the anvil. Then the next peg. Four to a turn of the shaft. The first time the carpenter knocked the wedge out and let the shaft take it, the hammer came down on the bare anvil so hard that the boys jumped back, and kept coming down, steady, a blow for every breath, and the whole floor of the third bay felt it through the soles of their boots.

Bram watched it fall twenty times.

"It doesn't get tired," he said, as though that counted against it.

Then he took a bar of the anchor iron out of the fire at a white heat, and put it under the head, and turned it between blows, and in the time it would have taken him and Tess to draw it down by hand to half its thickness the trip hammer had drawn it to a third, and he took it out and looked at it, and put it back.

The frame came out of the river iron in nine days. The two great arms of the field were the shanks of the two biggest anchors, drawn down square under the hammer and bent. The yoke that tied them together at the back was chain, links laid side by side in the fire and welded into a bar, one heat at a time, with Tess at the bellows lever for the heats the wheel could not judge and the boys on the sledges. The bolts that held it to its bed were bolts that had held a coaster together for forty years. When it was done Bram walked round it twice with his hands behind his back, as he walked round anything he had made, and said it was too heavy, which it was not, and too ugly, which it was.



The copper was Tess's, from the beginning.

Every length of it came through Harl's wheel in the long nights of December, at the drum in the mill's back room, with Tess feeding it in and Harl's lamp in the window above. It was thick wire, by the standard of the coil on the nail. It came through the holes it was asked to come through and did not break, not once, and she brought every length up to the dyehouse in the morning with its measure written on a tag, in knots, and laid it on the bench in a row, and did not say anything about not needing anybody to draw it.

It had to be covered before it could be wound, every inch, so that no turn of it could touch the turn beside it. Abloh covered it. He would not let anybody else do it until they had covered a whole yard in front of him without a gap, and three of the soap-yard women failed that before one passed, and he complained about all four of them equally. Linen thread run through hot beeswax, wrapped round the wire close and tight as a bandage on a finger. Between the layers of each coil went a wrap of old hemp twine from the rope walk, which had been standing silent with its racks still full since the year the rope trade went, and was brittle on the outside of every hank and sound an inch in.

Tess wound the field coils on wooden formers in the dyehouse, one for each arm, on a winder the carpenter made her out of a spindle and a crank. She counted the turns under her breath. If anybody spoke to her while she was counting she stopped turning and went back to the last hundred, without looking up and without answering, so that after the first day nobody spoke to her at all and the dyehouse was quieter than it had been since the vats were full.

The ring at the heart of the machine, the part that would turn, was iron too: iron wire, the same wire as the line, wound round and round on itself into a ring as thick as a man's arm, like a skein of wool. Emeric wound the copper onto that himself, in sections, in daylight, with the door open. It was slow, fiddly work, and he was the only one who knew where every section went. Tess stood at his elbow for the whole of the first day and watched, and on the second day she wound a section while he watched, and got it right, and wound the next.



They took it down to the mill in a cart on the fourteenth, in pieces, and Harl met them at the door.

He had agreed to it in December of last year, the hours his stones did not want, by a belt to whatever the prince liked in the back room, paid to the mill by the hour. He had not asked what the prince would like. He looked at the frame coming off the cart on rollers, and at the two big field coils on their formers wrapped in sacking, and at the ring, and at Bram swearing quietly at the rollers, and he stood aside and let it all go past him into the back room, and then he followed it in.

The carpenter had laid two oak beams across the floor for it to sit on. Harl looked at the beams.

"My father put that floor in," he said.

"It'll hold," said the carpenter.

"I know it'll hold." Harl did not look at him. "I said who put it in."

It went together in an afternoon. The arms on the yoke, the coils slid onto the arms, the ring between the arms on its shaft, with a gap all the way round it a man could hardly have slipped a knife blade into. At one end of the shaft, the pulley for the belt. At the other end, the place where the current would come off the turning ring, a short drum of copper bars set side by side with a slip of waxed linen between each pair, and two springs of copper strip pressing on it.

Harl watched all of it. When it was done he asked one thing.

"Does it spark?"

A no would have been easier, and Harl would not have known for a week, and by then it would have been running. He looked at the copper drum and the two springs.

"There," he said. "Where the springs touch. When it's running. Small ones."

"Flour burns," Harl said. "Not like wood. In the air. A cloud of it, in a closed room, and something to light it, and it goes all at once. I saw a mill do that when I was a boy. The roof came off. The miller came out, after, without his eyebrows, and they found his boy the next morning under the stones." Harl looked at the door to the stone room. Fine white dust lay along the top of it, and on every ledge in the mill, and in the creases of his own sleeves. "Not a spark in my mill."

Nobody said anything. Bram, who had been about to say something, closed his mouth.

"Nights," Emeric said. "Only nights, when the stones are still and the dust is down. The door to the stone room shut, and wet sacking over it, every time. And a house of its own beside the mill before you grind again after the thaw. Stone. Its own roof. Out of your mill."

Harl looked at the machine on his father's floor.

"Before the thaw," he said.

"Before the thaw."

Harl nodded once, and went up the stair to his rooms above the stones, and they heard him moving about overhead, and then nothing. Ferra wrote the house into her book the next morning, under the eight lamps, at a price that made Emeric wince.

That night the machine stood in the back room in the dark with its belt hanging slack from the pulley, waiting for the wheel. Outside the wall the wheel went on round in the dark.
