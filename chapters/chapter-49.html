Chapter 49 - The Duvrenne Line

The first ruling the factor made on the farm was about a pipe, and it took her one sentence.

Colm had a better pipe. He had spent the winter making it, harder fired and with a deeper collar, and it would last twice as long in the ground as the pipe it was meant to replace, and it cost a little more to make. The crushed run of the main wanted a great many sections. Emeric asked for Colm's.

Vantic read the request, and then the clause, and then the request again.

"The borrower may repair what existed," she said. "A better pipe isn't a repair. It's an improvement. Replace like with like."

She was right. That was the worst part of it.

So the crushed run was relaid with the same pipe it had been laid with in the third year, to the old collar, through the same hoop, and Colm made every section of it with his jaw set, while a rack of his better pipe stood against the kiln wall where the whole yard could see it, unused.

It went on like that all spring.

The sluices could be rebuilt. The two new ones Emeric had wanted since the first harvest, at the low corner of the fourth division where the water always pooled, could not be added. Ferra did not ask the factor to rule on that. She brought her the decision already made, on one sheet. Seven sluices to be rebuilt as they had stood, none to be added, the covenant clause cited, the reasoning in three lines underneath. Vantic read the sheet and initialled it and handed it back, and neither of them said anything about the fact that there had been nothing on it for Vantic to decide.

The spoil banks were moved by hand. The stubble was cleared. The main was tested and held. The sluices were set. The first seed went into the ground a week inside the planting window, and the farm came back exactly as good as it had been before the war, and not one division better.

It was on time. It was the only thing that mattered. Nobody enjoyed any of it.

Ferra went down the long water in the spring, as she had said she would, with two clerks, a boat, and a satchel of blank sheets. She was gone three weeks. She walked every hamlet on the tithe map and two that were not on it, and knocked on every door, and was offered fish at most of them, and wrote everything down.

She came back with a count. She did not change the old figure. She wrote the new one beneath it, on its own line, with its own date, so that anybody reading the book would see both numbers and the day she had learned the first one was not enough. Emeric asked her why she had not simply corrected it.

"Because it wasn't wrong," Ferra said. "It was incomplete. I want the next person who keeps this book to know the difference, and to know I didn't hide it."

Year five came in, and it came in properly.

Nine divisions. The ground had been burned and buried and dug and then left alone for a year, by accident, and it came back stronger for the rest, which Farris took as a personal insult. Everything the barony had built in its second year worked exactly as it had been built to work. The grain stood thick and even to the far edge of the ninth division, and when it came in, it kept coming in, cart after cart, for longer than anybody had planned for.

It was the largest harvest anybody alive in Kaldo had seen.

There was nowhere for it to go.

The morning of the last cart, the town celebrated. There was no deciding to. Children rode in on the sheaves. Doran blessed the bins, which he had not been asked to do, and did at some length. Farris was drunk by noon and made a speech to the drying shed.

The afternoon, Ferra sat in the long room over the threshing floor with the ledger and did arithmetic, and the arithmetic went like this. The town eats this much. The seed is this much, and it stays out of everything. The house holds the harvest as its security. And what is left over, which is a great deal, has no buyer. The cellars were full. The bins were full. The drying shed was full. And the surplus was worth exactly nothing until somebody paid for it, and nobody was going to.

Emeric came up at dusk and read the page over her shoulder, and went down again, and passed a crowd singing in the square on the way.

The pile was still there as well.

The foundry's store from the winter before had not moved. It was a year old now. It had been counted more times than anybody would admit. Ferra referred to it in the voice a woman uses for a relative who has come to stay and shows no sign of leaving.

"The pile is well," she said, when he asked. "It sends its regards."

What Emeric tried first failed for the same reason as before, and he watched it fail knowing that.

He wrote to Duvrenne himself.

He wrote in his own hand, in his best form, to the three grain houses Saban had named. Volumes. Terms. A price below the coast market, which he could afford because Kaldo's costs were Kaldo's and not a middleman's. The letters went down the long water on a coasting boat and came back up the same way, and what came back was a courteous nothing. A clerk thanked him for his letter. The house was not taking new supply that season. The house wished the barony well.

It was not political. That was what made it worse. Nobody in Duvrenne had any quarrel with Kaldo. Nobody in Duvrenne had ever heard of Kaldo, except as the place where the plague had been, and a factor does not sign for next year's grain from a name that has nothing behind it.

It was the same wall. He had now stood in front of it twice.

He sent Saban.

Saban had gone down the coast on a coasting boat out of the lower water, three days of grey water and a boatman who did not speak, with a sealed case of samples on his knees the whole way, because he did not trust the hold with them.

He had been in Duvrenne for thirteen days, and he had heard the same answer in twelve different rooms.

It was a good town. He had not expected to like it and he did. It sat on a wide bay behind a harbour wall, with a long row of counting houses facing the water, and it smelled of grain dust and tar, and the men who ran it were men he understood. They bought harvests. They bought them off half the coast, and they knew grain the way he knew tonnage.

Every one of them liked the grain.

He had brought samples, sealed and dated, and every factor he showed them to did the same things in the same order. Weighed a measure. Rubbed a handful. Bit a kernel. One of them sent a boy for a sieve and put a scoop through it and looked at what stayed behind, and looked up at Saban with a face that said the grain was better than the coast's. Nobody argued about the grain.

And every single conversation, at the end, turned the same way.

"Who stands behind it?" the factor at the twelfth house asked. He was an old man with a slow voice, and he asked it kindly, which was worse. "You understand me, Master Saban. I'm not buying this harvest. I'm buying the next one, and the one after. I'm buying the certainty that in three years there will still be a Kaldo, and that it will ship when it says it will ship. Who tells me that?"

Saban had no answer for him. He had not had one in any of the rooms before it either.

A prince, exiled, that nobody on this coast had ever met. A town that had been dying the last time anybody here heard of it. A war, which everybody here had heard of, and had heard wrong.

He had been trading for twenty years, and he knew exactly what he was up against, because he had spent the whole of those twenty years on the other side of it. There is no instrument in the world that makes a stranger believable. You cannot write it. You cannot swear it. You cannot buy it. A name is believed because it has been believed before, and it is believed before because it was believed before that, and a man who has never been believed has no first rung to stand on.

He thanked the old man and went home.

He did not dress it when he got there. Saban never dressed anything.

"Nothing," he told the prince, in the small room, with his coat still on. "Not a sack. They liked the grain. They don't know who you are, and nothing I said made them."

Ferra solved it, and it took her about a minute.

She had been listening from the end of the table with her pen down. When Saban stopped, she picked it up again.

"We have somebody standing behind us," she said.

Emeric looked at her.

"There is a factor of the largest lending house in Randillia living forty feet down that corridor," Ferra said. "She writes to her house every week. The people who read her letters lend money to half the grain houses on that coast. And her file on Kaldo is four years of our books, checked against her standard. Not ours. Hers."

Saban was very still.

"She won't do it as a favour," Emeric said.

"No," said Ferra. "So I won't ask her for one."

She did it at the weekly review, on paper, in front of Emeric, as a procedural question.

"The covenant makes the harvest the lender's security," Ferra said. "The security is now in store, in good condition, and has no buyer. I'd like to know whether the house's standing report on the condition of the security may be shared with a counterparty, on that counterparty's request. A buyer for the security is plainly in the lender's interest."

Vantic looked at her.

It was a long look, and Emeric could not read it, and he could not read the look Ferra gave back either. He had the strong impression that two things were being said and that he was hearing neither of them.

The factor knew exactly what was being done to her. It was airtight. She could have refused a favour in her sleep. She could not refuse a duty, because refusing it would have been a ruling against the interest of her own house, and she had no intention of handing anybody at the far end of her correspondence a ruling like that.

"It may," she said. "The report is the house's. I'll write it into the next one."

What she wrote was not a recommendation.

It was a single line in a routine report, in her ordinary hand, over her ordinary seal. It said that the borrower's accounts were sound and that the security was in good condition. It said nothing else. It went north in the week's letters, into the house's correspondence, and somewhere inside that correspondence it met a correspondent who wrote to a correspondent in Duvrenne, and the line travelled down the coast in somebody else's letter to the desk of a grain factor who had never heard of Kaldo, and who had heard of Ducatis every day of his working life.

A letter came up the long water in the third week of autumn. It was from the old man with the slow voice. He would take a trial volume at the terms Saban had offered, delivered at the bar, and if the trial was as described, he would take more.

That line of hers was worth more than anything Emeric had ever written. More than the four letters to the barons. More than the prospectus. More than every word he had sent north in four years. He knew it, and he went down the corridor and told her so, directly, once.

"That line did what nothing of mine could do," he said.

Vantic did not look up from the ledger.

"I haven't done you a service," she said. "I've protected the security. If the harvest fails, I'll write the opposite line, and I'll write it just as promptly."

He believed her entirely. He found, walking back up the corridor, that he liked her considerably more for it.

The grain did not go by road.

It went down the long water, on the coasting boats that had been going out through that channel for longer than anybody in the town above had known they existed. The current did the work in one direction. It had always done the work in one direction. The boats had always been there.

Fletcher did not build anything for it and was not asked to. What he did was worth more than a hull.

He stood on the quay at the lower water on the morning of the first loading, with the boatmen round him and Emeric at the back, and told them which way through the bar a loaded boat could take, and when.

"Not on the ebb," he said. "Never on the ebb, loaded like that. On the last of the flood. Keep her north of the stake, where the bar has built up since spring, and if the wind's in the south, you wait, and you don't argue with me about it."

Nobody in the town above could have told them that for any money. It was not written anywhere. It was in Fletcher's head, and in the heads of perhaps five other men on that beach, and it was the difference between a cargo that reached Duvrenne and a cargo that went onto the bar.

Abloh had come down on the first grain boat. He had asked Emeric for the passage the day the old man's letter came up the water, as flatly as he asked for tallow, and Emeric had not asked him what for.

Emeric saw them meet from the back of the quay. There was not much to see. Fletcher finished with the boatmen and turned round, and Abloh was already walking down the beach toward the frames, and Fletcher went down after him. They stood at the half-planked hull and looked at it, and not at each other. After a while Abloh put his hand flat on one of the ribs. Fletcher said something short that Emeric did not catch, and Abloh laughed, once, the way a man laughs at an old joke.

Emeric went and counted sacks.

The trial volume went on the last of the flood, north of the stake, with the wind out of the west. It reached Duvrenne. The old man took more.

The pile cleared.

It cleared because a grain contract opens a door, and once a door is open, other things can be carried through it. The old man's house bought iron as well as grain. Hinges, first, and then ploughshares, and then bar, and once one house on that coast was buying from Kaldo, a second one asked to see samples. The store that had been counted more times than anybody would admit emptied across the autumn, one boatload at a time.

Kaldo had income for the first time since the soap stopped. It was the first grain Kaldo had sold beyond its own valley since before the plague, and there were old people on the island who cried when they heard it, and could not have said precisely why.

The man with the mallet at the lower water, who had asked whether this meant they would be taxed, was loading Kaldo grain at a fee before the autumn was out, and had stopped asking. The salt house sold its salt to the boats as well as to the town. Children who had followed Emeric's horse to the quay and lost interest now followed the carts down and did not.

Ferra went down to count them in the summer. She took a clerk and the tithe map and most of a month, and walked the lower water house by house the way she had once walked the island, and would not take Abloh's boats or anybody else's guess for a single roof. The lagoon village, the salt house, the dune hamlets strung out toward the bar, and a scatter of fishing families on the bar itself who told her they belonged to nobody and were entered anyway. Three hundred and seventy-six.

Her second census put the barony at two thousand two hundred and sixty. She wrote it under the first, with both dates and both methods, and did not strike the first one out, because it had been true of what it counted.

Abloh had gone back up with the empty boats at the end of that first week. After that, the boats that came up for grain brought orders from the beach, and the boats that went down carried rolls of canvas, and Abloh paid the freight on them out of his own pocket.

The first families from outside came after the harvest, when the roads were dry and the grain was in and a man could see what the winter would cost him where he stood.

They came down the northern road through the junction, on carts or walking beside them, with what they owned roped on behind. A Wayford smallholder and three grown sons. Two sisters off Milford's east pasture, whose husbands had stood at the back of Paxton's line and come home talking. A widow from under the Grayrock hill with a boy who had lain in Kaldo's church with a pike wound and been sewn up next to a Kaldo man. Nobody had sent for any of them. They had heard it across a table over a winter, and it was not the war that brought them. It was the other half of the story, the half the drovers told last because they believed it least: that in Kaldo a man was paid in coin at the end of the week, and paid when he was hurt, and that the wells were sweet, and that nobody on the island had gone hungry since the second harvest.

Ferra took them at a table inside the Western Gate. Name, village, trade, and what they owed and to whom, because a tenant may leave any lord he likes and a lord may hold him for what he owes, and she was not going to have Kaldo sued by a neighbour over a debt she could have asked about across a table. Then each household got a key to a house on the lower streets, a name on a work roll, and a line under the census in a new column. There were a hundred and forty-one of them by the first frost.

Vantic, who ruled on everything, looked at the column once. A house that already stood was not plant, she said, and more hands on a farm and a foundry that already stood were rather the point of a loan. Then she went back to her own book.

Ferra's ledger did not turn round. It was not going to turn round that year, or the next.

But on an evening late in the autumn she put the sheet in front of him and waited, the way she did, and when he had read it she said the thing out loud, in a voice nobody in the keep had heard from her before.

"It's stopped," she said. "It isn't going up. It's stopped going down."

Emeric stood on the beach at the lower water on the last loading day of the year.

He had first set foot on this sand less than a year before, having owned it for three. There was Kaldo grain on the quay and Kaldo iron in the holds, and a boatload of both sitting low in the water at the mouth of the channel, waiting for the last of the flood.

Fletcher stood beside him, and did not speak.

The boat went. It went north of the stake, on the flood, with the wind in the west, the way it had been told, and it took the channel cleanly and cleared the bar and turned down the coast toward Duvrenne.

Fletcher watched it all the way out, and did not say anything at all, and Emeric did not either.

Kaldo was earning at last, and it was earning from the one part of itself that it had never known it had.
