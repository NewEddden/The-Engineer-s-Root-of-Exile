Chapter 373 - Towers

Bram threw the first brick himself, because he'd laid the tower and he said it was only fair.

It was a quarter to eight on the morning of the twentieth of November, a Thursday, and grey, with no wind to speak of and a long low swell coming in round the point and breaking on the rocks below the tower without much interest. The tower stood on the point across the harbour mouth from the sea wall's light, on the bare grey shelf of rock where the old signal post had been, and it was the second of the two. The first was on the sea wall's root, beside the Harbour Board in the customs tower, and the town could see that one from the quay and had been looking at it for a month. Nobody could see this one but the gulls and the pilot boats, which was why they were here.

It was not very tall. Three storeys of the same grey quay stone Bram had cut the Hollow Night stone out of, round, battered at the foot, with two bands of brass let into it a third and two thirds of the way up, which Bram said were for the earth strap and which everybody else said were because Bram had wanted them. At the top, where a lighthouse would have had its lamp, there was a cap of glass a little bigger than a man's head, in a brass cage, and inside the glass the stone. It was a grown stone, from the press, the first of the tempered ones Tess had pulled in September; sense, pale, about the size of a fist, set on a seat of stone dust and keyed into the ring of coded stones under it that did the work. It had a faint light in it all the time, like a lamp turned right down. At night, from the water, you could see it.

Round the tower, out to about eighty yards, there was nothing at all that you could see. That was the field.

"Ready?" said Tess.

She was sitting on a folding stool at the foot of the tower with her stick across her knees and a board on her lap with three sheets clipped to it, and Ambrose beside her with a field telephone in a box, its wire running back across the rock and down into the water and under the mouth to the Board. Armand stood a little way off with his hands in his cardigan pockets. Osric had come because the tower would be his to keep, and was standing between Tess and the edge of the shelf, which was where he usually stood. Emeric was further down the rock with Ferra, out of the way.

"Ready," said Bram.

He had a brick from the yard in his right hand. He weighed it once, and turned side on, and threw it, hard and flat and low, the way a man who'd been throwing things at walls for forty years throws them, straight at the tower's foot.

At about eighty yards out, the brick stopped.

It didn't slow. It stopped, in the air, the way a fly stops when it hits a window you can't see, and hung there for a moment at the height of a man's chest, turning very slowly over on itself; and the cap at the top of the tower brightened by a hair, as if the stone in it had looked up; and then the brick fell straight down onto the rock, and broke in half.

"Held and dropped," said Tess, writing. "Didn't read it."

"There wasn't anything to read," said Bram. "It's a brick."

"That's what I wrote." She looked up at the cap. "No glyph. No spell. Nothing it knows. So it held it and let go. That's what it's meant to do with a thing it doesn't know." She wrote again. "One. Good."

Bram walked out across the rock to the two halves of his brick and picked them up and looked at them, and then up at the tower, with the face of a man whose own wall has just been rude to him.



The fire was Armand's.

He'd grumbled about it. He'd grumbled about it for three days, at the long table and on the stair and at the Works, to anybody who would hold still: that he was ninety-one years old in every way that counted except the ones you could see, and that in ninety-one years he had been a great many things in the tower and out of it, but he had never once volunteered to be a target, and he did not see why he should start at his age for a pile of Bram's masonry with a glass hat.

"You wrote the counters," Tess had said.

"That's my point. I know what's in there."

He walked out to the eighty-yard mark now, which Osric had painted on the rock in a long white arc the day before, and then on past it another forty yards to be well outside, and turned round to face the tower. He took his hands out of his pockets. He looked, for a moment, exactly what he was, which was a man of thirty-two who had been a master of the Circle for fifty years, and the gulls on the rocks below him went up all at once, without anybody seeing why.

The glyph came up in front of his right palm, a ring of fire-red light the size of a plate, drawing itself line by line in the air; it closed; and the fire went out of it.

It wasn't a big fire. He'd said it wouldn't be; there was no sense in costing Tess a cap on the first morning. A ball of it the size of a man's head, very hot, white at the heart and orange at the edges, going flat and fast across the rock at the tower's foot with a sound like a sheet being torn.

It crossed the white line.

The cap came up bright. Not a hair, this time; a lamp turned full. And in the air in front of it, between the tower and the fire, a glyph drew itself: a ring, faint blue, going round once and closing, quite slowly, so that everybody on that rock had time to see it, and Emeric had a moment, watching, to understand that this was the point of the slowness, that it was meant to be seen.

Then the air in front of the fire caved in.

That was the only way he ever found to say it, afterwards, in the notebook. The air round the fireball, for a yard on every side, went in on it all at once, the way a sheet of paper goes in round a fist, and the fire went small and white and then out, crushed, with a flat hard cough like a door shut in a gale. A puff of smoke came out of the place where it had been and drifted off seaward. The swell went on breaking on the rocks.

Nobody said anything. Then Osric said, "Huh," and Tess wrote.

"Two," she said. "Fire. Read it at seventy-five, answered by sixty. Air. Crushed." She didn't look up. "Sixty's close."

"It's half a breath," said Armand, coming back across the rock with his hands in his pockets again. He looked pleased with himself, and was trying not to. "Fire's slow. Fire's the slowest thing I've got. If it's sixty for fire it'll be less for anything quick."

"I know."

"I'm only saying."

"I know you're only saying." Tess looked up at last, at the cap, which had gone back to its low lamp. "I put water first in the table for fire. You put air. You were right; air's quicker to throw. I'll not tell you that again."

"Then I'll treasure it," said Armand.

He threw three more. Water, which the tower broke with a hard little burst of fire in front of it and a great deal of steam; a stone hurled by air, not by a hand, which the tower read as a spell and answered with a gust of its own that turned the stone aside into the sea; and a second fire, bigger, to see what the cap did under load. The cap did the same thing a little faster. Tess wrote all of it. Ambrose read her numbers down the telephone to the Board and the Board read them back, and every time the cap came up bright, the faint blue ring drew itself in the air in front of it first, slowly, so that you could see it answer.

"Why's it slow?" said Ferra, beside Emeric. "The ring."

"Because Armand made it slow."

"On purpose?"

"On purpose." He watched the fifth ring close. "So that anyone watching can see it was the tower that answered. Not a mage on the wall. Not some knight. The tower." "You can't say we're casting at people if everyone can see it's a building."

Ferra wrote that down, without comment, under Towers: why the ring is slow.



Maeve came up the shelf at half past eight, from the pilot launch that had brought her across the mouth, in the grey coat and a pair of the yard's gumboots that were too big for her, and stood at the foot of the tower and looked up at it.

"Is that the hat?" she said.

"That's the hat," said Armand.

She looked round the rock. At Tess on her stool, at Ambrose with his box, at Bram, at Osric, at Armand, at Emeric and Ferra down the slope. She counted them; he saw her do it. Seven. She'd asked for these seven by name on Tuesday, at the long table, and no one else, and Tess had sent the pilot boat's men back across the mouth with the boat at eight and told them not to come back for her until she hung a towel on the rail.

"Anybody else up here?"

"Gulls," said Osric.

"Gulls," Maeve agreed. "They'll not tell my father." She looked at Emeric. "I asked him. About the turnip."

"What did he say?"

"'Draught.'" She went on looking at Emeric a moment longer. "He said it the same way as the first time. Exactly the same. I've been listening to that man not tell me things for thirty years and I've never once heard him do it badly." She turned back to the tower. "Right. What do you want?"

Tess turned a sheet over on her board. "What you'd throw at a man on a wall."

"I wouldn't throw it at a man on a wall."

"At a wall, then."

"That's different." Maeve took off the grey coat and gave it to Osric, who took it as though it were something that might go off. Under it she had a plain dark wool jersey and nothing on her hands. She walked out across the rock past the white arc, past where Armand had stood, to perhaps a hundred and fifty yards, and turned round.

"What's in the table?" said Emeric quietly.

"For her?" Tess didn't look round. "Nothing of Armand's."

"Nothing?"

"The tower's books had three pages on it. Two of them were wrong." Armand had come to stand beside them. "They had lightning down as fire's cousin. A hot spell. Answer it with water." He made a face. "Water's the worst thing you could put in front of it. Water carries it. You'd make it a road straight into your own wall."

"So what did you put?"

"I didn't," said Armand. "She did." He nodded at Tess.

"You did," said Tess. "In September. You came in off the yard at seven in the morning with your sleeve burned and told me she'd said it wanted to go home." She wrote something on the sheet and underlined it. "So I gave it one."

Out on the rock Maeve lifted her right hand, open, palm out, at the height of her shoulder, and stood very still.

There was no glyph.

Emeric had known there wouldn't be. He'd watched her at six every morning for two months; she didn't draw one, not in the Works yard, not for him, and she'd never told him why and he had never asked. She stood with her hand up and her face perfectly calm, and the air round her went tight. That was all you could feel of it: the air, tightening, the way it does in the minute before a summer storm breaks over the hills, so that the hair on your arms stands up. Ferra, beside him, put her hand flat against her own forearm and looked down at it.

Then it went.

It was not a bolt like his. His came out of him like a thrown rock, ragged and loud and wasteful, and the tail of it kicked back up his arm. Hers went out of her hand like a thread pulled tight between two fingers, very thin and very straight and blue-white, with a sound that wasn't a crack but a long hiss, like fat on a pan; and it was across the white line before anybody on the rock had properly seen it leave her.

The cap came up.

It came up late. Emeric saw it. Everyone saw it; there was no missing it. The bolt was most of the way to the tower's foot before the stone in the glass blazed, and there was no time for the slow blue ring, none, and Armand made a small sound beside him; and the ring came anyway, for an instant, a flicker of it, a ring drawn in the time it takes to blink. And then something went out of the cap to meet the thread, not a spell he knew, nothing he'd ever seen. A line. A thin pale line of something in the air, out from the tower's upper brass band to the bolt, like a rope thrown to a man in the water.

The bolt took it.

It bent. It was going straight at the tower's foot and it bent, all at once, at a sharp angle, upward along that pale line in the air, and hit the brass band two-thirds of the way up the tower, and went into it with a bang that Emeric felt through the rock in his heels; and then down. Down the strap, the copper strap Bram had run inside the stone from the bands to a plate in the rock at the foot; you could see it go, a fierce blue light under the mortar lines for the length of a breath, all the way down; and out into the wet rock and the sea, where the swell was breaking, in a spitting white flash over the weed.

Then nothing. Smoke off the band. A smell like a struck match and a hot pan. The swell broke on the rocks.

The cap went back to its lamp.

Maeve lowered her hand.

Nobody on the rock said anything for a while. Then Bram walked up to his tower and put his palm on the band, carefully, the way you'd test a stove, and took it away, and looked at it, and said, "Warm," and seemed to think that was the best news he'd had all year.

"Thirty yards," said Tess. She was writing very fast. "Read it at thirty. Answered at twenty-five. Road. Took it." She stopped writing and looked at the band, and at the black spatter on the rock at the foot where the plate was, and then up at the cap. "It's too late. It got there. It took it, but it took it at the wall. If that had been a bigger one..."

"That was a big one," said Maeve, walking back across the rock. Her hair had come up off her neck and was standing out in a soft dark cloud round her head, and she was smoothing it down with both hands and not making any progress. "That's as big as I throw at anything I'm not trying to kill."

"Then it's too late for anything you are."

"Yes," said Maeve. She stopped in front of Tess's stool. "What was the rope?"

"Air. Wet air." Tess turned her board round so Maeve could see the sheet, and the drawing on it, which was of the tower and the band and a line drawn out from the band, and a bolt drawn bending along it. "It throws a thread of damp air out off the band, ahead of the bolt, the way Bram's rod gives it a road into the strap. Lightning goes the easiest way home. So I made it an easier way." She looked up at Maeve. "You said that. To him. He said it to me."

Maeve looked at the drawing for a long time. Then she looked at Emeric.

"That's my lesson," she said. "That's what I told you at six in the morning in September with your sleeve on fire. She's put it in a tower."

"Yes."

"It works."

"Too late," said Tess.

"It works too late." Maeve gave the sheet back. "That's still better than anything at court." She looked up at the cap, at the pale stone in its glass, quite calm again, glowing very low. "Nobody's ever had a wall that answered me before. Not once." It didn't sound like a complaint. "Do it again. Bigger. I'll go further back."



She threw nine.

The first three were the same, and the tower took all three late, at thirty yards and then twenty-five and then thirty again, and Bram's band went from warm to hot and then to too hot to touch and he sat on the rock in front of it and refused to let anyone else touch it either. On the fourth Tess had Ambrose ring the Board and the Board ring the Works, and a runner went for Vesk on the etching floor, and in the hour before he came Tess sat on her stool with the cap's coded stones laid out on a cloth on her lap and a lens in her eye, talking to them under her breath.

"It's the reading," she said to Emeric, when he came and crouched beside her. "Not the rope. The rope's fine. The rope's quick. It's the reading. The cap reads a glyph first. That's how Armand built the table. It reads the ring, it knows the ring, it looks it up, it answers." She tapped the cloth. "She doesn't make a ring."

"No."

"So it doesn't know what it is until it's halfway here and it can feel what it is by the heat and the..." She waved her hand in the air, at her own forearm. "The hair. It reads the bolt, not the glyph. That's slow. Slow for lightning." She took the lens out of her eye. "Most of them'll draw a ring. Most mages do. You do. But anyone who's any good won't. And the Church's people are good."

"Can you fix it?"

"I can make it read the air before the bolt. The tightening. That comes first." She looked at Maeve, out on the rock, standing with her hands in her armpits against the cold, waiting. "If she'll stand there and do it twenty more times so I can see what it looks like."

Maeve did it twenty more times. Not the bolts: only the tightening, the minute before, held and let go without throwing, over and over, while Vesk recut two of the coded stones on a block of wood on his knees and Tess read the cap with her eye shut and her hand flat on the tower's foot. It took until past noon. Osric went back across the mouth in the launch at eleven and came back with bread and cheese and a can of tea, and nobody ate much of it except Bram, who ate most of it.

At a quarter to one Maeve threw the tenth.

The cap came up while she was still standing with her hand raised, before the thread had left her fingers, a clear bright lamp, and the blue ring drew itself in front of it, not slowly but not in a blink either, a ring you could see; and the pale rope went out off the band and down across the rock to her side of the white line; and the bolt went into it at eighty yards and bent up along it and into the band, and down the strap in a blue line under the mortar, and out into the sea.

"Eighty," said Tess. "Read it before it went. Answered at the line." She put her pencil down on the board. "That's it. That's the one."

Maeve lowered her hand. She stood out on the rock for a moment with the cold wind off the sea in her hair, looking at the tower. Then she bowed to it. Not much; a nod from the waist, the way you'd bow to a man you'd fenced who had beaten you fairly. And came back across the rock.



The launch came for them at two, when Osric hung his shirt on the rail because none of them had brought a towel. On the way across the mouth Maeve sat in the stern beside Emeric with her coat back on and her hands round the last of the cold tea, and watched the point and the tower going away behind them, a short grey round thing on a grey rock with a light on top of it that you'd never have looked at twice.

"Seven people," she said.

"Seven."

"And the four on that board, and a clerk. And the Motion Shop in September." She drank the cold tea and made a face at it. "I've lost count. More than a dozen people in the world know, who aren't my mother. In two months."

"Is that too many?"

She thought about it seriously, the way she thought about everything he asked her at six in the morning, as though it were a question about where to put her feet.

"I've been hiding it for twenty-six years," she said. "Since the curtains. I don't know what too many is. I've never had any." She turned the can in her hands. "My mother said, a girl who throws lightning gets married to whoever needs a war won. That's true. That's still true, at court." She looked up at the castle on its hill above the town, and the dome beside it. "I don't know if it's true here yet. Nobody here's asked me to win anything." She paused. "Your Tess asked me to stand on a rock and help her make a wall."

"Did you mind?"

"No," said Maeve, and then, after a moment, sounding surprised about it: "I liked it."

They came in under the sea wall's light. On the root of the wall, beside the Harbour Board in its customs tower, the first of the two towers stood with its cap glowing low, and on the quay beneath it a small crowd had gathered, as it had every day that month: a few carters, a woman with a basket, a gang of boys from the school who ought to have been in the school. One of the boys saw the launch and waved, and then they were all waving, at the king, for no reason at all.

"What do they call it?" said Maeve.

"The towers? Tess's towers. Some of them call them the hats." He watched the boys. "Armand calls them the Answer Towers. He says that's what they're for. Somebody asks a question with a spell and the tower answers it."

"And everybody can see the ring."

"Everybody can see the ring."

Maeve watched the tower on the wall root go past, and the boys, and the low pale light in the glass.

"Ten," she said. "And it answered me at the line on the tenth." She tipped the last of the tea over the side. "Tell Tess I'll come back. When she's got more of them. I'd like to try the next one before anyone else does."

"Why?"

"Because the next person who tries is going to mean it," said Maeve, "and I'd sooner it had met me first."
