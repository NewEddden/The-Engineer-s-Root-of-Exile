Chapter 194 - Verandel's Claim

The armourer sent his suit back at dawn with a new left shoulder on it.

It came down to the harbor house on the same cart that had taken Rowena's crate up, with a boy, and it was laid out on the dining room table in its pieces, black, all of it, except the left pauldron, which was bright. Emberfell steel, plain; a smooth round shell of polished plate, riveted where the black one had been, with the cape's bent catch taken out of the crushed one and straightened and set into it. Tied to the catch with a bit of string there was a card, in a square hand that had plainly not written anything in years.

The catch holds. The rest is ugly.

"He's right," said Ward, looking at it.

"It's beautiful," said Emeric.

"It's both," said Ward. "That's armourers."



Rowena would not fly over her own city.

"I'll not have the fishing families looking up from their nets and thinking I've turned into a gull," she said, on the quay, at first light. "They have enough to talk about." So the K Discovery took her down the inlet on her foils instead, an hour, to a bay on the western shore with no village on it and no road to it and a long pale beach and cliffs round it a hundred feet high, where nobody but the goats would see; and she stood on the open deck in her suit in the sun with the inlet wind pulling at her plume, and Ward stood in front of her in his.

She had all of it on. The armourer had taken the finger off the leg in the night, and it was right; she stood in it as if she had been born in it. Lorcan and Hesta's lace fell from the cuirass over her thighs in a long white skirt, edged at the hem with feathers that lifted in the wind; the snowflake cloak hung from her shoulders over the furled cape, white on white, with its down collar up round the helm; the blue scarf was knotted at her throat; and the plume went up from the crest of the winged helm and streamed out behind her a yard long. Her knights, the dozen of them who had come down the inlet with her on the deck, looked at her and did not say anything. Tobe looked at her with his mouth open again. Fletcher looked at her and then, for a long time, at the lace, with the face of a man calculating its drag.

"The stick," said Ward. "Push it the way you want to go. Twist it to turn. Let go and she holds where she is."

"And the lever lifts."

"A notch at a time. Don't go past the second notch till I say."

"Why not?"

"Because I said," said Ward.

Rowena looked at him through the blue pane. Then she moved the lever a notch, and her boots came up off the deck an inch; and another notch, and she was at Ward's eye level, hanging, with the lace stirring round her knees; and she said "Oh," once, quietly, in a voice Emeric had not heard her use, and then nothing at all for some time.

He had taken a week. A week of falls in the shed and the yard and the river, black and blue, learning to fly a suit with no levers through his own band, with Tess at the end of every day writing the falls in her book. Ward had taken longer, in the first suit, years ago; Osric had taken a fortnight and broken a wrist.

She took an hour.

She went up off the deck a notch at a time, as Ward had said, and hung, and came down, and went up again. She pushed the stick, and went forward over the water, slowly, and let go, and held. Twisted it, and turned, and turned back. Within a quarter of an hour she was going round the ship at the height of the dome, at a run's pace, with Ward beside her in his black and her white lace streaming. Within half an hour she had stopped listening to Ward. And a little after that she did the thing that nobody else in the world could have done in that suit, and Emeric, at the rail, felt her do it.

She woke the second ring.

She put her own mana into it, into the six asleep in their barrels at her forearms and thighs and spine; he felt the note go out of her, dense, the struck bell, and the nodes take it and wake. And she did not go where the stick went. She went where she went. A turn no lever could have given her, tight, on the inside of her own body, like a swallow coming round the eaves; and then a burst, straight up, fifty feet in a breath, the plume snapping behind her; and a drop, and a stop, a man's height over the water, hanging there with her lace dragging on the swell.

Ward came down beside her.

"That wasn't the second notch," he said.

"No," said Rowena. Her voice came through the helm a little breathless. "It wasn't the lever at all."

"I know," said Ward. He looked at her a while. "That was very good," he said, the way he said it to Emeric about once a year. "Don't do it again till I've seen you do the drop."



She did the drop at three hundred feet, over the middle of the bay.

Ward made her take the snowflake cloak off for it. She did not want to. Ward said that a cloak over the wing was a cloak over the wing, and that if Hesta had laced it to anything that mattered he would apologise to Hesta in person and in writing; and Rowena looked at him a long time through her pane, and then unpinned it, and handed it down to the deck, where Tobe took it as if he had been handed a baby.

"The count stone," said Ward, over the water. "It's armed now. It wasn't on the deck. It skips its first arming so it doesn't kill your hearts at a man's height when you go up." He hung beside her at three hundred feet, black against the cliffs. "When it counts out, your hearts die. Both. The suit's dead. You're a woman in plate three hundred feet up. You'll feel the bellows go slack on your back, and on the third count the springs throw the wing, and you'll glide. Then the chute. The ripcord's at your left breast. Then the water." He looked at her. "Imani's jacket'll bring you up."

"And my own," said Rowena. "My earth. The second ring."

"In a real one, dead," said Ward. "Everything you cast. Your earth. The second ring, which runs on what you pour into it. Anything that goes out of you stops at your skin. What's already in you stays: your arms and your legs keep the knight in them. That won't hold you up three hundred feet over a bay." He paused. "In this one, the count stone only kills the hearts. So you'll have your own gift, if you want it. You could hold yourself up."

"I'll not want it," said Rowena. "If it were real I'd have nothing to hold me up. So I'll have nothing." She settled her hands. "Count."

The count stone counted. Emeric watched it from the deck. Rowena's hearts went dark behind their blue panes all at once, both, and she dropped; a woman in bright plate with white lace round her, falling, straight down, out of the sky over the bay. He felt Ward go after her. He felt his own hand close on the rail.

One. Two. Three.

The wing threw. Abloh's cape went out from her shoulders on its battens like a hawk opening, grey and wide, and caught the air with a sound like a sheet on a line, and she was not falling; she was going, out across the bay, long and level and fast, with the lace streaming under the wing, toward the cliffs. A long way. Further than he had gone the first time. Then her right hand went to her breast, and the chute came off her back in a white bloom, and she hung under it, swinging, over the water, and came down into the bay with a splash that went up white in the sun.

The ship's boat had her in a minute, with Cade at the oars. She came up the ladder onto the deck streaming water from every plate, the lace sodden and grey and hanging off her like weed, the plume flat down her back, and Imani's cork jacket round her ribs under the wing like a fat brown waistcoat. She stood on the deck and dripped, and took her helm off, and her hair came down out of its rings, wet, and she was laughing.

Out loud, on the deck, in front of her knights. Not the smile in the long room that had pulled at the scar. A laugh, the whole of her face, the scar and all, like a girl who has jumped off a harbor wall for a dare.

"Again," said Rowena.

"No," said Ward.

"I want to do it again."

"You'll do it again," said Ward, "when Hesta's dried the lace. She's going to kill me as it is."



Ferra did not come on the run.

She had said so on the voyage, before they ever reached the inlet, and she said it again in the harbor house that afternoon when the ship was being made ready, at the table with the book. Somebody had to be in Emberfell with clean hands and a clean book while the Holder of Kaldo was somewhere else doing whatever he was going to be doing, she said; somebody who had sat at the Sovereign's table and could be seen sitting in the Sovereign's city, writing letters, all the two days. And it was the Duvrenne coast, and casks. She would rather be able to say she had never been near it.

She did not look at him when she said it.

Imani stayed too, on Ward's rule, which Ward made at the same table without being asked: a healer did not go onto a beach that might have to be left in a hurry, and the ship had a sick bay and a chest and Ward could pack a nose if he had to. Imani looked at Ward when he said it, for a long moment, and did not argue, which Ward plainly found more unnerving than an argument. Vantic stayed because the House, she said, had no business on any beach whatever.

Rowena came. That was Ward's rule too, and he made it to her face on the quay, in front of the grey knight, who did not like any of it: she could come, because it was her coast they went out by and she had a right to see what passed it; and she would not set foot on any shore that was not her own until she was back on her own quay. Not one foot. Not on a beach, not on a rock, not on a boat's thwart that was touching sand.

"You're very free with your rules on my quay," said Rowena.

"Yes," said Ward.

"I'm the Sovereign."

"You are," said Ward. "And if anybody on any beach between here and Duvrenne sees the Sovereign of Emberfell stepping out of a Kaldo boat, there'll be a war. With you in it." He looked at her. "You'll stay aboard."

Rowena weighed it.

"I'll stay aboard," she said.

Ferra came down to the quay when they went. She stood at the foot of the harbor house steps in her coat with the book under her arm, with Imani beside her, and Vantic a little behind; and the K Discovery went down the harbor on the motor and out round the mole and up onto her foils, and Emeric stood at the iris port in the saloon and watched the three of them get small. Imani lifted one hand. Ferra did not. She stood with the book and watched until the bend of the inlet took them, and he did not see whether she turned away first or the hill did.



They lifted at dusk past the inlet mouth, out of sight of anything but the sea.

Rowena had seen the Discovery fly before. She had not been in her. She sat at the long table in the saloon in a plain coat with her hands flat on the wood while the matrix took the ship up off the water a notch at a time, and the sea went away under the ports, and the coast, and the inlet, a long dark crack in the hills going back toward a faint glow that was her city's lamps under the mountain's pale plume; and she did not say anything at all. When they were up above the coast and turning north, Fletcher brought her up to the top cockpit, because Fletcher had decided in the bay that morning that she understood boats; and she stood behind his seat with one hand on the frame, as Ward had stood, and looked out of the glass at the whole of the south coast of Randillia going away below her in the dark, every town's lamps a scatter of sparks, and the stars coming out over the top of it.

"Harrold's," she said.

"Yes," said Emeric.

"All of it." She looked at it. "I lived in it fifteen years. I never saw it." She was quiet. "It's smaller than he thinks."

They went north across it, dark, high. Not in the Band; just under its floor, with the probe outside the bow glowing and every open stone full and nobody bleeding. Over the land, and over the capital's hills, which Fletcher pointed out to her by the glow of the capital itself far off in the dark; and over the north, which was black; and a little before the ninth hour, over the sea again. The strait, narrow and black, with the lights of Randillia's last fort on the near side of it, and on the far side, low and long and pale under the stars, the Isle of Shards.

"There's a pitch lake," said Fletcher, from the stick, to nobody. "Everybody knows that. Middle of the island, between Randillia's end and theirs. A lake of tar, a mile across, warm in the middle. You can walk on it in winter." He looked down at the island coming toward them out of the dark. "Everybody knows it's there and nobody takes it. Because it's in the middle, and whoever takes it, the other lot come for him. Fifty years. Sixty." He shook his head. "A mile of the best caulking in the world, and every shipwright on the coast paying Duvrenne for theirs."

Emeric looked at the island.

"Take her over it," he said. "High. Dark. And then hold her, two miles up, wherever Ward and I say."



They went down in the suits, Ward and he, from two miles, off the open deck, into the dark.

No legs. That had been the rule since the bay: the ship did not land. Three feet the size of doors coming down on the sand of a desert island would leave three marks a man could see from the Kesrath tower in the morning, and every patrol on the island would want to know what had made them. So the K Discovery hung two miles up over Verandel's Claim, dark, silent, with Fletcher at the stick and the steady clicking its trim keys, and Rowena at the port in the saloon with her hands on the sill; and the two of them stepped off the deck with their capes furled and their hearts lit, and fell.

He held the door open to Ward the whole way down. He had to; it was black. A desert under a sky with no moon, pale sand and dark rock and hardly a light anywhere on it. One, far off to the north-east, on a headland: a single yellow lamp, high up, steady. Kesrath's tower, at the far end of the island. And under the headland, low down, a few more, small, that went out and came back as he watched, one and then another, like men passing a word along a wall. He laid it into the overlay, a small mark, so that Ward would have it in his sight wherever he turned. Then the ground, as his sense found it, coming up: the shape of the island under them in faint lines on the dark, like a chart drawn on a window, the high ground and the low, the long pale patch of the shard fields to the north under the tower, glittering, faint, even in the dark; and in the middle, flat and black and round, a mile across, the lake.

He smelled it before he was down. Through the helm. Eggs. The same smell as the works at Emberfell, the same as the boatyard under the pitch kettle, but older, and heavier, and with something sweet and oily in it that the works had not had.

They came down on rock at the lake's south edge. Not on the sand; on a long flat tongue of grey stone that ran out into the lake's margin, where their boots would leave nothing; the overlay had marked it for Ward, a pale square on the dark, and Ward came down on it a breath after him and planted beside him without a sound.

The lake lay in front of them. It did not look like a lake. It looked like a field that somebody had poured hot tar over and let set, black, wrinkled, crusted, with a faint sheen on it under the stars, and cracks in the crust here and there where something softer showed through and gave off a little warmth into the cold desert night, and a little smell. Fletcher had been right. A man could have walked out on it.

Emeric did not walk out on it. He turned his back on it.

The smell was wrong for the lake. He had known it coming down. Pitch was heavy; it lay in its bed and stank and stayed. What he smelled now was lighter, and it was not coming off the lake but from behind him, from the low ground to the south-west of the rock, where the desert went down in a long shallow fold toward the sea. He stood on the tongue of stone with his helm off and his face to the wind, and smelled it. Then he looked at the ground.

The lie of it. He could see it faintly even in the dark, and his sense gave him the rest: the land rising, very gently, in a long low dome, from the lake out toward the coast, and falling away on the far side; a swelling under the sand like a buried loaf. He had seen that shape on Earth in a book, in a diagram, in a hand he could no longer picture: an arch in the rock under the ground, where something lighter than water collected at the top and could not get out, and seeped, at the edges, where the rock was broken.

At the edge of the dome, down in the fold, there was a gully.

They walked to it. On rock, all the way; Ward behind him, finding the rock in the overlay, saying nothing. The gully was a crack in the desert a man's height deep, with sand at its bottom and scrub at its lip, and the smell came up out of it so strong that Ward made a sound in his throat. Emeric went down into it on a shelf of stone, and crouched at the bottom, and put his gauntlet down to the sand.

It was wet.

Not with water. He took the gauntlet off and touched it with his bare fingers, and they came up dark, and thin, and they shone; and when he rubbed them together the stuff on them was slick and light and smelled of eggs and something sweet, and ran. There was a pool a little further along, the size of a washing tub, in a hollow of the rock, and he could see the stars in it; and on the stars, when he leaned over, there was a faint skin of colour, the way there is on a puddle in a yard where a cart has stood.

Not pitch. Pitch was what this became when it had lain in the sun a thousand years and the light parts of it had gone off into the air. This was the light part. Coming up out of the dome under his feet, through the cracks at its edge, into a gully on a desert island that everybody in two kingdoms knew for its tar lake and nobody walked across; and it had been coming up here for longer than there had been kingdoms, and nobody had ever crouched in this gully in the dark and put a finger in it, because why would they.

Light crude. Better than Duvrenne's burning water; lighter, cleaner, nearer to ready. Lamp oil and grease and spirit, a well's worth, under his feet.

He sat on his heels at the bottom of the gully with his fingers wet and the stars in the pool and did not move.

"What is it?" said Ward, from the lip.

"Oil," said Emeric.

"The lake's oil."

"The lake's what's left. This is the oil." He stood up. "Nobody knows it's here. Nobody's ever looked."



The tree was at the gully's lip, in the scrub.

He nearly put his hand on it climbing out. A small gnarled thing, no higher than his chest, grey, with thin twisted branches and small grey-green leaves like a sage bush's, growing out of the sand in a crack of the rock as if it had been put there to hold the rock together; and when his gauntlet scraped one of its branches, the branch broke, and where it broke, in the starlight, something white came out.

He took the gauntlet off again. He broke another twig, carefully, and held it up.

White sap. Milky, thick, beading at the break. He touched it with his finger and drew the finger away, and the sap came with it. It stretched. A thread of it, white, between his fingertip and the twig, the length of his thumb, and then longer, and did not break; and when he let go of the twig and pressed his finger and thumb together with the sap between them and drew them apart, it stretched again between them, and went back. Like the gum off a road on a hot day. Like a band round a jar.

He stood in the scrub at the edge of a desert gully with rubber between his fingers.

Seals. Gaskets. Hoses. The pumps at Kaldo all leaked at every joint because leather was the best thing anybody had to pack them with, and leather rotted. Tess's wires, sheathed in waxed cotton that cracked. The ship's seal, held with felt and pitch. A tyre. A belt that did not stretch and slip like a leather one. A diving suit, which he had never even let himself put on a list.

And the only other place on the map where anybody had ever found anything like this was in Duvrenne, across the strait, in the houses of a princess he had insulted at his father's table seven years ago; and Saban had said in the autumn that Duvrenne sold its little rubber by the ounce to doctors and nobody else.

He broke a twig with sap on it and put it in the pouch at his belt, in a twist of paper, and a handful of the leaves. Then he looked at the tower.

The yellow lamp on its headland, far off in the dark, steady. Kesrath's. And under his boots, Verandel's Claim, which was his father's. The Crown's land. Randillia's end of an island two crowns had been bleeding on for sixty years; the gully lay on the Claim's side of the lake, a mile inside it, near enough to the middle of the island that Kesrath would never believe it was not, and the light under his feet in it was on ground nobody could take without the other crown coming for him.

It was theft. He made himself say it, in his head, plainly. If he came back here and took this, he would be taking it from his father; from the Crown, from Harrold, whose land it was, without asking, in the dark. And if Kesrath ever saw a Randillian taking anything out of the middle of the island, it would not matter whose son he was or whose land it was; there would be ships in the strait inside a month, and men dead on the sand, as there had been before, and some of them would be Kaldo's.

He would do it anyway.

He knew that before he had finished thinking the rest of it. Not now. Not on this trip. He had come down to look, and he would go back up having looked, and that was all, tonight. But later, in secret, when Kaldo needed oil more than it could buy from Duvrenne's beaches by the cask, and rubber more than it could beg from Duvrenne's doctors by the ounce; when there was no other way to get either. Because the only other way to get either went through the houses of a woman who had every reason in the world to want him on his knees.

He did not like himself very much for it. He noted that too. Then he put his gauntlet back on.

"We're going," he said to Ward. "West. The cliffs. Then up."



The west cliffs went straight down into the sea, two hundred feet of grey rock with the surf going white at their foot even on a still night, and the overlay showed him the faces before his eyes did.

Cut faces. Above the tide line, all along the cliff, the rock had been worked: square-edged hollows in it the size of rooms, and the size of houses, one above another, with the marks of tools on their walls, old, very old, softened by a hundred years of salt wind. Seams. Whatever had been in them had been taken out of them a long time ago, by men on ropes and ledges, and nothing was left but the holes. He went down past them in the dark on the glide, close, with the cape spread, and felt the rock with his sense as he went: dead, all of it. Not a grain of anything left.

The beach at the foot was grey.

Not sand. He came down onto it at the tide's edge, on the hard wet part where the next wave would take his prints, and Ward beside him; and it crunched. Flakes. The whole beach, from the water to the cliff's foot, was made of them: thin grey flakes of stone the size of a thumbnail and smaller, millions of them, chipped off the seams by the men who had worked them and thrown down the cliff in their leavings, and ground by the sea for a hundred years until they lay in a long grey bank like a beach of old ice.

He bent and picked one up.

A grey flake, no bigger than a thumbnail, the colour of old ice. Dead; he felt nothing in it at all. A stone that had once stopped arrows.

He had seen one exactly like it. On a cord, at a woman's collar, in the snow outside the school at Kaldo, under her fingers, years ago; and he had known it then for a dead shard off the Isle, and had not told her about the posting-house yard, or the old soldier who had written a name and a year on the back of a toll ticket and said not to expect anything soon.

Sela's son had stood here. On this beach, or one just like it, under these cliffs; he had bent down, as Emeric just had, and picked a flake out of the grey and put it in his pocket for his mother. And then something had happened to him, between this beach and wherever he was now, and nobody knew what, and Varek was still asking.

A where. That was what he had now. He had had a where before, from Varek, a whole island of it; now he had a beach. He did not have a how, or a when, or a who. He did not have anything to put beside Varek's toll ticket except a flake of grey stone in his palm that a hundred other men had picked up on the same beach.

He would not tell her. Not with only this. He had decided that in the snow outside the school and it was still right: he would not hand Sela a beach.

He put the flake in his pouch beside the rubber twig.

Ward was standing at the edge of the surf looking up at the cut faces in the cliff.

"I know what that is," Ward said. "The men in the war used to bring them home. Every one of them had one." He did not look at Emeric's pouch. "Some of them came home."

They went up.



Rowena was at the port in the saloon when they came in over the deck rail out of the dark with their capes furled and their hearts spent and sand on their boots. She had not moved, Fletcher said afterward, the whole two hours. She had stood at the port with her hands on the sill and watched the island, and the lamp on the Kesrath tower, and the dark place over the middle of the island where two men in suits had gone down and not come up for a long time.

She looked at him as he came in. At the sand on his boots, and the pouch at his belt, and his face.

She did not ask anything.

He knew why. Verandel's Claim was the Crown's. And the Crown was Harrold, and Harrold was her husband still, on paper, whatever twenty years and six hundred miles and a scar had made of it; and whatever Harrold's third son had been doing in the dark on Harrold's land with sand on his boots, it was a thing she could either know or not know, and she had chosen. She stood at the port and looked at him a moment longer, and then turned back to the glass, and watched the island go away under them as Fletcher took her up and north-east toward Duvrenne, and the yellow lamp on the tower go out of sight behind the curve of the dark; and she said nothing about any of it, then or after.

He sat at the long table and took the twig out of his pouch, in its paper, and the flake, and looked at them both under the bulb. Then he put them away again.

They were off the Duvrenne coast two hours before dawn, on the water, dark, with nothing to do but wait for the light.



Imani had not meant to say anything at all.

She had meant to go to bed. It was late, past the eleventh hour, and the harbor house was quiet; Vantic had gone to her room after supper with a book and a candle and the door shut, and the two knights at the foot of the stair had become two others, and the city outside the shutters had gone to its own beds terrace by terrace up the mountain until there was nothing lit but the dome's foot and the lamps along the stairs and the plume going up pale over all of it. She had come up to Ferra's room to say good night. That was all. She had been doing it every night since the voyage, out of a habit that had made itself in the women's cabin and that neither of them had remarked on.

Ferra was at the window with the book.

She was not writing. She had the book open on the sill in front of her, and the pen beside it, and she was looking out through the gap in the shutters at the dark where the inlet went away between its hills toward the sea; and she did not turn round when Imani came in.

"They'll be over the strait by now," said Ferra.

"Probably."

"Or further. I don't know how fast she goes north. Nobody's ever written it down." Ferra put her finger on the edge of the page. "I'll make Fletcher write it down."

Imani sat on the bed. It was a wide bed, wider than the berths, with a white cover; the harbor house's best room, which the grey knight had given the chancellor of four baronies without being asked. She looked at Ferra's back. At the set of her shoulders, which were up round her ears, and her hair, which was pinned so hard that Imani could see the skin of her scalp pulling at the crown. At her hand on the edge of the book. Ferra's hands told you everything, if you watched them. Most people watched her face, which told you nothing, and missed it.

"You're cross with him," said Imani.

"I'm not cross with him."

"You didn't wave."

"I never wave." Ferra turned a page she was not reading. "I'm not cross. I don't know what he's doing out there. I know he's doing something. He had the face on at breakfast, and he went down to the ship with Ward afterward and the two of them were in it for an hour with the door shut." She turned another page. "He'll tell me. Term two. He'll tell both of us." She stopped. "He'll tell me when it's done. That's what I'm cross about."

"So you are cross."

"I'm cross about the timing," said Ferra, "which isn't the same."

Imani laughed. She had not meant to do that either.

Ferra turned round. She looked at Imani on the bed, and her face did the thing it did at the long table when somebody had laughed at a figure she had been very serious about, and then it did something else; and she came away from the window, and sat down on the other end of the bed, with the book in her lap, and did not open it.

They sat for a while.

"The berth," said Ferra.

Imani looked at her.

"On the ship. In the cabin. With him." Ferra was looking at the book in her lap. "We haven't talked about it."

"No."

"We talk about everything." Ferra's hands were flat on the book's cover. "We talked about the terms for three hours. We talked about the linen press. You asked me where his hands were." Something happened at the corner of her mouth. "For the chart."

"I did."

"And then the three of us were on one berth with your elbow in the upper bunk and my shirt half up my back, and his mouth on your shoulder, and we've had five days, and neither of us has said one word about it."

"No," said Imani. "We haven't."

She looked at Ferra. Ferra looked at the book.

"I'm not," said Ferra. She stopped, and started again, very precisely, the way she started a figure she had got wrong. "I want to say this so it's said. I'm not drawn to you. Not like that. I don't want you to think, after the berth, that I was. That I am." She went pink. "I looked at you. Once. On the berth. Over his shoulder. And I want you to know it wasn't that."

"I know it wasn't," said Imani. "I looked at you too."

Ferra looked up.

"It wasn't that for me either." Imani put her hands round her knees. "I've never once in my life wanted a woman. I've had my hands on more women than you've had hot dinners, and none of them ever made me want anything but to wash my hands." She considered. "You included. You've a very good back. I noticed it on the berth. Professionally."

Ferra made a sound that was very nearly a laugh.

"So that's not it," said Imani. "Neither of us."

"No."

"Then what was it?" She watched Ferra's hands. "The look. Over his shoulder."

Ferra did not answer at once. Her hands moved on the book, and stopped.

"I didn't mind," she said.

Imani waited.

"That's what it was. I looked at you, and you were there, with him, the way I was, and I waited to mind." Ferra's voice was very even. "I'd thought about it. For years. Since the terms. Before. What it would be like if it ever happened, with you in the room, and I'd thought, I'll hate it. I'll be polite and I'll hate it and I'll go home and write a column about it." She looked at the shutters. "And I looked at you over his shoulder and I didn't hate it. I didn't mind at all. You looked as frightened as I felt and you didn't let go, and I thought, good. Good, she's here." Ferra's hands were very still. "It surprised me more than the Band."

"That's what I said," said Imani. "On the ship. That it surprised me more than the sky."

"I know. I heard you." Ferra looked at her. "I didn't believe you. I thought you were being kind."

"I'm never kind," said Imani. "Ask anybody in the low town."

They looked at each other. And Ferra laughed, out loud, the thing she did so rarely that the whole of Kaldo talked about it when she did; and Imani laughed with her, on the white bed, with her arms round her knees, until her eyes were wet. Nothing was funny. They had been carrying it for five days, the both of them, separately, very carefully, like two people each carrying one end of a full tub across a yard and neither of them able to see the other over the rim; and they had just put it down.



"She knew," said Imani, when they had stopped. "Rowena. At the first dinner. Before we'd been at her table an hour."

"She told us she knew." Ferra wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist. "In the water. In the first breath. She sat down in the spring and looked at us both and said, 'So. Which of you is first?' And you said..."

"I said neither of us."

"And she said, 'Good. Then you'll want to know how it's done.'" Ferra opened the book. "And then she talked for an hour and a half in water so hot I couldn't think, about Harrold." She turned the pages. "I wrote it down on the stair. You held the lamp."

"I remember. You dropped the pen twice."

"It was very steep." Ferra found the page. She held the book on her knees and looked at it; and Imani could see, upside down, the lines in Ferra's hand, square and careful, and a little ragged at the ends where the stair had jolted, and numbered. Of course they were numbered.

"She said it's an art," said Ferra. "That was first. Before anything." She read it. "'It's an art. Sharing a man. Like a sword. Nobody's born holding it right, and the ones who think they are get cut. You learn the grip, and then you practise, and you're still learning it at fifty.'" She looked up. "I thought she was being clever. I don't think she was."

"No," said Imani. "She wasn't." She remembered the steam, and the hot rock of the pool's edge at her back, and Rowena in the water to her chin with her hair floating round her like weed and her scar white in the lamplight and her eyes closed. "Read the rest."

Ferra read the rest.

"'There were three of us. Two were friends. Guess which marriage I'd have again.'" Ferra stopped. "She said that and then she didn't say anything for a long time. And then she said, 'Amara. I'd have married Harrold again tomorrow for Amara. Not for him. Him I could take or leave. For her.'"

"I remember."

"One," said Ferra, reading. "'Be on each other's side first. Him second. Always. He'll try to make it the other way round without knowing he's doing it, because he's a man and he thinks he's the middle. He's not the middle. You are, the two of you. He's the thing you share.'" She looked at it. "Sabina was on nobody's side but her own, she said. So she and Amara were on each other's, against her, for eight years, and it was the best eight years she had in that court."

"Two," said Imani. She did not need the book for that one. "'Never let him carry tales. If he says, she said, don't believe him. Go and ask her. Every time. Even when it's nothing.'"

"'Especially when it's nothing,'" said Ferra. "She said that twice." She wrote a small mark in the margin. "Three. 'Don't count nights. Count years. Nights make you mean. Years make you kind, if you let them.'" She stopped, and looked at that one for a while. "I'd have counted," she said. "I know I would. I'd have had a column."

"You'd have had two columns," said Imani. "One for you and one for me. Balanced at the quarter."

"I would." Ferra did not smile. "I'm glad she said it."

"Four."

"'Keep a room he isn't in.'" Ferra read it slowly. "'Not a secret. You'll keep no secrets from each other, if you've any sense. A room. A thing that's yours that isn't him. Mine was the yard. Amara's was her stillroom, and she'd lock the door on Harrold and let me in.'" She looked up. "My books."

"My ward," said Imani.

"Five. 'Laugh at him together. It's the best thing two women can do with one man. He'll be the better for it, and so will you.'" Ferra's mouth moved. "We've been doing that for years."

"We're very good at it," said Imani. "Six."

Ferra went pink.

She went pink all at once, the way she had gone in Imani's kitchen in October, all the way up into her hair so that you could not see where the freckles stopped; and she looked down at the book, at the sixth line, and did not read it.

"I'll read it," said Imani.

"You don't know what it says."

"I was in the water. I know exactly what it says." Imani leaned over and turned the book toward her on Ferra's knees and read it aloud, in the voice she used for a chart. "'There's no winning in a bed. If one of you's trying to win, you've both lost, and so has he. It's not a race and it's not a ledger. And the first time is nearly always bad, and that's what the second time is for, and anybody who tells you different is selling something.'" She looked up. "She was right about that. Medically."

"You don't know that," said Ferra, very pink.

"I've been told it by every woman in the low town who ever came to me afterward," said Imani. "Which is most of them. I've never had any reason to doubt them." She paused. "I've never had any reason to find out."

Ferra looked at her.

The room was very quiet. Outside the shutters a cart went along the quay below, late, its wheels loud on the stone, and stopped.

"Neither have I," said Ferra.

"I know."

"I'm frightened of it." Ferra said it to the book. Precisely. As if she were stating a balance. "I've been frightened of it since the linen press. Before. I'm twenty-eight years old and I've never been with anybody, and I know what happens, I've read every book in Armand's study that says anything about it and two that Doran keeps locked up, and I know what happens, and I'm frightened of it." She turned a page back and forward. "I've got a list."

"Of course you have."

"It's not funny."

"It's a bit funny," said Imani. "What's on it?"

Ferra did not show her. She held the book shut on her knees with both hands.

"What I don't know," she said. "Which is everything that isn't in a book." She looked at Imani. "You know. You're a healer. You know where everything goes."

"I know where everything goes," said Imani. "I know what it's called, and what goes wrong with it, and how to stitch it when it does, and what to give a girl the morning after to stop her being sick with nerves. I've delivered half the children in the low town." She put her chin on her knees. "I've a body's facts. All of them. I've none of the rest." She was quiet. "I'm twenty-nine. I've never been kissed by anybody but him. I lay awake for a week after the north tower wondering if I'd done it wrong."

"You hadn't done it wrong."

"How would you know?"

"Because I was watching his face on the berth," said Ferra, "when you did it again."

Imani looked at her. And something in her chest, that had been tight since the north tower's leads in August, let go a little; not all the way.

"I'm frightened too," she said. "Of all of it. Of being bad at it. Of being looked at. Of my body doing something I know the name of and can't stop." She looked at her hands round her knees. "I've watched women be brave about worse. It doesn't help as much as you'd think."

They sat with it.

"Seven," said Ferra, after a long time, quietly. She opened the book again. "'Tell him what you want. Out loud. He'll not guess. They never guess, and the clever ones guess worst, because they think they've worked it out. And tell each other. Tell each other first.'"

"So we're telling each other."

"We're telling each other," said Ferra. She drew a breath. "I don't want to wait outside a door."

Imani looked up.

"Whatever he's planning," said Ferra. "And he is planning something; he's got the face, he had it on the berth, he told us so. I don't want it to be me and then you. Or you and then me. I don't want to lie awake one night in Kaldo knowing you're in a room with him for the first time and I'm in mine." She held the book. "And I don't want you to lie awake knowing it about me. That was the term. Nobody's first. If it's going to be a first, it's going to be the three of us. That's what I want." She looked at Imani. "I'm telling you first."

"Yes," said Imani.

"Yes what?"

"Yes. The three of us. Whatever he's planning." Imani found she had known it since the berth; since before, since the door of the sick bay on the climb with her box at her feet. "That's what I want too."

Ferra wrote it down.

She did it at once, on the page under Rowena's seventh line, in her square hand, steady now; the date, and the place, and then: Agreed. The first time, the three of us. Whatever it is. F. And held out the pen.

Imani took it, and looked at the line, and did not sign it.

"There's an eighth," she said.

Ferra looked at the book. "There isn't. I stopped at seven. She stopped at seven."

"She didn't stop at seven. You dropped the pen on the stair at seven, and I picked it up, and you said you'd remember the last one." Imani held the pen. "You didn't write it because you didn't like it."

Ferra said nothing, which was an answer.

"'When one of you needs him more,'" said Imani, from memory, from the steam and the hot rock and Rowena's eyes shut in the lamplight, "'let her have him. And don't make her pay for it after. Not with a look, not with a night, not with a word. There'll be a time it's you.'" She turned the pen in her fingers. "She said Amara needed Harrold a great deal, once, for a whole winter, and Rowena let her, and never once made her pay for it. And then the winter after, Rowena's father died, and it was Rowena who needed him. And Amara gave him back."

"I remember," said Ferra, very low.

"So." Imani put the nib on the page, under Ferra's line, and did not write yet. "I want a clause."

"It's not a contract."

"You've written it in a ledger with the date and initialled it," said Imani. "It's a contract." She held the pen still. "I'm a healer. I know what happens to people. Somebody falls ill, or somebody dies on the table, or there's a night in the ward that's so bad you can't be by yourself after it. It's not a plan. It's what bodies do. If that happens to one of us, before whatever he's planning, and she needs him that night, and he's there..." She looked at Ferra. "Then she has him. And she tells the other one the next morning. Herself, first thing, before anybody. And nobody keeps score. Not a look, not a night, not a word."

Ferra stared at her.

"That's the opposite of what I just wrote," she said.

"It's an exception," said Imani. She held her eye. "Rowena's eighth line. You said it yourself in the water: she was right about all of it. And I'll not have either of us lying awake in Kaldo with a bad night and a plan and a promise, choosing between the two." She paused. "Term two still holds. No secret. She tells the other in the morning. That's all."

Ferra did not answer at once.

Then she held out her hand for the pen. Imani gave it to her. Ferra wrote, under her own line, slowly, her mouth very straight, in the same square hand:

Except: if one of us needs him before, she has him, and tells the other the next morning herself. Nobody keeps score.

She looked at it. She did not like it; Imani could see her not liking it in the set of her jaw. She wrote, beneath it, in smaller letters: Under protest. F. And then, after another moment, as if it had cost her something: Agreed. F.

She gave the pen back. Imani signed both lines, I, twice, under Ferra's F's.

"Nobody's first," said Ferra. She closed the book. "That was the term."

"Then we'd better not be shy of each other," said Imani.

Ferra looked at her. And then, after a moment, she reached out across the white cover of the bed and took Imani's hand, the way she would have taken a pen, firmly; and held it; and looked at it, as if surprised to find she had.

"No," said Ferra. "We'd better not."



It was after midnight when Imani got up to go, and Ferra opened the book again at the back.

Past the manifest. Past everything, to the page Imani had seen in the cabin on the first night, with the heading in square capitals: WHAT HE IS PLANNING. It had four lines on it now. Imani leaned over and read them upside down, and Ferra let her.

1. The bath house at Emberfell. Struck out, with a neat ruled line and a note: Rowena's. Public.

2. The steamboat on the Mere, at night, with Fletcher's boy sent ashore. A question mark.

3. The north tower leads. Struck out: Imani's.

4. The inn at Milford, under other names. Struck out: He would be known. And so would we.

"Four," said Ferra. "And I don't like any of them." She looked at the page with real annoyance. "I don't know. I always know. I've known what he was going to do before he did it for seven years; I knew about the press in the standing room before he'd opened his mouth. And I don't know this, and he's laughing at me, I can see him laughing behind his face every time he looks at me."

"The ship," said Imani.

Ferra looked at her.

"It's the thing he loves best that isn't us," said Imani. "Write it down."

"He'd never take the ship out for that." Ferra put the pen on the page and did not write. "It's a working ship. Fletcher would know. Fletcher knows everything that happens in that shed. And there's a crew of twelve and a galley and Ward." She shook her head. "No. He'd not."

"Write it down anyway."

"No," said Ferra, and closed the book. "Four."

Imani went to bed. She lay in the narrow room next door with the shutters open on the warm night and the smell of the mountain coming in, eggs and salt and vines, and the lamplight of the stairs on the ceiling; and thought about the berth, and the book, and Ferra's hand on hers on the white cover, firm, like a pen; and about a man somewhere over a dark sea doing something with his face on that he would tell them when it was done.

She found that she was not frightened, just then. She noted it, the way she noted a fever breaking. And slept.
