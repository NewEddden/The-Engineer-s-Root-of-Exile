Chapter 194 - Two Drops

The prince brought him four sheets, the way he had brought four sheets the spring before last for the thing in the sawmill shed, and put them down on the bench in the pattern store one on top of another, and did not say what they were.

Bram did not ask. He had learned, with the sawmill, that there was a kind of drawing the prince made that he could not explain until it was built, not because he would not but because the words for it were not in the barony yet, and that the fastest way through a drawing like that was to build exactly what was on it, every line, and find out afterward what it had been. He had built the dynamo that way. It had turned on the wheel for a month and done nothing whatever, and then the prince had put the bell's crocks to it for the count of ten, and it had gone, and it had gone ever since. He had not understood it until it went. He was not sure he understood it now. He had built it right.

So he read the four sheets, and built what was on them.

Two weights. Iron, each the size of a small anvil, square in section, cast at the foundry off one pattern and faced on the lathe so that they were the same weight to the ounce on Ferra's big scale; he weighed them himself and made Jorrin take a shaving off the second. Each weight stood in its own sleeve, an iron tube upright on a base plate, with a hand's clearance round it greased with the last of the good grease off the pattern-store shelf, so that it could go up and down in the sleeve and could not go anywhere else.

In the top of each weight, a gravity stone. The drawing had them seated in a cup with a neck left open above them, drawn thin, the way the cells were drawn, so that the stone could drink through the neck from whatever air lay round the weight. The seating was not his work. It was the forge's. A stone set in a cup in iron at an exact depth, with a neck to an exact bore, was the one thing the forge did that his own hands could not, and he gave the two weights to the forge on its plate and watched through the smoked glass while it did it, and took them out in the morning with the stones in them, grey, set, asleep.

The stones themselves came out of the kept portion. Two blanks from the spring raising at Grayrock, which had been weighed on Ilsa's beam in front of Fenner's clerk Lusk and written in Lusk's book as raised and not sold, and carried down the Grayrock line in a locked box in the van with Hobb sitting on it. Lusk had watched them weighed. He had written down the kept weight and asked nothing, as he always asked nothing. The Crown's first purchase ran on stone sold out of the barony. These had not been sold. Ferra had written them into the gravity page under Kept, two, and their weight, and beside the kept column, where it had been since the spring before, the Crown's odd price for the spring's sold stone still sat in her book with a ring round it, open at the top, unanswered.

Under the sleeves, a ring. A cast ring, flat, a little wider than the two sleeves stood apart, lying on a bed of rollers so that it could turn, and in its top face, all the way round, a wave. Not a groove. A wave: the face of the ring rising and falling as it went round, gently, the way the sea rises and falls, high and low and high and low, three swells to the circle. Jorrin cut the pattern and the foundry poured it and it came out wrong the first time, the swells uneven, and Jorrin took it to the big lathe and turned it for four days with a template the prince had drawn full size on the fourth sheet, and at the end of the four days the swells were true, and Jorrin put a straight-edge on them and a lamp behind the straight-edge and there was no light under it anywhere.

At the foot of each weight, a roller, iron, on a pin, that sat on the wave. A weight that went down pressed its roller on the slope of the wave, and a thing pressing on a slope pushes it sideways, and the ring went round. A weight that came up let the wave come up under it. The prince had drawn the two weights across the ring from each other, so that when one was on the top of a swell the other was in the bottom of one. One going down while the other came up. Always.

The ring turned a shaft. One upright shaft, keyed to the ring's middle, standing up through the middle of everything and out through the roof of the box on a packed collar, the way a pump rod comes up out of a well-head. What was outside the box, the shaft turned. What was inside, nobody would see.

Two small levers of brass, one to each weight, on pins at the sleeve's side, that the ring's wave lifted as it went under them. At the top of a swell the wave lifted the lever. At the bottom it let the lever fall.

And round all of it, the box.

Riveted iron, heavy plate, the rail works' plate, in panels, closed on every side and on top and underneath, with the shaft coming out through the roof on its collar and nothing else coming out anywhere. Lined inside with firebrick, because, the drawing said in the margin in the prince's hand, a stone that let go let go as heat. Iron because iron held. Bram had stood in the glassworks shed and counted ten over the mouth of the iron box while the lamp stone came up white in it, and he did not need telling why it was iron.

One panel in the side of the box was different. It was held by rivets of soft iron, not hard, fewer than the others and further apart, and it faced a pit dug in the floor of the shed and filled with dry sand, a yard deep.

Bram looked at that panel on the drawing for a long time before he built it. Then he asked.

"What's this for?"

"For the day it's wrong," said the prince.

Bram looked at him. Then he built it exactly as drawn, and put the sand in the pit himself, and raked it level.

The deciding stone was his.

The prince asked him on the evening the box was closed. He asked it the way he asked most things of Bram now, as a thing he thought Bram might want and would not mind being told no to, sitting on the stool across the bench with the four sheets between them.

"The stone that reads the levers," he said. "I can write it tonight. Or you can. You wrote the sockets for the ship's veins. You've written cells since. You've never written one for a machine of your own, that nobody else had a hand in." He looked at the sheets. "This one's small. It reads two brass levers and tells two stones what to be. It's the simplest stone anybody in Kaldo has written since the pump house. I'd like it to be yours."

Bram did not answer at once. He sat with his hands on the bench and looked at them.

He had asked, once, years ago, to learn this. In the pattern store, with the first cell on the bench and the prince writing its deciding stone in the delve, and Bram standing at his shoulder watching a thing he could not see. He had said then that he would like to know how it was done, and the prince had looked at him with an expression Bram had not been able to read and said yes. He had not known then what he was asking for. He knew now. He had spent a winter building a boiler in his head, stay by stay, rivet by rivet, to get into a room he did not like, and every night for a year it had been easier, and since the summer before last he had not had to build anything at all. He sat down with a stone under his hand and the room was there. Like a door in a house he had lived in all his life.

He did not like it.

"I'll do it," he said.

He did it that night, alone, in the pattern store, with the prince gone up to the keep, because he would not do it with anybody watching. He put the stone under his hand on the bench and the room was there before he had finished putting his hand down, the way it always was now. He did not like that either. He stood in it, in the grey of it, with the stone in front of him the way a thing on a bench is in front of a man, and began.

It was not like building. It was like the rule on the back of the prince's first sheet for the rail works engine, the one Bram had written under in his own hand. It was a rule. That was all a deciding stone was: a rule, written into a stone so that the stone would keep it without anybody standing there. The MK1 harness had had one. The prince had written it for Ward's levers, and Bram had watched him do it: this lever forward, lift; back, let down; both forward, push. This was the same. It was smaller.

The left lever up: the left stone heavy. The left lever down: the left stone light. The right lever the same, for the right stone.

And no lever at all: nothing.

The prince had asked for that particularly. He had said it twice across the bench, which he did not often do. Its default is nothing. If the stone heard no word from a lever, whether because a pin had sheared or a lever had stuck or somebody had put a hand in the box where no hand should be, the stone it governed was to be nothing. Not heavy. Not light. Asleep. A weight with no word from its stone was only iron, and iron in a sleeve did nothing but sit.

Bram wrote it. It took him most of the night, not because it was hard but because he went over every line of it three times, the way he went over a casting with a hammer before he would let it out of the shop, tapping it for the note that meant a flaw. There was no flaw. He came up out of the room with his hand cold on the stone and the lamp burned down to its last inch, and sat there for a while with the stone under his hand, and did not like how easy it had been.

In the morning the prince went into the stone himself, in the delve, and read it, and came out and said nothing was wrong with it. Bram nodded and went to set it in the box.

The flask could not be opened in the shed and carried into the box. That was the first thing the prince had drawn, on the third sheet, before the ring or the weights. A flask opened was a room full of the band for three breaths, and the box was the only room that would hold it.

So there was a lock through the box's wall. An outer door, iron, on the shed side, the size of a bread oven's door. Inside it a little chamber, just big enough for a flask on its side. An inner door, the same, opening into the box. And a bar across both, cranked from outside, so shaped that while the outer door was open the inner could not move, and while the inner was open the outer could not; so that the box was never, for any breath, open to the shed. Bram had built one like it before, a long time ago, for a lime kiln's charging door, and he built this one better.

And through the lock's floor, on a packed collar, a key: an iron rod with a socket on its inner end the shape of a flask's lid, and a handle on its outer end. A man put the flask in the lock with its lid in the socket and shut the outer door and opened the inner and turned the handle, and the lid turned, the way the prince had turned a lid by hand in front of the mages at the handcart the first summer, and the flask gave up what it held into the box and not into the shed.

The flasks were the thing Bram had thought about most. Not the lock. The flasks themselves.

Every flask in Kaldo had been pitched shut since the winter with his own pitch, boiled down from the black tar that dripped out of the gas pipes at the lung, because Rowena's pitch and the Duvrenne black were all gone into the first avenue and the seals before it. His pitch was softer. A flask sealed with it held about a month, and then the seal began to go, and whatever was in the flask began to go with it. So a flask could not be kept. It came down from the band and it was used, and if it was not used inside the month it was let go over the side and filled again. There was no putting a year's flasks in a store against a bad winter. There was only this month's, and next month's, and what the ships brought down on the nights they could fly.

He did not say so to anybody. It was true, and everybody who needed to know it knew it. He wrote it in his own tally book, where he wrote the things he meant to fix: Pitch. A month. Wants better.

They ran it at night, in the shed behind the outside foundry, where the old master had sat at a bench with his hand on the stones for an hour at a time writing cells for hull two in the week after the Hod went into the sea. It was a long shed with a high roof and one door and no windows at the ends, and Ward's men stood at the door, and nobody came past it at night who had not been asked.

There were four of them inside. The prince. Bram. The old master on his stool with his stick across his knees, because he had asked to come, and nobody at the table had ever once refused Armand anything he asked to see. And at the lock, Tobe.

Tobe had been the prince's man in the foundry for as long as there had been a foundry. He had tended the racks on the long nights and knew the stones by the sound of them, and two springs ago he had knelt on the Hod's deck with Cade and held her in the sky with his two hands flat on a rail all the way down two miles to the sea. He had a knight's capacity now, the prince said, though he had never been in the chamber, and he had a knight's marks: thin lines across both palms, pale now, fine as thread, like frost on a window. He did not hide them. He did not show them either. He stood at the lock with his sleeves rolled and a flask in his hand, and looked at Bram.

Bram nodded.

Tobe put the flask in the lock, lid to the socket, and shut the outer door, and cranked the bar, and opened the inner door, and put his hand on the key's handle, and turned it.

Nothing.

Nothing to see. Nothing to hear. The box stood on its base plate in the lamp light, black, riveted, with the shaft coming up out of its roof on its collar, and the shaft did not move. Bram stood with his arms folded and did not breathe. The prince, beside him, had his hand flat on the box's side, and his eyes shut.

Then a knock.

It came from inside the box, low, heavy, like a man knocking once on a thick door with the side of his fist. The shaft on the roof turned a quarter, and stopped.

Another knock. The shaft turned again. Stopped.

Then another, sooner, and the shaft did not stop so long, and another, and another, and the knocks came closer together and settled, the way a man's steps settle when he has found the pace of a road, and the shaft went round. Round, and round, and round. Not fast. The pace of a mill wheel in a slow stream. And under it, in the box, the knock and the knock, one weight going down while the other came up, and the ring going round under them.

It did not hum. Bram had lived with the racks' hum for years, the sound like a finger on the rim of a glass, and the forge's; he had thought everything that ran on stones hummed. This did not. It knocked. Like a mill. Like a forge hammer at a slow heat. Like his own heart, if he listened to it in the dark, which he did not.

Nobody said anything for a while. The shaft went round. The knocking went on. The old master on his stool had his head on one side, listening, the way he listened to a stone.

"It's pressing," Armand said at last. "Both of them. By turns."

"Yes," said the prince.

"They'll spend." Armand did not look away from the box. "A stone that presses spends itself pressing. I wrote that on my own bench with a lead sheet over the pan. It's the oldest thing in the book."

"They're spending," said the prince. "And they're filling back up as fast as they spend, out of what's in the box. Through their necks. The way the ship's cells fill in the band." He took his hand off the iron. "The box is the band. That's all it is. A room full of the band, on the ground, with a door in it that doesn't open."

Armand sat and listened to it knock for a long time. He did not say anything else. But Bram saw his hands on the head of his stick, and they were not still.

It was going faster.

Bram saw it before anybody said so. The knocking had crept closer together, and the shaft had crept round faster, a little and a little, the way a mill wheel goes faster when nothing is on the stones; there was nothing on the shaft, nothing for it to turn, and a thing with nothing to push against goes faster and faster until something breaks. He had seen an engine run away on its stand when a belt came off. He uncrossed his arms.

"Watch the balls," said the prince.

There were two of them. The prince had drawn them on the fourth sheet, on the shaft, outside the box, above the collar: two iron balls the size of a man's fist on two jointed arms, hinged to a sleeve on the shaft, so that they went round with it. Bram had built them. He had not known what they were for. He watched them now.

As the shaft went faster, the balls swung out. They went round, and the faster they went round the further out they flew, the way a stone on a string flies out when a boy whirls it, and as they flew out their arms drew up a sleeve on the shaft, and the sleeve drew up a rod, and the rod went down through the roof of the box on its own small collar; and inside the box, Bram knew, because he had built it, the rod leaned on a third brass lever, beside the two, that the deciding stone also read.

He had written that into the stone himself. He had not known what it was for. The third lever leaned one way: both stones a little lighter. It leaned the other: both a little heavier. He had written it as he had been given it.

The balls flew out, and the rod went up, and the lever leaned, and the knocking eased. Softer. The shaft slowed. The balls came in a little. The rod came down. The knocking firmed. The shaft steadied, and ran on, round and round, at one pace, and held it, and the balls hung out at the same distance from the shaft, going round, level, like a pair of hands held out over a fire.

Bram stood and looked at them for a long time.

He understood it. He understood it before the prince could have explained it, and the prince did not try, and he was grateful for that. It was the rail works rule, the one he had written under the prince's rule on the back of the sheet, in his own hand, the week before they fired the rail works engine. The man who watches is not the man who drives. The watcher stood at the gauge with the right to stop the engine and nobody could countermand him. This was the same thing, turned into iron. The engine was watching itself. The balls were the watcher, and they stood on the shaft where they could see everything the shaft did, and they answered to nothing but how fast it went, and nobody could put a bar through their lever and wedge them down, because there was nobody in the box to do it.

He thought of a man who had put a bar through a valve lever and wedged it down, for one more heat.

"Aye," he said.

That was all.

The first thing it did was make wire.

Bram had set up a second draw bench in the shed before the box was ever closed, on the prince's fourth sheet, against the wall nearest the box: the same bench as the one on the rolls' wheel, a pair of grips and a chain and a die plate with a row of holes in it from thick to thin, the holes the prince had cut in the steel himself two autumns ago. From the shaft, over the box's roof, a pulley. From the pulley, a belt through a slot in the partition to the bench's drum.

The wheel on the rolls' race drew wire at night. It had drawn nine miles of iron wire for the road north the autumn before last and it drew it still, every night, when the rolls stood idle. By day it drew rail, as Ferra had written it should, and the line would not move.

The pair drew by day.

They drew copper. The new red copper, the copper out of the prince's hill two days out, that Hask's heading had found under the old men's rust, cast in rods at the foundry and brought to the shed in bundles. Bram fed the first rod into the first hole himself, and put the belt on, and the pair took the load, and the knocking slowed for a moment as it took it and the balls came in and the lever leaned and the knocking firmed, and the rod came through the die thinner and longer and bright, and went on through the next, and the next, and came off the last hole as wire and went round the reel.

It drew all summer. It drew every day. And Bram, who had spent his whole life making things one at a time and knew every one of them, stood at the reel on the first afternoon and watched the copper come off it in a thin red line, and knew what it was for, because the prince had told him. The house the pair was the small brother of. The big one, with eight weights in a ring instead of two, in a bottle of iron as big as a room, that would turn a dynamo twice the size of the one on the wheel. It would want wire. More copper than Kaldo had ever had. Miles of it, round its shoes and its drum, and more miles on poles from it to every tower in the city.

The small one was drawing the wire the big one would need.

Ned Tallis came up from the lake in the second week with the month's flasks in a sack, as he did every month now, because the pair drank flasks and flasks were what Ned brought.

He stood in the shed door for a long time and watched the shaft go round and listened to the box knock. He did not come in. He looked at the lock, and at Tobe beside it with a flask in his hand, and at the balls going round level over the roof, and at the belt, and the wire coming off the reel.

Ned had no gift. He could not feel a charged stone from a cold one with his hand on it. But he had been on hull two's deck on the first night she flew, at the top of the band with the stars all round them, nine days after the Hod went into the sea, and he had heard the prince say a thing, very low, to nobody, looking east. Bram knew he had heard it, because Ned had told him once, the only time Bram had ever known Ned tell anybody anything about the band. There are other ways to fill a rack. I won't build them.

"Is this one?" Ned said. He said it to the prince, who had come down to see the wire. "Of the other ways."

The prince looked at him for a while. He did not answer quickly, which Bram had learned meant the question had been a good one.

"No," he said. "This doesn't fill anything. It burns what you bring down. You fill the flasks in the band, the way you always have, with the ship. This eats them." He looked at the box. "It's the other end of the same road. Not a different road."

Ned thought about that. He looked at the box, knocking, and at Tobe turning the key in the lock, and at the flask in Tobe's other hand, one of his own, that he had carried down two miles of dark in a sack.

"Like a stove," Ned said.

"Like a stove."

Ned nodded, as if that settled it, and it did. He put the sack down inside the door and went back to the lake.

Ferra came down at the end of the first week with her book and did the count standing at the lock with Tobe, because she did not trust a count she had not stood beside.

One flask ran the pair at the draw bench's load for most of a day and a night. Not all of it. Most of it. Tobe knew when by the knocking, which slowed and went heavy as the box ran thin, and the balls came in, and the lever leaned to heavier, and the knocking slowed again anyway; and then he put the next flask in the lock and turned the key, and the knocking picked up, and the balls went out. Ferra wrote the hours. She wrote them again the next day, and the day after, until the figure did not move.

Then she ruled a new column in the works book, beside the coal. She wrote the heading herself. She did not ask anybody what to call it.

Flasks.

And under it, by the day, the count. One. One. Most of one. One.

Bram went back to the shed at midnight on the last night of the first week, alone, because he could not sleep and did not know why.

The shed was dark but for the lamp by the lock, turned low. Tobe had gone home. The night man, one of Ward's, was at the door outside and nodded to him and let him in. The belt was off; the bench stood idle; there was nothing for the pair to do at night. But the prince had said to let it run, empty, with the balls holding it slow, a night or two, to see what it did when nobody was watching, and so it ran. The shaft went round slowly in the dark. The balls went round over the roof, hardly out at all. And in the box, low and steady, the knocking. One weight down while the other came up. The ring going round under them.

Bram stood by the box for a long time. Then he put his hand flat on the iron, on the side panel, the way Armand put his hand on stones, the way the prince had put his on the first night with his eyes shut.

He felt nothing.

Iron under his palm, cool, a little rough where the scale had not been ground off, and the knock coming through it into his hand, dull, like a heart through a man's chest. That was all. No thickness. No band. Nothing of what the prince felt through iron, or the old master, or whatever it was they felt. He had a room in him that opened when he reached for it and he did not like, and a band on his wrist that went heavy in a dead field, and Armand's readings said he had moved, and was moving, and faster every winter. But through an iron wall with the band inside it, he could feel nothing at all.

He stood with his hand on the iron in the dark, and found that he was glad of it.

He did not ask himself why. He took his hand away, and went out past the night man, and home.
