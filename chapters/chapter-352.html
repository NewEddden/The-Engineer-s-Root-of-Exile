Chapter 352 - Heavy Fish

Zale had gone back for Armand in the night on Saban's word, and set him down on the Cradle's end an hour before the light, and at dawn on the eighth of November Armand came down the ladder like a man going into a bath he had been told was cold, and stopped on the bench end, and said nothing for so long that Imani turned round to see if he was still there.

He was. He stood in his old Tower robe, which he had put on over the cardigan because he had been told it was the islands and hadn't believed it would be warm, with his hands folded on the head of his cane. He didn't need the cane now. He carried it the way Ferra carried her ledger.

"Saban's note said a guest," he said finally.

"She is a guest," said Imani.

"I've had guests. None of them came with gills."

The guest looked back at him with her chin on her knees. Her skin had gone slate at the shoulders when his shadow came down the hatch, and it stayed slate. She was sitting the way she had sat for Saban, back to the wall, neck bent into the water so the slits under her ears were covered, and her eyes went from Armand's face to the cane and back.

"It's a stick," Armand told her, quite seriously. "I don't hit anyone with it. I've been tempted." He looked at Imani. "May I?"

"Don't touch. Just read. Slowly."

"I've read a great many people slowly," said Armand, and shut his eyes.

Imani let her own sense go down beside his. Emeric was on the hatch coaming above with his feet on the top rung, and she felt him come in on her other side, wider than either of them and lighter in the touch than he used to be. The three of them laid their sense along the woman in the water and held it there.

It was still the loudest thing on the island. Imani had begun to think of it as standing too near a bell after it had been struck: you didn't so much hear it as feel it in your teeth. But the bell was quieter than at dusk, and at dusk it had been quieter than at noon, and under it, low on the left, the crack was there. A thin sour place, like a wrong note in a hymn that everybody sings past.

Armand opened his eyes. "That is a great deal of mana."

"Yes."

"More than mine. And I'm told I'm a prodigy." He said it drily, the way he said most things about the stone, as if it had happened to someone he only knew slightly. "And it's going. Out of the left side. Like a cask with a bad stave."

"Ferra wants a number," said Imani.

"I want a number," said Ferra, from the hatch, with the pen already out.

"There's no gauge for a person," said Armand.

"Then you're the gauge. You're the densest man in Kaldo who isn't him, and he doesn't count, because he's never once told me the same number twice. You're one. What is she?"

Armand considered being one. He didn't seem to mind it.

"This morning, one and a half of me. If I'm generous to myself, which I am."

"And on the beach?" said Ferra, to Emeric.

"Two. Near enough. I didn't have him to measure against."

Ferra wrote it. 8 Nov, dawn. 1 1/2 A. Beach (E., guessed): 2 A. Imani thought it looked very small on the page for what it was.

"A stone drained to nothing goes to dust," said Armand. He was looking at the dressing on the woman's back. "I've watched it on a bench a thousand times. It's very undramatic. It goes grey and then it isn't there, and you sweep it up."

"She isn't a stone," said Imani.

"No." He rubbed his thumb along the head of the cane. "I'd prefer not to find out what she is instead."

Imani crouched and lifted the dressing with two fingers. The guest let her. She had let her every time since the beach, and every time she held still in a particular way, her whole long body going quiet, the way a horse goes quiet under a farrier it doesn't trust yet. The shallow hole on the right had opened again. A thread. A wet line the length of a fingernail, weeping thin pink water.

Three times now. On the sand, at dawn yesterday, at dusk, and she'd done it well each time; and each time it had waited for her to go up the ladder and then quietly undone itself, like a seam somebody was unpicking from the inside.

She put the dressing back without closing it.

"Not this time?" said Emeric.

"There's no point shutting a door while somebody's holding it open from the other side." Her knees had gone cold through her scrubs, and she found she was angry, which wasn't useful, and put it away. "It's the other one. Something in the deep one is pulling, and the little one's close enough to feel it. I can't fix the little one till I've fixed the big one, and I can't fix the big one till I can see it. And I'm not putting my sense deep into somebody who hasn't said yes."

"She let you close the skin," said Armand.

"You let a stranger put a bandage on you in the street. You don't let her put her hand inside you." Imani picked up her basin. "She has to say yes to that. Properly. And I don't know how to ask." She started up the ladder. "Meanwhile, she has to eat."



The island's cook did not believe in heavy fish.

She said so on the ninth, and the tenth, and again on the morning of the eleventh, in the door of the works mess with her arms folded and a cloth over one shoulder. She was a short square woman from Milford with forearms like a smith's, who had come out in the first summer to feed the slab crews and stayed because nobody on the mainland could make a proper fish stew. Ferra had put her on Saban's list on the ninth, and she had taken the oath at the mess table with a ladle in her other hand.

"Course I've heard of them. Everybody on the pier has. Way out past the cane islands, they say, where the water goes thick. There's a man on that pier says he had one once and lay on his back till dark. A knight. One of the Kalden, off one of the long runs. Three bites, he says, and down." She flicked the cloth. "And I say a knight on his back on a pier after a long run has had something out of a bottle, and I've said it to his face."

"What did he say?"

"Said he'd bring me one. He didn't."

"Zale's bringing you one," said Imani.

Nobody had asked Zale, exactly. Imani had sat on the Cradle's deck after the dusk reading on the eighth and said that the safest dose of anything was food, because a body stopped eating when it was full; and that if anything in the sea carried more mana than the thin stuff round this island, she wanted it on a plate. Emeric had said the far grounds, then, past the last cane islands, if anywhere. And Zale, at the rail with his back to them, had turned round and said, "How much," and Imani had said, "As much as you can carry cold," and he had nodded once and gone over the side to his ship.

"He'll not make port," Saban had said, watching him go. "He'll fish it himself or buy it off the water, and he'll not say who it's for, because he doesn't know, and he won't ask, because he's Zale." He'd sighed. "I'm going to have to give that man something one day, and I've no idea what he'd want."

The ship came back out of the west at four on the eleventh. Imani felt the box before Fletcher's men had it off the deck. She stopped on the slab with a jug of sea in her hands and turned round, and on the Cradle's edge Armand had turned round too, and Emeric, at the hatch, had lifted his head. Next to the woman in the cell it was a candle beside a forge. But it was a box of dead fish three days out of the water, and it hummed.

The cook opened it on the mess table and stopped talking for the first time in three days.

Six of them, packed in seaweed and broken ice. Long dark fish, as long as her arm, deep in the body, blue-black on the back and pewter underneath, with a look about the eye that Imani didn't like. When the cook got her hands under one and lifted it onto the board her arms dipped, and she said something about her mother that Imani hadn't heard from a woman since the garrison.

"That's not a fish. That's a fish's idea of a brick." She pushed her thumb into the flank and the flesh pushed back. "Hell's teeth. It's like pressing a ham. You're not putting a single bite of this in my mouth."

"I wasn't going to."

"Nor anybody's!" This was to the mess door, where two of the berth crew had appeared, drawn by nothing anybody could name. "You hear? I'll know!"

The berth crew went away.

"Plain," said Imani. "Steamed. No butter, no wine. I want to know what the fish does, not what your sauce does."

The cook gave her a look of deep personal injury, and steamed it.



They fed her at dusk on the twelfth, after the reading. 12 Nov, dusk. 1 1/4 A. A quarter of an Armand gone in four days. Armand had given the number and then sat on the bench end with his hands on the cane and said nothing else.

Imani took it down herself. Not a plate: a board, because a board could sit on the bench end in reach without looking like anything that might be a weapon. Half of one of the heavy fish, steamed, pale and flaking, on a bed of its own seaweed. The smell filled the cell, clean and cold and faintly sweet, and it hummed under her sense, so that she had the odd feeling of carrying a lamp down a dark stair.

The guest had turned her head before Imani's foot touched the second rung.

She watched the board set down, and Imani draw back and sit on her heels in the water a yard off.

"Bite," said Imani. "One. Then we see."

The guest looked at her. Then at the board. Then, as she always did when she wasn't sure, past Imani and up the ladder, where Emeric sat on the coaming with Ferra kneeling beside him, and Armand behind them, and behind Armand the cook, who had refused to stay in the mess.

Emeric sat with his hands loose on his knees where she could see them. Imani watched the guest look at him, and wondered, again, what she saw.

Then the guest took a piece of the fish in her fingers and put it in her mouth.

Imani had her sense down already, thin as she could make it, sitting on the edge of the woman's note the way you sit on the edge of a sickbed. She felt the bite go down. She felt it arrive. And she felt, so faintly she'd have doubted it from anyone else, the note lift. A hair. The way a fire lifts when you push a stick into it before the stick has caught.

The guest opened her eyes and took another piece. And another. She ate the way people eat who have been hungry a long time and stopped thinking about it: steadily, with both hands, taking the flesh off the bones and setting the bones in a tidy pile on the board's corner.

"That's three," said the cook, in a different voice.

The guest ate the fourth.

"That's four. Healer. That's four."

"I can count," said Imani, without looking round.

She was counting something else. Breath, steady through the mouth and slow and wide through the gills. Colour, which went as the eating went on from the guest's resting grey-white to something warmer; nothing on her was ever pink, but there was a pearl in it now, like the sheen on a shell held under water. Hands steady. The belly, which Imani watched for the cramp she had seen fold the forcing boys double, and which didn't come.

The board was bare in a quarter of an hour. The guest licked her fingers one at a time, and picked up the board in both hands, and held it out across the water toward Imani, and made the low sound with the catch in it.

"She wants more," said Ferra.

"Yes," said Imani. "I'd got there."

"Well, I'll be damned," said the cook.

"Before more," Imani told the guest, and touched her own lip and pulled it down to show her gums. She did it slowly, twice, and held out her open hand.

The guest's skin flickered dark at the throat and came back. Then, with the air of somebody humouring a foreigner's odd custom, she set the board on her knees and pulled down her own lower lip with one finger.

Pale gums. Clean. Not a speck of blood. More teeth than a woman's, Imani thought, smaller, sharper toward the back.

"Thank you," said Imani, and went on with the rest. The guest let her press two fingers into her belly under the dark garment's edge, and nothing hurt that the face showed. She let Imani turn her forearms over in the lamplight and look along the insides, and at the throat, and the backs of the hands, for the faint red threading that came before tracery, the mark that had been the last thing on the forcing boys before the end. There was none. The skin was clean and pearl and a little warmer than it had been at dawn.

"Armand," said Imani.

"Yes. I felt it." His voice came down the ladder thin and odd. "It went up. It's staying up. I'd like to put my hand on my heart and say a twentieth. I won't. Write up."

"I'm writing up," said Ferra.

The guest held out the board again.

"The other half," said Imani, up the ladder. "Same way."

The cook looked down at the woman with the empty board held out and the tidy pile of bones, and her mouth worked.

"Three bites," she said. "He said three bites and down. And I said he was drunk."

"He might have been both," said Armand.

The cook went, fast, and they heard her shouting at somebody to stoke the steamer before she was off the Cradle.

The guest ate the other half slower, the way anybody eats a second helping, and left a little on the board, and pushed it away an inch: full. Imani wrote it on her slate herself. One fish. Stopped on her own. Then she sat in the cold water a while longer and listened to the note sit there, higher than at dusk by a hair, and not going down.

It would go down. By morning the crack on the left would have taken it back and more. But in six days nothing had gone up, and this had.

When she came up at last her scrubs were wet to the waist and her hands smelled of fish, and Emeric gave her a hand over the coaming and didn't let go of it.

"You smell of dinner," he said.

"I smell of somebody else's dinner." She leaned on him without deciding to. "She liked it. Did you see? She ate it like the first decent meal she'd had since she left home."

"It probably was."

"Then I want more of it. How many did he bring?"

"Six," said Ferra. "Five now. A fish a day is five more days, and then Zale goes again." She looked up. "He'll go again?"

They all looked at the rail, where Zale stood by his ship's rope looking down into the hatch, at the woman in the water, who had turned her head and was looking up at him. He nodded once, as he'd have nodded to a pilot on another deck, and the guest's skin went pale and still, and she looked away first.

"He'll go again," said Ferra, and wrote it.



A gull came down on the hatch coaming at first light on the thirteenth and stood there on one leg, and Emeric, sitting the dawn watch against the rope coil with Imani asleep on his shoulder, watched the guest notice it.

She was lying on her side in the water, the way she'd taken to lying since the fish, head on her arm, gills under. The gull's shadow fell on the water by her face. She looked up at it, and made a sound he hadn't heard from her before: higher than the one with the catch in it, a soft rising call, like a question asked through the nose. It was aimed. He could feel a little thread of her mana going out along it toward the gull, the way you throw a line.

The gull turned its head about a quarter of the way round and looked at her with one eye. Then it looked away at the bay, as if somebody behind it had said something boring, and went back to standing on one leg.

The guest let her head go down on her arm.

Emeric thought of September. Himself on the castle hill on a spring morning, holding out a crust to a squirrel with all the mana he could put into it, and the squirrel looking at him a long time with a blossom in its hands and turning away like a woman at a dance. It had been the best he could do then. It had been about that.

He'd been a land man calling a squirrel in thin air, and he'd known no better. She was calling a gull in thin water, and from the way her shoulders went down he thought she knew a great deal better, and that it had once been easy for her.

"Is it morning?" said Imani, into his shoulder.

"Nearly. She tried to talk to a gull."

Imani opened her eyes. The gull looked back at her with the same contempt. "Did it answer?"

"No."

"Rude," said Imani, and shut her eyes, and opened them again, because she'd heard the rest of it in his voice. "She can, then. Where she's from."

"I think so."

"Hm." He felt her decide not to think about it before breakfast. "You didn't sleep."

"I slept."

"You didn't. Ferra put her foot on you at two and you said ow."

"Her feet are cold."

"Her feet have always been cold. You married them." Imani sat up and pushed her hair back with both hands, and looked at the hatch, and he watched her face change the way it had on the beach, the teasing going out of it and the ward coming in. "Today. I'm going to ask her today."

"How?"

"I don't know. I'll know when I'm down there. Get Ferra up. And don't let Armand bring the stick."



She went down at seven with nothing in her hands. Every time she'd come down that ladder she had brought something to do to the woman, and this once she wanted to come down with nothing.

She had told the other three to keep their sense out of the cell, and they had. It felt like a room with the door shut.

She sat in the water a yard from the guest and tried. She touched her own back, low on the left. She touched her own eyes, then laid her hand flat on her belly and shut her eyes, to show looking inward; she felt foolish, and kept doing it, because a foolish healer was better than a polite one. Then she held her open hand out toward the guest's back, and stopped a foot short, and left it in the air.

The guest watched all of it. Her skin had gone dark at the throat and stayed. Then she looked up the ladder.

Imani didn't turn round. She knew who was up there. She'd mostly stopped minding. She waited, with her arm getting tired and the water at her waist.

The guest took her wrist.

Her hand was cold and much stronger than it looked; Imani felt the bones of her own wrist shift in it. She drew the hand in, not fast, the last foot, and laid it flat on her own back over the dressing on the left. Pressed it there once with her palm on the back of Imani's hand. Let go. Then she put her face down on her arm in the water and lay still.

Imani's heart was going hard, which surprised her. "Thank you," she said to the back of the woman's head. Then, without looking round: "Nobody come down."

"Nobody's coming down," said Ferra.

She went in the way Emeric had taught her, the hollow view: her mana let out thinner than breath and pitched as near as she could get it to the guest's own note, so the body would take it for its own. With a man it was like pouring water into sand. With this one it was like pouring water into the sea. Her thread vanished in it, and she had to feel her way, the way you feel for the bottom of a pond with your feet, and the body pushed back the whole time, steadily, the way a current pushes on a wader's knees.

Shapes came. The great vessels down the back of her belly, bright. A heart, slower and bigger than a woman's and set further back. Lungs, dimmer than they should have been, and running from them up toward the throat a second set of passages, branching and fine, lit like the veins in a leaf held to a window, going to the gills. Imani looked at that longer than she needed to. Then she made herself go down and left.

She had expected dark. A dead thing reads dark, a splinter or a stone, a little shadow where the mana doesn't go. This wasn't a shadow. It was a hole. Two inches under the skin of the left wound, lodged against something firm she took for the back of the kidney or its cousin, was a place no bigger than the last joint of her little finger where the light went in and didn't come out. Her own thread bent toward it as she came near. She felt the pull, small and steady and hungry, like a flue drawing on a candle, and the guest's mana all round it running in like water down a drain.

She pulled her thread back fast, before it took any more.

"Imani," said Emeric, from the hatch.

"I'm all right." She was cold through, suddenly, the way you're cold after giving blood, and her fingertips on the guest's back had gone white. "It's a stone. A broken bit of one, against something in her back about two inches in, and it's drinking. Like the cuffs Ward puts on a mage. It drank off me just now when I went close. That's the crack. That's where it's all going. That's why the little one won't close."

"Can you see where?"

"Exactly where." That was the thing she'd turn over later. The hole was so plain in the light of the woman that she could have drawn the line on the skin in charcoal: in through the left wound, a finger's width below the old track, angled up, past one vessel she'd have to hold aside and short of a second she wouldn't have to touch. She saw where to cut before she had a knife in her hand. She had never seen a line so plainly before she made it.

She got up out of the water. "Today."



Emeric said tomorrow. She had known he would.

"She's eaten," he said. They were on the deck by the hatch in the wind, with the cook's steamer smoking along the shore. "She's up a twentieth. Give her three more days of fish. Let her come up. Let you rest. You've just had something drink off you..."

"She came up a twentieth and lost a quarter in four days. Fish won't outrun that." Imani had a towel round her shoulders and both hands round a mug of the yard's coffee to get the white out of her fingers. "Every day it's in her it drinks, and every day it drinks she's weaker, and every day she's weaker she's worse to cut."

"Can you not coax it out?" said Armand. "Without the knife?"

"You can't coax a stone. And anything I push at it with mana, it eats. It comes out by hand. Knife and forceps. Steel." She looked at Emeric. "It won't drink through iron, will it."

"No. Iron blinds it." He said it slowly, and she watched him get there and get past arguing. "You want her out of the water."

"Her back out, her neck in. On the sling, on her right side. Fletcher drops the water to six inches. Lamp low and close on the left. Basin boiled, needles boiled, silk boiled. And somebody to hold the lamp still." She looked at him. "You."

"I can do more than hold a lamp."

"I know you can. That's why I want you holding it. If I need you, I'll say. If I don't say, you hold the lamp."

"What about pain?" said Ferra.

Imani didn't like any of the answers. "I've got poppy. I don't know what poppy does to her. It might do nothing, it might stop her heart. I'm not finding out today. I can numb the skin with the sight. I can't numb deep, not with that thing in there eating what I put in. It'll hurt." She was quiet. "I'll ask again first, with the knife where she can see it. If she says no, it's tomorrow, and the day after, until she says yes or until it doesn't matter."

Ferra wrote that, and shut the book, and got up. "I'll boil the silk myself. Nobody in the yard knows what boiled means." She laid her hand on the back of Imani's neck under her hair, briefly, the way you'd check a child for fever. "Eat something first."

"I'm not hungry."

"Then eat something you're not hungry for," said Ferra, and went.



Imani showed her the knife in the lamplight, flat on her open palm. Then the forceps. Then she laid them on the boiled cloth on the bench and held her empty hand out toward the left wound, and stopped, as she had that morning. The guest looked at the knife a long time. Her skin went dark from throat to hip. Then she reached back with her own hand and laid it flat on the wound, and took it away, and turned her face into the water.

"All right," said Imani. "All right."

Emeric held the lamp. He knelt on the bench end with the yard's brightest lamp in both hands, the glass hooded so the light went down in a hard white circle on the small of her back, and didn't move it, and Imani was grateful for every minute of that and didn't say so. Ferra sat at the guest's head in the shallow water with her sleeves rolled, and the guest's hand had found hers the way it had found Emeric's jacket on the beach. Armand sat on the third rung with his sense down at the very edge of the cell, thin as a hair. "Tell me if her note drops," Imani had said. "Fast. Nothing else."

She numbed the skin. That much went in; the skin was only skin, and quiet, and the sight laid a palm-wide oval of it to sleep. She felt the guest feel it go, and tense, and ease a little when nothing hurt.

Then she cut.

The knife went in clean along the line. What welled up wasn't blood as she knew it, or not only; it was darker and thinner and came slowly. She swabbed it and went through the next layer, the hard sheet over the muscle, and that was where it began.

The cut closed behind the knife.

Not the skin, which she'd numbed. The muscle under it, as soon as the blade was through, drew itself together again, slow and strong, like a fist closing on a finger. Imani felt the guest's mana come up round the wound in a wave, all that weight of it turning toward the place she'd cut and pressing in to mend it. The body was doing what a body does, with three times the force of anybody she'd ever had on a table, and it was doing it against her.

She put the first spreader in and opened the cut by hand. The muscle pushed on its blades. She could feel it through the steel.

"It's fighting you," said Emeric.

"Yes."

"Go in on her note. Pitch it lower, under hers, so she takes it for..."

"I know how to pitch," said Imani. "Hold the lamp."

He held the lamp.

She tried it anyway, because he was right, and it was what she'd have done. She pitched her thread under the guest's note and sent it ahead of the knife, and the guest's mana took it the way the sea takes a cup of water poured off a jetty. Gone. She was standing in a strong current with her eyes shut, and she was cutting blind.

She stopped cutting.

She knelt in six inches of cold sea with a spreader in the wound and the muscle pushing on it and the guest's breath going fast and ragged through her mouth, and Ferra saying something low at the head end, not words, and the white light not moving at all. The guest's skin had gone near black down the whole left side.

Don't pull against the push.

It was a midwife's rule. Mother Sabel, who'd taught Imani the birthing ward, had said it every night for a year, to every girl who'd ever braced her feet and tried to haul a child out against the mother: the body's going one way, so go with it, and use where it takes you. Imani had thought at the time it was the sort of thing you said when you'd run out of better things to say.

The shard was pulling. That was the one thing in this woman's body she could be sure of. Everything round it ran toward it.

So she stopped trying to hide her thread. She let it out thin and slack and didn't steer it at all, and let it go where the guest's own mana was going, down and in and toward the hole. It slid along the current like a leaf down a gutter, and she went with it, and the picture came back all at once, not from her own looking but from where the drain took her: the hole, lit round its edge by everything pouring into it, two fingers deeper than her blade and a little to the left.

It drank her thread as she reached it. A small cold mouthful of her, gone.

"Her note's dropped," said Armand, very fast. "Not much. Dropped."

"It's me. It's taking me." She opened her eyes. "Fine. It can have me for two minutes."

She went in with the forceps along the line the drain had shown her, past the vessel, which she held aside with the back of the blade. The muscle closed on the steel. She opened it again. The guest made a long low sound into the water, and her knuckles in Ferra's hand went white, and Ferra didn't let go.

"Nearly," said Imani, to the guest, to nobody. "You're very good. Nearly."

The forceps touched something hard. She felt it through the steel the way you feel a pebble through the sole of a shoe, and she felt it with her sight, the drain, right there; and as the jaws closed on it the pull went thin, the iron cupping it like a hand round a candle.

The guest's whole body went slack.

Imani drew it out. It came slowly, because the muscle held it, and then it didn't, and as it came up out of the cut the pull went out of the wound behind it as if somebody had shut a door. She had it in the lamp's white circle in the forceps' jaws: a piece of stone the length of her last finger joint, narrow, broken off square at one end and tapered to a blunted point at the other. Dark, nearly black, but there was a green in it, deep down, like water under a jetty, and a grain through it in fine curved lines, the way the grain runs in a shell.

She dropped it in the basin. It made a small hard sound on the tin.

Then the wound began to close under her hands all by itself, muscle and sheet and fat pulling together in a long slow wave. The guest's mana had stopped going anywhere. All of it was coming back to the place that hurt.

"No you don't," said Imani sharply, and put her fingers in and held it open. "Not till I've cleaned it. You'll close dirt in." She worked the track clean with boiled silk and boiled water, fast, the muscle pushing on her fingers like something alive and impatient, and then let it go, and it closed, and she laid her sight over it, thin, not fighting now, only guiding: muscle to muscle, sheet to sheet. She sewed the skin with five stitches, because she didn't trust anything on this woman to hold by sight alone yet, and dressed it. The shallow one on the right she closed last, with two fingers, in a breath.

She sat back in the water. "Armand."

He took his time. "It's not going," he said. "Her note. It's not going anywhere. It's just sitting." He laughed once, short and surprised. "Like a full stone."

Emeric set the lamp down on the bench end, very carefully, as if it might still be needed, and looked at her.

Ferra told her later what she'd looked like. Grey to the lips, the hands going, and a bead of blood at one nostril that she didn't know was there until Ferra put her thumb on it.

"You held the lamp," Imani said to him.

"I held the lamp."

"You did it very well." She tried to get up and didn't, and Emeric came off the bench end into the water in his clothes and got his arms round her before she sat down in it. "I'm all right. It took about a cupful. I'll want it back. I want eggs. I want..." She stopped, because the guest had turned her head on her arm and opened her eyes and was looking at her.

Not past her, up the ladder. At her.

The guest put out her free hand and laid two fingers on the inside of Imani's wrist. Lightly. Exactly where Imani had laid hers on the beach. Then she shut her eyes again.

"Well," said Ferra, after a while, and her voice wasn't quite right. "That's a pulse taken."



The stone went into Ferra's cash box.

That was Ferra's idea, while Imani was still sitting on the bench end in a towel waiting for her knees to come back. The stores' cash box was iron, square and heavy, with a hasp and a lock. Ferra brought it to the hatch and counted out what was in it onto a cloth first, aloud, every coin, and wrote the total, because nobody was going to say afterward that the island's float had gone into the sea with a bit of fish's back. Then she lined it with clean linen and held it down through the hatch, and Emeric tipped the basin, and the shard dropped into the iron with a small dull click.

Imani had felt it the whole time it sat in the basin, pulling faintly at the edge of her sense like a draught under a door. When the lid came down it stopped.

"What is it?" said Ferra.

"I don't know," said Emeric. "I've never felt anything like it. It drinks the way a cuff stone drinks. Harder. I couldn't feel the bottom of it."

Armand had come up the ladder behind them, holding the rail. He looked at the shut box as if it were a snake asleep in a basket.

"Not in the Tower's cases. Not in any book I've read, and I've read the ones they don't let the students see. Seventy years of stones, and I've never seen one that colour, or a grain like that in anything that wasn't a shell." He was quiet. "Somebody made a spear's point out of that. On purpose. And put it in her as she ran."

Ferra locked the box and hung the key on the cord round her neck beside the yard key, and wrote under the guest's line: Shard, from left wound, 13 Nov. Iron box (stores). For Bram. Then, after a second: And Tess.

The wound held that night. Imani slept fourteen hours in the watch room. Ferra checked the dressing at nine and at midnight and at four, and wrote each one, and each one said dry.

Bram and Tess came out on the first of December, on a note from Saban that said only that there was a stone Bram would want to look at, in a box he'd want to bring his own lock for. Saban walked them along the shore from the works pier and told them the whole of it in a mile and a half, and Imani heard afterward that Bram had got down off the works cart twice to look at the sand and Tess not at all.

"They keep worse than this," Saban said, when Ferra asked why these two could go home again on his word and the eleven couldn't. "They've kept it for years. Tess has never told anybody anything Bram told her not to, and Bram's never told anybody anything except about steel, and you can't stop him doing that. And somebody has to look at that box who isn't a healer or a king."

Bram opened it on the Dust House bench with Tess at his elbow and Imani across from them, because she'd taken the thing out and wanted to see it looked at properly. He didn't touch it. He looked at it for a long while, and then at Tess.

"Well?"

"It's not ours," said Tess, frowning at it the way she frowned at a bad weld. "Look at the face where it broke. That's not a fracture, it's a cleave. It went along the grain. Somebody knew where the grain was and shaped it to it." She looked up. "Can I put a probe on it?"

"Not today. Not this year, maybe." Bram shut the lid himself and fitted his own lock on the hasp beside Ferra's, so that it took both keys. "This goes in the strongroom under the mill, behind two iron doors, and nobody touches it before Emeric's had his sense in it." He looked at Emeric. "When you've got a week with nothing in it."

"I haven't had one of those in fourteen years."

"Then you'll have to make one," said Bram, and carried the box down himself.



The cabin was Fletcher's. The dose was Imani's. The numbers were Ferra's.

Fletcher built it on the Cradle's deck in two days and a night, over the cell's hatch, so the guest could come up the ladder into it and go down again when she liked: planks with sheet iron behind them and the seams soldered, like the inner skin of the Hod, and a long trough of sea down one wall that his men filled from the deck pump twice a day. Wren came down from the mill in her rest hours and held the solder pot and didn't once ask what it was for. In the corner stood a head tank from the yard's stores, iron-jacketed with a sight slit, and a brass valve on it with a dial, and on the dial a row of notches Imani had scratched with a nail.

"A notch a day," she said. She'd been on her feet since the fifteenth, a day earlier than Ferra had written she would be. "Not two. Not if she asks. Not if she looks better. One."

"And if it goes wrong?" said Fletcher, who had got used to asking her things.

"Then I'll see it before she does." She tapped each place on herself. "Blood at the gums. Pain in the belly. Any red in the skin like threads. Any one of them and the valve goes back two, and nobody argues with me."

"Nobody argues with you anyway."

"Then nobody will have to start," said Imani.

"And my lads?" said Fletcher. "They're in and out of here all day."

"In and out. A quarter of an hour at a time past the third notch, nobody sleeps in it, and anybody who isn't changed and feels it in his teeth comes out and tells me." She chalked it on the inside of the door, and it stayed there.

Ferra ruled the dose register the night before the first notch, sitting up on the watch room cot with her knees up and her hair in the yard's marking sticks again, while Imani lay face down beside her eating an egg out of the shell and Emeric sat on the floor against the cot because there wasn't room on it.

Date. Notch. Reading (A). Fish (lb). Gums. Belly. Skin.

"You've left out sleep," said Imani, with her mouth full.

"She doesn't sleep. Not that I can tell."

"She sleeps. She just doesn't close her eyes to do it. Put sleep."

Ferra ruled an eighth column, very straight, and wrote Sleep at the top. "And you," she said, without looking down at Emeric, "are not to put your sense in that cabin after dusk. I've got a column for you too. It's in the back. It says hours."

"I'm not working."

"You're sitting on the floor of a honeymoon with your eyes shut," said Ferra. "I know what that is."

Imani laughed into her egg.

The first notch went on at dawn on the sixteenth. Imani turned the valve herself and stood by the dial with her sense on the guest, who sat in the trough with her back to the iron and her eyes on the valve, as if she knew what it was for. Armand stood in the door. He'd stayed. Saban had asked him if he'd like to go home, and he had said he was nearly ninety, whatever his face said, and had never seen anything as interesting as this and wasn't going home to look at a fire.

The note didn't move at one notch. Imani hadn't expected it to. At five it did. At nine, on the twenty-fourth, Armand put his hand on the door frame as he read her and said, "One and a half," and then, carefully, "of me, and I'm not being generous," and Ferra underlined it, which she never did.

Every morning the guest pulled her lip down for Imani with one finger, unasked, with an expression of great tolerance, and every morning the gums were pale and whole. The belly stayed soft. The skin stayed pearl, clean of any thread. The dressing came off on the twentieth, and under it were five neat stitch marks and a pale line, and it held, and the shallow one on the right didn't show at all.

Zale went for fish three times more. He brought each box over the rail himself and took his look down the hatch, and each time she was a little further up the ladder. The third time, in the second week of December, she was in the cabin, sitting on the trough's edge with her feet in the water, and as he set the box down she looked at him across the iron room and made a short sound, not the question, something lower, and he stopped with his hand on the lid.

"Aye," said Zale to her, after a while, as if she'd said something about the weather. "It's cold out there."

Then he went back to his ship.



The box sat on the cabin floor for perhaps a minute after Zale had gone, which was a minute longer than it ever stayed in the mess.

Emeric was on the bench with the dose register open on his knee, because Ferra had gone up to the watch room for more ink and left him holding it like a baby, when the guest leaned out of the trough on one arm, put her hand into the seaweed, and came out with a fish by the gill.

She didn't look round to see if anyone minded. She turned it on her knee, found the soft place behind the head with her thumb, and opened the back down the length of the spine as neatly as the cook did it with a knife. Then she lifted a strip of the dark raw flesh with two fingers and ate it, and took another, and her skin, which had been the pearl it always was after feeding, went up a shade brighter at the shoulders, as if somebody had turned a lamp up behind her.

"Hell's teeth," said the cook, from the door. She'd come for the box. "She's eating it cold."

"She's eating it raw," said Emeric.

"That's what I said."

Imani came in at the noise, saw what was happening, and went straight to the slate. "Don't take it off her."

"I wasn't going to take it off her," said the cook. "I'm not putting my hand near that mouth."

"The register." Imani looked at Emeric. "Is it the same? Cooked or not?"

"Same fish. Same weight. Steaming doesn't take the mana out, you've had weeks of her eating it steamed." He watched the guest lift the third strip. She ate it slower than the first two, the way a person eats a thing they have been missing. "Weigh what's left on the bones after and take it off the fish's weight. You'll be within an ounce."

Imani wrote that. Then she looked at the guest, and at the tidy way she was laying the bones out along the trough's edge, the same way she'd laid them on the board every day since November, and said quietly, "She's been eating it steamed to be polite."

"Or because it was what she was given."

"That's the same thing," said Imani.

Emeric didn't answer. He was thinking about a counter, eleven seats long, pale wood worn soft at the edge where people rested their wrists, and an old man behind it who never said more than four words to anyone and could cut a fish so thin you could read through it. Marcus had gone on Fridays. He'd gone so often the old man had started setting the first piece down before he'd finished sitting.

"Give me tomorrow," he said, "and the sack of rice you've got in the back."

The cook looked at him with real suspicion. "That's pudding rice. Zale brought me that off a cane-island boat in the spring and nobody'll eat it but me."

"Then you'll get some of it back," said Emeric.



He did it in the works mess the next afternoon, on the cook's own board, with the cook standing at his elbow like a magistrate.

The rice first. He washed it in the big tin basin and the water came off white as milk, and he poured it off and washed it again, and again, until it ran nearly clear, and the cook said that was a great deal of good water to throw away on a grain and he said that the white was what made it paste. He set it to soak. He had sent a note out on the morning supply run, and the boat had come back at noon with a stoppered crock of Maren's soy sauce wrapped in a dishcloth, and a line on the label in her square hand that said Bring the crock back, underlined twice.

The vinegar was the still house's. He warmed it in a small pan with Milford sugar and a pinch of salt until the sugar went, and no further.

"You're making a pickle," said the cook.

"Nearly."

When the rice was cooked he turned it out into the widest wooden tub the mess had, the one she proved bread dough in, and poured the vinegar over it and began to cut through it with a flat paddle, sideways, folding and turning, never stirring. Steam came off it in a sour sweet cloud. Ferra, who had come in behind Imani to see what the noise was about, was handed a ledger that wasn't hers, a spare from the stores, and told to fan.

"Fan what?"

"The rice. It wants to cool fast. If it sits warm it goes to glue."

Ferra fanned. She did it with great dignity for about a minute and then with great energy, because Imani had started laughing, and the steam bent away from the tub, and the grains went from wet to glossy under the paddle, each one separate and shining.

"Where did you learn this?" said Armand. He had come in last and found the only chair. "Not in the Tower. We ate mutton in the Tower, and complained about it."

"Foreign lands," said Emeric.

Ferra's fan didn't stop, but she glanced across the tub at Imani, and Imani took a sudden interest in the rice.

"Which foreign lands?" said Armand. "I've read every map the Tower owns. Some of them twice."

"Far off. Over the sea." He took the first of the heavy fish off the ice and laid it on the board, and the cook took half a step back from it out of habit. "They're not on the Tower's maps."

"There's nothing over the sea."

"Then it's a long way past the sea." He opened the fish down the spine and took the side off it whole, and turned it skin down. "Do you want to hear where it comes from, or do you want to argue about the maps?"

Armand thought about it, which was fair. "Where it comes from," he said. "I'll argue about the maps after."

Emeric cut while he talked. He kept the knife flat and drew it once through the flesh toward himself, one long pull from the heel to the point and never a saw, and every slice came off the same thickness as the last.

"It started with rice farmers," he said. "A long way south of where it ended up. They grew rice in fields they flooded off the rivers, and when the rivers came up every year, the fish came up with them into the fields, more than anybody could eat in a season. So they gutted them and salted them and packed them down in cooked rice in jars, and weighted the lids, and left them."

"For how long?" said the cook.

"Months. A year, some of them. The rice goes sour round the fish and the sour keeps it from rotting. You'd open the jar in the dry season when there was nothing else and the fish was still good."

"And the rice?"

"Threw it away."

The cook made a small pained noise, as if he'd said they'd thrown away a child.

"So did everybody, for a few hundred years," said Emeric. "It went north, and across to the islands where I learned it, and they made it the same way. Lake fish in sour rice. They paid their taxes in it."

"Taxes," said Ferra, fanning, with the look of a woman filing something away.

"Then people got impatient. They started opening the jars early, half done, while the rice was still something you could stand to eat, and they ate the rice with it. And then somebody worked out that if you put vinegar on the rice, it tasted sour straight away, and you didn't have to wait for the jar at all." He tipped his head at the tub. "That."

"So it's a lazy pickle," said the cook.

"It's a fast one. And then there was a city." He put the knife down and wet his hands in a bowl of water with a splash of the vinegar in it. "A big one, on a bay, full of people who worked long hours and had nowhere to cook, and the bay was full of fish. And a man there started selling it from a stall in the street. He didn't bother with a jar or a box. He pressed a little rice in his hand, like this," and he did it, a mouthful of rice cupped and squeezed in his palm until it held, "and laid a slice of fish from that morning's catch over the top, and handed it across. You ate it standing up, with your fingers, on your way somewhere. Two bites. Then you went back to work."

He set the piece down on the board. The fish lay over the rice like a coat over a chair, dark and glossy at the edge, and the rice under it held its shape.

"That's what it is," he said. "Street food. Workmen's food. It caught on in that city because it was quick and cheap and the fish was right there, and then every city wanted it, and now it's on every table in the country, from a dock stall to a lord's wedding. They call it sushi. That one's nigiri, the pressed kind."

"Sushi," said Ferra, testing it.

"Street food," said the cook darkly, looking at the raw fish. "I'll tell you what you get from raw fish off a street stall. Worms."

"Yes," said Emeric.

That stopped her.

"You do," he said. "Some fish carry them, in the flesh. Cooking kills them. So does cold, if it's cold enough and long enough." He nodded at the far end of the board, where two ordinary grey sea fish lay on a cloth, frosted white and stiff as planks, out of the island's cold store. "Those two have been in your cold room since October, frozen through. Nothing in them's alive. Those are for us." He touched the heavy fish. "This one's for her. She's eaten them raw her whole life, and Imani's watching her gut every morning anyway."

"I am," said Imani.

"And none of us is eating a mouthful of that," said the cook, pointing at the heavy fish with her whole arm.

"None of us is eating a mouthful of that," Emeric agreed.

He made eight for the guest, from the heavy fish, and stopped at eight because Imani had weighed the side and told him to. Then he cut the frozen fish, which had softened on the board enough to slice, and made a plate for the rest of them, and poured Maren's soy sauce into a saucer.

He thought, briefly, of the green paste the old man used to put under the fish with one fingertip, and of the pink pickled ginger, and of everything else that was a world away and not coming. Then he put it down and picked up the board.



The guest was in the trough when they came in, lying on her side with her head on her arm. She sat up when she saw the board. She knew a board by now.

Imani set it on the trough's edge, with the saucer beside it, and drew back.

The guest looked at the eight small shapes for some time. Then she picked one up between finger and thumb, as carefully as if it might go off, and lifted the fish away from the rice, and ate the fish. She looked at the rice in her other hand. She smelled it. Then she ate that too, by itself, and chewed it for a long time with an expression Emeric had last seen on Armand's face in front of one of Tess's diagrams.

"She doesn't like it," said the cook, and sounded pleased.

"Give her a minute," said Imani.

The guest picked up the second one and, this time, ate it whole. Her eyes went to the saucer. Emeric leaned in slowly, so she could watch his hand the whole way, took one from the other plate, turned it over, touched the fish side to the soy sauce, and ate it.

She did the same. Exactly the same, down to turning it fish side down. Then she sat very still with her cheek full.

Her skin went pearl from the throat to the shoulders, bright, in a long slow wash, like light going up a wet shell.

"Oh," said Imani softly.

The guest made a sound none of them had heard before, short and rising and a little surprised, and reached for the saucer and pulled it closer to her side of the board.

"That's mine," said Emeric. "That's Maren's, actually. She wants the crock back."

The guest moved the saucer another inch.

Armand laughed, which he didn't do often, a dry short bark that he seemed to find as surprising as anyone. He took a piece from the plate, dipped it the way he had watched it done, and ate it, and was quiet afterward.

"Well?" said Ferra.

"It's very good," said Armand. "I don't believe in it at all." He took another one.

Ferra ate hers in two bites, the way the man at the street stall had meant it, and licked the soy sauce off her thumb, and said, "That's cheap. That's very cheap. Rice and a fish and a hand." She was already pricing it, Emeric could see her doing it. "The cold room's the cost. And the knife."

"The knife's the cost," he said.

The cook hadn't touched the plate. She stood with her arms folded and the cloth on her shoulder and watched the guest eat the fifth, and the sixth, dipping each one. There were no bones to lay out along the board's edge now, and twice her hand went there anyway and came back empty.

Then, when nobody was looking at her, she took one off the plate and ate it.

She chewed it. She didn't say anything. Emeric didn't look round.

"How long do you soak the rice," she said at last, to the wall.

"Half an hour. Wash it till the water runs clear first."

"That's a lot of water."

"It is."

"Hm," said the cook, and took a second one, and went out.

The guest ate seven and left the eighth, and pushed the board away an inch: full. Imani wrote it on the slate. Raw, pressed on rice. 7 of 8. Stopped on her own. Then, after a moment, underneath: Likes the sauce.

"Where does that go?" said Ferra, looking over her shoulder at the register. "There's no column for sauce."

"There is now," said Imani.

Ferra sighed, and took out her pen, and ruled a ninth column very straight down the edge of the page.

That night in the watch room, with the lamp out and Imani's cold feet on the backs of his legs and Ferra already asleep with her face in his shoulder, Imani said into the dark, very low, so as not to wake her: "Foreign lands."

"Foreign lands," he said.

"A long way past the sea."

"A very long way."

He felt her smile against his back.

"I want the green stuff," she said. "The one you thought about. I saw your face."

"There isn't any."

"Then grow some," said Imani, and was asleep before he could tell her it took two years.



On the twelfth of December the dial stood at the twenty-seventh notch and the column said 2 A, which was the beach, which was where she had started. Two fish that day, about eleven pounds, stopped on her own both times. Gums clean. Belly soft. Skin clean. Sleep: yes, eyes open.

Imani sat on the cabin bench that evening with her back to the iron and her feet up on the trough's lip, out of the water, because she'd been wet for five weeks and was making a point. Ferra sat beside her with the ledger shut on her knee for once, and Emeric on Ferra's other side, and none of them had their sense down. They didn't need it. The note filled the room like the heat off a stove, steady, going nowhere.

The guest lay in the trough with her head on her arm and her gills going slow and wide, and watched the three of them sitting there doing nothing.

"She's looking at you again," said Imani.

"She's looking at all of us."

"She looks at you first. Always. It's your face. You've got a face people look at." Imani yawned. "It's very annoying."

"I'll write it down," said Ferra, and didn't open the book, and put her cold feet up on the trough beside Imani's, and the guest watched that too.
