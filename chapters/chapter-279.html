Chapter 279 - Not Yet

Ferra read the proofs at the printing works at six in the morning, as she did every day that anything went out under Kaldo's name, standing at the slanted desk by the press-room door with a lamp at her elbow and her pencil behind her ear and the smell of the ink and the wet paper and the night shift's tea all round her.

There were four that morning. The Almanac's weather sheet, which was Linus's and never wrong; a notice from the station about the coal ground's new line, which was Bram's and always too long; a notice from her own office about the quarter's tolls at the Grayrock lock, which she had written herself and read anyway; and a half-sheet, set in the big type for walls, with HOLLOW NIGHT across the top of it in capitals an inch high.

She read it slowly.

It was a good notice. It said what it had to and stopped. A night for stories, from dusk. Children in costume, with lanterns. Any door that showed a lit lantern in its window; no knocking at a dark door. Sweet or story. Turnips at cost at the Milford stall. He had always been able to write a notice; it was one of the few things he did that she had never had to mend. She read it down to the bottom, to his name, and the line above his name.

For the peoples we lost.

She stood at the desk with the proof in her hand.

The press-room door was open. Behind her the first press was already running the weather sheet, with its great slow thump and hiss, and the printer's boy was waiting at her elbow to take the proofs back to the stone, shifting from foot to foot, because she was never this long over a half-sheet.

She knew exactly what had happened. She could see it as if she had been in the study with him: the lamp, the late hour, the notice nearly done, and the line coming out of the end of his pen on its own, the way the reason had come out in the middle of the council's letter, because it was what he meant and he had been holding it in for three months. And he had read it back, and this time he hadn't struck it out. Because it wasn't a reason he was ashamed of. Because it was true.

She thought about the six at the long table in the wine store on the Sunday, and her own voice saying not one word about where the stories came from, or why. Not on any paper. Not ever. And him saying agreed. He had meant it. She knew he had meant it. He had meant this too.

And she thought, standing there, of a cardinal's secretary going up a stair in the capital twice a month to Corbin's rooms, with a portfolio under his arm; and of what a portfolio was for; and of a half-sheet in the big type for walls, with Kaldo's seal at the foot of it and the duke's name, saying that Kaldo kept a night for peoples, and that Kaldo had lost them.

Peoples. Not people. The word was the whole of it. A clerk in the capital who had never seen the wall would read it twice, and then a third time, and then put it in the portfolio.

She took the pencil from behind her ear and drew it through the line. Once, firmly. Then again. Then a third time, exactly as he had drawn his through the reason in the council's letter, three strokes, so that anybody who held the proof up to the light might read what was under them if they were patient.

She did not mark it for the stone to be reset. She marked it out. In the margin, in her square hand, the printer's mark for delete, and beside it, small: F.

"Take that one out," she said to the boy, and gave him the four proofs. "The line at the foot. The rest as it is."

"Yes, Chancellor."

"And bring me the proof back. That one. Not the corrected one. The one I've marked."

The boy looked at her, and then went to the stone without asking why, because nobody at the printing works asked the Chancellor why; and came back in a quarter of an hour with the half-sheet, damp, and her three pencil strokes across its last line, and gave it to her.

She folded it in half, and in half again, and put it in her book, at the back, beside the other one.



He found it on his table in the study at noon.

He did not know how it had got there. Ferra had not come up. Nobody had come up. But it was there, on his blotter, under the lamp, unfolded and laid flat: the proof of the notice, with its HOLLOW NIGHT in the big type, and at its foot the line he had forgotten he had written, and through it, three times, in pencil, Ferra's strokes.

He read it through the pencil. It was easy. She had not meant him not to.

For the peoples we lost.

He sat down at the table and looked at it for a long time.

Then he went to the window. Down in the market square, the notice was already up. He could see it from here, on the board by the well, where the market notices went: the big black capitals, the half-sheet, a cluster of women from the north well round it reading it aloud to each other, and two children hopping up and down at the edge of the cluster because they had read it already; and it ended, he knew without having to read it, at the line above his name.

She had struck his line the way he had struck his own. Three times. So that it could be read. So that he would know she had read it, and why, and that she had not thrown it away.

He thought about the council's letter, and the line in it, and her not saying anything, and taking it, and keeping it.

He folded the proof, and put it in the drawer in the table, with the tooth, and the scale, and the slip of paper that said drained; and went down to the hall to find out what Maren was doing with forty pounds of Milford sugar.



What Maren was doing with forty pounds of Milford sugar was disapproving of it.

"Begging," she said. She was in her kitchen with three girls from the north well and both her ranges lit, and the gas burner at the end that Emeric had put in for her the winter before last going blue under a copper pan the size of a cartwheel, in which something was turning slowly from white to gold to brown. "That's what it is. Children going round doors in the dark with their hands out." She stirred. "In my day, that was beggars. Proper ones. Coming round at the back of the year with a lantern and a tale, for a bit of bread. My mother gave them a crust and a cold look."

"They'll be telling stories for it," said Emeric.

"Beggars told stories. Best liars in the barony." Maren lifted the pan off the flame with a cloth, and the girls jumped back, and she poured it, a long slow ribbon of gold, onto a slab of the cold grey stone that she kept in the scullery for it, and let it run. "Caramel," she said, not looking at him. "Since you've no shame. A thousand pieces by six o'clock, wrapped in Kaldo paper, twisted at the ends. Bram's girl's put a cold stone in the scullery so it sets." She looked at the slab. "And don't think I don't know what you're after with the ice cream."

"I'm not after anything."

"You've been after it since August." She wiped her hands. "Sweetened with that stuff from the coke ovens. The white stuff that tastes of sugar and isn't sugar. Two hundredweight of it in my store that cost less than one of sugar, Ferra says, and five hundred times as sweet." She sniffed. "So the whole town can have a dish of ice cream on a cold night and nobody's teeth fall out." She looked at him at last. "In the square. Under the new lamps. With the big churns." She paused. "I'll do it. Don't stand in my kitchen."

He went out of her kitchen.



Dusk came at a little before six, grey and cold, with no wind and a thin high cloud that the moon, coming up late, would turn to silver; and Kaldo came out into it carrying turnips.

He had not known what it would be like. He had thought about it for three months and he had not known. He stood on the keep's steps at six with Ferra on one side of him and Imani on the other and a bowl of Maren's caramels in his arms, and looked down the castle hill at the town in the dusk; and in every street, and every lane, and every window, there were lights.

Not the town's lights. Not the arc lamps on their poles, or the new electric street lamps the station had lit for the first time a week ago, though those were on too, white and steady down the market road and round the square. The other lights. Small ones. Yellow, and low, and moving: carried in people's hands, swinging on strings, set in windows and on doorsteps and on the tops of walls. Hundreds of them. Every one of them a turnip, hollowed out, with a face cut in its side, and a candle in it, or a stub of one, or, in a few windows up the hill where the families had a little money, a light stone the size of a thumb; and the faces glowing out of them yellow and orange in the dusk, grinning, scowling, gaping, every one different, every one cut by somebody's hand that week at somebody's kitchen table.

Milford had sent four wagons of them. Milford's rotation grew them for the cattle; nobody in Kaldo had ever eaten a turnip by choice. And the Milford stall in the market had sold every one of the four wagons by Wednesday, at cost, a copper for three, and sent for a fifth.

"Oh," said Imani, beside him.

He did not say anything. He found he could not.

"You didn't say it would look like this," said Ferra.

"I didn't know."

"You didn't know." She looked down the hill at the lights. "You've done this before. On your side. You said it was the best night of the year when you were seven."

"We didn't have turnips. We had..." He stopped. There was no word for a pumpkin in this world, because there were no pumpkins in it. "Something else. Bigger. Orange. And the lights were electric, mostly. And you went round in a car." He looked down at the lanes, at the lights moving, at the small dark shapes carrying them. "It didn't look like this."

Ferra looked at him a moment longer.

"Stolen too?" she said quietly. "Like the Long Table?"

"From a harvest feast," he said. "Thousands of years ago. Then from the dead." He shifted the bowl in his arms. "The old people, on my side, before anybody wrote anything down, said that at the end of the harvest, when the year turned, the door between the living and the dead stood open one night. And the dead came back to visit. And other things came through with them. So people lit fires, and left food out, and put on masks so the things that came through wouldn't know them." He watched a cluster of lights go down the lane below the castle, bobbing, five or six of them, small. "Then a church came, and put a feast for its holy dead on the next day, and the night before it got the name. And the children kept the masks."

"And the turnips?"

"Before the orange things came, they were turnips. Where my grandmother's grandmother came from." He did not know if that was true. He had read it once. "Hollowed out. With faces. To scare off what came through."

Imani was looking at the town.

"You named it after the tree," said Ferra.

He looked at her.

"Hollow Night. I saw it on the proof this morning." She did not look away from the lights. "The tree's a Hollow Crystal. The stones are hollow crystals. The tree the winged ones carried, before they all went." She was quiet. "You named it after the tree."

"I named it after a lot of things."

"Yes," said Ferra. "You do that." And then, without looking at him, she took one of Maren's caramels out of the bowl in his arms, and unwrapped it, and put it in her mouth.

The first knock came at the castle gate at ten past six.



It was three of them, and one of them was on fire.

Not on fire. Red. A small boy wrapped from his chin to his feet in a red blanket, with two red cloth horns sewn to a cap on his head and a tail of red rope dragging behind him on the cobbles, and a turnip lantern in his fist that was nearly as big as his head; and beside him a girl in a sheet with holes cut for her eyes, and another girl in her mother's green petticoat stuffed with straw, so that she was nearly round, with a face painted on the front of it in charcoal, and no face of her own showing at all.

They stood at the castle's postern in the lantern light, in a row, the three of them, and looked up at the duke of the south, and the Chancellor of the duchy, and the Secretary of Health; and the round green one said, from inside the petticoat, muffled, "Sweet or story."

Emeric held out the bowl.

"Story," said Imani.

The three of them looked at her.

"I'm a slime," said the round one, from inside the petticoat, at once. "I live in cellars. Old ones. And I'm green and I've got no face and I wobble." She wobbled, to show them; the whole petticoat went from side to side. "And I eat whatever falls in me. Anything. Mice. Shoes. A man fell in me once and I ate him and his hat." She stopped wobbling. "That's my story."

"What happened to his hat?" said Imani.

The slime thought about it.

"It's still in me," she said. "I can't do hats."

Imani took three caramels out of the bowl and gave one to each of them, very gravely, and the slime's hand came out of a slit in the side of the petticoat and took hers, and went back in; and the three of them turned round and went off down the castle hill toward the next lit window, with the red one's rope tail dragging on the cobbles behind them and the slime wobbling on purpose, and the ghost in the sheet saying, quite loudly, "She didn't ask me."

Imani watched them go. Her shoulders were shaking.

"The Milford girl," said Emeric. "With her mother's petticoat. She was in the sail loft on Thursday arguing with Tomas about who got to be green."

"I don't care whose it was." Imani wiped her eyes. "I'm going to remember that hat for the rest of my life."



They came all evening. Emeric stopped counting at a hundred and twenty.

They came in twos and fives and tens, up the castle hill and along the inner ward to the postern, with their lanterns, in everything that Kaldo had in its cupboards and its rag bags and its sail loft. There were wolves in fur coats turned inside out and grey felt ears. There were ghosts in sheets. There were a great many of the ones that drank blood, because the costume was a black cloak and a white face and two teeth cut out of a turnip's skin, and every house had a black cloak; and they all came to the postern with their cloaks drawn across their faces, and said "Sweet or story" in deep voices, and when Imani asked one of them for his story he told it with enormous seriousness, the coffin and the cellar and the running water, and then asked if he could have two because he had to go a long way before the sun came up.

There were short broad ones with beards made of rope, and tool belts, and hammers. A great many hammers. One of them had a hammer from his father's shop that was nearly as big as he was, which he could barely lift, and which he carried the whole way up the castle hill on his shoulder, and put down at the postern with a great clank, and leaned on, breathing hard, before he could say his line. He was a smith from the mountains, he said, and he could make anything out of iron, and he kept his word for ever, and he liked beer, which he wasn't allowed. And then he sang. He sang one verse of something the barge crews sang, very loud and very badly, as Emeric had told them the mountain people sang; and Ferra laughed out loud, which she almost never did, and gave him three.

And there were the tall ones from the woods.

They came in the second hour, a whole lane of them together from the lower town, a dozen or more, in long green cloaks and brown ones made from sacking, carrying sticks with leaves tied to them; and every one of them had the ears. Paper ears, card ears, ears of felt and of folded linen and one pair, magnificently, of leather, tooled, long and pointed, sticking up out of their hair on either side. And the hair under the ears was every kind of hair in Kaldo. Straight and fair, and straight and black, and curled tight against the head, and great soft clouds of it round small brown faces, and red, and plaited; and in the middle of them, three or four, the darkest of them, with their hair done in rope. Long strands of tarred yarn and dyed wool twisted into locs and tied at the back of their heads, down to their shoulders, with rings of gold paper wound round them here and there, catching the lantern light.

They stopped at the postern, all of them, in a row, with their leaves and their lanterns and their ears.

The tallest of the ones with the rope locs was Nell Tanner. She had changed her mind about the tail. She stood at the front of them with her chin up and her paper ears very straight, and looked up at Emeric, and he looked down at her, and at her hair, and at the gold paper rings in it; and she looked at his, tied back at his neck, with the gold cuffs in it that Abloh had made him for the investiture and Imani had put in that evening, because, she said, the tall ones in the stories wore rings; and back at his face, and said, with great satisfaction:

"Like yours."

"Like mine," said Emeric.

"With the ears."

"With the ears."

"Sweet or story," said Nell.

Ferra, beside him, said nothing at all. She was looking at the row of them. At the dozen faces under the paper ears, dark and brown and pale, and the hair, every kind; and then, sideways, at him. And whatever she saw on his face made her look away again quickly, at the lanterns, and take the bowl out of his arms, and give the tall ones of the woods two caramels each without asking any of them for anything.



The square was full by eight.

The station had lit it for the first time a week ago: twelve electric lamps on iron posts round its edge, and four on the corners of the well, white and steady and so bright you could read a paper under them, as no square in the kingdom could be read in at night, and people had come out every evening since just to stand under them. Tonight there was nobody standing. Tonight the square under its white lamps was a slow wheel of small yellow lights going round it, hundreds, in every costume in Kaldo, and the grown people in the middle round the well and at the edges in the doorways, watching, with lanterns of their own; and on the market side, under the arcade, Maren's churns.

There were six of them, the big ones from the dairy, in a row on trestles, with Maren's girls turning the handles and Maren herself at the end in a clean apron with a ladle, serving ice cream into anything anybody held out: a cup, a dish, a hat. It was the ice cream she had made at the lobster supper in July, and at midwinter before that; but sweetened, tonight, with the white powder from the coke ovens' tar, saccharin, Emeric's word, which Ferra said had cost less by the hundredweight than a sack of sugar and went five hundred times as far; and it was cold, and sweet, and there was enough of it for the whole of Kaldo, and the whole of Kaldo was having some.

Bram had three.

He stood by the end churn with the third dish in his big hand and his spoon in it, and said to Maren, who had not asked, "It's cold milk."

"It's ice cream," said Maren.

"It's cold milk with that tar stuff in it." He ate a spoonful. "I'll have another."

"You've had three."

"Then it'll be four," said Bram, and held out the dish.

He had carved the best turnip in the lane. Everybody said so. He had said all week that he would not carve a turnip, that he was a smith and not a cook's boy, that it was a waste of a good knife and a worse waste of a turnip; and on the Wednesday evening Ferra had come home and found him at the kitchen table with a turnip the size of a man's head and his smallest graver and a face on him like a man doing a fine weld, and had gone out again without a word. It sat now on the low wall by the forge's door at the top of the smiths' lane, lit, where everybody going up or down could see it: a face, an old man's face, deep-lined, carved in the turnip's skin so finely that the candle shone through the thin places as gold and through the thick places as red and through the lines as nothing at all, so that the face seemed to move when the flame did. It looked like nobody. It looked, Ferra said, a little like their grandfather, whom Emeric had never met; and Bram, asked about it, said he'd just started cutting and that was what came out.

Tess was dancing.

There was a fiddler on the steps of the market hall and a man with a drum, and in the space under the lamps between the well and the steps, where the wheel of small lights went round, the young people were dancing; and in the middle of them, in her chair, Tess. She had the chair's little motor going and her hand on its stick, and she was turning it, one way and the other, in time to the fiddle, in a circle and back, and spinning it on the spot on the beat, so that her skirts went out round her and her hair came down; and the dancers had made a space for her and were going round her, and she was laughing with her head back, and at the edge of the space, not dancing, because he did not dance, Osric stood with his hands at his sides and watched her, and every time she came round to face him she pointed at him, and every time he shook his head, and every time she laughed harder.

On the fourth time round she stopped the chair in front of him and held out her hand.

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he took it. And she spun the chair, slowly, on the spot, with her hand in his, so that he had to walk round with it, in a circle, holding her hand, in front of the whole of Kaldo, under the white lamps, with his ears going red; and the dancers round them stopped dancing and clapped, in time, and the fiddler played faster, and Osric walked round and round in his garrison coat with Tess's hand in his and his face like a man walking to his own hanging, and did not let go.

"Six years," said Imani, beside Emeric, under the arcade, with a dish of ice cream.

"Six years what?"

"Since the crate. Since he missed pushing her chair." She ate a spoonful. "Six years for that. He'll ask her to walk out in about four more."

"Imani."

"I'm her doctor. I know these things." She ate another spoonful. "Look at his ears."



Rylan came into the square at half past eight carrying a mermaid.

She was in his arms, the way he would have carried a child, with one arm under her knees and one round her back; and she was not a child. She was fifteen, and nearly as tall as her mother, and she was furious. She had a tail. Abloh had made it. It was green and blue and silver, sewn in scales of cut silk from the investiture's offcuts, one by one, overlapping, hundreds of them, from her waist down to a great double fin at the bottom stiffened with whalebone that trailed over Rylan's arm almost to the cobbles; and it was made to fit, as Abloh made everything, exactly. Exactly. It fitted her from the waist to the ankle like a second skin, and she could not take one step in it, not one, and had not been able to since she had been sewn into it at five o'clock.

"Don't," said Wren, to Emeric, as Rylan carried her up under the arcade.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to." She had her arms folded across her chest, in Rylan's arms, with her hair down her back with shells tied in it, and her face red under the lamps. "I told Abloh it was too tight. I told him at the fitting. I said, I can't walk in it. He said, the sea people don't walk." She glared at the square. "I said I wasn't one of the sea people, I was Wren. He said, then why do you want a tail."

"Fair question," said Rylan.

"Don't you start." She did not unfold her arms. "I've been carried up and down every lane in the lower town. I've been carried up the castle hill. I said I was past being carried when I was thirteen, and Father said so was the whole street, and now look." She looked at the dancers. "Everybody's seen."

"Everybody's seen a mermaid," said Imani. "It's the best costume in the square."

Wren looked at her suspiciously.

"It is," said Ferra. "Abloh's done every scale by hand. I watched him. Three nights." She looked at the tail, at the silk scales overlapping in the lamplight, green going to blue going to silver at the fin. "Nobody else has got scales. Nobody else has got a fin. Nobody else has anything that looks like it came out of the sea."

Wren unfolded her arms a little.

"It does look like it came out of the sea," she said. "Doesn't it."

"It does," said Emeric.

She looked down at it, over Rylan's arm, at the fin trailing toward the cobbles; and for a moment the fury went out of her face altogether, and she looked at the tail the way she looked at a machine she had just understood.

"It'd be no good on land," she said slowly. "A tail. That's the trouble. You'd be stuck. You'd have to be carried everywhere, by somebody, like this." She frowned at it. "If you were one of them, really, and you came up out of the sea, you'd be helpless. Wouldn't you. On land."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Unless," said Wren, and stopped, and thought, and then shook her head and folded her arms again. "Father, put me down by the churns. I want ice cream. I can sit on a trestle."

Rylan carried her off toward the churns. Emeric watched them go: the big quiet knight with his daughter in his arms in her silk tail, and the fin trailing, and her shells catching the lamplight; and found that Ferra was watching him watch them, and looked away.



Finn was a Demon of the Isle.

Emeric saw him across the square at nine, with four of the port boys, in the middle of the wheel of lights, and stopped in the middle of what he was saying to Saban; and Saban followed his look, and stopped too.

They were all in black. Black from head to foot: black jerseys and black trousers and black stockings pulled over their boots, and their faces blacked with soot from the forge, and over their heads, each of them, a black hood, pulled forward; and on their chests, nothing. No crest, no badge, no device of any kind. They carried no lanterns. They went round the square's edge in the shadow under the arcade, five of them, in a line, silent, not saying sweet or story to anybody, only stepping out of the shadow every so often in front of a group of children, all five at once, with their arms raised; and the children screamed, and laughed, and ran.

"The Demons of the Isle," said Saban.

"Yes."

"The ones the freed men talk about. Who came out of the dark on the Isle in black with no crest, and took the camp in a night without a sound, and went up into the sky." Saban watched the five black shapes go along the arcade. "The wardens who ran told Kesrath's fishermen. Kesrath's fishermen told ours. And our boys..." He stopped. "Who's been telling children that story?"

"Everybody," said Emeric.

"Everybody." Saban watched them a while longer. "Every child in the port knows the Demons of the Isle. And not one of them knows it was their own town's knights." He laughed, very quietly, a sound Emeric seldom heard him make. "And there's Rylan's boy. In black. With no crest. Being one."

Rylan was at the churns, with Wren sitting on a trestle beside him in her tail eating ice cream. He had seen them too. He stood with a dish of ice cream in his hand that he was not eating, and watched his son go along the arcade in a black hood with soot on his face, and step out of the shadow at a cluster of small children with his arms up, silent; and the children screamed and ran; and Finn stepped back into the shadow, and the five of them went on.

Rylan did not say a word.

He stood there. His face did nothing at all. He watched Finn all the way along the arcade to the corner, and round it, and out of sight; and then he looked down at the dish of ice cream in his hand, and ate a spoonful of it, very slowly, and kept his eyes on the dish.

Wren, beside him on the trestle, looked up at his face.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing," said Rylan. "Eat your ice cream."



Abel Dunning was by the well, with his hand.

He had not been going to wear it. Tess had said so at supper the night before; that he had told her he would wear the hook, because it was a holiday, and people would look. And he was wearing the hand. He stood by the well in his good coat with the varnished box on its strap under his coat, and his left sleeve pulled down to the kid glove's wrist, holding a dish of ice cream in it, in the hand, by its rim. Not well. The dish was tilted. He had to watch it. But he was holding it.

A boy came past him in a wolf's fur coat turned inside out, and stopped, and looked at the hand. He was perhaps seven.

"Is that a costume?" said the boy.

Abel looked down at him.

"No," he said. "It's mine."

The boy looked at the hand for a while longer, and then up at Abel's face; and then he nodded, as if that were perfectly reasonable, and went off after the other wolves. Abel stood by the well and watched him go, and then looked at the hand on the dish, and moved one finger. Just one. And stood there.

Tess, dancing, did not see. Emeric did, from under the arcade, and did not go over, and did not say anything to anybody about it, then or after.



Doran came at half past nine.

He came up from the chapel in his black, with his lantern and his book under his arm, as he came up to the square on every feast day there was, and stood by the well in the white light among the children, smiling; and the children who knew him, which was all of them, came round him in their costumes to show him, as they would have shown their grandfathers. A wolf. A ghost. A slime, wobbling. Three of the ones who drank blood, with their turnip-skin teeth, very serious. A short broad one with a rope beard, who sang. And Doran looked at each of them, and nodded, and asked them their stories, and listened to every one, with his head on one side, the way he listened to confessions.

"In the capital," he said to Emeric, when Emeric came down to the well, "they'd call this a heresy." He looked round at the square, at the wheel of lights and the wolves and the ghosts and the paper ears. "Children walking about in the dark dressed as things from under the hill." He smiled. "Here I'll call it a costume."

"Thank you, Father."

"Don't thank me. My grandmother would have loved it." He looked at the slime, who was wobbling at him hopefully. "She'd have given that one two."

He gave the slime two. And then he turned round, in the middle of the square, under the white lamps, with the children round him and half of Kaldo within earshot, and saw Ferra and Imani standing together under the arcade by the churns; and his face lit up, the way it did, and he said, in his feast-day voice, which carried:

"And when are you three going to let me marry you, then? My chapel's there any day you like. Any day at all. I've had the banns written out since the spring."

The square heard it.

Not all of it; not the children. But the grown people round the well, and in the doorways, and at the churns, heard it, and a good many of them turned round to look, and a laugh went round the well, warm, and somebody near the churns called out "Hear, hear," and somebody else whistled.

Ferra and Imani stood under the arcade and smiled at Doran, both of them, the way you smile at a good old man on a feast day, and did not say anything.

Emeric, by the well, did not say anything either.

And at the square's far edge, under the last lamp by the corner of the river road, a man in a Wayford traveller's coat, who had been standing there since nine with his hands in his pockets watching the children go by, and who nobody had spoken to, took his hands out of his pockets, and looked at Doran by the well for a long moment, and at the children round him in their paper ears and their black cloaks; and then turned, and went away down the river road, without hurrying, toward the inn.

Saban, at the arcade's end, watched him go. Then he took his small book out of his pocket, and wrote something in it, and put it back.



They told him on the keep leads, at eleven.

The square had emptied by then, slowly, the wheel of small lights breaking up and going away down the lanes in twos and threes, and the children carried home asleep over their fathers' shoulders with their paper ears crushed and their lanterns guttering; and Maren's churns were empty, and Bram had had five; and the white lamps burned on round the empty square, and all over the town below the keep, on doorsteps and in windows and along the tops of walls, the turnips burned down in their hundreds, low and orange, as the candles in them came to their ends.

He had gone up to the leads to watch them go out. He had not asked either of them to come. They came anyway, up the leads stair behind him, with a rug, and stood one each side of him at the parapet, looking down at the town.

Nobody said anything for a while.

Then Ferra said, still looking at the town:

"Not yet."

And Imani, at the same moment, in the same breath, exactly with her, so that the two words came out of both of them as one:

"Not yet."

He turned his head.

They were both looking at the town. Neither of them looked at the other. They had not planned it, he understood; or they had, and not the timing; or they had planned it exactly, down to the breath, and he would never know which, and it did not matter.

"I didn't ask," he said.

"You were going to," said Ferra. "Not tonight. Some night. Soon. You've been going to since Doran opened his mouth in the square." She did not look at him. "Your face went like it does. I saw it from the arcade. So did Imani."

"And Doran's going to keep asking," said Imani. "In front of everybody. Every feast day. Because he loves us, and he's a good man, and he doesn't know."

"So we're saying it first," said Ferra. "Before you ask. So you don't have to. So you never have to hear us say it to a question." She looked at him then, at last, in the dark, with the orange of the dying turnips below her and the white of the square's lamps beyond. "Not yet. And not because of you."

"Not because of you," said Imani.

He waited.

"Keller called it evidence," said Ferra. "On the platform. What's on our faces. And the faces are the least of it. There's a cardinal's man going up the stair to Corbin's rooms twice a month with a portfolio, Saban says; and you know what goes in a portfolio. Paper. Our paper. Every list we keep, every census, every book. Every notice." She did not look away from him. "This morning I took a line out of one."

"I know."

"I know you know. I left it on your blotter." She was quiet. "A wedding is paper. A wedding in Doran's chapel is a page in his register with the duke's name on it, and mine, and Imani's, and the date, and Doran's hand. Two women and a man, in a church that the Church says has one door for one woman. And Doran officiating, with his own book, who is the Church's man in Kaldo." She stopped. "I won't hand them that page. Not now. Not while there's a portfolio. Not for anything."

"And they've already got a page with me on it," said Imani.

He looked at her.

"The ward." Imani was looking at the town. "Mana medicine. The cartridges. The dosed water. The tank. The scrubbers. Everything I do that a priest in the capital would call craft, if he wanted to, and he will. It's in every report Kaldo's ever sent north. It's in the Almanac." She was quiet. "If there's a case, I'm in it. I'm in it already. As the duke's healer, who does strange things to people with stones." She turned and looked at him. "And if I marry you, I'm in it as the duke's wife who does strange things to people with stones. And so is the ward. And so is every patient in it." She shook her head, slowly. "I won't hand them my ward. Not for anything either."

He stood at the parapet between them with his hands on the cold stone.

He could have argued. He could see the arguments; he could see all of them, the way he saw the ghosts in the yard, laid out faint across the dark: that Keller was honest, that Harrold was king, that a page was only a page, that he could protect them, that he could protect the ward. And every one of them, as he looked at it, went faint and thin and wrong, because every one of them was a man saying trust me to two women who had heard him say it before and had been right, both times, not to.

They were right. That was the whole of it. They were right, and he knew they were right, and he did not argue because there was nothing to argue with.

He did not say anything. There was one word he could have said, that he had never said to either of them, that might have changed it; he could feel it standing just behind his teeth, very large. He did not say it. Not tonight. It was not a word to say to change somebody's mind.

"Not yet, Your Grace," said Ferra, very softly.

He had called them duchesses since last year, as a joke. Duchess Ferra. Duchess Imani. Every time either of them gave him an order. He heard it now in her mouth, turned round, and it was not a joke, and he did not want it to be one.

"All right," he said. "Not yet."

And Ferra kissed him.

She did it the way she did everything that mattered, without warning and completely: turned from the parapet and took his face in her two cold hands and kissed him, hard, on the mouth, for a long time, in the dark on the keep leads above the whole town, so that he would know exactly what not yet meant, and what it didn't. It meant not yet. It did not mean no. It did not mean less. It did not mean anything at all about the two of them except what it said, and the kiss said the rest.

When she let him go, Imani was waiting. She did not take his face. She put her hand flat on his chest, over his heart, where she always put it, and stood there with her palm on him counting for a moment, in the dark; and then she reached up and kissed him, slowly, much more slowly than Ferra, as if she had all the time in the world and meant to use it, with her hand still on his chest.

"A hundred and four," she said against his mouth, when she was done.

"That's high."

"That's mine." She did not take her hand away. "I'm keeping it."



He flew them over the town after.

He had not planned it. He had been thinking about it since April, since the bare flight, since he had stood on his own window ledge in the rain and known he could; but he had not planned it for tonight. He found that he had put one arm round Ferra's waist and the other round Imani's, at the parapet, and that both of them had put their arms round his neck as if they had planned it, and that Ferra was saying, "If you drop me I'll never speak to you again," and Imani was saying, "If you drop me I'll be dead and it won't matter," and that he was laughing; and then he stepped up onto the parapet with both of them held against him, and stepped off.

It was one cast. That was the thing he had worked out in the spring and never tried. A man who flew carried what he held fast, as part of himself, and a body that wanted to be carried did not fight him; so it was not three weights to lift but one weight, his, made heavier by theirs. One gravity cast, round all three of them at once, as if they were one thing, and the air under it. A dual hold, and nothing more. It cost him three bodies' weight in mana instead of one, and the pod stone's capacity in him paid it, and did not notice.

They did not fall.

They went out from the keep's wall into the dark over the inner ward, slowly, low, the three of them, held together; and then out over the castle's outer wall and the hill below it, and over the town.

The turnips were still burning.

Not all of them now. Half. Fewer. But enough. All down the lanes below them and along the tops of the walls and in the windows, the small orange lights were going out one by one, as the candles in them guttered; and the faces in them, cut by a thousand hands that week, grinned and scowled and gaped up at the three shapes going over them in the dark, low, and the light of them came up orange on the undersides of the three of them, on Ferra's face and Imani's, as they went over.

He took them over the smiths' lane, low, so that they went over Bram's turnip on the wall by the forge's door, the old man's face, still burning, gold and red; and Ferra, holding on, said, "Oh," once, very quietly, and looked back at it as they went.

He took them over the square, where the white lamps still burned round the empty well, and the ice cream churns stood empty under the arcade, and somebody had left a pair of paper ears on the well's rim. Over the lower town, where the lights were mostly out. Over the river, and back.

Imani counted his pulse the whole way. He could feel her fingers on the side of his neck, under his jaw, where her arm was round him; light, steady, counting. At the top of the turn over the river, at the highest they went, which was not very high, she said the number aloud, into the wind, close to his ear.

"Ninety-eight."

"Is that good?"

"It's lower than on the leads." He could hear her smiling. "You're calmer up here than you are kissing us. I'm writing that down."

Ferra did not say anything at all. Ferra held on. She held on with both arms round his neck and her face against his shoulder, and did not look down after the first time, and did not let go, and did not loosen her hold by a hair the whole way, from the parapet out and over the town and the river and back; and when they came down onto the keep leads again and his boots touched the stone, she did not let go then either, for a while.

His arms were shaking when he set them down. Not from the weight. From something else, that had been in them since the parapet, and that he did not have a name for, and did not try to find one.

Imani noticed. She took his hands, both of them, and held them still between hers, and did not say anything about it.

They stood on the leads together for a while longer, the three of them, with the rug round all three, looking down at the town; at the last of the turnips going out, one by one, in the lanes and the windows and on the walls.

On the parapet beside them, where nobody in the town could see it, there was one more.

He had carved it himself, that afternoon, alone, in the study, with the door locked; and put it there at dusk, before the first knock, and lit it, and not said anything. It had no face. He had cut no face in it. He had cut, all round its side, very small, in a ring, a row of shapes: a broad figure with a hammer, and a tall one with long ears among trees, and a figure half in water, and a small one the height of a thumb, and others, too many and too small to make out, and over all of them, cut in the turnip's skin at the top, wings. And the candle in it was nearly out, and the shapes on its side were faint, gold on dark, as the flame sank.

Ferra looked at it.

She had seen it when they came up. He knew she had. She had not said anything then.

"Say it," she said now. "Here. There's nobody to hear."

He looked at the turnip on the parapet, and the shapes on it, and the last of the light in them.

"For the peoples we lost," he said.

Ferra put her hand on his arm.

"Good," she said. "That's where that line goes. Up here. Said out loud, to us." She did not take her hand away. "Not on a wall."

The candle in the turnip went out. They stood on the leads in the dark a while longer, the three of them, under the rug, with the whole town below them gone dark too now except the square's white lamps; and then Imani said she was cold, and Ferra said so was she, and they went down.
