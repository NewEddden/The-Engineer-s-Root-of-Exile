Chapter 244 - Four Lines

The man who brought it had come down the Wayford line on the evening train and walked the last four miles from the junction in the dark, because he did not know there was a cart, and he stood in the gatehouse at New Kaldo with the snow melting off his shoulders and asked for the duke by name and would not give his own.

Ward went down to him. Ward went down to everybody who would not give his name at that gate; it was the rule. He came back up a quarter of an hour later with a sealed letter in his hand and an expression Emeric had not seen on him very often, which was the expression of a man who has been made to wait on somebody else's terms and has not minded.

"He's in grey," Ward said. "No badge. He came from the capital in seven days. He says he was told to give it into your hand or into mine, and nobody else's, and to wait for an answer if there was one, and to say nothing whatever about anything if anybody asked him, and he's said nothing whatever. I've had him put by the fire. He's asleep on the bench."

He put the letter down on the table.

It was small, and folded small, under her own seal, and Emeric knew it before he had looked at it properly. He had perhaps a dozen letters under that seal, over all the years since he came south, and most of them were about nothing. He had kept one. He had read it more times than it deserved.

He did not open it at once. He sat and looked at the seal.

Keller's letter in the winter had come in the bag. The news of the sitting after the new year had not come at all. It was nine days since the first sitting after the new year, and every one of them he had spent at this table or at the window or in the dome, not asking, and the table had spent them not asking him, and nothing had come down the road from the north except the ordinary post, which had nothing in it, and the weather.

And now this, from the one person at court who had never once in her life asked him for anything.

He broke the seal.

It was four lines. It was always four lines.

The council found against him by one voice, Grier's, and named Corbin lieutenant; Father is in the Church's keeping in his own rooms, until they appoint a place.

Of my household I answer now for Wald, Corin, Mistress Oake and Mistress Lune, and for no one else; Mother's secretary's sister is still at Brede.

Wald brings this, and will carry back whatever you send, and will not read it.

I am asking you to bring him out, and I will put my name to it. M.

He read it twice. Then he read it again, a line at a time, slowly, the way he read a drawing he had been handed by someone else to see what they had meant by every mark.

The first line was the news. It was all of the news, the whole of the sitting, the verdict and the vote and the lieutenancy and his father, in one sentence, with a semicolon in the middle of it where another woman would have put a page of feeling. By one voice. Grier's. He had been at the elevation; he had seen Grier look left and right along the bench and say aye for him, rather loudly, and somebody laugh. He sat with that for a moment. It did not surprise him that Grier had turned. It surprised him a little that Maeve knew to name him. Then it did not, because she had placed every man on those benches herself, and would know how every one of them had voted before the clerk added the columns.

The second line was the thing nobody else could have given him.

Of my household I answer now for. Four names, and then no one else. He thought of the hall at the elevation, and the eight or nine of them in grey, moving among the benches with their lists and never speaking to one another, because each of them knew where every other would be. He had admired it without understanding it, the way a man admires a watch. It had been a household that ran like one thing. And now it was four, out of eight or nine, and she did not say what had happened to the others, or how she knew, and she did not need to. She had been at court all her life. She had been Rowena's daughter at a court where Sabina's son was heir, and the Countess of Brede's household was where Aldane's men had their supper. She had told him that herself, in the long gallery, the week of the duke's file: that her mother's letters were not as private as her mother thought, because her mother's secretary had a sister at Brede. She had been counting her own people ever since, the way Ferra counted stores. Now she had counted them for him, and it came to four.

The third line was the man asleep on the bench by the gatehouse fire.

And the fourth line was the one he had read three times, and would read, he thought, a good many more.

I am asking you to bring him out.

She had never asked him for anything. Not once. Not at the banquet, where her chair had stood empty at the high table the whole evening and neither of them had ever mentioned it. Not in the winter of the duke's file, when her mother had written asking her to speak for him to the people who would be at supper with the people who would decide it, and she had not, and had told him so, and had not told him why. Not in the four lines that had come south after the Staple, which was the only letter from the capital that autumn that had not wanted anything from him. I do not know what you found in that cellar. I should like to, one day, from you. Until then I have no opinion I am entitled to. Even that had not been a request. It had been a statement of what she would like, and a refusal to presume on it.

She had spent her whole life at that court being careful not to be on anyone's side, because a woman who is on a side can be counted, and a woman who can be counted can be moved. She had refused both parties at the Countess of Brede's in front of the whole room, gently, so that everybody heard. She had refused her own mother. She had kept an office that nobody in the building knew was an office, and put every man in the kingdom in his place at every sitting for years, and never once been seen to want anything from any of them.

And I will put my name to it.

She had signed every letter she ever sent him with one initial. It was not her name. It was a letter of the alphabet that meant her, in the way a seal means a house, and could be denied. She was telling him that this time it could not be.

He took it to the table that night. He did not see what else to do with it, and he had stopped, some time in the last three weeks, being able to carry things alone that he could have carried at twenty-two.

They were all there, because they were all there every night now: Ferra with her book, Bram with his hands on the table, Saban with nothing in front of him, which was how you knew Saban was worried. Imani, back from the ward. Armand, who had come across the bridge every evening since the letter in the winter and sat in the same chair and said almost nothing. Ward by the door.

He read it out. It did not take long.

"One voice," Saban said, when he had finished. He said it the way he had said four at the inn after the elevation, entering it. "Grier's."

"You had him at the elevation."

"I had him at the elevation as a man who owed everyone and voted with whoever he owed most that week. That week it was us, or it was nobody." Saban looked at the letter on the table. "This week it was somebody. A man who owes everybody doesn't turn against a king for somebody he owes. He turns for somebody who's stopped him owing." He was quiet a moment. "That's a line in a book. Somewhere."

"Can you find it?"

"From here, no. From the capital, perhaps." Saban did not look up. "I'll need to know what I'm looking for before I go looking. Let me think about it."

Ferra had her pencil out and was not writing. "Four," she said.

"Four of her own. Out of eight or nine." Emeric put his hand on the letter. "The rest she doesn't answer for. That's not the same as saying they're Corbin's. It's saying she doesn't know, and won't guess, and won't let me guess."

"She's told you who you can trust in the palace," Ferra said, "the week the palace became Corbin's house." She laid the pencil down. "Nobody else could have given you that. Keller couldn't. Nobody at the king's end could. Not for hers." She looked at the letter again. "And she's told you in a letter, under her own seal, that she can only count four of her own household. If this goes astray, it's a list."

"It won't go astray. The man's asleep by the gate."

"It'll go astray," Ferra said, "the day anybody asks Wald where he was for a fortnight in the new year." She did not say it unkindly. She said it as a figure. "She'll have thought of that. She sent him anyway."

Nobody said anything.

Emeric looked down at the letter under his hand and thought about what it cost.

She was Rowena's daughter. Rowena had come to Randillia as a treaty and gone home to Emberfell when the treaty stopped needing her, and her daughter had stayed, at a court where the first queen's son was heir and the first queen's brother sat among the old blood with the most men. Maeve had kept her place at that court for all the years since by being nothing to anybody: not her mother's party, not the old blood's, not Corbin's, not the exiled half-brother in the south who hanged lords in a square. And now the first queen's son was lieutenant of the realm and kept the house she lived in, and she had put her name, in writing, under her own seal, to taking the king out of it. If Corbin ever held this letter in his hand, she would not be refusing parties at the Countess of Brede's any more. She would be a party. She would be the other one.

And she had said nothing about that. Not a word. Four lines, and the last of them was the only one that was about her, and all it said was what she would do.

"Well," said Bram.

That was all he said. He looked round the table, slowly, from face to face, the way he looked along a row of castings to see which one had a flaw in it. Then he put his hands flat on the table in front of him and waited.

Emeric waited too. He had sat at this table through a great many nights when the whole of it had gone against him: when Ferra had turned her book round to show him three tons that were not four times three tons; when Imani had said he'll drink the water and let the silence do the rest; when Ward had stood by the door and said you're not sent for. He knew what it sounded like when somebody at this table was about to say wait. He had heard it a hundred times, and it had been right most of them.

He waited for somebody to say it.

Saban did not. Saban had his hands folded and was looking at the letter, and Emeric could see him already in the capital, going round the chancery lanes from tavern to tavern, asking about a man who owed everyone and had stopped.

Ferra did not. She picked up her pencil, and turned to a clean page in her book, and wrote the date at the head of it, and nothing under it, and left it there.

Imani did not. She was looking at him the way she had looked at him across the table the night the school list came up, and it was not the look she gave a patient who was about to do something stupid. It was the other one, which he had seen perhaps twice, which she gave when she had already decided the stupid thing was the right one and was only waiting to see whether he knew it yet.

Armand did not. Armand sat in his chair with his hands folded on the table where they shook, as he had sat every night for three weeks, and for the first time in those three weeks he lifted his head and looked straight down the table at Emeric, and there was something in the old face that had not been there since the night of the letter. He did not say anything. He did not need to.

Ward, by the door, said, "She's asked."

It was the whole of what he said. Emeric understood it the way he understood most of what Ward said, which was entirely and a moment late. You're not sent for. That had been Ward's answer in the winter, and it had been right, and nobody had moved him off it. The king had said nobody would be sent for, and nobody had been, and the king had gone into a room so that nobody would need to be.

The king was still in the room. But somebody had asked.

It was not a writ. It was not the king's word. It would not stand up in any court in the realm, and if he rode north on it he would be breaking his father's order exactly as surely as if he had ridden north in the winter with Keller's letter in his coat. None of that had changed. Ward knew it had not changed. Ward had simply looked at the letter on the table, and at the four lines, and at the one at the bottom with a name put to it, and found that he no longer had an answer to it that he was willing to give.

Nobody argued for waiting.

Emeric sat at the table with his hand on the letter and waited a long time to be sure of it, and nobody did. The lamp burned. The dome over the court was dark. Somewhere down in the gatehouse a young man in grey with no badge was asleep on a bench by the fire, having ridden and walked seven days to put four lines into a stranger's hand, and would wake in the morning and ask, without being told anything, whether there was an answer.

"Saban," Emeric said.

"Yes."

"Think about it tonight. What you'd be looking for." He took his hand off the letter at last, and folded it along its own creases, and gave it to Ferra, who put it in the box without being asked. "Tomorrow we'll know what we're doing."

He went up to his rooms and did not sleep, and in the morning he went down to the gatehouse himself, before anybody else was up, and woke the man in grey, and gave him bread, and said that there was an answer, and that it was one word, and that he would not write it down.

Wald listened, and nodded, and did not ask what it meant, and went north on the first train.

The word was Yes.
