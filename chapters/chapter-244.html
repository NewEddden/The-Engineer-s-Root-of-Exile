Chapter 244 - The Dune Drum

He told her on the first of February, at nine at night, in the counting room, standing up, as she had asked.

"Tomorrow," he said. "Dusk, from the secret dock. The K Discovery. Fletcher at the stick, Ward, Linus, Paskin, Fennick and two of Fennick's bore crew. Me." He gave her the rest the way he would have given her a manifest. "Five hours down, under the Band. The gully by the lake. Paskin bores the first well the first night. Fennick's men stay on the ground in the gully under cloth for three days and nights and learn a rope rig for the second, and the ship comes home before light each morning and goes back each night with water and food and takes on what there is. Back for good on the morning of the sixth. Saturday."

Ferra wrote it. He watched her write it: the date, the place, the ship, the names, the nights, the day home. ISLE. 2 FEB, DUSK. BACK SAT 6, DAWN. Then she looked at the line for a while with her pencil above it.

"Weight," she said.

"What weight?"

"What you'll bring back. The ship can carry so much. I want to know what it is before you go, so that when you come back and say it was more, or less, I know which it was."

"A few hundredweight a night, once the drum's in. Nothing the first night. Nothing the second, probably. The third and the fourth, if the well runs."

"A few," said Ferra.

"Four. Six. I don't know how it runs. Nobody knows how it runs. It's never been drilled."

She wrote 4-6 CWT/NIGHT, NIGHTS 3-4, and a question mark, and drew a ring round the question mark so that it was a question she meant to have answered. Then she put the pencil down, parallel, and stood up and came round the table, and stood in front of him.

"Before," she said. "You said it. Thank you."

"I said I would."

"You've said a great many things you would." She put her hand flat on his chest, over his coat, where Imani's cup had been at breakfast a fortnight ago, and held it there, not counting, only holding. "This one you did."

Imani came up the stair at ten with a tin pail, and gave it to him, and a list.

The pail was new from the tinsmith's, with a lid that pressed on tight. The list was in her hard hand: the sap off the grey trees at the gully's lip, as much as the pail would hold; cut the bark slantwise, not round, so as not to ring the tree and kill it; leave each tree after a cup; keep it out of the sun; don't touch it with bare hands more than he had to, because nobody knew what it did to skin; and do not, in any circumstances, taste it.

"I wasn't going to taste it."

"You tasted the fever bark," said Imani. "Spring, you said. On the card. For the gloves." She put her finger on the pail's lid. "It's February."



The K Discovery went off the slip at the secret dock in the last of the light, with the lake grey and the reeds black and the far shore already gone, and climbed in the dark without a sound.

She was crowded. Seven men and the ship's own gear, and below decks in the hold the rest: Bram's pipe in short lengths, flanged and bolted, wrapped in sacking so that it would not ring; the parts of a rig that Bram had made in a week from a drawing, a sheave the size of a cartwheel and an iron bit as long as a man and as thick as his thigh and a heavy iron bar called the stem that went on top of it to give it weight; coils of new rope; picks and shovels and a sledge; casks of water, which on a desert island would be the first thing to run out and the last thing anybody could get more of; and four rolls of Abloh's cloth, dyed the colour of dry sand, enough to hide a camp in a gully from anybody who was not standing on its lip.

In the after locker, where they had travelled since the first voyage and had never yet been used, lay the diving suits. Fletcher had asked, packing, whether they need go. Emeric had said yes, and had not said why, and Fletcher had looked at him for a while and stowed them, and said that a ship was only as good as what she didn't need yet.

Linus Vance sat on a coil of rope against the hold's bulkhead with his survey case across his knees and his chain in a canvas bag at his feet, and watched the dark go past the port. He was sixty-five and had measured every road and ditch and field in four baronies with that chain, and had been up in the lift hull over the falls in the autumn, but never out of Randillia; and he sat with his case on his knees like a man on a cart going to market, and said nothing at all, and once, when the ship crossed the coast and the lights of a fishing town went by under them very small and far down, he leaned his forehead against the glass and kept it there for some time.

Paskin slept. He had never flown, and had said at the slip that if he was going to be frightened he would rather be asleep for it. Fennick did not sleep. He sat by the after hatch with his short fingers laced together and his two bore men on either side of him like bookends, and looked at the iron bit in its sacking with deep suspicion, as he looked at anything that had not been his idea.

"A rope and a bar," he said, to nobody.

"A rope and a bar," said Emeric.

"You lift the bar and you drop it."

"A cable tool, they call it. And it breaks the rock at the bottom of the hole a little at a time, and you turn it a little every time so it doesn't stick in its own groove, and every so often you pull it out and put a bucket down to bring up what it's broken. And you lift it and drop it again." He sat down on the deck by the hatch. "Men drilled for salt water that way for a thousand years, in the east of the world I read about, with bamboo and rope. A thousand feet. Some of them two."

"With an earth mage stood right there."

"Paskin's one man. There are two in Kaldo who can bore. If one of them's sick and the other's at the railhead, the rope still works."

Fennick looked at the iron bit again.

"Slow," he said.

"Slow."

"I'll have that bar down quicker than you think," said Fennick, and folded his arms, and was silent the rest of the way.



The island came up out of the dark at a little after one in the morning, as it had two winters before: pale sand and black rock and nothing; and far off at its north-eastern end, on the headland, the one yellow lamp on Kesrath's tower, high and steady, and below it now not a few small lights but a cluster of them, more than there had been, low down at the headland's foot inside the line of the old fort's walls, where Zale's chandler had said the well was.

Emeric stood at the forward port with his helm under his arm and looked at them.

"They've built since," said Ward, beside him.

"They've a well to work."

"They will have a man on that tower with a glass."

"In the dark. Looking at a desert. Two miles off, with no moon." Emeric put his helm on. "He'll see nothing he hasn't seen every night for sixty years."

Fletcher brought her down over the Claim with the steady clicking under his hand, down from two miles to one, to half, to a few hundred feet, until the pitch lake lay under them flat and black and round and the long low dome of the ground ran away from its south-western edge toward the sea; and held her there, dark, silent, hanging over the gully like a cloud nobody could see, while the steady kept her still against the night wind off the water.

Emeric and Ward went down first, as they had before, with the capes furled and their hearts lit.

It was the same drop and it was not the same at all. Two winters ago he had held the door open to Ward the whole way down and come down on the rock at the lake's edge like a sack of meal, with his knees jarred and his teeth together, a breath before Ward came down beside him without a sound. He had flown the suit since more times than he could count. He had flown it in the war and in the snow and over the Band's foot with Ward on his wing, and in the last year he had put into his legs what had taken Rowena two, a morning at a time, with the barbell and Ward counting; and now, when the rock came up out of the dark at him in the overlay, the pale square of it marked where he had marked it himself two years ago, he did not brace for it. He let the cape's air take the last of his weight and turned his heel, and touched.

He came down so lightly that he did not hear himself land.

Ward came down a breath behind him, on the same rock, as he always did, without a sound.

Almost without a sound. There was one: the small hard click of a boot sole meeting stone, the one Emeric had heard every time Ward landed since the first suit, and never once made himself.

Ward stood up straight and looked at the rock under Emeric's boots, and then at Emeric, through the helm's slit, for some time.

"Hm," said Ward.

It was all he said. It was more than he had said about anything Emeric had done with his feet since the road south.



They lowered the rest down on lines.

Fletcher brought the ship lower still, to a hundred feet over the gully's lip, and held her there while the crew paid out the hold's gear on the big hoist in the dark: the pipe in its sacking, the rig's parts, the water casks, the cloth, the tools; and then the men, one at a time, in the bosun's chair, Paskin first, sitting in it with his hands folded on his stomach as if he were going down a well to look at a pump, and Linus last, with his case clutched to his chest and his eyes shut, who opened them at the bottom and looked round at the desert in the starlight and said, quite quietly, "Well," and then nothing else for a quarter of an hour.

Ward set the camp. He chose the place himself, at the gully's head where the rock walls were highest and an overhang of stone made a roof over a floor of hard sand ten paces long; and Abloh's cloth went up over the gully's mouth on poles and pegs, and was weighted at its edges with sand, so that from the lip a man saw a fold in the desert with sand at its bottom and nothing else. The water casks went in under the overhang. Fennick's two bore men, who had never been further from Kaldo than Grayrock, stood in the middle of the camp in the dark with their hands at their sides, as men stand in a church in a strange town, and were set to work by Ward before they could think about it.

Then Emeric took Paskin down to the bottom of the gully, to the pool the size of a washing tub with the stars in it, and the smell coming up off it; and showed him the ground.



Paskin had been a quarryman's son in the north before he was a mage, and he looked at ground the way Bram looked at iron: as a thing that had already decided what it would and would not let you do, and could be argued with only so far.

He knelt on the sand at the pool's edge and put both hands flat on it, and shut his eyes.

"Sand," he said, after a while. "Ten feet. Twelve. Then rock, soft, a sandstone, broken, wet with the stuff all the way down; that's where it's coming up through. Then harder." He opened his eyes. "How far down do you want me?"

"Till you're through the hard and into where it's lying. Under the dome. It'll be in the top of it, under a cap of rock it can't get through."

"How far's that?"

"I don't know. Sixty feet. A hundred. Less, here, at the edge, where it's leaking."

Paskin grunted, and put his hands back on the sand.

The glyph that came up off him was the brown of turned earth, broad and plain, and went down into the ground under his palms like water into a sponge; and the sand in front of his knees began to move.

It did not dig. Nothing was thrown out. The sand simply drew back from a place, the size of a man's two fists together, as if a finger were pressed into it from below; drew back, and kept drawing back, down and down, a round smooth-walled hole going into the ground in the starlight with the sand of its walls packed so hard it shone. Paskin's breath came slowly and evenly. At twelve feet the hole met the rock and slowed, and Emeric, crouched beside him with his sense down the hole, felt the rock resist: not refuse, as stone refused him when he tried to push it, but give unwillingly, a little at a time, as a knot in a plank gives to a chisel. Paskin's shoulders came up. The brown glyph went darker on his hands.

"Bram's pipe," said Paskin, without opening his eyes. "Now. Before the sand comes back in on me."

They fed the pipe down the hole in its flanged lengths, Fennick's men bolting each to the next on the sand by feel in the dark and lowering it, so that the sand walls had a sleeve of iron inside them and could not fall back; and Paskin went on down through the rock below it, slower, and slower.

At forty feet he stopped and sat back on his heels and wiped his face.

"Your turn," he said.

Emeric looked at him.

"You've been feeling down the hole beside me the whole hour. I can feel you doing it. It's like having a man breathe on the back of your neck." Paskin held out his hand, palm down, over the pipe's mouth. "Go on. You'll be bad at it. Do the sand first, at the side here, not the rock. The rock you'll not shift."

Earth was not his. Nothing was his but fire and weight, and everything else cost him three times what it cost the people it belonged to; he had stood soaked to the skin at the castle well a fortnight ago learning that again. He put his hand flat on the sand beside the pipe, where Paskin pointed.

The sand came. It came so easily that he nearly laughed: a hand's width of it drawing back under his palm, packing itself hard at the sides as he had seen Paskin's do, as if the sand had only been waiting for somebody to ask. Loose grains, lying on each other with nothing between them but air; there was nothing to fight. He pressed down and it went down. He felt how it packed under the pressure, the grains locking on each other, the sand at the hole's walls going hard as fired brick under his attention; and thought, for no reason he could see at the time, of a soft beach, and a boot sinking in it to the ankle, and how a man who could do this could make the ground under his own feet as hard as a road at every step.

Then he reached down the pipe to the rock, at the bottom, forty feet down, and asked it.

The rock did not move.

It did not refuse him, exactly. It was there, and his asking went into it as rain goes into a roof, and nothing at all happened; and he pushed, harder, with the three times on top of it, and felt something give, at the very bottom, a crumb's worth, a single grain of sandstone come loose from the rest and lie on the bottom of the hole. His head began to ache. He took his hand back.

Paskin was watching him with an expression of great satisfaction.

"One," said Paskin.

"One what?"

"One grain. I counted." Paskin put his own hands back over the pipe. "Sand's anybody's. Sand's barely there. Rock's somebody's. It's been rock for longer than anybody's been anything, and it doesn't want to be asked." The brown glyph came up off his palms again. "That's why the tower takes thirty years to make an earth mage and three to make a fire one."

At eighty feet, a little before four in the morning, the bottom of the hole went soft under him.

Paskin felt it before Emeric did. He jerked his hands back off the pipe as if it had bitten him, and sat down hard on the sand; and up the pipe from eighty feet down came a sound like a man breathing out through his teeth, long and low, and then a smell, so strong and sudden that Fennick's bore men stepped back to the gully's wall: eggs, and something sweet, and something under both of them that caught at the back of the throat like smoke. Then a gurgle. Then, slowly, so slowly that at first it was only a darkening of the iron at the pipe's lip in the starlight, the oil came up.

It did not gush. It was not under that kind of pressure; the dome had been leaking at its edge for longer than there had been kingdoms, and had very little left to push with. It rose in the pipe, and stood at the lip, and spilled over, a slow dark brimming that ran down the iron's side onto the packed sand and lay there shining in the starlight, and went on coming, a thin steady spill, like a spring in a hillside.

Nobody said anything. Then Fennick, at the gully's wall, said a word that Emeric had not heard him use since the fifth fuse went early, and said it again, with feeling.

"A night," said Fennick. "One night. And a rope and a bar."

"The rope and the bar don't need Paskin," said Emeric.

"They need four nights."

"Yes."

Fennick looked at the oil, coming up out of the pipe in the dark. Then at Paskin, sitting on the sand with his hands in his lap and his face grey and his eyes shut, too tired to look at anything. Then at his own two men, who could not bore a hole in anything without powder, and never would.

"Four nights," he said. "We'll see."



The gas came up with it. It always did; there was no oil anywhere in any world that did not come up out of the ground with its gas, the light end of it, too light to stay liquid, that had lain over the oil under the dome's cap like air in the top of a bottle.

It came hissing up the pipe in breaths, all the rest of that night, between the slow pulses of oil, and he could smell it in the gully and feel it in the air at the back of his throat, and he knew what it would do if it gathered in the gully's low places under Abloh's cloth and somebody struck a light.

So before dawn, before the ship came down for them, he capped the pipe.

A flange cap of Bram's with a short side arm, a tap, and a length of iron pipe running off the side arm along the gully's floor and up its wall, under the sand, to a pit forty yards off in the lee of a dune, which Fennick's men dug for him in the last hour of the dark. In the pit he put the thing he had brought for it: a pot still, Bram's, a fat copper belly with a long neck and a coiled worm, the twin of the ones in the still house at Kaldo, which Bram had made before midwinter because Emeric had asked for it and had not asked why. The gas went down the pipe under the sand into a burner of punched iron under the still's belly, and burned there, in the bottom of a pit, under a lid, with the flame a hand's breadth high and blue and no light at all coming up out of the sand.

It was a waste. It was a fortune in gas, burning in a hole to boil oil, on a desert, because there was no way on earth to carry it home in secret in a ship and no way to leave it in the gully without killing somebody. But it would boil the oil. The still would take the first of what came up the pipe and send off the light part, the lamp oil, the spirit, clean, into its worm and out into a cask; and what was left in the copper belly could go into the drum heavy, for Tess and the plant at home. And the burner would never show above the sand, and the gas would never gather in the gully.

"It's a still," said Ward, looking down into the pit in the grey light.

"It's a still."

"You have built a still house on the king's island."

"A small one."

Ward looked at it for some time.

"Bram," he said. "Before midwinter. He asked me what you wanted it for." He looked back toward the gully. "I told him I did not know. I told him I did not want to know." He turned away from the pit. "I still do not."



The ship came home before the light each morning and went back down each night, and the gully lived under Abloh's cloth through the days.

The days were the worst of it. The sun came up over the island white and hard even in February and lay on the sand like a hand on a stove, and under the cloth the air was hot and still and stank of oil and eggs and four men who could not wash; and they lay in the shade of the overhang and slept when they could and drank the water by Ward's measure, which was less than any of them wanted, and did not go out from under the cloth for anything. Twice in three days a patrol went by: Randillian soldiers from the Claim's own fort at the south end, three men on foot with a mule, walking the line of the lake's southern edge as they had walked it for sixty years, a long way off, never coming near the dome, never looking at it, because why would they. Ward watched them through a gap in the cloth both times with his hand on the hilt and his face like wood until they were out of sight.

The nights were work.

Fennick's rig went up the first night after the well: three poles lashed at the top into a tripod over a mark twenty paces down the gully from Paskin's pipe, the cartwheel sheave hung at the top, and the rope over the sheave, with the iron bit and the heavy stem on the hole end of it and a springy ash pole bolted across the tripod's leg on the other, so that a man stood on a little step and kicked down on the pole, and the pole lifted the bit, and he let it go, and the bit dropped. Thump. Lift. Thump. A quarter turn of the rope between each drop so that the bit's chisel edge came down on fresh rock. Every half hour the bit came up on the rope and a long tin bucket with a flap in its bottom went down instead and came up full of grey slurry, and was emptied, and the bit went down again.

Fennick hated it. He hated it all of the first night, and said so, at length, to the rope, and to the bit, and to the tripod, and to his two men, who took turns on the pole and did not say anything back because they were too tired. He hated it until about two in the morning of the second night, when the bit went through the sand into the rock, and he stood beside the hole with his hand on the rope and felt the rock come up the rope into his hand at every drop: the bit striking, and the jar of it, and the way the jar changed when the rock changed. A shale; then a harder band; then soft again. He stood with his hand on it for a long while and stopped talking.

"You can feel it," he said at last.

"Through the rope," said Emeric.

"You can feel what's down there. Every foot of it." Fennick's short fingers moved on the rope as the bit came up and fell. "Better than a powder hole. A powder hole, you guess. This, you know."

By the third night the rig was down sixty feet and Fennick's men had stopped needing to be told when to turn the rope and when to bail; and Fennick had stopped saying that it was slow, and had started telling Paskin, who was resting on the sand with his back to the rock and his eyes shut, exactly what band of stone the bit was going through, a little before Paskin could have told him.

Paskin did not open his eyes.

"A rope and a bar," he said.

"A rope and a bar," said Fennick, with great dignity, "and a man who knows what to do with them."



The drum went in on the second night, under the dune by the still.

It had to be big, and it had to be invisible, and it had to hold oil for a week between runs without leaking it into the sand where a man might smell it a mile off; and it had to be filled from the well and emptied into the ship in the dark in an hour. So it was a pit, twelve feet across and eight deep, dug into the lee of the dune where the wind would always blow fresh sand back over its lid; and it was lined with pitch.

That was the joke of it, and he did not tell it to anybody but Ward: that the pitch lake, which everybody in two kingdoms had known about for sixty years and nobody took, lay a quarter of a mile away across the rock, and Fennick's men went over to its edge in the dark with buckets and the sledge and broke lumps off its crust where it was warm, and carried them back, and Emeric melted them in a pan over the still's burner and they painted it on the pit's walls and floor in coats, thick, black, stinking, until the pit was a pitch-lined bowl that would hold water, or oil, as tight as any cask. A lid of planks over it, pitched too. A hatch in the lid. A pipe under the sand from the well's side arm to the hatch, and another from the hatch to the gully's mouth, where the ship's hose would come down on its line at night and be made fast and the ship's own pump would draw it up.

The dune drum, Fennick's men called it, by the third night, and the name stuck.

It was digging the drum's pit that he found the crust.

The bore men had gone down four feet through dune sand when the shovel struck something that was not sand; a layer of something white and hard and crumbly, a hand thick, lying under the dune like a frost under snow. One of the men threw it out with the rest. Emeric saw it go by in the starlight, white on the dark heap, and picked a piece up off the heap and turned it over in his fingers.

It broke like old plaster. It was cold on his tongue when he touched it there, cold the way a thing is cold that takes heat out of you rather than a thing that is cold already, and salty and bitter at once, with something else behind the bitter that made his tongue go numb at the tip.

He held his finger and thumb a little apart, and let the smallest thread of fire come up between them, and put the crumb of white stuff in it.

It flared. Not much; a spit, a sputter, a little violet-white hiss and a puff of smoke that smelled sharp; the flame went brighter round it for a breath, as a fire goes brighter when somebody blows on it, and settled.

Saltpetre.

A desert in a sea, rainless, with a dry wind coming off it that never let anything wash away: the salts in the ground came up with what little water there was, and dried, and stayed, and lay under the sand in a crust for ten thousand years. On his other world there had been a desert like that on the far side of an ocean, a narrow one, between mountains and the sea, so dry that nothing rotted in it; and its crust had been worth so much that three countries had gone to war over it, in the century before he was born, and men had dug it by the shipload for a hundred years to make powder for every army in the world and to grow wheat in every field.

Kaldo's powder came out of the scrapings of old mine workings, and the floors of byres, and the walls of dovecotes, a little at a time, under a licence the crown had put on it after the war. Milford's fields wanted feeding faster than its middens could feed them.

And under his feet was a white crust a hand thick, under a dune, under a quarter mile of desert, on a king's island, that nobody had ever dug.

"What is it?" said Ward.

"Powder," said Emeric. "And bread." He put the piece in his pocket. "And one more thing nobody can know we have."

Ward looked at the white heap on the dark, and at the dune, and at the long low country beyond it going away pale under the stars.

"How much?" he said.

"I don't know. Linus will know, by Saturday."



Linus walked the Claim.

He did it in the dark, all three nights, with his chain and a shuttered lantern and Fennick's second man to hold the chain's far end, from the gully south to the Claim's fort and west to the cliffs and east as far as the lake's middle, and back; and in the days, under the cloth, with the sun beating on it, he drew. He drew the way Emeric had seen him draw every field in four baronies, slowly, with a ruler and a set of fine pens and his tongue between his teeth, on a sheet pinned to a board across his knees; and the island came up out of his sheet the way the margins of Vey's book had come up out of Zale's ear, a line at a time.

The lake. The dome, with its contours drawn round it like the rings in a cut log. The gully, the well, the rig, the drum; Linus marked them with small letters and a key in the corner that said only WORKS. The patrol's line along the lake's south edge, a dotted line, with the times. The nitrate crust, wherever the bore men's spade or his own chain's pin had found it, a little shaded mark each time, until there were so many little shaded marks across the sheet's southern half that he stopped making them and shaded the whole of it with fine hatching, and wrote in the corner, NITRE, AT LEAST THIS.

The rest of the island he did not draw.

He drew its edges, from what could be seen from the Claim at night and from the ship's port coming in, and he drew the line of the shard fields to the north under the tower, glittering faintly, as far as the eye went. But the Kesrath end, and the country north of the lake, and the long run of the western cliffs past the point where the Claim's chain could reach, and the sea off them, he left blank. White paper, with a ruled border round it. And across each blank, in his small upright surveyor's hand, a single word.

UNWALKED.

"You could guess," said Emeric, on the third day, under the cloth, looking at it.

"I could," said Linus. "And somebody would build on my guess one day, and the guess would be wrong, and something would fall down." He capped his pen. "A survey that says what it doesn't know is worth ten that don't."

He had never said so much in one breath in all the years Emeric had known him.



On the third night, before the drum was full, he did two more things.

The first was the sand. Up at the far end of the dome, on the seaward side, where the dunes ran down toward the beach, the sand changed: it went from the warm grey-gold of the desert to white. Pure white, fine, so white that even under the stars it shone, as if a strip of the coast had been bleached. He scooped it up in his palm and let it run out through his fingers, and felt it with his sense: nothing in it. No iron, no clay, no shell, nothing but the one clean stone ground to flour by the sea for longer than there had been anything else. Ivo had been asking him for two years for a sand without iron in it, because iron put a green into his glass that he could not get out, and every lens he ground had a ghost of green at its edge. Emeric filled a sack with it, and tied the sack, and wrote IVO on its label.

The second was the trees.

They grew where he remembered them, along the gully's lip in the cracks of the rock, small and gnarled and grey, chest high, with their sage-green leaves rustling in the night wind off the sea. He went along them with Imani's pail and Imani's list, and a knife, and did it the way the list said: a slant cut in the bark of each, not round, never round, and the pail held under the cut, and the white sap beading out of the cut and running down the bark into the tin in a slow thread. A cup from each tree, no more. Then the next.

It took him two hours to fill it, alone, along the gully's lip in the dark with the oil smell coming up from below, and his fingers were white and sticky to the knuckles and stank of something green and sharp that did not wash off; and he did not taste it.

When the pail was full he pressed its lid on hard and sat with it on the rock at the gully's lip and looked down at the camp.

Kaldo had leather for its pumps and felt and pitch for its ship's seals, and Tess's wires were sheathed in waxed cotton that cracked in the cold. Imani cut into people with her bare hands, and washed them afterward in carbolic until the skin of her fingers split, and nobody in the world had ever made her anything to put between her hands and the wound. He had promised her a pair, on a card, for the spring. This was the spring. It was a pail.



The drum filled on the fourth night, the fifth of February, a little after midnight.

Paskin's well had been running the whole time into it through the pipe under the sand, slow and steady, the thin dark spill of a spring in a hillside, and the still had been taking the first of it and boiling it down over the buried blue flame; and when Emeric lifted the hatch in the drum's lid and put his lantern's slit to the gap, there was oil in the pit almost to the hatch's lip, black, shining, still, with the lantern's thin line of light lying on it.

He knelt at the hatch and put his hand into it.

He did not need to. It was the first time in his life, in either life, that he had put his hand into a pit of crude oil that he had brought up out of the ground himself, and he wanted to. It was cold and thin and slick, lighter than he had expected, and it closed round his fingers and slid off them when he took them out, and left them dark to the wrist, and shining, smelling of eggs and something sweet.

Ward was standing behind him in the dark.

"Every stone needs a floor under it," said Emeric, without turning round.

Ward said nothing.

"Everything we've built that runs on a stone, I've built something under it that runs without one. The lamps have the dynamo. The pumps have the steam. The ship has the sweeps and the lugsail stowed flat on the Hod's deck so that if every stone aboard her dies she's still a boat." He looked at his hand. "The ships don't have a floor. Not the big ones. Not the ones I'm going to build. The air crystals push them, and the stones lift them, and if the stones die over the sea, they're a hull in the water with a sail if they're lucky and nothing if they're not."

"Steam," said Ward.

"Steam's a fire and a boiler and a coal bunker the size of a house. On a ship that has to fly, that's the ship. You can't lift it." He turned his hand over in the lantern's thin light and looked at the oil running down into the cuff of his glove. "This, you put in an engine. No boiler. No fire, either, not as you'd know it; you squeeze the air in the engine so hard it gets hot, hotter than a forge, and squirt this into it, and it lights itself. No spark. Nothing to fail. It goes on as long as you feed it this, and it'll take a ship across the sea on a few casks." He looked at the pit. "An oil engine. Nobody needs it yet. Not one ship we have needs it. But the day they need it, it'll be too late to start."

Ward was quiet for some time.

"And when the stones die over the sea," he said at last, "this engine is still going."

"Yes."

"Then it is the same as the lugsail."

"It's the same as the lugsail."

Ward nodded, once, as if that settled it, and turned away up the gully to see to the hose.

They drew the drum into the ship that night on the long hose, the K Discovery hanging fifty feet over the gully's mouth in the dark and the ship's pump thudding in her hold, and the oil going up the hose out of the pit in the dune; and it came to five hundredweight and a little over, by the gauge on the ship's tank, which was within Ferra's ring round her question mark, and Emeric was glad of it in a way he would not have admitted to anybody.



Fennick's bit went into the oil on the last night, at a hundred and ten feet, a little after two in the morning.

It did not come up the rope into his hand the way the rock had. It went soft. The bit dropped and did not ring, and Fennick, at the rope, stood very still, and then pulled it up, slowly, hand over hand, a hundred feet of wet rope coming up over the sheave in the starlight; and the bit came up out of the hole black from its point to the stem.

Fennick held the lantern's slit to it and looked at it for some time.

Then he looked at Paskin.

"Four nights," said Fennick.

"Four nights," said Paskin.

"A rope and a bar." Fennick wiped his short black fingers on his trousers. "And when you're at your railhead, and the other one's sick, and Kaldo wants a second well in a hurry, it'll be me." He looked at his two men, grey with dust and black to the elbows with oil, standing by the tripod. "Us."

"It'll be you," said Emeric.

Fennick did not say anything to that. He did not have to. He went to the tripod and began, himself, with his own hands, to cap the second well the way he had watched Emeric cap the first, flange and side arm and tap, and did not ask anybody how.



They struck the camp before dawn on the sixth. The cloth came down and was rolled; the sand in the gully was raked smooth and swept with a branch of the grey trees till the wind could finish it. The wells were capped and their caps buried a foot under the sand, with a stone at each that only Linus's sheet would ever find again. The still's pit was closed, the burner left in it on its lowest, so that the gas would burn away under the sand where it came up and not gather; the drum's hatch was buried under the dune's slope with the wind already moving fresh sand across it. Linus walked the whole of the gully once with his lantern and said that he could see nothing, and that he had been looking at ground for forty years.

They came up into the ship on the lines, one at a time, Paskin asleep in the bosun's chair halfway up with his hands folded on his stomach.

The ship climbed into the last of the dark, and turned north over the island for home.

And as she turned, with the sky going grey in the east, Emeric at the forward port saw the inland country for the first time from above in the light: north and west of the dome, beyond the lake, out where Linus's sheet said UNWALKED. A long flat white country, low down in a bowl of the hills, white from edge to edge, so white that it caught the dawn before anything else on the island did and lay there shining, flat as a floor. Not sand. Salt. A dry lake, a sea that had come in and gone out ten thousand times and left its salt behind every time in a crust over the bowl's floor, white as the nitre, flat as a table.

He knew what was in a flat like that. Not the salt. Lithium. Under it, in the brine between the crusts, in the dry places of his other world where the rain never came. A metal so light it floated on oil, so eager it burned in water, that every battery he had ever carried in his pocket on his other world had been made with; that would have let Tess put a year of the dynamo's work into a box the size of a book.

He looked at it until the ship's turn took it behind the curve of the hills.

He did not say anything. There was nothing in the world yet that could use it, and nobody in the world yet who would know what it was, and a white flat on an island two crowns were bleeding over was the worst place he could think of to have to explain to anybody; and there was, besides, the matter of how much a man could carry home from one island in one week and still be the sort of man who could stand in front of Ferra and say what he had brought. He left it in the ground.

Nobody aboard asked him what he was looking at. Linus had his eyes shut. Paskin was asleep. Fennick was asleep sitting up, with his arms folded and his chin on his chest and black to the elbows, and his two men asleep against him on either side like bookends that had fallen over.

Ward was at the after port, watching the island go.

Emeric sat down at the chart table under the lamp with his hands still faintly black at the nails from the drum, and took a clean sheet from the drawer, and a pencil, and began to draw an engine. A cylinder. A piston. The space at the top of the stroke, small, very small, where the air would be squeezed until it was hotter than a forge. The little nozzle in the head.

On his other world it had carried the name of the man who built the first one, and he gave it that name in his head as he drew, a diesel, though to Ward he had called it an oil engine and would go on calling it that. He drew until the coast came up under them in the full light, and then went on.
