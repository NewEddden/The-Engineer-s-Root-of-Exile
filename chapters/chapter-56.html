Chapter 56 - The Deep Channel

The hatch went back again before anybody had answered the boy, and a man came down the ladder in steel, too fast, and missed the last rung.

"I am here," Rylan said. "Finn. I am here. Put your head down."

They had set Emeric against the curve of the hull with his shoulder on a rib and his hands behind him, and somewhere to his left, close, a boy was breathing in the particular way of a child who has been crying for hours and has stopped because he was told to.

Two more came down after Rylan. Emeric heard their feet on the rungs, and the feet were wrapped, the same muffled weight that had come up off the ledge at him a few minutes before. One of them stopped at the foot of the ladder. The other went on to the far end of the hold with a lantern that had three of its sides shuttered, because the light through the hood came and went as the man moved and never came from more than one direction.

They tied Rylan where he sat. Emeric listened to it being done and heard what it was: a wrap and a knot at the wrists over the steel and nothing at the elbows, a rope put on a man who has already been used and is not going to be needed again.

He had heard them take the sword off him on the deck above, the belt and the scabbard going down onto planks. They had left him his harness. The man with the lantern sat down on something at the far end, and it was close enough to the boy that Emeric heard Finn stop breathing for a moment when it creaked.

Then nobody spoke for a while except Rylan, who talked to his son in a low flat voice about nothing at all. The cold. The boat. That it would not be long now. That he would be back at his aunt's before it was light and Una would have the porridge on, and when the cough was done he would come home on the cart the way he had gone and walk up the lane, and his mother would have the fire in. That he was to keep his coat buttoned, which was a thing he had been told at the Western Gate on the morning he left by every adult who could reach him, and was apparently going to be told for the rest of his life.

Emeric listened to the voice rather than the words. It was steady all the way through and it did not vary once, and that was a piece of work he had seen men fail at in better circumstances.

He listened to the words afterward, because they were the terms. A boy put back at his aunt's in Milford as if he had never left it. A cart home. A story in which Finn had been breathing dry air a day west the whole time, which was the story the barony had been told at the brazier and in the lane, and was still telling itself tonight. Rylan had been sold that, and had paid for it with a bar and a word, and he was telling it to his son in the dark so that it would stay true for one of them.

The two guards talked to each other over their heads, the way men talk in front of a hooded prisoner they have been told does not matter. The cold. The wait. That there was no sense in any of it, and the whole night could have been done at the stair.

"Marrow wants us down here till he whistles," the one by the ladder said. "So we are down here till he whistles."

Emeric put the name where he put things, which was next to the voice from the ledge, and did nothing else with it.

He counted instead, because counting was the only instrument he had.

Four with wrapped boots had come up off the ledge at him. The voice made five, and the voice had never come up the stair at all. Two of the wrapped pairs were down here now. On the deck he could hear the other two, and the voice when it moved, which was seldom and without hurry, and a man forward with a pole, and two oars that did not sound like one man's hands. Six at the most, five if the pole had gone to an oar. He wrote it down as six with a question beside it, which is how an honest man writes a number he has not measured.

The hull moved.

Poles first, short pushes, the shaft knocking the gunwale twice. Then oars, and not many strokes of them. Then nothing, and a man forward calling something in one syllable, and the anchor going over.

The rope ran out.

Emeric counted it, because he counted things. It ran, and kept running, and went on running long past the length of any water Kaldo's boats normally sat in, and somewhere in the middle of it Rylan stopped talking to his son in the middle of a word.

Rylan had grown up two streets from that ledge. He had said so the autumn before last with his hand flat on the wall by the water gate: that boys used to go off it before the bar, straight down, because it was as deep as anything in the barony under there, and came up twenty yards along if the water was moving. He did not need to count the rope. He had been to the bottom of it.

When it stopped, Emeric had a number in his head that meant one place and only one place. They were in the channel. The deep one, off the blind run, under the sixty yards of wall he had stood on for four nights that same autumn, working out what a man with a boat could do there.

He did the rest of it sitting against the rib, and it took about as long as it takes to read a column down.

A boat does not anchor to deliver anything. It anchors to stay where it is, or to leave something where it is. When the hatch had gone back for Rylan, no light had come down with him, and there was no light in the seams now anywhere except the shuttered lantern at the far end. The lamps on that deck were out, on a night with no moon, under the one stretch of wall the keep could not see. And six men, or five, on an open deck on the coldest night of the winter, on a boat that was going nowhere, and not one of them had come below out of the wind. They were not sitting to oars. They were walking. They were busy.

And the boy was here.

That was the one that settled it. Nobody takes a boy home to his aunt in Milford by way of the deepest water in the barony. If there had ever been a cart, the boy would have been on it, a day west, where there was nobody to see him but Una. He was in the bottom of this boat with his father and the man his father had been bought to deliver, because this was the one place in Kaldo where all three of them could be put at once and never looked for.

Two feet to his left, Rylan's breathing changed.

He had got there too. He had got there at the same moment and off the same rope, and he did not say anything, and when he took up with Finn again his voice was exactly as steady as it had been. Only the breathing under it had gone.

Then the smell changed.

It came down through the hatch seams and through the boards, over the pitch and the bilge, unmistakable and generous. Lamp oil. A great deal of it, being poured on a deck by somebody who was not being careful about the corners.

Nobody oils a deck he means to sail home.

A prince vanishes on a black night. The guard who sat his board vanishes with him. The guard's son, who everybody in Kaldo has been told is at his aunt's in Milford, vanishes the same night. And in the morning the watch finds the water gate open with the bar standing tidy against the wall, off its pegs, from the inside.

The story wrote itself. It would write itself in the hall by noon and be in the capital inside the week, and every name in it would be Rylan's, and whoever had paid for it would not appear in it anywhere.

Rylan had been walked along a road with his son held at the far end of it, and the road ended here, over the deepest water in the barony, with all three of them in the bottom of a boat that was about to burn.

Emeric went through what he had, once, properly, the way he would have gone through it on paper.

He had a hood on. In all the years he had been drilling he had never once worked at anything he could not see. Not in the delve, not in a courtyard, not in a lane with an army in it; every drill he had built started with his eyes on the thing he meant to move. The only one of the three with force in it was a blade of moving air, and a blade he could not see, in a hold six feet across, went into whatever was nearest, and whatever was nearest was ten years old.

There was no stone underfoot. There were planks and a foot of filthy water.

His mother's stone was in the inside pocket of his coat, four inches from his ribs and as far out of reach as the capital. The delve wanted it in his hand, and his hands were tied behind him, and a man in the delve sat like a man in the middle of a stroke for as long as he was in there, which was a thing to do behind a barred door and not in front of two guards.

That left the one thing he could reach with his hands tied, and he went for it because it was there.

A stone that has been learned by hand can be spoken to at a distance. He had said so himself at the gatehouse brazier, over four nights of good questions, to the man now sitting two feet away in a loose rope. The gate stone behind the backing of the board was his in the way a dog is somebody's, and he reached for it the way a man whistles for one, and it came up in him at once, slow and heavy and entirely unsurprised.

Through it, the ring. Eleven fields lying quiet along the wall. And nine, somewhere up and back toward the stair, doing what it had been doing for an hour: straining at a stone on a cord and letting go of it and straining again, telling the card to wake a man who was sitting in the bottom of a boat.

He could wake every lamp on that board. He could throw every latch on the farm main. That was the whole of what he had. He had built every stone on that wall to hold one small steady push against the air in front of it and do nothing else, as shallow as a working can be made, because a shallow working was cheap and a cheap working could be swapped by a boy out of a box. There was nothing on the ring that could push a man over, or stop his heart. He had seen to that himself, on purpose.

And the lamps would light in an empty room. He had walked through it an hour ago. The chair was empty and the brazier was going out, and Hollis was not on his way down, because the only man who had said so was the one who had lifted the bar. Ward had written theatre on a slip of paper and would come back when he had decided the play was not for him, in his own time, and whatever the board had to say by then, a burning boat would be saying louder to the whole east side of the town.

The only thing in him that had ever reached inside a living creature needed a hand on it. He had found the fear in a bird with a broken wing that way, and since then in Imani's ward, and he had only ever learned how to ease it. He noticed, in the place where he kept that kind of note, that tonight he would have tried it the other way without a moment's hesitation if he had had a hand free and a man within reach of it. He noticed that he did not mind noticing.

He let the ring go. What he did not let go of was the reach.

It had never had a better name in his head than the pull, and had never needed one: the line he ran out from the middle of his attention to a stone that knew him. With the gate stone gone off the end of it, it did what a hand does on a dark stair when the rail runs out. It went looking for the next thing to hold.

It went looking for stone, because every working he had ever done had started with one.

There was none. Pitched oak, bilge, a coil of wet rope, a keel, and under the keel the whole depth of the channel, and at the bottom of that a bed of rock he could not have reached from a dry floor with his hand flat on it. The pull went down through all of it and found nothing to close on, and did not stop.

It went into whatever a stone sits in.

He had never felt that on its own. A stone in the hand is loud, the way a lamp in a room is bright, and whatever the room is made of never gets its turn. He had only ever known it by its absence: on frozen mud below the east wall, when a page of Armand's came true under his boots, and in the hole it left at the lane. He had never once felt it there.

It was there. Thin and even and everywhere, through the planks and the bilge and the black air over the deck, lying the way the fields of the ring lay when nothing was in them, like still water in a basin.

It was not still everywhere.

Two feet to his left it thickened. Something sat in it that the pull had to go round rather than through, the way a hand in a basin has to go round a cup, and whatever it was had weight in it, and moved all the time without going anywhere: the small endless restlessness of breath and blood that a stone never has and a living thing never loses.

Rylan.

He knew what he was doing before he had finished doing it, because he had built a ring of stones on it. Put a body in front of a stone that is already pushing, something with weight in it and blood in it, and the push costs more. The stone strains. The strain is the message. He had been holding the pull out into the dark the way a node holds its push against the air, and the dark was telling him what it cost.

He was the stone.

It was nothing like seeing. The word that came up for it came from the other life, from a picture in a book of a ship in black water sending a sound down and listening for what came back. That was wrong too. It was nearer than anything Randillia had a word for, and he did not have time to find a better one.

He went round the hold with it, slowly, because he did not trust it and wanted to see whether it would lie.

Rylan, big, very still, the stillness of a man holding himself still on purpose. Against him and a little beyond, small, everything in it running fast, Finn. At the foot of the ladder a man sitting low with his weight tipped forward. At the far end a man on something that put him no higher than the boy, close enough that the two shapes nearly touched, and when the lantern light moved through the hood the far shape had moved with it.

The hold itself was in there too, if he held still and looked for it: the curve of the hull, the ladder, the crate, all of it flat and motionless and faint, fainter than the men by as much as a candle is fainter than a forge. He let it go. The living things were so much louder that the rest was not worth what it would have cost to hold.

So that was the leash. The rope on Rylan's wrists was for form; a man in charged harness could have parted it like thread. What held him was a man on a crate within a lunge of a ten-year-old, and Rylan had been sitting inside that arithmetic since the hatch closed, and had not moved a finger, and would not.

He went up.

The planks were almost nothing to it, a thin flat layer the pull went through without stopping. The deck was a sheet of the same thin stillness with shapes standing in it, and he counted them the way he counted anything, twice, and got seven both times. His ears had given him six at the most. The seventh was at the stern, low, and had not moved since the anchor went over and had not made a sound, and would not have been in any count a man could make with a bag on his head.

Every one of them had a faint grain to it once he looked, the way every plank has a grain and no two the same. One of them had more than that. He found it because it was different, the way he had found the fear in the bird because it was the loudest thing there. Of seven men on that deck it was the only one that did not sound like the rest.

It had a beat in it. Slow and even and underneath everything, the way two strings tuned a hair apart will beat when they are struck together, rising and falling against each other on a rhythm that neither of them has alone. He did not know what it was. He did not know whether it belonged to the man or to something the man had on him. He knew that he would know it again.

It moved forward along the deck, unhurried, and stopped over the hatch, close enough that the planks above Emeric's head were all that lay between them.

"Along the coaming," the level voice said. "All of it."

It put its weight on no word. The beat did not change while it spoke, or after.

Emeric set it down beside the other two: a name a guard had given away, a voice off a ledge, and now this. Three entries that agree about one man are the beginning of a column. The shape moved aft again, and the smell of oil came down thick through the boards along the line of the hatch, exactly where he had just been told it was going.

The pull was costing him. It cost the way a long evening in the delve cost, in attention, and he could feel the bill running and had no idea what the total would be. He kept it open anyway.

He leaned his head over until the cloth touched Rylan's shoulder and spoke under his breath.

He had grown up in a house where a thing said at the volume of a room was for the room and a thing said under the breath was true, and he had never once in his life given a plan he meant in any other register.

"Your hands."

"Out," Rylan said, in the same register, without moving.

"The one by the boy."

A breath. "Aye."

"They were told to keep me whole until he whistles. That is what gently meant. When I go down, both of them come, and I will not go down until he is off that crate and standing between us." Emeric kept it level. "The first one to reach me, I take with my legs. You take the other."

There was a pause about as long as it takes a man to decide that he is not going to ask how somebody with a bag on his head means to know where anybody is standing.

"Finn," Rylan said, low. "Eyes shut. Keep them shut whatever you hear. That is an order from your da."

Then there was nothing to do but hold the pull open and wait, and waiting with it was worse than waiting without, because now he could watch the time go.

The shape with the beat walked the deck. Two others went forward and came back and went forward again, and the oil came down through the seams in a new place each time. The man at the stern did not move. Below, the man at the ladder rubbed his hands together, and the man on the crate shifted his weight twice and settled twice, and Finn breathed fast and small against his father's side, and Rylan did not move at all.

Then the man on the crate stood up.

Emeric felt him come up off it, a weight lifting away from the boy and becoming a shape that walked. The lantern light came with it through the hood. It came forward along the hold, because the cold had got into him or because he wanted a word at the ladder about how long it takes to pour a jar, and it passed the small fast shape that was Finn, and it passed the big still one that was Rylan, and for one step it was square in the gap between Rylan and Emeric with its back to Rylan and its face toward the ladder, and the man at the ladder was two strides off and leaning forward to hear what it had to say.

Emeric went down on the boards.

He did it the way he had seen it done in Imani's ward, which was badly, in the wrong order, all of it at once: the heels first and the back arching and the breath going in and not coming out, the whole body doing something that no one who has watched it happen ever mistakes for anything else.

He heard the lantern go down on a crate and both of them come.

It was the gently that did it. Two men who had been told by a voice they were afraid of to keep a prince whole until a whistle, and the prince was going rigid at their feet before the whistle came. Neither of them looked at anything else. Neither of them stayed back. The one from the ladder was on his knees at Emeric's head before the other had finished turning, and the other came down beside him in the same breath, shoulder to shoulder with him, his back to the man they had both stopped thinking about.

"Get it off his head."

"Leave the bag. Turn him."

"He is going. If he goes before the whistle, it is us."

The first one got a hand under his neck, and Emeric brought his legs up and over and closed them, one across the back of the man's neck and the shin hooked behind his own knee with the man's arm caught inside, the hold he had taught every crew in the barony in a hot yard one summer, and been laughed at for, and gone on teaching anyway.

The man got a hand between them, which they always did.

It took longer than anything Emeric had ever done. It took long enough for him to hear the whole of the other thing happening beside him: a scuffle that lasted no time at all, a knee going onto boards, and then a short dull sound with a wet edge on it that he did not look toward and could not have seen if he had.

Then the man in his legs stopped pulling at his knee, and stopped doing anything, and Emeric held on for a count he had read somewhere in the other life and never expected to use, and then let go.

The hood came off. Rylan's hands were shaking so badly that he tore the cloth getting it clear.

The hold was orange at the seams. Above them the deck was already going.

Finn had his eyes shut with both hands pressed over them, the way he had been told, and Rylan got to him in one step and put the boy's face into his shoulder and held him there.

The other thing was still open in Emeric, the way a door you have forgotten about stays open, and through it he felt the deck empty.

Feet went aft, fast. The shapes went with them, and at the stern they stepped down one after another into something he could barely feel, a hull as faint and flat as the one he was sitting in, with nothing alive in it until they filled it. A voice said one word. A hull knocked a hull. Then oars, and the shapes going away together across the water with the current, downriver, and the slow beat among them, thinning as it went. He held it as far as it would go, which was about the length of the boat again past the stern, and then there was only the medium, lying still.

The whistle never came. Nobody had ever meant to give it. The two men on the boards had been left below to wait for a signal that was going downriver in somebody's mouth, on a boat that had just been set on fire over their heads, and they had been on the list of things to be tidied away as surely as anybody.

"Hatch," Emeric said. "Now. They are off."

Rylan did not ask how he knew. He put Finn down against the hull and went up the ladder in two strides and set his shoulder into the hatch, and the hatch lifted. Something heavy ground on top of it. A finger's width of orange came in round the edge, and the rungs creaked under a man in charged steel putting his whole back into it.

Then the water beside them cracked.

It was a wet crack and a burst going outward all at once, and a sound Emeric did not hear so much as feel in the roots of his teeth, and he felt it a second way as well, through the thing he had left open. The whole still sheet of the medium was shoved outward from a point in the water off the side, the way a hand shoves water out of a basin, and then there was nothing where it had been, as sudden as a sound stopping.

On the ladder, something went with a flat snap. Bram's breaker, which Bram had argued against for two days, doing the one thing it was for. The finger of orange closed. The hatch came down on whatever was on it and stayed down, and Rylan was a man in harness on a ladder, which was all he was going to be for as long as this lasted.

The ice of it came through the hull and through Emeric's boots and through the water in the bilge.

He knew it. He had made it himself on frozen mud below the east wall, with a chip of river crystal and a number in his head, and he had watched its cousin roll over his own side at the lane. Rylan knew it better than he did. Rylan had walked out of the middle of three of them in a staked field at the end of the ninth division with a charged crystal in his hand, calling where it died.

Somebody had pushed a stone past what it could hold and put it in the channel beside them.

Emeric sat with his back against a rib in a burning boat and understood, with great precision, what had been taken, and why.

A boat burns without anybody's help. The stone was for what was in it: a knight in charged steel who could have lifted that hatch and whatever was lying on it, and a man who had put out a fire on the bank at the lane with water pulled thin across it, in front of both armies, in broad daylight. Whoever had planned tonight had been told about the lane.

The first working he had ever built for himself was water pulled thin across a flame. He had run it ten thousand times in a room nobody knew about. There was fire over his head, and at his shoulder, on the other side of two inches of pitched oak, a river, more water than the barony could use in a year.

And there was nothing here to work with. It had gone the way a room goes when the door is opened in winter, out to some distance he could not measure from the inside, and it would come back from the edges, slowly, long after the fire had finished. The other thing had gone with it, the one he had owned for perhaps a quarter of an hour, and he found that he minded that more than he had any time to.

Two ordinary men and a boy in a burning box, over the deepest water in Kaldo.

Rylan came down off the ladder.

"Coaming," Emeric said. "Where the oil went. I heard him put it there. It will be through there first. Do not put your face in it."

He got two rungs up the ladder with his shoulder against it and kicked sideways into the boards beside the hatch, where the orange was brightest. The fire had been in them longer than anywhere else and they came apart under his boot in three kicks, and then there was a hole with orange round the edge of it and black beyond, and the smoke came down into the hold instead of going up out of it, and the air at floor level went bad in seconds.

Rylan came to him first.

He got both hands on the cord at Emeric's wrists and started working it, and the cord was wet and swollen and had been pulled hard by a man who tied knots for a living, and it had been lying in the bilge, and it was not going to come off in under a minute.

"Leave it."

"My lord."

"The boy. Now. Over the side and clear of the hull."

"I am not carrying you both and I am not leaving you tied in a fire," Rylan said, and his hands did not stop.

Emeric looked at him. Smoke was coming down on them and Finn had started to cough, and there was one right answer and about eleven seconds to give it in.

"Rylan." He said it quietly, which by then was a thing between them. "He is ten. Take him out. Go over at the bow and put your arm through the cable. You will not swim in that."

Rylan took his hands off the cord.

He went up through the breach with his son held against his chest and the steel of him taking the fire the way cloth would not have, and for a moment the pair of them were a shape in the orange and then no shape at all, and then there were feet going forward on the deck, and a heavy sound in the water off the bow.

Emeric got up the ladder by putting his shoulder into each rung and walking his feet, which took three attempts, and went out through the burning coaming with his coat catching and the heat going across his face, and hit the gunwale at the hip, and went over.

The cold was a hand closing.

He went under, and his body did what a body does with its arms behind it, which is to fight in a straight line downward, and he made himself stop, and that was the hardest single act of the night.

Face down. Loose. Let it hold you, because it will.

He had stood in the shallows below the Works one July with his wrists tied behind him and forty men on the bank finding it funny, and had made every one of them do it once, and some of them twice, and Declan three times after he went under on the first like a dropped anvil.

He let the water have him, and it took him, and his back came up. He turned his head, got his mouth clear for one breath, and went down again on purpose, and came up, and did it again.

The current along the rock was faster than it looked from the wall. It carried him down the length of the burning hull, and the light of it went by on his right and turned the water the colour of the inside of a furnace. When he turned his head for the next breath he could see back up the whole length of it to the bow, and under the bow, in the light, a man hanging off the anchor cable with one arm through it to the shoulder and a boy on his chest, and the fire standing up over the pair of them like a wall.

Behind him the fire got into the tar of the whole port side at once.

Three breaths. Four. The cold was the clock and he could already hear it running.

Then there were oars.

He got his face up and saw a lantern low on the water, and a boat that was coming on hard and then, for four or five strokes, was not coming on hard at all. A man swore, once, flatly, like a man who has put his foot in a hole he has put his foot in before.

Then the stroke came back, shorter and heavier, a big man rowing on nothing but himself.

Ward took Finn first, out of Rylan's arms, straight up over the bow with a fist in the boy's collar and one in his belt. Then Rylan, which took Ward and both his men and most of the gunwale, and a noise out of the boat's planking that no boat ought to make. Then Ward came astern with the lantern and found Emeric by the flare of white where the coat had gone, and got a hand in it, and hauled.

He came in over the side in the ugly way and lay on the boards with the water running off him and somebody's knife going at the cord.

When his hands came free, the first thing he did with them was not what he would have chosen if he had been thinking. His right hand went inside his coat, flat against his ribs, to the pocket.

The stone was there.

He lay on the floor of the boat with his fingers round it and heard Finn coughing in the bow under Ward's cloak, and Rylan saying his name, and the oars going.

He did the one piece of work left to him, which was to set it down before the cold took it. A name, which a man could change between one river and the next. A voice, which a man could change as well, if he ever learned that it had been listened to. And a slow beat in the dark like two strings a hair apart, which Emeric did not think a man could change at all, because he did not think the man knew he had one.

He put the three of them together in the place where he kept such things, and did not draw the line under them yet.

Then Rylan said, to Ward, not to him: "I opened the stair."

Ward said nothing at all, and rowed.
