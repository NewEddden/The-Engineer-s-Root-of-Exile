Chapter 56 - The Wharf

The timber had been lying on the piers since December, where the gate crew had left it after they took the iron out, because somebody would want it.

In March somebody did. The carpenter walked the piers below the dock quarter with a mallet, knocking on every post, and listening, and marking with chalk the ones that answered him wrong, and in a week he had a list of what had to come out and what could stay, and the list was shorter than anybody had expected. The old piers had been built by men who meant them to last. Under the soft grey skin of every post was oak as hard as horn, an inch in, as the gate crew had found out the hard way with their crowbars. It was the decking that had gone, and the stringers, and a few of the posts at the river end where the ice came down in the spring.

So the dead boats gave up their timber, as they had given up their iron. Their planking and their frames came apart under the carpenter's saw into good seasoned oak and elm, black on the outside and pale as bread inside, and went back down as new deck on the piers they had been tied up to since the port died. Rylan, who had learned to swim off one of those hulls in the sense that he had not drowned, walked across the first finished length of it and stamped, and said it was the first time he had ever stood on that boat without falling off it.

The men who did the work were the dock quarter's. Ferra had them on her count: the ones who had worked the port before it silted, and the sons of the ones who had, and a few who had been mending fish boxes for years, as the carpenter once had, for want of anything else. The barony paid them by the day, on Saturdays, at the exchange door, from the same trestle the households had been paid from. On the first Saturday there were forty of them in the line.



On the ninth of March a beam came down.

It was nobody's fault, which did not help. A stringer was being lowered onto the posts at the river end on the old crane, the one that screamed, and the rope slipped on the drum, and the beam came down a foot before the man on the drum could hold it, and one of the dock quarter's men had his hand on the post where the beam landed. He was a big man, young, one of the sons, and he did not scream. He went white and sat down on the deck and held his arm up by the wrist with his other hand and looked at what was at the end of it.

Emeric was on the pier. He got to him first. He saw the hand, and saw that the fingers were all there and most of them were straight, and that two were not, and that there was a great deal of blood, and he did what he knew how to do for the next quarter of an hour while two of the others went running over the east bridge for the ward.

They carried him to the ward in the end, on a door, with Emeric walking alongside holding the hand up, wrapped, and talking to him about nothing, and the man's mother running behind.

Imani met them at the side step.

She looked at the hand once, and at the man's face, and said three words to her women, and the door opened, and the man went in on his door, and his mother went in after him, and Imani's sleeves were already rolled and her hair already tied up high for work with a strip of grey cloth. She held up one hand to stop Emeric on the step. He stopped.

"Spirit," she said, to one of her women, over her shoulder. "The jar from the shelf. And the boiled needles." Then, to Emeric, without turning round: "You held it up. Good. Who wrapped it?"

"I did."

"Too tight." She was already going in. "I'll have it off him."

He waited on the step. It was what anybody did at Imani's step without her yes. He waited for most of an hour, sitting on the wall of the laundry yard, while the sounds came out through the side door: the man's voice once, high, and his mother's, and then Imani's, low and level and going on and on without stopping, the way she talked to people while she hurt them.

When she came out she was drying her hands on a cloth. She stood on the step above him and did not come down it.

"Two fingers set," she said. "He'll keep them. He won't close that hand properly again, and he won't work a rope for a month. Tell your carpenter that crane wants a pawl on the drum before he puts a man under it again."

"I'll tell him."

"You tell him." She folded the cloth, once, and then again, exactly, into a square. "And you'll pay the man his days while he can't work them. Not his mother. Him."

"Yes."

She nodded. She was looking at the cloth in her hands, and he waited for the rest of it, because with Imani there was usually more, and it usually came straight, and he had learned to stand still for it.

It did not come straight. It did not come at all, and he began to think she had finished. Then she said, in the same voice, as though it were one more thing about the hand:

"The whole ward knows where you walk at night."

He did not understand her. He heard the words, and he looked up at her on the step, and the first thing he thought, quite clearly, was that she meant the wharf: that he had been down at the river every night that week with a lamp, walking the new decking, and that she had a man in her ward with a crushed hand because of it.

"The crane," he said. "That was the crane. I'll have the pawl on it tomorrow."

Imani looked at him. It was not a long look. Then she turned round and went in, and shut the side door behind her, not hard, and he heard her voice inside telling somebody that the water in the second basin was not hot enough and had not been since noon.

He sat on the laundry-yard wall until a lamp was lit in the side window, and then went back to the wharf to see about the pawl.



She was cool with him for a day.

He would not have put it that way. He would have said, if anybody had asked him, which nobody did, that she was busy. When he went back the next morning to ask after the man's hand, she told him how it was from the step, in four sentences, all of them true and all of them short, and did not ask him anything, and went in. When he passed the ward at noon on his way to the bridge she was in the laundry yard with her back to the gate, and stayed with her back to it. The second time he went, in the evening, with the pawl on the crane and the carpenter's promise about it, one of her women came to the step instead of her and took the message.

He was halfway back up the hill before it occurred to him that he did not know what he had done.

The day after that, it was over. He did not know why that was either.

He brought the ward's share of the spirit down himself, in the morning, one of the journeyman's small casks on his shoulder, the first of the oak-casked spirit that was not going to be drunk, because Imani had asked for a cask of the strong kind for the shelf and he had promised it to her before the wharf. She came to the step. She looked at the cask on his shoulder and then at him, and stood aside to let him put it down inside the door, which she had not done for anybody but her own women in two days.

He put it down. He straightened up.

"For hands," Imani said, as she had said it in October, and went back to her basins.

She did not say anything else about it, and he did not ask.



Behind the wharf, on the flat ground between the piers and the first houses, the gate crew built a yard: a low wall of rough stone round three sides, open to the river on the fourth, with a gate on the road. The ore yard. Kaldo's own ore would come into it by cart down the long road from the hills, two days of it, and lie in it in heaps until the dyehouse wanted it for the acid and bay three wanted it for whatever bay three could make out of iron that crumbled at a red heat.

The first carts came down the road in the third week of March, two of them, axle-deep in spring mud, with a note from the old miner tucked into the harness of the first. Emeric had told them, in November, that he would not be up the hill again before the spring. The note said, in a hand that had plainly not held a pen for years, that they had lit the face without him in February, on their own, the old way, and that it had gone a hand a day and not two, and that they had not died of it.

Emeric read it twice. He gave it to Tess when she asked what he was smiling at, and she read it, and took it up to the dyehouse, and under the line on the wall that said the fire on the rock face at the mines, she wrote: the old way. A hand a day.



Saban came in at the end of the third week of March with his carts, on the first of his spring round, a week later than last year because the roads were worse.

He took the arch bar, as he always did. Then he tasted the spirit.

He did it at Ferra's table, out of one of Bram's cups, with his hat on his knee. He held it in his mouth, and swallowed, and looked at the wall above Ferra's head, the way he looked at a price.

"Not in Wayford," he said. "They make their own in Wayford, out of something worse, and they're proud of it. Milford will take some, for the steward's house and the steward's friends, if it's in a better cask than that and the cask's got a mark on it." He drank the rest. "The coast'll take all you can make. Sailors'll drink anything that's stronger than water and doesn't kill them, and this is both. Only you can't get it to the coast."

"We can," Emeric said. "By the river. There's a man with a boat."

Saban looked at him with his trader's patience.

"There's a man with a boat," he agreed. "Who takes salt up for you once a month, and gets paid badly for it, and who can carry four casks if the river's kind and three if it isn't." He turned his hat round on his knee, once. "That's not a trade. That's a man with a boat."

He took six casks north anyway, for Milford, on a new line in Ferra's book with his name against it and a price that made Ferra sit back in her chair. And on the twenty-sixth of March the salt boatman came up to the new wharf with the month's salt, and tied up at it, the first boat to tie up there, and went back down the river with four casks of Kaldo's spirit in the bottom of his boat under a tarred cloth, and a fair price for the carrying, which Emeric paid him himself, on the wharf, out of his own purse, because of the first summer.

Saban had come down to the wharf to watch the boatman load. When the boat had gone he stayed where he was, at the end of the new deck, looking up the river.

"You'll wait a while for Grayrock's barges," he said.

He told them why that evening, at Ferra's table, on the last night of his stay, with his hat on his knee.

"They've shut the scale house. I came down by Grayrock this time. First time in twenty years I've been through Grayrock and not been weighed. Nobody in the yard. Barges tied up at the bank, a lot of them, more than they've got ore for." He turned the hat round once. "I asked a boy where the clerk was. The boy said the clerk was busy."

The table was quiet.

"I'm going north in the morning," Saban said. "I'd go north in the morning anyway. I'm only telling you."



The river had been high and brown with the thaw for three weeks. Last year the first Grayrock barge of the season had come round the bend a week after Saban's wagons left. Ferra had the date of every first barge in a column, as far back as Hale's ledgers went, and not one of them was later than the last day of March.

"They'll come," she said, at her table, with the column open in front of her. "Grayrock's sold iron down this river every spring since the ledgers start. They need the coin more than we need the iron."

It was the last day of March, and nothing had come round the bend. Nothing came in the first week of April either, and she did not say it again.

Bram's stock of Grayrock bar, the good iron he meant for the breastplates now that the river iron was spent, stood in a stack in bay three that he measured every morning with his eye and did not mention. Ferra went over to bay three on the Wednesday and stood beside the stack while Bram told her, plainly, how many breastplates it would make, and she wrote the number down, and the number of the garrison under it, and drew a line between them, and did not take the difference, because both of them could see it. The ore yard filled up slowly with Kaldo's own stinking ore, two carts at a time, which would make acid and a little lead and iron that broke under the hammer. The new wharf stood empty in the spring sun with its pale new deck, waiting for barges, and the dock quarter's forty men were paid to finish it anyway.

One night that week Emeric went up onto the wall above the river tower, where Ward was, and stood beside him. Under the lamp the river ran brown and fast and empty, and past the edge of the light it went on empty into the dark toward the bend.

Ward did not take his eyes off the bend. Neither of them said anything. They stood there together, the prince and his knight, under the light, and watched the empty river come round the bend in the dark and go past below them toward the sea.
