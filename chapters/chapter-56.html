Chapter 56 - The Deep Channel

"He is here," Rylan said. "He is here, Finn, I am here, put your head down."

They had set Emeric against the curve of the hull with his shoulder on a rib and his hands behind him, and somewhere to his left, close, a boy was breathing in the particular way of a child who has been crying for hours and has stopped because he was told to.

Two of them had come down after. He could hear both, one by the ladder and one at the far end, and the far one had a lantern with three of its sides shuttered, because the light through the hood came and went as the man moved and never came from more than one direction.

They tied Rylan too. Emeric listened to it being done and heard what it was: a wrap and a knot at the wrists and nothing at the elbows, a rope put on a man who has already been used and is not going to be needed again.

Then nobody spoke for a while except Rylan, who talked to his son in a low flat voice about nothing at all. The cold. The boat. That his mother would have the fire in. That he was to keep his coat buttoned, which was a thing he had been told at the gate eleven days ago by somebody else and was apparently going to be told for the rest of his life.

Emeric listened to the voice rather than the words. It was steady all the way through and it did not vary once, and that was a piece of work he had seen men fail at in better circumstances.

The two guards talked to each other over their heads the way men talk in front of a hooded prisoner they have been told does not matter.

The cold. The wait. That there was no sense in any of it and the whole night could have been done at the stair.

"Marrow wants us down here till he whistles," the one by the ladder said. "So we are down here till he whistles."

Emeric put the name where he put things, which was next to the voice from the ledge, and did not do anything else with it.

The hull moved.

Poles first, short pushes, the sound of the shaft knocking the gunwale twice. Then oars, and not many strokes of them. Then nothing, and a man forward calling something in one syllable, and the anchor going over.

The rope ran out.

Emeric counted it, because he counted things, and because it was the only measurement available to a man with a bag over his head. It ran, and kept running, and went on running long past the length of any water Kaldo's boats normally sat in, and when it finally slowed and stopped he had a number in his head that meant one place and only one place.

They were in the channel. The deep one, off the blind run, under the wall he had been standing on eleven weeks ago doing arithmetic.

Then the smell changed.

It came down through the hatch seams and through the boards, over the pitch and the bilge, unmistakable and generous. Lamp oil. A great deal of it, being poured on a deck by somebody who was not being careful about the corners.

Emeric sat with his back on the rib and did the sum, and it took about four seconds, and every part of it was already in his hands.

Nobody oils a deck he means to sail home.

A prince vanishes on a black night. The guard who sat his board vanishes with him. The guard's son, who everybody in Kaldo has been told is at his aunt's in Milford, vanishes the same night. And in the morning the watch finds the water gate open with the bar standing tidy against the wall, off its pegs, from the inside.

The story wrote itself. It would write itself in the hall by noon and it would be in the capital in twelve days, and every name in it would be Rylan's.

There had never been a deal. There had been a man being walked along a road with his son held at the far end of it, and the road ended here, over the deepest water in the barony, with all three of them in the bottom of a boat that was about to burn.

Two feet to his left, Rylan's breathing changed.

He had got there too. Of course he had got there too. He had been a soldier for eleven years and he had smelled lamp oil before.

Emeric went through what he had, once, properly, the way he would have gone through it on paper.

He had a hood on. He had never in four years worked at anything he could not see, not once, not in the delve, not in a courtyard, not on a hill with an army on it; every drill he had built started with his eyes on the thing he meant to move. The only one of the three with force in it was a blade of moving air, and a blade he could not see, in a hold six feet across, went into whatever was nearest, and what was nearest was a ten-year-old.

There was no stone underfoot. There were planks and a foot of filthy water.

His mother's stone was in the inside pocket of his coat, four inches from his ribs and as far out of reach as the capital, because his hands were tied behind him and because delving took time and left him sitting like a man asleep with his eyes open, which was a thing to do in a locked room and not in front of two guards.

So the sensible use of his magic tonight was to leave it entirely alone, which he did, and which cost him more than he expected.

That left two men, a boy, four seconds of oil, and whatever Rylan had done with his wrists in the last quarter of an hour.

He leaned his head over until the cloth touched Rylan's shoulder and spoke under his breath.

He had grown up in a house where a thing said at the volume of a room was for the room and a thing said under the breath was true, and he had never once in his life given a plan he meant in any other register.

"Your hands."

"Out," Rylan said, in the same register, without moving.

"They were told to keep me alive until he whistles. That is what gently meant. So when I go down, both of them come." A breath. "The one that reaches me first, I take with my legs. You take the other."

There was a pause that lasted about as long as it takes a man to decide that he is not going to say anything about whether it will work.

"Finn," Rylan said, low. "Eyes shut. Keep them shut whatever you hear. That is an order from your da."

Emeric went down on the boards.

He did it the way he had seen it done, which was badly, in the wrong order, all of it at once: the heels first and the back arching and the breath going in and not coming out, the whole body doing something that no one who has watched it happen ever mistakes for anything else.

He heard the lantern go down on a crate and both of them coming.

"Get it off his head."

"Leave the bag. Turn him."

The first one knelt at his shoulder and got a hand under his neck, and Emeric brought his legs up and over and closed them, one across the back of the man's neck and the shin hooked behind his own knee with the arm in it, the hold he had taught every crew in the barony in a hot yard in summer and had been laughed at for and had gone on teaching anyway.

The man got a hand between them, which they always did.

It took longer than anything Emeric had ever done. It took long enough for him to hear the whole of the other thing happening four feet away: a scuffle that lasted no time at all, a knee going onto boards, and then a short dull sound with a wet edge on it that he did not look toward and would not have been able to see if he had.

Then the man in his legs stopped pulling at his knee, and stopped doing anything, and Emeric held on for a count he had read somewhere and never expected to use, and then let go.

The hood came off. Rylan's hands were shaking so badly he tore the cloth getting it clear.

The hold was orange at the seams. Above them the deck was already going.

Finn had his eyes shut with both hands pressed over them, the way he had been told, and Rylan got to him in one step and put his face into his shoulder and held him there.

Up on deck, feet went aft, fast. A voice said one word. Then the sound of a hull knocking a hull, and oars, and the oars going away.

The whistle never came. Nobody came down the ladder. Whoever had spoken from the ledge in that level, unhurried way was already thirty yards off and getting further, and the two men he had left below to wait for a signal he had no intention of giving were lying on the boards of the boat he had just set on fire.

Then the water beside them cracked.

It was a wet crack and a burst going outward all at once, and a sound Emeric did not hear so much as feel in the roots of his teeth, and he had felt it twice before in his life: once in his own courtyard with a chip of river crystal and a number in his head, and once on a road with three mages standing in it.

The ice of it came through the hull and through his boots and through the water in the bilge.

Somebody had pushed a stone past what it could hold and dropped it in the channel beside them.

Emeric sat with his back against a rib in a burning boat and understood, with great precision, what had just been taken.

The first working he had ever built for himself was water pulled thin across a flame. He had run it ten thousand times in a room nobody knew about. There was fire over his head and there was a river on the other side of two inches of pitched oak, four inches from his shoulder, more water than the barony could use in a year.

And there was nothing in the world here to work with. Not weak. Gone, the way a room goes when the door is opened in winter, out to some distance he could not measure from the inside, and it would come back slowly and it would come back long after the fire had finished.

Rylan's enhancement had gone with it. He would not know why. He would simply find, in about a minute, that his arms were his own arms.

Two ordinary men and a boy in a burning box, over the deepest water in Kaldo.

"Hatch," Emeric said.

Rylan was already at the ladder with his shoulder into it. It did not move. Something heavy was on it, or through the ring, and it did not matter which.

"Coaming," Emeric said. "Where the oil ran. It will be through there first. Do not put your face in it."

It took him a while and it cost him the backs of both hands. The fire had been in the boards above the coaming longer than anywhere else and it came apart under his boot in three kicks, and then there was a hole with orange around the edge of it and black beyond that, and smoke came down into the hold instead of up out of it and the air at floor level went bad in seconds.

Rylan came back to him first.

He got both hands on the cord at Emeric's wrists and started working it, and the cord was wet and swollen and had been pulled hard by a man who tied knots for a living, and it had been under water in the bilge, and it was not a thing that was going to come off in under a minute.

"Leave it."

"My lord."

"The boy. Now. Over the side and get him clear of the hull."

"I am not carrying you both and I am not leaving you tied in a fire," Rylan said, and his hands did not stop.

Emeric looked at him. Smoke was coming down on them and Finn had started to cough and there was one right answer and about eleven seconds to give it in.

"Rylan." He said it quietly, which by then was a thing between them. "He is ten. Take him out."

Rylan took his hands off the cord.

He went up through the breach with his son held against his chest with one arm, and there was a moment where the pair of them were a shape in the orange and then no shape at all, and then a heavy sound in the water off the side.

Emeric got up the ladder by putting his shoulder into each rung and walking his feet, which took three attempts, and went out through the burning coaming with his coat catching and the heat going across his face, and hit the gunwale at the hip, and went over.

The cold was not cold. It was a hand closing.

He went under and his body did what a body does with its arms behind it, which is to fight in a straight line downward, and he made himself stop, and that was the hardest single act of the night.

Face down. Loose. Let it hold you, because it will.

He had stood in the shallows below the Works in July with his wrists tied behind him and forty men on the bank finding it funny, and had made every one of them do it, and had written it into the sheets as a drill with a line for each man's name.

He let the water have him, and it took him, and his back came up. He turned his head, got his mouth clear for one breath, and went down again on purpose, and came up, and did it again.

The current along the rock was faster than it looked from the wall. It carried him past the burning hull and the light of it went by on his right and turned the water the colour of the inside of a furnace, and behind him, somewhere, the fire got into the tar of the whole port side at once.

Three breaths. Four. The cold was the clock and he could already hear it running.

Then there were oars.

He got his face up and saw a lantern low on the water, and a boat that was coming on hard and then, for four or five strokes, was not coming on hard at all. A man swore, once, flatly, as if he had put his foot in a hole he had put his foot in before.

Then the stroke came back, shorter, heavier, a big man rowing on nothing but himself.

Ward took Finn first, out of Rylan's arms, straight up over the bow with a fist in the boy's collar and one on his belt. Then Rylan. Then he came astern with the lantern and found Emeric by the flare of white where the coat had gone, and got a hand in it, and hauled.

He came in over the side in the ugly way and lay on the boards with the water running off him and somebody's knife going at the cord.

When his hands came free, the first thing he did with them was not what he would have chosen if he had been thinking. His right hand went inside his coat, flat against his ribs, to the pocket.

The stone was there.

He lay on the floor of the boat with his fingers round it and heard Finn coughing in the bow under Ward's cloak, and Rylan saying his name, and the oars going.

Then Rylan said, to Ward, not to him: "I opened the stair."

Ward said nothing at all, and rowed.
