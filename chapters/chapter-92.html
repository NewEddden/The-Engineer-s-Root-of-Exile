Chapter 92 - Weight

Armand came into the yard at first light on the third morning of autumn, carrying a wooden box lined with straw, and stood by the well until Ward had finished leaving Emeric alive for rather less time than usual.

Then he set the box down on the flagstones and opened it. The four carriage stones lay in the straw where they had lain since the bank below the foundry, black and flecked and carved over every face in their dense patient lattice.

"You wanted a teacher," he said. "The yard will do."

Ward looked at the box, and at the old man, and went and sat on the mounting block with his sword across his knees, which was his way of saying that he was not going anywhere. Emeric looked round the yard. There were perhaps a dozen people in it already, the way there always were at that hour: two of the garrison drawing water, a boy with the keep's bread, a stable hand who had been watching the blade work with the frank pleasure of a man seeing his lord put on his back. By the time Armand had set the first stone on the flagstones in the middle of the yard and stepped back from it, there were twenty. By the time Emeric had failed the first time, there were more, and he did not count them.

He had never once in ten years been seen to practise.

Everybody in the barony knew he could do things. They had seen water go thin across a fire on a hillside, and a blade of air go across a line of men at forty feet. What nobody had ever seen was the part before that. He drilled the garrison on written schedules. He posted the bucket line's times on the church door. He had the evening classes counting to twelve on the master foot. And in ten years not one person in Kaldo had watched him try something and get it wrong at the first go and try it again, because he had done all of that in a room nobody else could enter, with the door barred.

Orrin came in through the gate arch with a slate under his arm and stopped where he was. A moment later Vesk came and stood beside him, and said something to Orrin that Emeric did not hear, and Orrin laughed, once, and then stopped laughing and watched.

"Take its weight," Armand said.

It was a carriage stone the size of a fist. Emeric had held heavier things than that with the third drill since his first year in the south: a flagstone, a brick, a pebble, a blank of steel no bigger than a fingernail. He reached for it the way he always reached, found the weight of it, and took it onto himself. The stone rose an inch off the flagstones and hung there.

"No," said Armand.

Emeric let it down.

"That is a hold," the old man said. "Any mage in the kingdom can hold a stone off a floor. Orrin can do it. Vesk can do it. I have seen a girl of eleven do it in the tower with a candle in her other hand. You took that stone's weight and put it on your own back, and your back carried it, and if I asked you to carry it to the gate you would be tired when you got there." He pointed at the stone with two fingers. "That is gravity stone. It is the one stone in the world that does the carrying itself, if it is asked properly. You do not take its weight. You give it your work, and it takes its own."

"How?"

"Put your work into it," said Armand, "and leave the weight where it is."

He tried.

It was very much worse than failing at the blade. At the blade he at least knew which way he was wrong. Here he reached for the stone and found, every time, that his whole body had already gone to the weight, the way a hand goes to a cup it is about to knock over. He had spent eight years teaching himself exactly how heavy things were so that he could stop them falling. Everything he knew about lifting was the slab drill: find the weight, take it on, hold it with the least there was. And every time he found the weight, he took it on, and the stone came up off the flagstones on his back, and Armand said no.

When he tried not to find it, there was nothing to reach for at all. He poured something into the stone that went nowhere, and the stone lay on the flagstones like a stone, and the yard watched him stand over it red in the face with his hands out, doing nothing that anybody could see.

On the fifth try the stone jerked sideways an inch and fell over.

Somebody at the back of the crowd laughed, and was shushed, and Emeric found that he did not mind the laugh at all and minded the shushing a great deal.

"Again," said Armand.

He came every morning after that, for eleven days, after Ward had finished with him, and the yard came too. It did not come to mock. He understood that by the third morning. It came the way people come to watch a man learn to swim, to see whether he will. The garrison stood about with their water buckets. The boy with the bread stayed until he was sent for. On the fourth morning Imani stood in the arch of the long street for the length of one try, and went away without saying anything, and on the seventh Vantic watched from the gallery with her book shut under her arm, which he did not think he had ever seen before.

He was bad at it. He went on being bad at it. By the ninth morning he could put his work into a carriage stone without the weight coming onto him, and hold it there, and feel the stone take up the work and begin to do something with it, and then the lattice would take the work and meet the stone's own weight at its threshold and give, and push back, and give, and the stone would settle on the flagstones in three small movements that he had watched ten years ago in a courtyard at dawn, and that was all the carriage stones would ever do.

"Threshold stones hold," Armand said, watching them settle. "A caster lifts. The lattice takes your work and spends it at one weight, and no other. Carve it for a carriage and it will hold a carriage. It will never do anything else. That is why the tower carves them." He looked at Emeric over the stone. "Now do it again and end it properly."

"End it how?"

"Take it back."

Emeric looked at him.

"Your work," said Armand, patiently, the way he had once explained baselines to a pupil who would not sit still. "It is still in the stone. You are still attached to it. You feel that?"

He did. A thread, very thin, running from somewhere under his breastbone into the black lattice on the flagstones.

"When you are finished, you draw it back into yourself. All of it. Cleanly. You do not leave your work in a stone, ever. That is the first rule the tower teaches and the only one it never relaxes." Armand's voice did not change, but something in his face did. "An acolyte who leaves himself in a stone and walks away is tied to it. When the stone runs dry, it runs dry through him. I have seen what that does. Every acolyte in the capital has seen it once, on purpose, in the first year, so that they will never have to see it twice. Take it back."

Emeric took it back. It came up the thread into him like a breath drawn in, and the stone on the flagstones was only a stone.

That night he did not go into the delve.

He sat in the long room with the notebook open to a clean page and wrote nothing on it for an hour, and then he wrote down what he had felt in the yard, all eleven mornings of it, in order. He had learned the lesson Armand was teaching and he had learned it badly, which was the ordinary way to learn anything. But there had been something else in the yard every morning, and he had not been paying attention to it, because he had been too busy being bad at the lesson.

Every morning, when Armand showed him how it was done, Emeric had stood beside him with the medium sense open. He could not help it. Since a boat's hold on the Deep Channel he kept it open more often than not, the way other men kept listening. And through it, every morning, he had felt Armand do three things.

He had felt the old man's work go into the stone. He had felt the stone take it, and hold. And at the end, every time, he had felt Armand take it back: the work drawn up the thread and out of the stone and home, and in the same instant the stone let go of its lift and fell.

He had watched it fall eleven times and put it down to the work running out.

The work had not run out. He read it again, in his own hand, and it was plain on the page. The work had still been in the stone, holding, right up to the moment Armand took it away. The stone had not stopped because the man stopped. It had stopped because the man had pulled the work out after him when he went.

He went to find Armand, and found him at the bench, going over the week's tracings from Nevin by lamplight, and asked him.

"Has anybody ever let go without taking it back?"

Armand did not look up from the tracing for a moment. Then he did.

"I told you what happens."

"You told me what happens if he's still attached. I'm asking about the stone. If a man puts his work into a stone, and then lets go of the thread without drawing the work back, what does the stone do?"

"It falls."

"Has anybody seen it fall?"

The old man said nothing.

"The tower tests stones on a bench like this one," Emeric said. "A man pours, and the bench reads the stone, and then the man stops and the bench reads it again. Every time, the stone falls. I've read the method. I've read you describe it. But the man always stops the way you stopped this morning. He takes it back, because it's the first rule, because every acolyte has seen what happens if he doesn't. So every test the tower has ever run has the man taking his work out of the stone before anybody looks at the stone." He put his hands flat on the bench. "I've been standing next to you for eleven days with the thing I found on the Deep Channel. I can't feel what the stone does. I can feel what you do. And when you stop, the work doesn't go out of the stone by itself. You take it."

Armand looked at him for a long time across the brass.

"You want me to leave myself in a stone."

"I want you to let go of the thread without drawing it in. Not a carved stone. The carving spends the work at its threshold, and that's all it does. A blank."

"There is no such thing as a working blank. The tower gave up on raw stone before my grandfather was born. It does nothing anybody can predict."

"Then it's never been asked properly."

He heard how that sounded, in that room, to that man, and did not take it back either.

Nevin cut the blank the next morning, off one of the two black pieces in the strongroom chest, with Armand standing over him holding the key and the strongroom ledger both. It took him most of the day. He could not read, and he did not need to, and he cut a cube the size of the end of a thumb with six faces so flat that when he laid it on the bench's pan it did not rock at all, and there was no mark on any face of it, no line, no lattice, nothing. When it was done he looked at it for a while with the expression of a man who has made something correctly and does not know what for, and asked whether he should carve it now.

"No," said Armand. "Not yet. Perhaps not ever."

Nevin went home puzzled. Armand entered the blank in the ledger by weight, against the two pounds: two ounces, a little under, cut and unmarked.

They did it that night on the bench, with the door shut.

Orrin and Vesk were there because Armand said a thing that had never been done should have more than two witnesses, and Ward was there because Ward was always there, and he stood by the door. The blank sat on the bench's pan in the lamplight, with the needle over it reading two ounces and a little under.

Armand put his hands out, palms down, the way he had on the bank.

It was nothing like the boat. It was a thumb-sized piece of stone and he had spent fifty years in the tower. Emeric felt the old man's work go in, small and exact, and felt the blank take it, and the needle came back and back and settled at nothing, and the blank rose off the pan the width of a fingernail and hung there, turning very slowly, touched by nothing.

"Now," said Emeric.

Armand did not move.

He stood there with his hands out over a stone the size of a thumb, an old man who had once lifted twenty-five tons for two minutes, and he did not move, and Emeric understood what he was being asked to do. Every lesson of fifty years was a hand on his wrist. He had seen what happened to the ones who did this. He had seen it on purpose, in his first year, so that he would never see it twice.

"You won't be tied to it," Emeric said. "I'll feel the thread. The moment you let go of it, I'll know if anything's still on it. If it is, you take it back, and we've learned nothing and lost nothing."

"And if I cannot take it back."

"Then I'll tell you, and you'll have learned something the tower never did, and we'll deal with it."

Armand let go.

Emeric felt it happen. He felt the thread go slack, and then go, and nothing come up it. The old man's work stayed where he had put it. And Armand stood with his hands out over the bench, breathing, untouched, with nothing running out of him into the stone at all.

The blank did not fall.

It hung over the pan the width of a fingernail, turning very slowly. The needle read nothing. Nobody's hands were over it. Orrin made a small sound in his throat and did not say anything. Vesk, who had been a hired mage under a baron who died on Kaldo's road and had seen a great many things done with stones, took one step toward the bench and then stopped himself.

Armand lowered his hands, very slowly, as though they might set something off.

"It is holding," he said. His voice was quite steady. "Nobody is holding it. It is holding."

"Yes."

A year ago, in his own room with the door barred, Emeric had written down that he did not know whether gravity stone could be told anything, or only cut to one thing forever. He knew now. It could be told. Once.

"How long?"

Emeric had the medium open, and he was reading the stone now, not the man, and there was something in it he had not expected and did not like. The blank was holding, and it was spending itself to do it. The work Armand had left in it was running on the stone itself, slowly, steadily, the way a lamp runs on its own oil, and the dense strange hot place in the medium that the blank had been when Nevin cut it was very slightly less dense than it had been a minute ago.

"It drains," he said. "Nobody's feeding it. It feeds on itself. While it holds, it spends. When it's spent, it'll come down."

"Spent."

"Dead. Rock." He watched the flecks in the black stone, which caught the lamp and held it. "It's a stock. It was always a stock. This is just the stock being spent a thumb at a time."

They watched it all night.

Armand sat on a stool beside the bench with his book open and wrote down the hour every hour, and the needle, and what Emeric said the stone was doing, and nothing else. Orrin fell asleep in the corner at about the fourth hour and woke ashamed at the fifth. Vesk did not sleep at all. At a little after six hours by the hourglass, the flecks in the blank began to go out, one and then another and then a great many together, like a town going dark street by street, and the blank settled onto the pan in a single small movement and lay there, grey, dull, a thumb-sized cube of stone that weighed, when the needle came to rest, a little under two ounces.

Nobody touched it for some time.

"Six hours and a quarter," Armand said, and wrote it. "Two ounces, level, a fingernail's lift. Nobody holding."

He put the pen down and sat back on the stool, and looked at the dead grey cube for a long while, and when he spoke again he spoke to the book, or the bench, or fifty years, and not to Emeric.

"The tower pointed every test it ever made at the stone," the old man said. "Every one. What does the stone do with a man pouring. What does the stone do when he stops. Since the founding. And you pointed yours at the man holding it."

Emeric did not answer. There was nothing to answer. It was simply true, and it was the old man's to say, and he had said it.

The failure was his own, and it came the next night, and it came from exactly the place his teacher had said it would.

He wanted to do it himself. That was the whole of the reason and he did not pretend there was a better one. Nevin cut a second blank. Emeric stood over the bench with the medium open and the blank on the pan, and put his work into it the way Armand had taught him in the yard, without taking the weight onto himself, and it worked. The needle came back. The blank rose.

And then eight years of the slab drill did what eight years of anything will do, the moment a man's attention goes to the next thing. He felt the lift, and it felt thin, and his whole body went to the weight to make sure of it, and added to it, a little, the way he had always added to a hold that felt thin. The blank went up a finger. He felt the excess in it and reached to draw the excess back, and he had already let go of the thread.

There was no way back.

The work was in the stone and the stone was shut on it, and when he reached for the thread there was no thread. There was nothing to pull. The blank had been given a little more than its own weight to carry and nobody could take the little more out of it now, and it did what that meant. It rose off the pan, slowly, steadily, past the top of the bench and the lamp and his face, turning.

Ward caught it.

He came off the door in two steps and closed his hand round it at the height of Emeric's head, and stood there with his fist raised, looking at it with an expression Emeric had never seen on him, which was surprise. The stone pulled upward in his hand. Not hard. About as hard as a small bird wanting to be let go.

"It wants to go up," said Ward.

"Yes."

"Take it back, then."

"I can't," Emeric said. "There's nothing to take it back by. It's set."

Bram's iron box came up from the works at midnight, the one he packed bearings in for the hardening, and they put the blank in it and shut the lid and set a flagstone on the lid, and all night and all the next day the box sat on the bench with the stone inside it pressing very gently up against the iron, and the box ticked now and then as it shifted on the brass. Armand wrote the hour every hour. Nobody could do anything else. You could not reach into a set stone and change your mind. You could only wait while it spent itself on the mistake.

It lasted a little over five hours, which was less than Armand's, because it had been given more to carry, and Emeric wrote that down too, because it was the most important number either of them had yet.

When the box was quiet they opened it. The blank lay in the bottom, grey and dead, a small cube of worthless rock that had cost a thumb's worth of the only stone in the barony, and bought nothing but a number and a rule.

Armand entered it in the strongroom ledger in his crabbed hand, against the two pounds. Second blank, two ounces, spent. And under it, because he was a man who wrote down what was true, the cause, in six words that Emeric read over his shoulder and did not argue with.

Set above weight. No way back.
