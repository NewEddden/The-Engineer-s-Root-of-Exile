Chapter 92 - The Reckoning

Ferra and Vantic reckoned the year on the last Friday in August, at the trestle in the back room of the exchange, in two columns, as they had reckoned it the year before.

It took them the morning. Ferra did hers from her own books, all of them, open round her on the trestle like the petals of something. Vantic did hers from the copies Hale had carried up the east tower stair every week for a year, and from her own book, and from nothing else, and did not once look across at Ferra's page, and Ferra did not once look across at hers. Emeric sat on the bench by the door with his hands between his knees, as he had sat the year before, and Saban sat at the other end of the bench pretending to read a letter, and nobody said anything for three hours except, now and then, a figure under someone's breath.

At a little before noon they both drew the line.

They turned their books round on the trestle and read each other's foot. Then they read the lines above it, all of them, from the bottom up, the way they always did, and came back down to the foot, and agreed.

To the copper. Again.

The year before it had been a small number, and a fifth of it had been smaller. This year it was not small. It was the second harvest, sold and eaten and banked. It was the spirit, which had sat in the cooper's shop all last summer with nobody to buy it and was now going down the coast thirty casks at a time on Vantic's one line, with the coast buyers writing to Saban for more. It was Paxton's twenty casks, paid to the copper on the day, and the first of his tallow. It was the soap, which had never stopped. It was the neighbors' stones, filled in Armand's room by five mages at a fee, which had not covered the mages' wages in April and covered them twice over by August. It was a year in which nobody had burned anything.

A fifth of it was real money.

Ferra looked at the foot of her column without writing anything. Then she turned back in the year's book, a long way back, to a page from the September before. It was the page she had reckoned the first fifth on, the small one, with Vantic's initials beside the total, and under the total, in her own hand, at the time, two words that Emeric had seen then and not remarked on.

Two years.

She had said it at her table the week the terms were read. Two years of nothing new, she had said, not a house, not a pipe, not one of your machines. She had written it under the small number when the small number came, as a woman writes down a debt she expects to be paying for a long time.

She dipped her pen. Under the two words, she wrote three more.

Said so. Wrong.

And the date. And blotted it, and turned the book back to the year, and went on with the next thing.

He saw it. She knew he saw it. She did not look at him, and he did not say anything. It was her book.



What it would buy was a different question, and the whole table had an answer.

Emeric had his list. He had kept it for a year on the back pages of Armand's old notebook: the engine, the coke ovens, the furnace, the slip, the glass, the kiln, every work he meant to build and what he thought each one would cost. The fifth did not reach the bottom of it. It did not reach the middle. It reached, Ferra said, reading down his figures with her pen, about one large thing, and some small ones, if the small ones were small.

"The engine," Emeric said.

Nobody in the room was surprised. The river had been down for nine days. Two lamps burned on the wall at night and six were dark, and Ward's men were walking the six in turns, and the dynamo down at the race was turning so slowly that Tess said you could count the spokes. An engine, a boiler and a cylinder and a beam, fired on coal from Grayrock and made in Kaldo out of Grayrock's plate, would turn the dynamo whether the river was up or down, and the wall would never go dark in August again.

Imani came in while he was saying it.

She had a roll of papers under one arm and the register under the other, and she put both down on the trestle beside his list without asking whether there was room, and unrolled the papers and held them flat with the register at one end and her hand at the other.

It was a hospital.

Not a ward. A hospital, drawn in her own hand, with the measurements in Kaldo inches in a column down the side, which meant she had been to the tally room. A long stone building on the ground beside the ward, where the old market's yard was. A room for the catching cases, with its own door to the outside and its own well. A room for mothers, and for children being born, with nobody coughing in it. A room with a high window and a scrubbed table under it and nothing else, for cutting and closing. Beds, forty of them, in two rows, each with its own space round it a man could walk through. A laundry with a copper. And at the foot of the drawing a figure, in her square hand, every line of it costed, the stone and the timber and the lime and the labour and the beds and the copper, as carefully as Ferra would have done it.

He had told her once, in the first autumn, on her first teaching morning in the ward, what a ward could become. She had remembered every word of it.

"This," Imani said, "is what the register says Kaldo needs."

Ferra read the figure at the foot. She read it twice. Then she laid her pen down beside it, carefully, as she laid it beside a column she did not want to have to add.

"It's the fifth," she said. "Nearly all of it. Or the engine. Not both."

There was a long quiet in the back room.

Imani did not look at Ferra. She looked at Emeric. She did not ask him anything. She had laid it on the table and she was waiting for what he would do with it, and she was not going to make it easier.

He knew what he was going to say before he said it, and hated it, and said it.

"The engine."

Imani did not move.

"Without the engine the wall goes dark every August," he said. "And the line room. And the powerhouse goes down to two lamps' worth, and the still runs short of heat for the barony's spirit, and the spirit is half of what's on that page." He made himself look at her while he said it. "The engine makes next year's fifth. Next year's fifth builds that." He put his hand on the edge of her drawing, not on it. "This year it's the engine."

"Next year," Imani said.

"Next year."

She looked at him a while longer. Then she rolled up the drawing, the way she rolled a bandage, tight and even from one end, and put it under her arm, and picked up the register, and said nothing else at all, to him or to anyone. She went out. She did not slam the door. Imani never slammed a door. She closed it, behind her, all the way, which was worse.

Vantic, who had watched the whole of it without moving, wrote one line in her book. The engine, under the cap, from the year's fifth. And the date.

"Three standpipes," Ferra said into the quiet, after a while, in a different voice, reading down the small things at the bottom of the list. "In the lower ward, off the clay line. They cost less than the engine's chimney. They go in."

Nobody argued.



The stones came last, because Ferra had put them last.

She had laid them before Vantic already decided, in the House's form, and it was the shortest sheet she had ever laid: twelve stones, the prince's own property, privately held, lent by him to the barony's wall for its watch. No cost to the barony. No sale. No figure.

Vantic read it. She read it again. She looked up at Emeric, and then at Ferra, and then down at the sheet.

She did not ask what kind of stones. She did not ask where a prince with a debt to the House had got twelve stones to lend that were not on any book she had ever been shown. Emeric watched her not ask. It was a very precise thing, the way she did not ask: a pause the exact length of the question, in which the question was considered, and set down, and left where it lay.

She wrote under Ferra's line, in her small grey hand.

The prince's property is the prince's.

And initialed it, and blotted it, and turned the page.



Armand saw the drawings that evening, because Emeric left them on the end of his bed while he moved a stack of the coast off the chair, and Armand had never in his life been able to leave a drawing face down.

He looked at the first sheet until Emeric had cleared the chair and sat. Then the second. Then he put them both down on the blanket and looked at Emeric over the top of them with an expression Emeric had last seen on him in the tower, when Emeric was twelve, and had asked whether a stone could be made to hold two things at once.

"Watch stones," Armand said.

"Is that what they're called?"

"That's what they're called in every treasury in the kingdom. The crown has them on its strongroom doors. The Circle has a row of them down the stair to the vault where it keeps the stones it doesn't want the Church to count. Every merchant on the coast with more silver than sense has one over his counting house door." Armand tapped the drawing. "They're useless."

"Why?"

"Because of how they work." Armand settled back, and his voice changed, the way it always changed when there was a thing to be explained and somebody in the room who did not know it yet. "Every charged stone flinches. You know that. You've felt it, if you've ever had one on a bench while somebody walked past. Something living comes into the stone's field and the stone feels it come, and stirs. In the tower we call it the flinch, and in the catalogue it's down among a stone's faults, with cracking and clouding, because it's a waste. A stone that flinches spends a little of itself every time. Nobody wants it."

"Except the treasuries."

"Except the treasuries. Some mage, four hundred years ago, had the bright idea of cutting a sign on the face of a lamp stone that turns the flinch into light. So when something comes near the door, the stone glows." Armand held up one finger. "It glows for a thief. It also glows for the rat. It glows for the cat that's after the rat. It glows for the guard, who's been told to stand next to it. Every time it glows it spends a little more of itself, so a door that anybody actually goes through drains the stone in a week, and you need a mage to fill it again." A second finger. "It glows, which tells the thief exactly where it is, so he goes round it. You need a man sitting where he can see it, so you need a man for every stone, so you might as well have the man and save the stone." A third. "It reaches a few paces. It's lit or it isn't; it can't tell you a rat from a regiment. And anybody with a good eye can copy the sign off the face of it in an afternoon, so every thief worth hanging knows how it's cut and how to cheat it." He put his hand down on the blanket. "So they sit on vault doors nobody goes through, and glow for rats, and nobody trusts them. Useless."

"Mine won't do any of that."

Armand looked at him.

"It won't glow for a rat. It won't glow at all. It won't spend anything while it's waiting. It'll tell the board how much is there, not just that something is. And there won't be anything on the face of it for anyone to copy."

"How?"

The fire ticked. Emeric thought of the room, and the bench in it, and the light, and the hours that were minutes, and the stones he had sat with there with his whole attention inside them as if they were rooms of their own.

"I'll show you when it works," he said.

Armand did not say anything for a while. He picked up the drawing again and looked at it, and then put it down, and looked at Emeric, and Emeric could see him trying the question from three directions, the way he tried every question, and finding that none of the directions went anywhere he could see.

"When it works," Armand said at last, quite quietly. "Yes. I'd like that."

He did not look like a man who had been told no. He looked like a man who had just realized that his old student had been somewhere he had not, and was not yet going to say where.

Walking up the keep stair afterward, Emeric thought about what he had not said.

A sign cut on the face of a stone was a word painted on a door. One word. Glow. It could say that much and no more, because the face of a stone would hold only a few such words before they ran into each other, and every one of them was there for anyone to read. The armourers had been cutting their workings on the outsides of stones the same way for four hundred years, correct and clumsy and copied from master to apprentice, because the outside was the only place anybody could reach.

In the room he could go inside. Not cut the face. Cut the stone, from within, the way you would write in a book and not on its cover, line after line, as much as it would hold. Wake for this much and no less. Press so hard for this much, and harder for more. Spend nothing, ever, until something comes. And all of it inside, where nobody would ever see it but a man who could do what he did, and he had never yet met one.

The treasuries had a word on a door. He was going to put a page inside the wall.



The lamp was lit in the ward when he went past it, very late, and the side door was open for the heat.

Imani was at the desk by the door. She had the register open on one side of her and a new book open on the other, a clean one, bound in the grey board Ferra used for her own, and she was copying. Line by line, name by name, every entry since the ward opened, in her square careful hand, the pen going steadily and the lamp drawn close. She did not look up when he stood in the door. She had heard him coming up the street; he could see that she had.

"A copy," he said.

"So next year runs on." She did not stop writing. "Ferra keeps one. I keep one. If the ward burns, the case doesn't."

He stood there. He did not know what he had come to say. He had known, at the trestle, that there was nothing to say, and he had come anyway.

"Give me half," he said.

The pen stopped.

She looked at him then, for the first time, a long flat look in the lamplight, as if she were deciding whether he had earned it. Then she pulled the register toward her, and found a page about half way through, and tore a strip off the corner of a spoiled sheet and laid it in as a marker, and pushed the second half of the register across the desk to him with a second pen and the inkpot between them.

He sat down on the stool on the other side of the desk and began.

They copied for most of two hours without speaking. The names went by under his pen, and the days, and the causes in her square hand, burns and fevers and the coughing sickness and a child's broken arm and a dock hand's crushed fingers and a woman from Cooper's Row who had come in four times in one winter with the same cough and gone home the fifth time under a sheet. He knew some of the names. He knew more of them than he would have thought. The lamp burned down and she trimmed it without stopping.

His hand began to go somewhere in the second hour. The right one. The skin had healed on it a year ago, after the bridge, but it had healed tight, and when he held a pen too long the fingers drew in toward the palm of their own accord and stayed there, and he could not open them by wanting to. They did it now. He put the pen down and tried to work them open against the edge of the desk without her seeing.

She saw.

She put her own pen down. She reached across the desk and took his hand, without asking, the way she took any hand that was put in front of her at that desk, and turned it palm up under the lamp, and looked at it, the old shine of the burns across the heel and the fingers curled in. Then she put her thumbs into the palm, both of them, and pressed, hard, and worked them out slowly toward the base of each finger in turn, one at a time, the way you would work a knot out of wet rope.

It hurt. Then it stopped hurting. The fingers came open under her thumbs one by one and lay flat.

She did not let go.

She held his hand open on the desk between them under the lamp, with her thumbs still in the palm of it, for longer than it needed, a good deal longer, looking down at it and not at him. He did not move. He could feel her pulse in her thumbs, or his own, and could not tell which. Out in the street a cart went past, late, and its wheels went away down the hill toward the lower ward until he could not hear them any more.

Then she let go, and picked up his pen from where he had put it down, and put it back in his hand, and closed his fingers round it with hers.

"Next year," she said, and went back to her half.
