Chapter 199 - The Drop House

The house went up in the upper corner of the yard in seven weeks, against the rock, where Fletcher had said it would.

Cade watched it go up from the slip, between jobs, the way he watched everything on the lower water that he was not himself doing. He had been Fletcher's second since there had been a yard to be second in, and he had learned in that time that a man who kept one eye on the whole yard never had to run to any part of it. So he saw the footings go in, on the rock the two earth mages had cut the wall's footing down to the year before, and the walls come up in the ridge's own grey stone, and the roof go on in slate, and the stack rise beside it in brick, square, the height of four men, with an iron cap. It was an engine house. It looked like any engine house at the rail works. Anybody who had ever walked past the works would have said so, and nobody who was not already inside the yard wall would ever walk past this one.

What went into it came through the yard gate on the works' wagons, a piece at a time, under canvas, and some of it was plain and some of it was not.

The plain part came first. A boiler from Bram's shop, riveted and proved on the works' own pump to twice its mark with Tobe going round the seams with a lamp, the way it had been done since the first engine. An engine off the rail works pattern, a little smaller. A dynamo, which Cade had never seen before, though he had heard the one in the sawmill shed humming at night when the wind was right: two great iron shoes and a drum between them, wound and wound again with copper, the new red copper from the prince's hill, that the island women had wrapped in thread and run through the soap house's wax the way they had wrapped the first. This one was twice the size of the first. It came on a wagon of its own and took eight men and a sheerlegs to set on its bed.

The other part came in pieces nobody on the wagons could have put a name to. Curved iron segments that bolted into a ring as wide as the house's back room. Eight iron weights, each the size of a small anvil, each with a cup cast in its top. Eight sleeves. Rollers, pins, a shaft as thick as Cade's wrist, brass levers in a box of straw. And plate, a great deal of plate, the rail works' heavy plate, and firebrick, and sand.

On the fourth day of the fittings, one of the yard men came through the door with a cam segment on his shoulder, bent under it, and stopped in the middle of the floor and looked round at the boiler and the engine and the dynamo and the half-built partition at the back.

"Where's this go?" he said.

Cade was at the partition with a plumb line. He looked at the segment, and then through the gap in the partition to the ring half laid on its rollers behind it, and the weights standing in a row against the back wall where the forge's men had left them, each with its grey stone set asleep in its cup.

"In the back," he said. "With the drops."

He did not mean anything by it. The weights dropped; Wes had told him so, standing at the pair's lock in the shed behind the outside foundry in the first week, with the knock going in the box like a mill. One goes down while the other comes up. The drops. It was only what they were.

The yard man took the segment through. The next morning Cade heard him tell another man to take a crate of pins "down the drop house," and the other man did not ask what he meant. By the end of the month somebody had written it on the door in chalk, DROP HOUSE, in the square capitals of a man who had learned his letters at the works school, and nobody rubbed it off. Ferra came down on the quarter's last Saturday to walk the yard's accounts with Fletcher, and stood in front of the door for a moment with her book open, and wrote the name at the head of a new page, and that was what it was.

Inside, when it was finished, it was one long room with a wall across the back of it.

From the door: the boiler and its firebox on the left, with the coal bunker behind, and the engine beside it on its bed. The dynamo in the middle of the floor, squat and heavy, its drum on a shaft that ran the width of the room. On the engine's side of the dynamo, a clutch; on the far side, another. Bram's clutches, the pattern he had put on the rolls' wheel so that one wheel could draw rail by day and wire by night, and throw one out and the other in without stopping. Here the shaft ran on through the partition in a packed collar, and what turned it from that side was not coal.

And the partition. It went from floor to roof and wall to wall: rail works plate, riveted, lined on the back with a course of firebrick and behind that, Cade had seen before they closed it, sand. Sand between two walls of iron, a hand's breadth thick. The prince had drawn it that way. Cade had heard him and Bram argue about the thickness of it for most of an afternoon, and had not understood the argument, and had understood that the prince wanted a man standing on this side of it, with his hand flat on the iron, to feel nothing through it at all.

In the partition, at a man's chest height, the lock: the outer door, the bar, the inner door, and the key through its collar, the same as on the pair, made heavier. Above the lock, the dial. A brass face as broad as a dinner plate, a needle, and one line scribed across the brass, and nothing else on it at all. Under the dial, on a card screwed to the iron, in the prince's hand and then again underneath in Wes's larger one, so that nobody could say he had not been able to read it: One flask to a mark. Not before the needle reaches it. Never two. Write it down.

And on a shelf by the lock, the book. Ferra's board-bound book, four columns ruled, and a pot with two pens in it, and a cloth.

Behind the partition, Cade knew, because he had helped set the ring on its rollers before they closed it: eight sleeves standing round a cast ring as wide as a cart is long, eight weights in them, each with its stone in its cup and its neck drawn open, the ring's top face cut in a wave of five swells that Jorrin had spent eleven days truing on a lathe the works had had to build to take it. The shaft up through the middle of the ring and out through the bottle's side into the collar. The deciding stone in its iron housing on the inner wall, where the levers could reach it: Bram's, written by Bram alone in the pattern store over three nights, as he had written the pair's, the same rule made eightfold. Lever up, heavy. Lever down, light. No lever, nothing. And the governor's balls outside on the shaft, where anybody could see them.

The eight stones had come down from Grayrock in the locked box in the van, with Hobb sitting on it. Eight blanks of the kept portion, four ounces each, weighed on Ilsa's beam and written into Lusk's book as raised and not sold. Cade had been at the gate when Ferra's clerk walked them in, and had heard the clerk say the figure to Bram under his breath, the way a man says the price of a horse he could never afford: two pounds of stone at the Crown's figure, twenty-two Gold and eight Silver, that the barony would never see a Copper of because it would never sell it. Bram had said nothing. He had carried the box to the forge himself.

Nobody called it the bottle but the prince, and Bram when he forgot. The yard called it the back.

It ran for the first time at dusk on a Thursday, with the prince and Bram and Armand on his stool and Fletcher in the door, and Wes at the lock, because the prince had said Wes should pour the first one and Wes had not argued. It took six flasks to fill, one after another, with the needle on the dial lying on its pin for the first five as if nothing had happened, and Wes pouring each one only when the prince nodded, and writing each one down before he poured the next, even with the prince standing there. On the sixth the needle lifted. It went up the brass slowly, like water up a tank, and past the mark, and stood near the top, and behind the partition, dull through the iron and the sand and the iron, came the knock.

It was not the pair's knock. It was deeper, and it did not come singly. It came in a roll, eight weights on a ring of five swells, never two at the same place, so that the knocks ran into each other the way a heavy cart's wheels run over a plank road, a long low continuous grumble with a beat in it. The shaft came through the collar turning. The governor's balls lifted and swung out and settled. Bram threw the clutch on the drop side, and the dynamo's drum took the shaft, and slowed it for a breath, and the balls came in and the lever leaned and the grumble deepened, and the drum went round.

Nobody said anything for a while. Then Fletcher, in the door, said: "Well. That's a noise," and went back to his slip.

The wire had been going up all through the building, a pole at a time.

It was the pair's wire, the summer's copper, drawn day and night in the shed behind the outside foundry while the pair knocked and Tobe fed it flasks. Bram's men took it out of the yard gate on its reels and up the slope, on poles of peeled larch with the little white glazed knobs at the top that Cade had seen at the quarry, a mile of it, up to the south gate of New Kaldo and through the gate and along the inside of the ring road, round, to the first quarter's southern corner, where the second tower stood at the canal's edge.

The prince asked Cade to come up for the plug. He asked on the morning of the third day the house had run, at the lock, with Wes standing by.

"Wes can mind it," the prince said. "The needle won't want him till the evening. I'd like the man who pours to see what the pouring does." He looked at the dial. "Once. So that you know it's for something."

So Cade walked up the mile beside the wire with the prince and Bram, in the heat, with the lower water at their backs and the wall of the city ahead, and it was the first time he had been through the south gate since the wall was closed. He had built boats his whole life. He had not been inside many buildings as big as the one he was walking into, which was a city.

The second tower stood on its course at the corner of the quarter, new stone, with its wind pump on the mast and its vane turning lazily in a breeze that was barely there. At its foot, the hand pump, with its beam lashed down, and nobody on it. And beside the hand pump the pump house, the little square stone house with the slate roof, with its door open, and a crowd round the door that was not meant to be a crowd.

Weekes was there. He spoke as little as a man could and still be a master builder, and he was standing just inside the pump house door with his hands behind his back, looking at the floor. Imani was there, with her arms folded. Ferra, with her book. A dozen of Weekes's men who had no business being there and had found reasons. And the tower's ganger, with his slate.

On the pump house floor, where Cade had heard there had been nothing but a wooden plug in the mouth of a clay pipe, there was a machine.

It was small. It was the dynamo in the drop house made little, a pair of iron shoes the size of loaves and a wound drum between them the size of a cheese, on a bed of oak, with a belt from its pulley to the pump beside it: an iron pump on the ring canal's intake, with a rising main going up into the tower. Bram's second machine. Cade had heard the prince tell Bram in the drop house that it would go, and heard Bram ask how, and heard the prince say that a thing that made current when you turned it would turn when you gave it current, and heard Bram not believe him, and build it anyway.

The wire came along the ring road on its poles to the corner, and down the last pole, and stopped, coiled, on the pump house's outside wall, beside the mouth of the duct, where the clay pipe came up out of the ground from the trench it had lain empty in since the first quarter was drawn.

The prince looked at Weekes.

"It's yours," he said.

Weekes looked at him. Then he knelt down on the pump house floor, slowly, because he was not young, and put his hand on the wooden plug in the mouth of the duct. He did not pull it at once. He knelt there with his hand on it for a moment, and Cade saw his face, which did not do much, and understood that this was a man who had laid that duct in the trench a hand wider than the main because the prince had asked for it, without knowing what it was for, and had walked past the plug in its mouth every week since, and had looked at it every time.

He pulled it.

Bram's men fed the wire in through the duct from outside, and it came up out of the clay pipe on the pump house floor, and Bram took the ends and made them fast to the little machine's posts with a spanner, and stood back.

Nothing happened. Cade had half expected that; nothing ever happened the first time, on the lower water or anywhere else. Then the prince said something to Bram about a switch at the drop house end, and a boy ran, and they stood in the pump house and waited for a boy to run a mile, which was a long time, and Imani said so, and nobody answered her.

Then the little drum turned.

It turned slowly at first, as if it were thinking about it, and then faster, with a hum rising in it like the hum from the sawmill shed, and the belt took up, and the pump beside it began to stroke. On the ring, the intake sucked. In the tower's rising main there was a cough, and a gulp, and then a sound Cade knew from every bilge pump he had ever stood over, water going up a pipe in pulses.

Outside, the vane on the wind pump's mast hung almost still in the heat. The hand pump's beam was lashed down. Nobody was pumping. The water went up the tower anyway.

The ganger went outside and stood under the tower with his slate and looked up at the tank as though he might see through the timber. Weekes got up off his knees. He did not say anything. He brushed the dust off his breeches and went out and stood beside the ganger. Imani did not unfold her arms.

"Three dawns," she said to the prince. "The tank at the mark at dawn, three mornings running, with the wind or without it. Then I'll lift the stay."

"Three," said the prince.

She got her three dawns. Cade heard about them on the lower water, because by then everybody on the lower water heard everything that happened at the tower, since it was the lower water's wire. The tank stood at its mark at dawn on the first morning and the second and the third, and on the fourth Imani wrote under her stay in the book, in her own small square hand, Lifted, and the date, and Ferra countersigned it. The fourth draw came off the island that week with their beds and their chests on handcarts, forty households, up the road and through the north gate and down the ring road to the second street by the tower, with children running ahead to the troughs to turn the taps and see.

The river dropped the same week.

It did it every year, the lighter men said, at the end of summer, when the snow was gone off the hills to the north and the rain had not come yet. The lower water went down a foot at the quays and showed its mud, and the stream that fed the sawmill race went down with it. Cade did not think about the sawmill. He had no reason to. But on the evening of the day the fourth draw moved in he walked up to the south wall with Bram's wire gang to look at the last of the poles, and stood on the wall walk at dusk, and saw it.

To the north, across the flat, where the ridge came down to the quarry, the two white lights on their masts had come on at dusk as they did every night. They were not white. They were yellow, like a lamp with its wick turned down, and as he watched they went yellower, and dimmer, and one of them flickered. Down on the race, a mile off, the sawmill wheel was turning slow in the low water, and the thing in the shed beside it was humming low, and the light it made at the end of its half mile of copper was not enough to cut stone by. Before the full dark came, he saw the lights of the night gang's lanterns moving away from the quarry face in a line, going home early.

And to the south, behind him, over the fold of the land where the yard lay at the ridge foot, a white glow stood up into the dusk. Steady. Hard. The masts over the dock.

Bram, beside him on the wall, had seen it too. He looked north at the quarry, and south at the yard, and did not say anything, and Cade remembered that he had heard the prince say in the spring, in the pattern store, that the river would drop at the end of summer and the wheel would slow. Cade had not been listening then. He had been fetching a flask.

From the wall he could also see the second quarter, across the ring canal, to the east of the first. Its first two streets were roofed now, in new slate, and on the road from the island, a long way off in the last of the light, there was a line of carts already coming, loaded high, with people walking beside them. The fifth draw, Bram said. Ilsa had moved it up a month.

The dock took the lights at night from the first week.

They were on masts, four of them, at the corners of the great hole that had been cut in the rock at the ridge foot where hull four would one day lie, and under them Bram put a night gang to work on the dock's walls. The walls went up in the ridge's grey stone, laid in mortar of lime and the Emberfell pumice that the prince had stopped selling in the winter, the mortar that set under water where lime alone would lie soft as cheese in its joints forever. By day one gang laid it. By night, under the white lights, another. The dock had been a thing that could not be built in a season, because a season was not long enough and the tides would not wait for the day. It was going up in one.

Cade watched it from the drop house door, on his breaks, with the grumble of the drops going on behind him through the iron. The men on the dock's walls under the lights, and the mortar boards, and the long shadows. The tide coming in at the gate of the hole, black, and going out again. Fletcher, some nights, standing on the edge of it with his hands behind his back, looking down into it the way he looked at a hull on the slip.

And the stack.

He noticed it on the first evening, from the yard gate, walking back from the quay with a sack of flasks on his shoulder from the Hod's night. The coal engine was kept warm, the way the book said. The fire was banked in the box, and the boiler kept in steam, and the engine stood turning nothing, with its clutch out, ready; and at its gauge, day and night, stood a watcher from the works, a lean slow man called Wick who had worked the force pump on the rail works boiler's proof and never let anybody forget it, who had the right to stop it and answered to nobody. That was the rule at every boiler in the barony. It did not matter that this one was not driving anything. It was a boiler.

From the stack came a thread of smoke. Thin, grey, going straight up in the still evening and bending over at the top where the air moved.

A stack like that should be black, Cade thought. He had seen the rail works' stacks when the engine was driving the stand, and the lung's, and the Emberfell's when she was under way. A stack that was lighting a dock with four white lights and pumping a tower a mile off should be pouring it out.

He thought about it once, standing at the yard gate with the sack on his shoulder. Then he thought that it was not his job to think about it, and that he knew exactly why it was a thread and not a column, because he was carrying the reason on his shoulder; and that whoever else looked at it and wondered was somebody else's business, the prince's or Ferra's or Ward's. He went in and put the sack in the flask store, the iron room behind the lock, and locked it, and wrote the count on the slate on its door, and did not think about the stack again.

The first night he stood the whole of it alone was the eleventh.

Wes had taught him for ten. He had taught him standing beside him at the lock with his hands behind his back, and the first thing he had shown him was not the door or the bar or the key, but the needle and the line across the brass, and the book. He had told him about the pair's box, and the night the bench snagged, and the second flask, and the soft panel going into the sand, in about as many words as Cade would have used himself, and then he had said: "It doesn't matter what she sounds like. She'll sound like she's dying when she's only working. You look at the needle. You don't listen to her."

Cade had held a hull in the air with his hands on a rail two miles over the sea, with Tobe beside him and the deck going away underneath them, until the skin came off his palms. He had the lines on them still, fine and pale. He understood about not listening to her.

So on the eleventh night Wes went home to sleep, and Cade stood at the dial.

The house was quiet but for the drops. The boiler ticked in its banked heat; Wick sat on his stool by the gauge with his arms folded and his eyes open. The dynamo's drum turned, and hummed, and out through the wall the wire ran up the slope in the dark to the south gate and round the ring road to the tower, and to the four masts over the dock, where the white lights burned over the night gang. And behind the partition, through the iron and the sand and the iron, the long low grumble with a beat in it.

The needle was falling. It fell slowly. It had been falling since the last flask, which was in the book in Wes's hand eight hours before, and it would fall for a while yet. Cade watched it. He had nothing else to do but watch it, and he was a man who had stood the helm-watch on a ship hanging in the band for three summers of long nights with nothing to do but watch a lever, and he did not mind.

When it was a finger's breadth above the line, he took a flask from the rack and read the number on its lid, and opened the outer door of the lock, and put the flask in, lid to the socket, and shut the outer door, and threw the bar. He opened the inner door. He put his hand on the key's handle.

And then he did not turn it.

He stood with his hand on the key and his eyes on the needle and waited. The needle fell. Behind the partition the grumble went a little longer, a little heavier, the knock in it stretching like a man walking uphill, and he did not listen to it. The needle came down the last of the brass, a hair at a time, and touched the line.

He turned the key a half turn and held it, and felt the shiver go through the handle into his hand, and back, and the inner door, and the bar, and the outer, and the empty out and into the rack with its lid up. The needle climbed. The grumble deepened and quickened. Out on the shaft the governor's balls swung out and eased it.

He took the book off the shelf and wrote the line. The hour. On the mark. The number off the lid. Cade.

Then he put the book back, and wiped the pen, and stood at the dial again with his hands behind his back, the way Wes did, and watched the needle, which had a long way to fall before it would want him.
