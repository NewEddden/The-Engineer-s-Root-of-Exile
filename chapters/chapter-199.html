Chapter 199 - Wayford

The paper cart went north on the ninth of April behind the morning train, on a flat wagon at the back with its shafts tied up and its horse in the box behind the engine, complaining.

It was the first time a horse had ridden the Wayford line, and the line was not long enough yet to make it worth the horse's trouble. Fifty-three miles of rail, thirteen past the river crossing, to where the rails ended in the middle of a field with a water tower and a hut and a stack of sleepers and a sign Paskin had painted himself, RAILHEAD, with the date under it in chalk because he moved the sign every month. Beyond it the capital road went on north on its own, the old way, as it had for three hundred years. Ward had wanted the cart to go by road the whole way, so that anybody watching would see a prince's cart go north by road like any prince's cart. Ferra had wanted the horse spared fifty miles. Ferra had won, as far as the railhead; and from the railhead on it was road.

So it was a prince's cart going north, from there, and anybody watching could see it.

That was the point. Emeric rode beside it in a good coat with the four baronies' arms on his saddle cloth and did not hide, and the cart behind him was painted and lettered on both sides in Tess's square capitals, KALDO PAPER, and under its tarpaulin were forty reams of it. Not rag. Straw. The first forty reams off the straw line, pale and even and a little stiff, with a faint smell of struck matches still in them if you put your nose to the bale; and at the bottom of the load, in a small strong box with a hasp, a set of steel punches wrapped in oiled cloth that had come out of the cellar under the long room on the second of April and nowhere else on earth.

Ward rode on his other side. Saban rode behind, with the carter, talking about the price of horses. Two of the garrison rode at the back, for the look of it. It was six days from the railhead to Wayford with fresh horses at every posting house, along a road that was dry for once and full of other carts, and on every one of those days somebody they passed read the lettering on the cart's side out loud to somebody else.



The junction was a mile short of the town, at the top of the long hill down to the river, where the woods came down to the road.

Emeric had been through it several times before, by carriage, every time with something else to think about; and every time he had seen it without seeing it. A cluster of buildings at a fork. The capital road going on down the hill into Wayford, and a second road coming in from the left out of the woods, Lisle's woods, a carters' road rutted deep with the wheels of a hundred years; and between the two, in the fork, a long stone house of two storeys with a weighing shed built against its side, open at the front, with a great iron beam hung from its roof truss on a chain. A weathervane on the house's ridge in the shape of a bale. A painted board on a post at the roadside, a big one, covered in figures.

And a chain across the road.

He had not seen a chain before. Perhaps they only put it up for carts. It hung between two posts at the height of a horse's chest, black iron, sagging in the middle, and beside the near post a man in a grey coat sat on a stool with a ledger on his knee and a cudgel leaning on the post beside him, and stood up as the cart came on, unhurried, and put his hand on the chain.

The carter pulled up. The horse put its head down to the grass at the roadside.

"Paper," said the man in the grey coat. He read it off the cart's side. He did not look at Emeric at all; he looked at the cart, the way a man looks at a thing that is going to be his business for the next quarter of an hour. "Into the shed."

"What for?" said Saban pleasantly, from behind.

"Weighing." The man nodded at the board on its post. "Paper's by the bale, twenty reams the bale, at the posted rate, through the junction. Rag's by the hundredweight. Galls by the sack." He looked at the cart again. "Forty reams. Two bales." He looked, at last, at Saban. "Into the shed, master, and we'll have you through in no time."

There were four of them, Emeric saw now. The man with the ledger. Two more by the shed in the same grey coats with the same cudgels, leaning on the posts, watching. And a fourth in the window of the stone house, behind the glass, at a high clerk's desk, with his pen up, waiting to write.

Ward's horse moved, under him, a half step forward, without Ward having seemed to touch it. His right hand had come off the rein and was resting on his thigh, an inch from his sword's grip, open and still.

"Ward," said Emeric.

The hand did not move. It did not go to the grip either.

"Whose junction is it?" said Emeric.

The man in the grey coat looked up at him. Took in the coat, and the saddle cloth with the four baronies' arms worked on it in thread, and the face; and something went across his own face and was put away again, very fast, by somebody who had been told what to do with it.

"The Staple's, Highness," he said. "Under the crown's charter. Same as it's always been." He did not take his hand off the chain. "Into the shed, if you please. Every cart through the junction. Rag, gall, ream. The charter doesn't say except princes."

Emeric sat his horse in the road and looked at the man, and at the chain, and at the board.

He held Wayford. He had held it for four years, by a judgment in the crown's own court with a fat clerk's seal on it. He had Wayford's books every week in Aster's narrow hand. He had read them all; he had read every line, barns and boats and the ferry dues and the mill rents and the price of rope, and he had never once read the word Staple in any of them, because it was not in any of them. He knew that now, sitting there. It was not the barony's. It sat on the barony's road, in the fork of the barony's two roads, with a chain across them, and it was not his.

He could have the chain down in a breath. Ward could have it down in less, and the man on the stool would not get to his cudgel. And then there would be a prince of the blood who had broken a crown charter on a public road in front of a clerk at a window with his pen up, on the first day he had ever come to Wayford openly.

"Into the shed," said Emeric.

Ward's hand went back to the rein.

They weighed it. Both bales, on the iron beam, with the carter and the two grey coats heaving them up onto the hook and the beam's long arm going down and settling and the clerk in the window calling the weight out through the glass. They weighed the box of punches, too, because it was on the cart; and the man with the ledger looked at it, and at the hasp, and asked what it was, and Emeric said tools, which was true. The man wrote tools. The clerk in the window wrote something else, longer, and did not say what.

It came to two Silver and three Copper. Saban paid it, counting the coin out into the ledger man's palm as carefully as he had counted it onto the Duvrenne beach-reeve's book; and asked for a receipt; and got one, which surprised Emeric, on a printed form with the Staple's bale at its head, with the weight filled in and the rate and the sum, and a clerk's mark, and, at the foot, small, in type, the words By Charter of the Crown.

The chain came down. The cart went through.

Halfway down the hill, out of earshot, Saban rode up beside him and held out the receipt without a word. Emeric took it and read it twice.

"Two Silver," said Saban. "On forty reams. That's a little over half a Copper the ream." He was quiet. "I've sold Kaldo paper in Wayford since the autumn. Small lots. Through a factor here." He looked at the receipt. "I knew there was a toll at the junction. Everybody pays the junction. I put it in my costs as the junction, the way you put in the ferry." He looked down the hill at the town. "I never once asked whose it was."

"Neither did I," said Emeric.

Ward, on his other side, said nothing at all, all the way down the hill.



Wayford was a paper town. You smelled it before you saw it, coming down to the river: the sour, wet, rotting smell of rags in the retting vats, and under it lime, and under that the river, which ran brown and fast below the town over a long weir, and turned the wheels of eight mills along its near bank one after another, each with its own leat and its own wheel and its own vat house, long and low and steaming. The vats. Every family in Wayford that mattered had a vat house, or had had one, or had a cousin with one; and down the lanes between them, in every yard, on every line, on every fence, hung sheets of paper drying in the spring wind, thousands of them, so that the whole lower town flapped.

Grey paper. Rag. He had seen sheets of it in Kaldo. He had seen one, last summer, on the long table under Rylan's hand, with THE BOOKKEEPER'S BOX across its top in heavy black letter; and he looked at the sheets on the lines as they rode down past them and thought about that, and then made himself stop, because Saban had followed that grey paper north for nine days and lost it, and it had not been Wayford's.

Ambry's ink house was on the square.

It had been a fine house once. Three storeys, timber-framed, with a carved bracket over the door in the shape of an inkhorn, and the name painted under the eaves in gold letters gone brown, AMBRY, INK AND PRINT, SINCE; and the date after SINCE had flaked off entirely and nobody had painted it back. The ground-floor windows were full of jars. Iron gall, the black writing ink every clerk in the north used, that bit into the paper so that it never came off; in stone jars, and glass ones, and little horn ones for pens in a pocket. In the back, through the yard, a press, idle. The type for it lay in a case beside it, worn so far that half the letters printed as smudges, and the e's had all gone round.

Old Ambry was seventy if he was a day, with ink in every crack of his hands and the cracks very deep; and he sat in his counting room at the back of the shop with Emeric across the table from him and the whole of Kaldo's offer in front of him on a sheet of Kaldo paper, and read it with his nose an inch from the page, and put it down.

"You want to buy my house," he said.

"Yes."

"And keep me in it."

"As master. Of the ink and the press both. At a wage, and a share of what the press makes, for as long as you want to work." Emeric put his hand flat on the table. "And then your journeyman after you, if he's any good. I don't want a Kaldo man running a Wayford ink house. I want Wayford ink made in Wayford, by the people who know how."

Ambry looked at him with his old wet eyes.

"Why?"

"Because Kaldo can't make enough paper, and can't print enough on what it makes, and we've promised a queen ten thousand sheets by the summer." Emeric looked at the press in the back, through the door. "And because I want there to be a press in the north that isn't mine."

The old man thought about that for a while. Then he said, as if it followed: "I can't buy type."

"I know."

"I've not been able to buy type in twenty years. It comes from the capital, from two founders, and they sell it to the capital's printers first and to everybody else when the capital's done with it, at the capital's price." He moved his ink-black hand at the case of smudged letters through the door. "I've been printing broadsides on that since my father died. Fair notices. Funeral cards. The e's went the year of the plague." He looked at the offer. "You can buy my house, Highness. Every lord in the north could've bought it any time these twenty years for what you're offering, and none of them wanted it. But you'll not get anything off that press that anybody'll want to read."

Emeric reached down beside his chair for the strong box, and put it on the table, and undid the hasp.

The punches lay in their oiled cloth in two rows, one for every letter and every figure and every stop, upper case and lower: little bars of hard steel the length of a finger, square in section, and on the end of each one, cut in relief, perfect, the letter. Backward. He had cut them in the forge on the second of April in a single night, and written the reason in the forge's log above the run, as he wrote every run's: For Wayford's type. To leave Wayford. Not to come back.

Ambry looked at them and did not touch them.

"Do you know what these are?" said Emeric.

"My grandfather had a set." The old man's voice had changed. "Iron. He cut them himself, by hand, with a graver and a file, for one fount, over two winters, and went blind doing it. They went to the founder in the capital to pay his debts when he died." He put out one finger, at last, and laid it on the end of a punch, on the face of a lower-case e. "These aren't cut by hand."

"No."

"Nobody could cut these by hand." Ambry lifted the e out of the cloth and held it to the window, and turned it, and looked down the face of it at the light. "Not one line out. Not one." He put it down very carefully. "Who did?"

"Kaldo," said Emeric. "Ask me nothing else about them, and nobody else either, and they're yours." He put the lid back on the box. "There's a bell founder on your river. Down by the weir. Saban asked."

"He casts bells and pots. Badly, his father says."

"He casts brass." Emeric pushed the box across the table. "You strike each of these into a little bar of copper, cold, with a hammer, once, and it leaves the letter in the copper, sunk, the right way round. That's your matrix. One for every letter. You put the matrix in the bottom of a hand mould, a little steel box the founder can make you, that opens in two halves, and pour type metal in through the top, lead and tin and a little antimony, and open the mould, and out drops a letter. A whole letter, on its own body, the right height, the right width, to a hair. Then you do it again. Ten thousand times." He looked at the old man. "That's how the founders in the capital do it. They just don't tell you. And when your e's wear round, you don't wait twenty years for the capital. You cast new e's in an afternoon, from the matrix, as many as you like."

Ambry sat a good while with his hand on the box.

"Two founders in the capital," he said at last, slowly, "and one in Wayford."

"One in Wayford," said Emeric.

The old man took his hand off the box and held it out across the table, ink-black to the wrist, and Emeric shook it.



The Keelings partnered. It took an afternoon and two pots of tea in the Keelings' kitchen, which was the kitchen of the biggest vat house on the river, with the vat house roaring through the wall; and in the end it was the grandmother who decided it, a tiny bent old woman in a lace cap who had sat at the end of the table through the whole of Saban's figures without saying a word, and at the end of them put out her hand for one of the straw sheets that Emeric had brought, and held it to the window, and tore it, slowly, across the grain, and looked at the torn edge, and smelled it.

"Matches," she said. "Not rags." She put the halves down. "It's weak across. It'll want a little rag in it, for letters. Not for books." She looked at her sons. "We'll take the straw. And we'll put a tenth rag in, our way, and it'll be better than his." She looked at Emeric. "No offence, Highness."

"None," said Emeric, and meant it.

Hesketh supplied. He was a farmer with eight hundred acres of wheat on the flats below the weir and a grandson at a smith's anvil, and he had been burning his straw off every autumn for forty years and cursing the smoke, and when Saban told him what a cartload of it would fetch at the Keelings' yard he laughed so hard he had to sit down on his own mounting block.

Jonas Pell refused.

He had refused Saban's letter in the autumn, politely, and he refused Emeric in person, at the gate of his vat house, which stood alone a mile up the river above the town, below the woods, on land that was not Wayford's but Lord Lisle's; and he refused politely again, with his hat in his hand, a broad grey careful man who looked past Emeric's shoulder up the hill toward the junction the whole time he was talking.

"I've my own rag men," he said. "And my own vats. And my own terms." He did not say whose. "I'll not change them, Highness. Not for you, nor anybody. I'm sorry."

"Whose terms?" said Emeric.

Pell looked at him, then, properly; and then away, up the hill again.

"My own," he said, and put his hat on, and went in, and shut the gate.



Ottoline Fesk was on her doorstep when he crossed the square at dusk, with her spectacles on her forehead and her arms folded, watching the lamplighter go round.

The stopped clock over her door still said twenty minutes to four. She kept the Wayford shop still, and came south to the end of Ferra's counting-room table when the work wanted her, and rang Ferra on the telephone most mornings to argue about carrying; and the two of them had long since settled into the friendship of two women who would never once agree about the right way to add.

"I heard you bought Ambry," she said.

"I did."

"And the Keelings. And Hesketh's straw." She looked at the lamplighter. "The whole square knew by noon. The whole square knew what you paid at the junction before you were at the bottom of the hill."

"Did it."

"It always does." Ottoline unfolded her arms and folded them the other way. "Wayford's made paper for a hundred years, Highness. More. My husband's grandfather used to say the first sheet in Randillia was dipped on that river." She nodded down the lane, at the vat houses going dark one by one along the water, the last of the sheets coming in off the lines. "And in a hundred years Wayford's never once been paid what it was worth. Not once. The vats make it, and the rag men make it, and the carters cart it, and it goes up the hill and through the junction, and comes out the other side worth twice what it went in, and none of the twice ever comes back down the hill." She looked at him. "I've watched it go up the hill for twenty years. I'm a clockmaker. I notice what goes round."

"Where does the twice go?"

Ottoline looked at him over the top of the spectacles she was not wearing.

"Ask your steward," she said. "He's been waiting four years for somebody to." And went in, and the bell over her door rang behind her.



Aster was waiting in the posting house parlour, which he had taken for the night so that nobody else would be in it.

He had met them at the edge of town at noon, as he always met them, in his good brown coat with the cuffs mended, and taken them to Sethe's hall, which was Emeric's hall now and still smelled of Sethe; and had gone through the week's books with Saban in the afternoon, the barns and the boats, true to the line; and had not said anything that was not in the books. He stood by the parlour fire now with his hat in his hands, as he had stood by the posting house trough four years ago, and when Emeric came in he did not sit.

"You paid at the junction," said Aster.

"Two Silver and three."

"I know. The clerk in the window sent the slip down to the square before you were through the chain." Aster turned his hat. "He sends every slip down. Every cart. It goes into a box at the Staple's counting house on the square, and on the first of every month the five houses' clerks come and empty the box, and count it." He looked at the fire. "I watched it go in today. Your slip. With your name on it."

"Aster," said Emeric. "Sit down."

Aster did not sit.

"Who do you think owns the junction above the vats?" he said.

The fire settled. Ward, by the door, did not move. Saban, who had been about to pour himself a cup of something from the jug on the sideboard, put the jug down again without pouring it.

"I thought," said Emeric slowly, "that it was a toll. Like the ferry. The barony's, or the crown's, collected by somebody's men. I didn't think about it." He looked at Aster. "I've held Wayford four years. I've read every page you've sent me. It isn't in any of them."

"No," said Aster. "It wouldn't be. It isn't Wayford's." He turned his hat once in his hands, all the way round, the brim going through his fingers. "It's the Staple. That's its name, in the charter. A staple, the way the old towns had them: a place the crown names where a trade has to stop and be weighed and pay. The crown granted it two hundred years ago, to a company of five houses, for the paper trade of the north, and the grant was renewed by every king since, and the five houses hold it still. Placit. Hollin. Dray. Vennor. Strade." He said the names flatly, one after another, like a clerk reading a list of debtors. "Every rag that comes down Lisle's woods road to the vats, they weigh and tax. Every sack of galls for the ink. Every ream that goes up the hill. Every cart, every day, for two hundred years."

"Under the crown's charter."

"Under the crown's charter. Not the barony's. That's the whole of it." Aster looked at him. "A baron of Wayford can't touch it. Sethe couldn't. You can't. It sits on your road and taxes your people's paper and pays a tenth to the crown on the quarter days, at the crown's account here, and the rest stays with the five; and it answers to nobody between here and the king's own court." He paused. "Lord Lisle owns the woods road, and the woods, and lets the Staple's rag carts use his road, and takes his own toll for it at his bridge, and has nothing to do with them otherwise. He's very careful about that. He's careful about everything."

"And the twice," said Emeric. "Ottoline's twice. The paper goes up the hill worth one and comes out worth two."

"The Staple buys it." Aster's voice had not changed at all. "At the junction. That's the other half of the charter. The vats can sell to whoever they like, as long as it goes through the junction; and at the junction, the Staple has the right to buy first, at the posted price. Their price. Which they post." He looked at the fire. "So nobody ever sells to anybody else. Why would a buyer come all the way to Wayford, when the Staple buys everything at the top of the hill first and sells it on in the capital at the capital's price? The Keelings have never sold a ream to anybody but the Staple in their lives. Nor has anybody on the river." He paused. "Except Pell."

"Pell sells?"

"Pell is the Staple's," said Aster. "His vats are on Lisle's land, above the junction, so his paper never goes through the chain. The Staple owns his rag men. He makes their best paper, the grey letter paper for the capital's offices, and they pay him over the posted price to keep him theirs." He looked at Emeric. "He refused you. Saban told me."

"He refused me twice."

"He'll refuse you as long as there's a Staple." Aster turned his hat. "I'm sorry."

Emeric sat down at the parlour table, because his legs had told him to.

He had come north to settle a paper trade. He had bought an ink house and partnered a vat house and found a farmer's straw, and handed an old man a box of steel that would make Wayford the fourth type founder in Randillia; and he had thought, riding down the hill behind the cart, that he understood Wayford. That it was a paper town that needed a better paper and a press of its own, and he had brought both.

And above the town, on its own two roads, for two hundred years, five houses had sat in a stone house with a chain across the road and bought everything it made at their own price.

"How long have you known?" he said.

"All of it?" Aster looked at the fire. "Seventeen years. Since my second year in Sethe's counting room. The Staple's clerk came to dine with him every quarter, and I kept the barns and the boats, and I wasn't given the other books, and I didn't ask for them." He was quiet. "But I could add. I watched the paper go up the hill. I watched what came back down. And when you have the barony's books in front of you every day and they don't balance, and you know they're true, then something else has to be the reason."

"You never told me."

"You never asked." Aster said it simply, without reproach; and then, after a breath, as if that were not fair and he knew it: "And I'd nobody to tell, Highness. Not in Sethe's time. Not after. It's the crown's charter. Sethe's other books went to Kaldo under the crown's seal with the grant, four years ago, in four boxes, and I never saw inside them; and whatever's in them about the Staple, if anything is, went with them." He looked at Emeric at last. "And I thought, if I told you, you'd do what you did at the chain today. Look at it. And pay it. And go home." He stopped. "I didn't want to be the man who told you, and then had to watch you pay it."

Emeric looked at the slip on the table in front of him. The Staple's bale at its head. By Charter of the Crown.

"Four boxes," he said.

"Four. Sealed. Sethe's private papers." Aster put his hat on the chair back, at last, and sat down across from him, slowly, a neat narrow tired man who had kept one set of true books in a dishonest house for fifteen years. "Ferra signed for them. I watched her do it."

Ward, at the door, spoke for the first time since the hill.

"Then she has them," said Ward.
