Chapter 345 - Crowned

The collar stud went at a quarter to eight.

Abloh had warned him. In the fitting room over the clothing works, with the coat on its form and pins in his lapel, he had held up a stud of white bone the size of a lentil and explained that it went through the shirt from the inside, gently, and that the shirt was new and stiff and would argue. Imani, on the window seat with her boots up, had said Emeric didn't know what gently was any more. Abloh had looked at her, and then at Emeric's hands, and said nothing at all, which was how Abloh agreed with people.

Now Emeric stood in the dressing room off his father's old bedchamber in the capital, the front of the shirt open, half a stud between his finger and thumb.

"Where's the rest of it?"

"Under the press," said Imani. She had not got up. She was on the end of the bed with a plate on her knee, eating his breakfast; she had brought it in at seven, put it down in front of him, watched him look at it, and taken it back. "Two eggs, a heel of bread, and some sort of ham the capital is proud of. You'll want something at noon. I'll find you something at noon."

"I'm not hungry."

"I know. That's why I'm eating it." She pointed her fork at the chair by the door, where a twist of brown paper lay on the folded tie. "He sent spares."

Inside the paper were four more studs and a slip in Abloh's hand. Four. You will break the first. A.

Emeric broke the second as well. It was not even difficult. He had it at the buttonhole and pressed, as he would have pressed a pin into cork, and there was a small dry click and two halves of bone in his palm, and he stood looking at them in the glass with his shirt open and his ears going red.

Imani put the plate down on the bed, came across the room, and took the third stud out of the paper herself.

"Chin up. Don't breathe."

Her fingers were cold on his throat. She had the stud through the band in about the time it took him to decide not to say anything, and she left two fingers where they were afterwards, under the angle of his jaw.

"Ninety-six."

"I'm standing still."

"I know," said Imani. "That's the interesting part." She took the fingers away and went back to the eggs.

He looked at himself in the glass. The coat had been the argument of a fortnight. Black cloth, close in the shoulder, cut away short in front and long behind; a short black waistcoat with Bram's steel in it in thin plates sewn between the lining and the cloth, as there had been in every formal coat since the investiture and the knife; the white shirt; a narrow black tie that Ferra had tied twice the night before and then written down how, in four numbered steps, on the back of an envelope that was now on the washstand. A tuxedo, his head had said at the first fitting, and went on saying, a word nobody between this window and the Band had ever heard. Aloud it was the black coat. Abloh called it the evening coat and was offended every time anybody called it anything else.

The capital crowned its kings in doublets. Slashed sleeves, gold chains of office, fur in June. He would be standing on a platform in front of the whole of it in a coat with no gold on it anywhere, and Saban had said that was the point, and Ferra had said Saban was right, and Imani had said it would at least be cooler.

Waylon came in without knocking, as he had come into every room of Emeric's life, in the blue doublet with the slashed sleeves that the crowning wanted from a king's brother. It had been their grandfather's. It was too long in the body. He stopped in the doorway and looked at the black coat for a long, expressionless time.

"You look like a crow at a wedding."

"Thank you."

"A clean crow." He sat down on the press, then got up again, because the press had half a stud under it and he'd felt it through the doublet. He looked at the bone in his hand. "You did that?"

"Two."

"Ward did a fork at Tamsy's," said Waylon, to Imani. "I heard about it from four people."

"Five," said Imani. "Bram's still telling it."

Their father came in behind Waylon in a plain dark coat with no chain on it, and that was strange too. Emeric had never in his life seen Harrold of Randillia on a crowning morning without a chain. He had never seen him at thirty-two, either, except in the last month; and the shape of that face in his own old rooms, with the light from the courtyard on it and no grey anywhere, still caught him on the turn of the stair, like a step that wasn't there.

Harrold looked at the coat.

"Half the square will hate that."

"Which half?"

"The half on benches." He looked a little longer. "Your mother says stand up straight and don't pull at your cuffs. She says she'll know."

"How will she know?"

"I'll tell her. I'm on the express down this afternoon with the rest of you, and at Saban's supper, and under the glass by midnight, and she'll want all of it." He took Emeric's left cuff in his fingers and settled it so the shirt showed a quarter of an inch, as if he had done it every morning for years and not, in fact, never. "I was crowned at nineteen. The cardinal oiled my head from a gold spoon, the old way, and it ran into my eyes, and I couldn't wipe it because I was holding the orb. I cried through the oath. Half of them thought I was moved." He let go of the cuff. "You've no oil and no cardinal. You'll be fine."

"I'd rather take the Hod into a storm."

"So would I," said Harrold. "Everybody would. That's why they make you do it in front of people."

Maeve put her head in at the door and then the rest of her. She was in Abloh's June twelve at last, a dark blue linen with no sleeves at all and a white collar, and she had come down the long gallery past every lady of the capital in it and was plainly delighted by what she'd heard said behind her.

"Oh," she said, looking at her brother. "Oh, they're going to hate it."

"Everybody keeps saying that."

"Because it's true. Good." She looked at Imani on the bed with the plate. "Is that his?"

"He isn't hungry."

"Is there more ham?"

Imani held out the plate, and Maeve sat on the bed beside her in a crowning dress and ate the king's ham with her fingers, and Harrold, who had once sent her from his table for taking a pear before the grace, watched her do it and said nothing whatever.

No bells rang at nine. The capital's great bells had come down with the cathedral.



They walked.

Saban had settled it a week before, in two sentences, at the long table in Kaldo. A carriage was the old king. Walking was the new one. Harrold had said, "Thank you, Saban," in a voice that ended the matter, and it had stayed ended.

So they went up through the old town on foot from the castle gate, Emeric in front in the black coat and Waylon at his left shoulder, with the six behind in Abloh's grey lounge suits and Harrold and Maeve behind them, and the streets on either side of them were so full that the knights at the corners were standing with their backs to the walls to make room. People leaned out of windows.

The square at the top was the one he had drawn a ring round with a pin and a string.

It looked smaller from inside it. The cathedral had come down at noon on the day the leaflets said, and the masons had spent the winter taking the loose stone off the square and stacking it along the close wall in long ranks with chalk numbers on the ends, so that the paving was swept and bare and the cathedral itself was a long grey hill behind it with the stump of one tower at the near end. Its steps had come through. Kaldo's carpenters had built the platform on them in three days out of new deal, and it smelled of the yard, of planed wood and size, which was the one thing in the square he recognised.

On the left of the platform the lords sat on benches under striped awnings, in their doublets and their chains, heirs beside them; On the right, on a bench of their own, sat the Church: a provost of the capital's lesser churches in his violet, two priors, a dozen priests in black, and a Gilded Eye at every throat, the gold catching the sun each time one of them breathed. There was no cardinal. They sat very upright with their hands in their laps and looked at the platform as people look at a bill. Next to them the tower had sent five elders in their robes. Then the envoys: the Empire's, Duvrenne's, a long-faced man from Velmoor in green, and the envoy from Solreth, who had come furthest of anyone and looked it.

And in front, the city. The square full to its edges and the streets off it full to the corners and beyond the corners; people on the close wall, people sitting on the stacked stone with their legs hanging, boys on the gatehouse roof where nobody could possibly have let them go.

He felt it as he came up the last step.

Since the stone and the long sleep his sense had been sharp in a way he was still learning to live with. In Kaldo he had stopped noticing it, mostly, the way he had stopped noticing the noise of the works. He noticed it now. The square gave off mana the way a crowd in winter gives off heat: a great flat warmth, so many thousand people's worth that it pressed on him at the edges. That much he had expected. What he had not expected was that it was not flat.

Through the warmth ran lines. Thin, fine, like the threads that drift across a lane in autumn and catch on your face before you see them; and they came to him. Not from everybody. From a carter's wife at the front of the crowd with her hands clasped. From a man on the stacked stone who had his hat off. From whole streets, it felt like, down the hill toward the river, where the plague had been worst. From the lords' benches hardly any; from the Church's bench none at all. When somebody near the north well shouted "Blessed," and the shout was taken up and went back over the square in a long rough wave, the threads drew tight and leaned on him, all of them together, at the skin, and found nowhere to go in, because he was full.

He did not like it. He could not have said why, and there was no time to think about it. He put it down to the crowd and the nerves and the four thousand faces, and stood up straight, because his mother would know.

Near the north well, under the stacked stone, stood Corbin.

A plain brown coat. A hat. No sword, no ring, the anklet out of sight under his hose; struck from the succession by his own law, and here, in the crowd, among carters, on his own feet, because he had asked to come and Saban had said there was no rule against it. Beside him a broad man in a waggoner's smock was eating an apple. Corbin had never been told who watched him. He stood a little turned from the waggoner, as if he had guessed, and looked up at the platform with their father's eyes.

Emeric looked back. Then Keller stood up at the table, and the square went quiet from the front backwards, like a field when the wind drops.



Ferra had counted the benches at half past five, with two clerks and a lantern, because somebody had to.

Two hundred and twelve lords and heirs had answered, for benches built to seat two hundred. Every one of them had come. A lord who stayed away from a crowning had to explain it for the rest of his life, and in the last fortnight she had read eleven letters from men trying to find a way to stay away that would not need explaining, and none of them had found one.

Her table stood at the platform's right-hand end, a long one with a green cloth. On it lay the charter in three fair copies, the instrument of the stepping aside, Keller's reading copy with his marks in the margin, the inks, the wax, the seamed pen, and her book, open at a clean page with the date ruled off at the top right. 14 June. Under the table, in a box, the morning's post to the crown, which was hers to open, because all of it was.

The Solreth envoy brought his own letter to the table before the reading began, which was not how it was done; the Empire's envoy had sent his up by a page. He was an old man, brown and creased, in a long coat of undyed wool that had been good once and had come a very long way, and he put the letter into her hand himself and bowed to her as he would have bowed to a minister of his own king's.

"From my king to yours," he said. "Read it when you like, Chancellor. It says what such letters say."

It did. She read it while Keller found his place. Solreth's king rejoiced in the crowning of his brother of Randillia; prayed for long life and fair harvests; looked to the old friendship of the two crowns, which was mostly a friendship of grain; and was his brother's in all things not forbidden by his conscience. It was dated at the foot, as every letter on the continent was dated, in the year of the Church.

And under that, in the same hand, a second date.

A different count of years. Its numbers were much larger. She read them twice, because she always read numbers twice, and then she did the subtraction, which took no time; and the second count ran past the Church's Year One. Whatever Solreth was counting from, it had happened before the Church had begun to count anything at all.

She looked up. The envoy had not gone back to his bench. He was watching her read with the patient face of an old man who has seen this before.

"We keep the old count as well," he said. "Our grandmothers would want it. Nobody minds."

"Nobody minds," said Ferra.

"Nobody in Solreth." He bowed again and went.

She wrote both dates on the clean page, one under the other, exactly as they stood on the letter. Under them she wrote the difference, and drew a box round it. She did not write what it meant, because she did not know.

Then the treasury came up the steps.

It came as the Keeper of the Seals, an old man in a black gown with a face like a shut drawer, and a younger one behind him with a box. The box was black wood with brass corners, and when he lifted the lid it was lined with green velvet worn to the weave. In it lay two seals.

The one on the right she knew; every document of the crown she had ever handled bore it. Bright bronze, crisp at every edge. The one on the left was the same seal and not the same. The same arms, the same lettering round the rim, but softer, darker, the edges gone round with handling, a small crack across the bottom of the field. The Keeper put his hand flat on the air above it without touching.

"The first matrix," he said. "Cut for the founder. We bring it out for a crowning and for nothing else. The other's been recut from copies for longer than anyone's kept a record of the recutting. They're the same seal."

They were not quite. Ferra compared things the way other people breathed; she had done it to his columns at twenty-one, to Vantic's ledgers, to the two halves of the quay book. Her eye went over the old matrix and the new one side by side, field by field, letter by letter, and stopped at the foot of the old one, below the arms, at the very bottom of the rim. Something was cut there, very small, worn nearly smooth, that the copyists had let go somewhere in all those centuries because it was too worn to read and they had not known it was meant to be there.

A ring, with one stroke through it that stopped short of the far side.

She had seen it in the bottom corner of every drawing he had made since the first summer. On the backs of envelopes. On the sentry sheets and the airship hulls and Tess's manual and a page of sums he had once done for her about soap. She had seen it scratched into the margin of the top sheet when he wrote to the pope.

She looked once at Emeric.

He was standing at the front of the platform in his black coat with his hands at his sides, not pulling at his cuffs, listening to Keller, who had begun. He did not feel her look. He was being crowned.

Ferra turned back to her page. Under the box with the difference in it she wrote what she had seen: which seal, where on it, how it was shaped. She drew it, small, the ring and the stroke that stopped short. She did not ask anyone anything. She dipped the pen and went on with the morning's work.

Keller read the instrument first, the stepping aside, in the voice he gave to verdicts, the voice that had made men in his court sit up who had not sat up for anything else. Harrold of Randillia, sound in mind and body, laid down the crown of his own free choosing, in daylight, before witnesses, and named his son. The six signatures. The date in May. In the crowd somebody near the front began to cry, an old woman, loudly, and the people round her let her.

Then the charter.

The Hearing Rule, first: that no subject of the crown, of whatever degree, should be punished, fined, dispossessed or bound without a hearing, written down, with the right to answer, and appeal from it to Keller's court. Ferra knew where to look while it was read. Along the lords' benches nobody moved; that was how she knew they had heard it. The thirty-two hours went next, and the half again over, and the hour that was the worker's to give or keep; and at the end of the third bench a mill lord from the west road, a heavy man with a beautiful beard, shut his eyes, as a man shuts them when the surgeon starts. The stones clause. Nobody under eighteen. Not by birth. Not by coin. Not by the crown. Three benches back a lord from the north sat with his face turned very slightly away from the platform, as though there was something on the close wall he was interested in. The Board had refused his son in May, both reasons, either would do. She had written the refusal herself.

She saw Saban's head move.

He stood at the far end of the platform from her with his hands behind his back, in the grey suit, looking at nothing, which was how he always stood. He had stood like that, she realised, all week. At the long table, on the stair, at the back of the hall in the castle while she did the seating: always a little apart, always looking at nothing. And three times this week he had answered something she had said under her breath across a room. Two days before she had said eleven to herself at the far end of the long gallery, counting the window bays, and he had said "twelve, there's one behind the curtain," from forty feet off, and gone on reading. She had put that down to Saban. She was not putting it down to Saban any more.

His head had turned north, toward the well, and stopped. His face did not change. He lifted two fingers of his right hand from behind his back, a little, and let them fall.

At the edge of the crowd below the platform, Mira, in her grey work dress with a market basket on her arm, put the basket down and went into the crowd as if she had remembered she'd left the kettle on. On the other side of the square, so did Arthur.

Ferra counted. She did not decide to.

"What is it," she said, under her breath, to nobody.

"Later," said Saban, twenty feet away, without turning his head.

Thirty-one. Saban's hand was behind his back again. Somewhere near the north well there was a small disturbance in the crowd that might have been a man stumbling, or three; then nothing. Keller had not paused. Nobody on the benches had looked round. Ferra wrote 31 in the margin of her page, with no word beside it, and looked up for her own clause.

Keller read it in full. She had known he would; she had asked him to. Any act of the crown could be stayed by the Chancellor, in writing, until the Assembly had heard it and voted; and while a stay stood the crown could not put the Chancellor out. Along the benches there was a sound, not a murmur but a shift: two hundred and twelve men in heavy cloth changing how they sat.

"Do you swear to keep it," said Keller, "all of it, and to be bound by it, as the least of your subjects is bound?"

"I will," said Emeric. Then, because the old form wanted the words and Keller was waiting with his eyebrows: "I swear it."

He came to the table to sign. He signed the first copy. He began to put the pen down, and she looked at him, and he picked it up again and dated it at the top right, and she wrote nothing at all about that in her book, though she wanted to. The Keeper lifted the first matrix from its velvet, the founder's, and pressed it into the wax at the foot of the charter with both hands, and lifted it away. The wax took the arms. It took the rim and the lettering. The worn thing at the foot it did not take at all; it was too shallow now for wax. Ferra watched it not be there.

Waylon held the cushion. Harrold took the crown off it.

It was the old crown, the heavy one, gold on a band of iron, and they had padded the band inside with felt because Emeric's head was not his father's. Harrold lifted it and stepped to his son; and found, in front of four thousand people, that he would have to reach. Emeric had been shorter than his father all his life. He had come out of the long sleep taller than him. Harrold looked up at him, crown in his hands, with an expression Ferra had never seen on the old king's face and had seen often on Waylon's, and said something she didn't hear. Emeric bent his head.

A laugh went along the front of the crowd, not unkind. Then the crown was on, and the square came up at them.

It was not a cheer. It was too big for one. It came off the stacked stone and the close wall and the gatehouse roof and up from the streets where they couldn't see, and it went on, and she felt it in the planks under her feet. Along the benches the lords stood, because everyone stood. The mill lord with the beard stood last. The lord from the north stood after his wife had put her hand on his arm and left it there. On the right, the Church stood with the rest, all together, as if somebody had given a signal, and not one of them opened his mouth.

Ferra wrote the time. Eleven, and seven minutes. She ruled a line under it. Then she looked at the page: the two counts of years, the box, the small worn ring with its stroke, and the thirty-one; all on one sheet, which was not like her, because she kept unlike things apart.

She left them together. She turned the page.



The crown was too heavy and Imani had told him not to touch it.

"Don't adjust it," she had said in the castle, holding it up to the light from the courtyard. "Don't grip it. Don't steady it on a stair. If it slips, let it slip, and Waylon will catch it. He's been told." So he stood with his hands at his sides while the envoys came up to the table to bow, one at a time, with the sweat starting under the felt, and did not touch it.

The threads had not gone slack since the square came up at them. He stood in them like a man in a hedge of spider silk at first light, too fine to see, there on every side but the right.

The envoys bowed. The Empire's was gracious and Duvrenne's was cold. Then the orders. The provost came, and bowed, exactly as low as the form required and not the depth of a hair lower, and his Eye swung out on its chain and back against his violet; he said the Church of Ostrel acknowledged the crown of Randillia, and gave Emeric a long, level look that acknowledged nothing else at all, and went back to his bench.

The tower came last.

Elder Hesper Vosse was small and very upright. Emeric had seen the one sheet she wrote to Armand in the autumn, in her brown ink, going past every figure in his count of the dead as a clerk goes past a column she has already checked. She came up the steps without help, though she was well past seventy, and she did not come to Emeric.

She went to Ferra's table. She stood in front of the charter, the copy with the founder's wax on it still soft, and read the first article where it lay, all of it, with her hands folded at her waist. Then she bowed to it.

Not to him. To the paper. A tower bow, from the waist, slow, the bow an elder gives the Circle's law when it is read out at a sitting.

Then she straightened, and turned, and looked at Armand.

He stood at Emeric's right shoulder. He had Abloh's grey suit on, and the bow tie he had worn every day since he came out of the stone young; and he stood under her look and did not move.

"Master Armand."

"Elder."

"I read your News," said Hesper Vosse. "The stones lengthen life. Who is given one is in an open book, which anyone may read."

"Yes."

"I read that too." She looked at his face, at the hair dark at his temples, at his steady hands, as if she were reading a column she had not checked. "You look well."

"I am well."

"Yes," said Hesper Vosse. "I can see that you are." And she bowed to him.

It was lower than the bow she had given the charter. It was the bow of an elder to a master of the tower, the full one, the one Emeric had read about in Armand's own books and never seen given. She held it. Then she went back down the steps without help, and sat down among her four elders, and folded her hands.

Armand had not moved at all. After a while, without turning his head, he said, very low, "She has not bowed to me like that since I was sixty."

"Is it good?"

"It is a great courtesy," said Armand. "She is telling me she knows exactly what I am now, and that she has taken note." He looked at the elders' bench, where five old faces looked back at him. "The tower will want to know what it costs. And who decides. She'll write. She'll write very politely."

Then the lords came, in their order, and that took an hour.

The mill lord with the beautiful beard came near the end. He bowed properly. Then he stood his ground in front of the new king with his hands at his sides and said, in a carrying, courteous voice, that he had three mills on the west road and four hundred hands, and that thirty-two hours and the half again would close two of the mills by spring, and he wished his majesty to know it from his own mouth before he heard it from his clerks.

"Then come and say it to the Assembly," said Emeric. "It has to hear you. That's the first article."

"I know what the first article says, your majesty. I read it on the express." The lord looked, briefly, at the black coat, and at the six behind it, the young faces that were not young, and at Ferra's table. "I have no quarrel with what it says. I don't like who wrote it, and I don't like where."

"That's fair," said Emeric.

The lord had not expected that. He looked at the king a while longer, as though he had been handed change in a coin he didn't know, and bowed again, and went back to his bench, and did not sit down as heavily as he had got up.



Saban told him on the steps behind the platform, while the square emptied downhill with a noise like a river.

"Three men by the north well," he said. "A tanner, his brother, and a lad who works for them. Down the hill, where the plague was. Good men, as far as I could hear. The tanner nursed his whole street in the bad year."

"What were they going to do?"

"When the provost bowed to you and didn't kneel, they were going to go up the side of the platform, take the Eye off his neck, and put him on his knees on the cathedral's stones. In your name. The lad had a length of rope." Saban's face did not move. "For the Blessed. That's what they said, all three, several times. They'd been talking about it since the crowning was in the News."

Emeric stood on the step in the crown. The sweat had got under the felt and was running down behind his ear.

"And then?"

"Then the priests would have come off their bench for the provost, and the people round the well would have come for the priests, and the knights would have had to come for the people. On the cathedral steps. At your crowning. With the Empire's envoy taking notes." Saban looked out over the emptying square. "It would have been in every pulpit from here to Ostrel within the week. The Church would have paid a great deal for it, and it didn't have to pay anything."

"Where are they?"

"In the gatehouse lane, sitting on a step. Mira's with them. Arthur put an arm round the lad like an old friend at a fair and walked him off, and the brothers followed. The rope's in Mira's basket." He paused. "Nobody else knows. The provost doesn't know."

Emeric took the crown off and gave it to Waylon, who had been standing a step below for exactly that.

The gatehouse lane was narrow and cool and smelled of horses. The three of them sat on a step under the arch in a row, the tanner in the middle with his big stained hands on his knees, and Mira stood across the lane from them with her basket, eating a bread roll out of it. The lad saw the black coat and stood up so fast he hit his head on the arch.

Emeric felt the threads before he reached them. From these three they were not thin. They came off the step at him like warmth off a stove, and leaned, and he had nowhere to put them.

"Sit down," he said, and the lad sat. Emeric did not. "You meant it for me."

"We did, your majesty," said the tanner. He had a slow, deep voice. "They didn't kneel. They sat there in their gold eyes while the whole city stood up for you, and they'd have let us all die in the bad year, and they didn't kneel."

"They don't have to."

The tanner looked at him as though he'd spoken in another language.

"That's the charter," said Emeric. "That's what I swore to an hour ago, up there. Nobody's put on his knees. Not by the crown. Not by anybody for the crown. The provost can hate me in a chair in front of four thousand people, and he can go home after and write to Ostrel and say so, and nobody touches him, because if anybody can be dragged onto those stones for not kneeling, then the next man dragged is you." He stopped. He had not meant to make a speech in a lane. "You'd have started a fight on the cathedral steps, and people would have died in it, and it would have been in my name, and it would have been mine. So it's mine anyway. I'm telling you that. It's mine."

Nobody said anything. A horse in the gatehouse stable shifted and blew.

"We never meant him hurt," said the brother at last. "Only knelt."

"I know."

The tanner looked at his hands for a long time. "What happens to us?"

"You go home." Emeric looked at Mira, who had finished the roll and was dusting her fingers. "Mira will give you your rope back at the bottom of the hill. If you want to be angry at the Church, come to Kaldo and be angry at the Assembly; there's a hearing every week, and they write it all down, and the Church's people can come and answer. It's slower. It's better."

When they had gone down the lane, the tanner last, looking back twice, Emeric stood under the arch with his hand flat against the cold stone.

"You did that very well," said Mira.

"Saban did it."

"Saban heard it. Arthur and I did it. You did the last bit." She picked up the basket. "That's how it's meant to go, isn't it?"

Saban was waiting where he had left him, at the top of the steps behind the platform, with the crown in his hands now instead of Waylon; Waylon had gone to find their father. He waited until Emeric was beside him.

"One more thing. Your brother spoke to his watcher."

"He doesn't know who his watcher is."

"He does now. While Keller was reading the stones, he turned to the man with the apple and said, 'You're the one who brings the keep its coal, aren't you.' Our man said nothing, which was right. Corbin said, 'There's a lay brother at the back of the well, in the brown, with the long face. That's Brother Anselm. He was one of Augustine's confessors. He's not on my list because I'd forgotten him, and I'd forgotten him because he only ever heard the palace kitchens.'" Saban turned the crown a little in his hands, carefully, by the band. "He said, 'Write that down.'"

"Was he there? Anselm?"

"There was a long face in brown at the back of the well. By the time our man could look properly it had gone. It was about ten yards from my three, and I didn't hear it say anything to them, and I can't tell you it did." Saban looked at him. "I'd like to know what the palace kitchens said to Brother Anselm, in twenty years. Kitchens hear more than kings."

"Corbin didn't have to tell him."

"No," said Saban. "He didn't. I've written it down. Ferra will want it in the review."

Emeric looked out from the top of the cathedral steps, over the square, which was nearly empty now, a few hundred people lingering by the well and on the stacked stone; and past it, down the long fall of the capital, the roofs and the river and the bridges; and past the walls the plain, green in June, going away south and west in fields and hedges and small dark woods until it was only colour, and somewhere past the colour, forty towns he had never been to, and a thousand villages, and the coast.

All of it, then. Not one town on a river any more. All of it.

Corbin was at the well still, with the waggoner. He looked up at the steps, at the black coat at the top of them, and did not wave, and did not look away either. Then the waggoner said something, and Corbin turned, and the two of them went down the hill together toward the express.



Saban had been right about the queue.

The lords came down to Kaldo on the afternoon express, a hundred and forty of the two hundred and twelve, in three carriages that Saban had had cleaned twice. They came out of the station into the long June evening in their doublets and their chains and rode the tram up to the square in front of Tamsy Brook's, holding their chains in their fists so the links wouldn't swing. Prudence Fairley punched every one of their tickets. She charged the Empire's envoy a copper and he paid it, looking at the coin afterwards as if it might explain something.

Tamsy had six tables, all of them under the pear on the terrace with the glass doors folded back, and on the far side of the square the bakery had lent four more and the ice cart had parked itself in the corner by the fountain and was doing a trade in lemon ice that it would talk about till winter. There were two sittings, and then three. The rest queued along the front of the bakery. They had never queued in their lives. They stood in their furs in June and talked about it in low, outraged voices, and did not leave, because the smell coming out of Tamsy's kitchen was the best smell any of them had met, and because the lord in front of them hadn't.

The trout came with its head on. Two lords sent it back. Tamsy sent it out again, still with its head on, and a note, and they ate it.

"She wrote, That's where the flavour is," said Imani, who had read the note on its way past. "And then a word I'm not saying in front of your father."

"I've heard it," said Harrold.

The peas had come off the Milford farms that morning, with lamb and a mint sauce the Duvrenne envoy asked about twice. Bram ate with steady attention and no conversation, the way he ate everything, and said "Hm" once, at the sauce, which Tamsy heard from the kitchen door and went in again looking pleased with herself for a full minute. Ward used a wooden spoon. He had brought his own. Nobody at the table mentioned the fork, all evening, with a restraint so complete and so visible that by the lamb Ward put the spoon down and said, "Go on, then," and Osric, at the end, lost his composure entirely and had to go and stand by the fountain.

Maeve had a duke's heir and a baron's widow talking about horses inside a sitting; Waylon, two seats down, listened with deep suspicion and then offered the widow a filly. Vantic had come down on the express in grey and sat at the corner table with a glass of something she had brought herself; when Emeric passed she looked at the black coat for a long time over her glass and said, "Well. That'll cost the doublet trade," and raised the glass an inch, and he laughed and went on.

Mira had a place at the end of the terrace, off duty, with Arthur and Rylan, and was eating peas.

Harrold got up from beside his son at the end of the lamb, went the length of the terrace between the tables, past lords who half rose as he went, which he did not seem to see, and stopped behind her chair. She looked up. Then she put her fork down and started to get up, and he put a hand on her shoulder and she stayed where she was.

He put two coppers down on the cloth beside her plate.

"One for the tram," said Harrold. "And one for the year."

Mira looked at the coins. She looked up at him.

"That's a hundred on the copper, sir."

"Over eleven months," said Ferra, two tables away, not looking up from her lamb.

"I've no treasury to answer to any more," said Harrold. "I'm told I'm allowed to be generous."

Mira put both coppers in her pocket without another word, which Rylan said afterwards was the most knightly thing he had ever seen anybody do; and Harrold went back up the terrace, and sat down, and ate a pea off Emeric's plate, because Emeric had not touched them.

"You've not eaten all day," said Harrold.

"Imani had my breakfast."

"I had his lunch, too," said Imani. "He's having his supper." She moved his plate an inch toward him with one finger, which was not a suggestion. He ate the peas.

His father left at ten, for the dome. He kissed Maeve, which she had not expected and did not mind; he shook Waylon by the hand and then, clumsily, pulled him in by it and held him, which Waylon had not expected either and stood very still under. To Emeric he said only, "Straight. All day. She'll be pleased," and went off down the dark square under the lamps toward the inner moat, walking fast, like a young man late for something, which he was.



It was past eleven when the three of them went down to the quay.

Nobody said where they were going. Emeric turned left at the bottom of the Gate Avenue instead of right, and Ferra, on his arm, noticed, and did not say so; he felt her notice through his sleeve. Imani walked on his other side with her hand in his coat pocket, all the way in, against the cold that there wasn't, because it was June.

The K Amara lay along the quay with her riding lights lit and a watch on her market deck, talking quietly over the rail, who saw who it was and went forward. The water was black and very still. Along the quay the lamps stood every twenty yards, as they had stood in February, when the fleet came in and the town came down to meet it, and he had stood right here with his left arm in a sling and a coat over it and had not said it.

He stopped under a lamp.

Ferra stopped with him. She took her arm out of his and turned round and looked at him, and he saw her know.

"It's the quay," she said.

"I know."

"I said I'd be counting."

"I know you did."

"A hundred and eighteen days," said Ferra. Her voice was perfectly level. Her hands were not; she had her notebook in one of them, and he didn't think she knew she'd taken it out. "From the sixteenth of February. I didn't mind. I want you to know I didn't mind. I knew you would. I just kept a column."

"She's been saying numbers in her sleep," said Imani. "I thought it was the seating."

"It was partly the seating."

He had planned it. That was the stupid part. He had planned it on the express with the plain going by, and in the castle before that; the plan had sentences in it, in order, and when he opened his mouth what came out was the first of them: "I wanted to put something on the record."

Ferra looked at him.

"On the record," she said.

"He's going to minute it," said Imani.

"I'm not going to minute it."

"You said on the record."

"I know what I said." He shut his eyes. In the dark behind them there was the platform in the square, the thread-warmth of four thousand people and his father reaching up; and before that, a long way before that, a ward in another world with a window and a vending machine at the end of a corridor, and a man who had built things with his hands most of his life and gone home every night to nobody, and had not minded, he'd thought, and had been wrong. He opened his eyes again. They were both still there. They were both looking at him. That was the whole of it, really.

"I love you," he said. "Both of you. Ferra. Imani. I love you, I have for years, I don't know how many, Ferra will know."

It came out in one breath, and at the end of it he had none left.

Nobody said anything. The watch on the K Amara's deck had gone a long way forward.

Ferra did not cry; it was Imani, oddly, whose eyes went bright, and who looked crossly at the water until they stopped. Ferra stood quite still with the notebook in her hand and looked at him as she looked at a column that had come out right after a long time of coming out wrong: not surprised, exactly. Checking it. Then she let her breath go.

"Since the steamboat," she said. "I worked it out once, to the day. I'm not telling you the days."

"I've a date as well," said Imani. "It's written down. It's in with my stockings."

"You wrote it down?"

"I'm a healer. I write down when things start." She looked at him. "It isn't the steamboat."

"Different days, then."

"We can," said Ferra. "It's not a sum. It's two people. You can have more than one start."

He had a second sentence. He had had it ready for months, worded and reworded; this one he got out nearly as he'd meant it.

"Will you marry me." It did not come out as a question. He tried again. "Both of you. Will you. One wedding. I don't know how it's done here. I don't think it's ever been done here. Where I come from you go down on one knee..."

"Don't," said Imani. "You'll go through the planks."

He looked down. The quay was oak, old, two inches thick, and he thought she was very probably right.

"It hasn't," said Ferra.

"Hasn't what?"

"Been done here. I looked." She lifted her chin, red to the fringe again and not stopping for it. "In April. While you were asleep. I went through the marriage law in the records hall, all of it, back to the oldest roll they had, and the Church's canon besides, and the charter as I'd drafted it. There's nothing in the charter against it. The Church's canon says one; the crown's law doesn't say how many, because nobody ever thought to write it down. I wrote it down." She stopped. "I didn't put it in. I just wrote down that it wasn't there."

"You checked the law."

"Of course I checked the law," said Ferra. "I wasn't going to say yes to something that wasn't allowed."

"Is that a yes?"

"Yes," said Ferra. "It's a yes. You knew it was a yes. You've known since..." She stopped herself, because she was about to give him another number. "Yes."

Imani had taken her hand out of his pocket. She took his wrist instead, as she had at eight o'clock that morning, two cold fingers on the inside of it, and stood there in the lamplight, counting under her breath, with her face perfectly grave.

"A hundred and twenty-two," she said. "Yes. Obviously yes. We said not yet, both of us, and it's yet." She let go of his wrist. "And I'd like you to sit down before you fall in, because you've had a pea and some lamb since six o'clock yesterday and you've just been crowned, and I'm not fishing a king out of the harbour on the first night."

He sat down on a bollard. It held.

Ferra opened her notebook on her knee, there under the lamp, and found a clean page, and wrote the date at the top right and ruled a line under it, the way she had ruled every page he had ever seen her write; and under the line, in her small upright hand, E. asked. Both. Yes. And her initial: F.

She held out the pen.

Imani took it and wrote I. beside the F, pressing so hard the nib went through, and gave it back.

"One wedding," said Ferra, putting the notebook away. "I'm not doing the seating twice."

Then, because he was on a bollard and they were standing, they had to bend down to kiss him, which made Imani laugh, and Ferra was laughing too by the end of it, with her hand on the back of his neck and her forehead against his; and he sat between them on a bollard on the quay of his own town in the black coat, king of all of it since seven minutes past eleven, and did not feel the threads at all. There was nothing on the quay but the two of them, and the lamps, and the water, very still.

Up on the K Amara's market deck, the watch, who had gone forward as far as the ship allowed, found they could go no further, and stood in the bow looking with great attention at the harbour mouth, where nothing was happening at all.
