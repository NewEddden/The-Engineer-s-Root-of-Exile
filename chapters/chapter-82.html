Chapter 82 - Trusted Hands

Saban had bought coal once, years ago, and it had taken him three weeks to remember where.

He had been running salt and cloth up the river road the season before the plague, for a man in Milford who was dead now, and at a ford somewhere above Grayrock a carter coming the other way had offered him two sacks of black stone at a price that was nearly an insult, and he had taken them because a merchant takes anything offered at nearly an insult. He had sold them to a smith in Milford who burned them and complained about the smell. That was the whole of it. He had not written it down. There had been no reason to write it down, because nobody in the south burned coal, because there was charcoal, and charcoal was what everybody had always burned.

"The smith said it held its heat," Saban said. "That's the only thing I remember him saying. He complained about the smell for a quarter of an hour and then said it held its heat."

He had come back down the river road in the first sleet of the winter with the answer in a leather wallet, and he laid it out at the table in the long room on a morning so dark the lamps were lit at the ninth bell.

The carter's black stone had come off the far side of the Grayrock hill. Not the mine side, where the workings were and the tally house and Hask's men, but the back of it, where the hill fell away steeply into a wooded valley that nobody at Grayrock went into because there was nothing in it. There was something in it. There was a seam of coal in the valley wall, a yard thick, running level into the hillside, and there was a pit on it, and every summer a gang of eight men came up from the coast and dug it, and filled carts, and took the carts down a track to the river below the ford, and put the coal on a flat boat to the coast.

"Bryda knew they were there," Saban said, before anybody could ask. "She told me the moment I asked. She'd always taken them for charcoal burners Renner had licensed for a rent. Nobody at Grayrock had ever gone down to look, because Renner told them it was his business." He turned a sheet over. "He'd sold it. The year before the war. A lease on the seam for twenty years, to a coal house on the coast, paid in one sum, up front. It's not in the Grayrock book. It's in the coal house's book. I saw their copy." He did not say how. "Thirteen years to run."

"How much did he get?"

Saban told them. It was less than a quarter of what the seam was worth on any honest reckoning, and it was, to within a few gold, what Renner had spent on arming the men he took up Kaldo's road.

Nobody said anything about that. It was the same shape as everything else Renner had ever done with his hill, and they had all seen the shape before.

"So it's yours," Saban said to Emeric, "and it isn't. The ground is Grayrock's and Grayrock is yours. The digging is theirs for thirteen years. They sell at the pit mouth to anybody, four silver the cartload, and it's two silver more to get a cart down the road to us, which is a price I'd kill for on charcoal." He put the wallet down. "And that's the problem. It belongs to someone."

Vantic had been sitting at the end of the table, as she did whenever an expenditure above the line was on it, with her small book open and her quill. She had not written anything.

"Which house?" she said.

"Tarrant's. Out of Duvrenne."

"Ah," said Vantic.

She wrote one word in the book. Then she looked up, and found the whole table looking at her, and gave nothing back.

"They're a sound house," she said. "They pay late and they never go to law. If you want to buy from them, buy from them. If you want to buy out the lease, they'll sell it, but only to a buyer they've had dealings with for a while."

"How do you know that?" said Saban. He did not mean anything by it. He was a merchant, and she had just told him a thing about a counterparty that he had spent three weeks not finding out.

"Houses know houses," Vantic said, and went back to her book.

Saban looked at her for a moment longer than a man looks at a factor, and then let it go, because it was a perfectly good answer and he could not see what was wrong with it. Ferra, who was writing, did not look up at all.

They bought from Tarrant's. Emeric had considered, for about the length of a breath, the other thing, which was that Tarrant's had paid a dead man a quarter of what the seam was worth for a lease that was never entered in the book it should have been entered in, and that a lease like that could be argued. He could see the argument. He could see exactly how it would go, and he could see that it would win, and that at the end of it Tarrant's would have lost a seam, and a house that had never gone to law in its life would have learned how, and would practise on Kaldo for the rest of it. He did not want that. He wanted coal.

"Buy it at the pit mouth," he said. "Forty carts by the thaw, and a standing order after. Pay on the day."

"On the day," said Ferra, and wrote it. "They'll wonder why."

"Let them wonder why Kaldo pays on the day."

Ferra had one more line on her sheet, and she read it without looking up. "Fletcher. Ninety-eight."

"The section."

"I saw where you wrote it." She entered it under the new year and went on to the next thing, and did not come back to it, and he found that he would rather she had.

Vantic wrote something too, and he did not ask what, and it was only afterward, on the stairs, that it occurred to him that she had said the lease could be bought, and by whom, and that she had said it the way a woman says a thing about a family she has sat at dinner with.

The engine was gated on the coal, and the coal was now a line in a book, and the engine was gated on something else as well, which was people.

He had known that since the summer, and had put off knowing it. The lathe had been a thing that made parts, and he had handed it to Neta and Jorrin after Ferra read him a page, and it had been the right decision and he had hated the page. The engine was worse. A boiler was a thing a man could carry to another barony in his head. You did not need the drawing once you had built one. You needed to have watched the fire, and felt the plate go warm, and heard the valve lift, and after that you knew, and you could not unknow it, and you could walk up any road you liked with it.

So he went to Ferra's room and asked for four names, and she sent a boy for Ward.

Ward came up with the satchel under his arm and put it on the table and went to the window with his back to them. Ferra unbuckled it. She took out a sheet in the hand that was not hers and read it with her lips closed, the way she had in the spring, and then looked up and told him what it said.

"Tobe. Twenty-five. Bram's apprentice from the first year of the foundry, journeyman these three years. Mother dead, father dead, one sister who married a drover and went to Milford. Owes the tap-house on the quay eight coppers and has owed it since the midsummer fair, which he's forgotten and the keeper hasn't. Nobody has ever asked him anything about the foundry. He wouldn't know what to say if they did."

She turned the page.

"Wick. Twenty-three. Bram's other apprentice, same years. Father still has the boat. Writes once a month to a girl in Milford whose father is a cattle factor's clerk. The girl's father dislikes him and says so to anybody who asks. The letters go by the post. Ward has never read one." She did not look at Ward. "Wick borrows books from Orrin's shelf at the school and brings them back on time. He's read everything on it twice."

"Rudd," she said, and turned another. "Fifty-one. Furnace crew since the first blast. Twice widowed. No children living. Owes nobody. Nobody owes him. Drinks nothing. Goes to the church on feast days and nowhere else. Has not been further than the Left Bridge in six years." She laid the page flat. "There's nothing to take hold of."

"There's always something."

"Then I haven't found it." She said it plainly, and he believed her, and noticed that she was not pleased to be saying it. "That isn't the same as there being nothing. It's the same as my not having found it."

"And the fourth."

The fourth was a carter's boy off the ore road called Sim, nineteen, whose mother had come through the plague on one of Imani's pallets in the church, which she had told everybody on the long street ever since. There was nothing in his page that anybody could want except that his mother was proud of him, and Ferra read it at the same speed as the others.

He sat with the four of them for a while after she had finished. Ward took the satchel and went out, and neither of them had said a word to each other the whole time, again.

It was only on the stair that he noticed he had not minded.

In the spring he had sat in that chair and heard about Jorrin's father's slate and felt it as a physical thing, a hand on the back of his neck. Today he had heard about Tobe's eight coppers and Wick's letters and a widower who went nowhere, and it had been information. He had known Tobe since Tobe was a boy on the foundry floor asking the one question about a pattern that mattered, and it had still been information. Useful, accurate, invasive information, which he had asked for and got, and which he had then used to decide something. He had not flinched once.

He stood on the stair for a moment and looked at that. He did not like it much. He did not know what to do with it either, and after a while he went down.

The pens were held that winter, and held for a reason that took one sentence: the first die Bram struck off the master cracked on its second blank, and the mill's strip still varied more across its width than a nib could stand.

The boiler was a drum of iron plate as long as a man was tall, with domed ends, lying on its side in a brick setting with a firebox under it and a flue that went back along its belly and up a chimney. The plates came off the mill, which by then rolled them even enough for this and not yet for anything finer, and Bram's men riveted them hot in two rows along every seam, as Emeric had drawn it for Fletcher, and caulked the edges. It took five weeks. When it was done Bram filled it with water to the top and then went round it with a hand pump, forcing more in, and watched every seam for weeping.

Three wept. They caulked them again. Then it held.

"How hard's it going to push?" Bram said.

"Not very. Not this one." Emeric put his hand on the cold iron. "This one pushes about as hard as a man leaning on a door. The next one will push harder. The one after that harder again. Every one we build tells us how much the next one can take."

The cylinder was bored on the second lathe, which was the reason for the second lathe. A thick iron casting with a hollow down its middle, set up on the carriage, and a boring bar run through it on the lathe's own centres, taking a curl at a time, until the hole was the same all the way along to within what Bram's calipers could find. Then the piston, turned to fit it, with a groove round it for a ring of packing. Then a rod through the top of the cylinder, and a gland round the rod, a little box packed with greased hemp that let the rod slide in and out and was supposed to keep the steam in.

Then a beam, oak, on a pivot, one end on the piston rod and the other on a crank. Then a shaft. Then a wheel on the shaft, heavy, cast iron, to carry the crank round past the places where the piston could not push it.

It stood in a shed of its own at the far end of the works, away from everything, with a brick wall between it and the finishing shop that was thicker than any wall on the island except the keep's.

Bram had asked about the wall. Emeric had told him that he did not intend to learn what a failing boiler did through a wall with people on the other side of it.

That was the reason for Rudd.

He had thought about it for most of a month. A cut that went wrong on a bench took a strip off the oak and anybody's hand that was in the way of it. A boiler that went wrong took the shed and the men in it and possibly the shed next door, and it did it all at once, with no breath's warning to anybody, including him. Nobody would be standing at the far end of the bench. There would be no far end.

So he wrote it down.

The man who watched the fire held the works. That was the whole of it, and it took Ferra most of a day to make it as short as he wanted. The watcher had a valve on the boiler, a plain thing, a plug held down on a hole by a lever with a weight hung on the end, so that if the steam pushed harder than the weight it lifted the plug and let itself out with a scream. The watcher had a cock at the level where the water should be and another below it, and opened them on a count, and knew by what came out whether there was water in the boiler or not. And the watcher had the right to put the fire out and open the valve and clear the shed, at any hour, for any reason he judged sufficient, and nobody could countermand him, and nobody could relight the fire until he said so.

Nobody. The line said: not the Secretary of Works, not the Chancellor, not the holder of Kaldo. Emeric had written the last four words himself.

"You want me to countersign it," Ferra said.

"Yes."

"It binds you."

"That's what it's for."

She read it once more, and signed it under his name, and dated it, and sanded it, and when she gave it back to him she did not say anything, and she did not need to, because he had already seen what she was not saying. He had built three of these now. A factor with a permanent window into every book he kept. A Chancellor with a stay he could not set aside. And now a furnace man who could stop his works and tell him to go away. Three brakes, and he had made every one of them. The first because a lender required it and he had signed, the other two on purpose, against himself, and somewhere in the last year he had stopped wishing he could be rid of the first.

He did not say any of this out loud.

Rudd read the sheet standing in the engine shed, holding it at arm's length, because he had learned his letters at forty-five in the evening classes and still did not trust them close up. He read it twice.

"Nothing on the lever," Emeric said. "No weight added. No hand on it. No rag tied round it to stop it screaming. It screams because it's working. If anybody ever puts anything on that lever, you put the fire out first and ask them why afterward."

"Anybody."

"Anybody."

Rudd folded the sheet, and put it inside his shirt, and looked at the prince for a long moment, and then nodded, once, the way he nodded at a blast when it had come up to colour.

They lit it in the second week of the winter, with coal off the first cart from Tarrant's pit.

It took most of the morning to raise steam. The coal burned hotter and slower than anything the stokers had ever fed, and filled the shed with a yellow stinking smoke until the chimney drew, and then it drew and the smoke went up and the firebox went white. The boiler ticked and groaned as it warmed. Rudd stood by the water cocks with his count. Bram stood by the door. Emeric stood where he could see the gland and the valve both, and Ward stood where he could see Emeric.

When Rudd said there was steam, Emeric opened the cock to the cylinder, and the piston went up.

It went up slowly, as though it were thinking about it, and the beam rose on the far side, and the crank came round a quarter turn and stopped. Then the steam came out of the gland.

It came out of the little packed box round the piston rod in a hard white plume sideways across the shed, hissing like a kettle the size of a cart, and everybody took a step back, and Rudd put the fire out and opened the valve without being told, and the valve screamed, and the shed filled with steam so thick nobody could see anybody, and it was a long minute before it cleared.

Nobody was hurt. Nothing was broken. The gland had been packed too loose, with hemp that was too dry, and the steam had simply walked out past it along the rod.

It took a fortnight to put right. Not because it was hard, but because every attempt meant letting the boiler cool, and packing the gland, and raising steam again, which took a morning, and finding it leaked less, or more, or in a different place. The hemp needed tallow worked into it, and then it needed to be wound in rings rather than stuffed, and then the gland's nut needed to be taken up a little and not too much, because too much and the rod seized. By the end of the fortnight Rudd knew more about packing a gland than anybody in the kingdom, and he had written it down, in large careful letters, on a sheet he kept inside his shirt with the other one.

It was wet and dull and nobody would ever tell a story about it. Emeric was grateful for every day of it. The boiler had held. The first thing that went wrong had gone wrong at the pressure of a man leaning on a door, in front of a man with a written right to put the fire out, and he had put it out.

The Wayford leg of the relay was finished that month, inside the year he had given it. The last post went up on the ridge where Ward had said to put an archer, and the first message came down it from Wayford on a grey afternoon, in the Wayford line's marks, a string of signs that meant nothing to eleven men between there and here and meant, at the Kaldo end, the tolls for the quarter, correct to the coin. Ferra read it off the log and entered it. Nobody made anything of it. It was what the relay was for.

Imani came to the long room the same week, with a roll of drawings under her arm.

She did not ask for a place at the table. She waited until the ore tallies were done, and then unrolled the drawings over them, and weighted the corners with Ferra's books without asking, and stood back.

It was the hospital. Not a picture of a building, because she could not draw a building and did not pretend to. It was the thing he had asked her for at midwinter, and she had spent a year making it. Where the water came in, off the main, and where it went out, into its own drain and not the street's. Which rooms could not share air, with arrows showing which way the air in each was to go. The fever beds at the north end, the lying-in beds at the south, and between them two walls and a passage and a room where every person going from one end to the other was to wash, with the basins drawn in. How far each bed stood from the next, in feet, off the master rule. Where the dead went, and by which door, so that nobody carrying a body ever went past a woman in labour.

It was the plainest drawing he had seen in years and there was not a line in it that was not a death she had watched happen.

Nobody said anything for a while. Bram looked at the walls and began, without meaning to, to count stones.

Ferra took the drawings, when Imani had finished, and entered them. Then she turned back a year, to the midwinter page, where she had copied the line the day Imani took the signed sheet away inside her coat.

"Ground broken at the thaw after next," she read. She looked up. "That's this one."

That was all anybody said. Imani rolled the drawings up and took them away with her.

In the last week of the winter's first month the engine ran all day.

It ran from the first bell to the last. The firebox glowed, and Rudd walked his count, and the valve lifted and screamed twice in the afternoon and settled, and the gland held. The piston went up and down, and the beam rose and fell, and the crank went round, and the shaft turned, and the heavy wheel on the shaft turned with it, round and round, a steady even thing like a big slow breath.

It was not connected to anything. There was nothing on the far end of the shaft but the wheel. There was nothing yet for it to drive.

Emeric stood in the door of the shed at the end of the day and watched it turn in the lamplight. Bram came and stood beside him. Neither of them said what it was for. Bram did not ask. After a while he went home, and Emeric stayed and watched it go round, doing nothing, perfectly, until Rudd put the fire out.
