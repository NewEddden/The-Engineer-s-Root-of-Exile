Chapter 82 - Round

The billhook man came to the South Gate in the third week of February, with the river loud under the bridge and the thaw running brown in every gutter of the lower ward, and asked the gate crew for a man called Hale.

Emeric was coming in from the garrison yard at the time, with straw down the back of his shirt and Ward a pace behind him, and he did not know the man at first. He was thinner than in December, which Emeric would not have thought possible, and he had walked the whole of the south road in the same boots, and the boots had not enjoyed it. The billhook was wrapped in sacking and tied to his pack with a cord. He was holding his hat in both hands in front of him the way men held hats at gates where they expected to be turned away.

"I was sent," he said to the gate crew, when they asked what he wanted with the steward. And then, because they went on looking at him: "By a man who owed me for a tree."

Hale came down from the keep with his book under his arm, because a gate crew did not send for the steward twice. He looked at the man and at the hat and at the wrapped billhook, and then, for no longer than it took to turn a page, at Emeric, who was standing by the well pretending to have stopped there for water.

"Your name," Hale said, and opened the book.

The man gave it. Hale wrote it down, and the village it came from, a day east of the forest, and the date.

"There is stone to carry at the aqueduct from Monday," Hale said. "The masons are cutting for the arch. A copper and a half a day, and your bread at noon. Paid on the Saturday."

"Every Saturday?"

"That is what Saturday is for."

The man stood there with his hat. He seemed to be waiting for the rest of it, the part where it turned out to be something else, and when nothing else came he did not seem to know what to do with his hands.

"The others," he said. "If I wrote and said it was true. They'd come." He looked at the book. "I can't write."

"I can," said Hale, and turned to a clean page.



Bram's round furnace in the corner of the forge yard had been as high as his chest since the winter before last. By the end of February it was taller than he was.

He had built it up out of tithe-house firebrick, course on course, a fat chimney lined with clay, with a door at the bottom for the tap and two holes for the bellows, and he had taken the crucible out of the heart of it and thrown it on the scrap heap, and looked at it there afterward as though it had let him down. A crucible held what a crucible held. What Emeric wanted was a furnace you could fill from the top, charcoal and broken iron in layers, the way you build a fire you mean to keep, so that the iron melted as it came down through the heat and ran out at the bottom, and you could go on feeding it from above for as long as there was iron to feed. On his other world they had called it a cupola, and he called it that now, and Bram said that was a silly name for a chimney and used it anyway by the end of the week.

The iron was grates.

Every one of the tithe houses had had a grate in it, heavy and black and cast, and Bram had been fetching them down the hill since October, one handcart at a time, and stacking them against the forge wall. He broke them now with a sledge on the anvil block into pieces the size of a fist. Cast iron broke clean. It did not bend first, as good iron did. It simply gave, with a dry flat crack, and the boys who worked the bellows jumped at the first few and then stopped jumping.

"You want me to pour a grate," Bram said, "the length of a man."

"Yes."

"It'll crack."

"Not if it cools slow. Under sand. For three days."

Bram looked at the pit he had dug in the floor of the forge yard, where the carpenter's pattern had been rammed round with damp sand and lifted out again, so that the shape of the thing lay in the ground like a footprint: a long flat bed with two raised rails along its top, and feet under it, and a heavy block at one end.

"What does it do?" he said. "When it's a grate."

"It makes things round."

"I make things round."

"You do." Emeric looked at him. "Nobody else in Kaldo can. At the moment, round is a property of Bram. I want it to be a property of a machine."

Bram took that as he took most things Emeric said about the future, which was with the face of a man being told a horse can be taught to read. Then he thought about it, which took a while, and Emeric watched it go through him, the work he did by eye that nobody could check, and the boys he had tried to teach it to, and he picked up the sledge again and broke another grate without saying anything at all.

They lit it on the Thursday. Charcoal first, a deep bed of it, and then a layer of broken grate, and a shovel of broken limestone from the kiln to take the dirt out, and charcoal again, and grate again, up to the top. Two boys on the bellows, one at each hole, pumping in turn. And Orrin, standing at the lower of the two holes with his sleeves pushed up and his hands held out flat toward the fire, because a charcoal fire under a bellows got hot, and iron wanted hotter.

"Is this a day rate," Orrin said, about an hour in, "or by the job?"

"Day," said Bram.

"Then I'm in no hurry." He was running with sweat. "I'd like that noted."

It came at the fourth hour. Bram knocked the clay plug out of the tap hole with a bar and the iron came out after it, not orange, nothing like forge heat, but a white so bright it had no color in it at all, and ran down the clay channel in the floor with a skin on it like milk and a smell like a struck flint, and went into the sand. Sparks came off the top of it in little stars. Nobody spoke. The boys on the bellows forgot to pump, and Bram shouted at them, and they pumped.

When the mould was full and the tap was stopped Bram stood over the pit and looked at the iron going from white to orange to a dull angry red under its crust, and put his hands on his hips.

"It's a grate," he said. "A long one."

The spindle he forged out of the cranes, the swinging iron arms that had held the pots over every hearth in the tithe houses, three of them welded together in the fire into one bar as thick as a wrist. The bushes it would turn in came out of the box under his bench, where the brass pump fittings had been lying since the winter of the kettles, because brass would not do for wire and Bram did not throw things away. Everything in it had belonged to somebody once. Emeric did not say so. Bram knew it better than he did.



The lathe went into a lean-to against the back wall of the powerhouse, where a belt could come off the shaft through a slot in the wall and the river could turn it.

It looked like nothing when it was set up. A long rough grey bed on four stone blocks, a heavy head at one end with the spindle through it, a tail at the other that slid along the rails and could be clamped, and a rest between them for the tool. The rails on top were the whole of it. If the rails were true, everything that ran along them would be true. If they were out by a hair, everything that came off the machine would be out by a hair, forever, and so would everything made on anything made on it.

The rails, fresh out of the sand, were out by a good deal more than a hair.

Vesk dressed them. He put his hands on the iron at one end, flat, and stood with his head down, and then moved a hand's width along, and stood again, the way he had gone round Tess's wheel in October. Orrin followed him with his palms out, warming the iron wherever Vesk pointed with his chin, because Vesk said, in the longest speech anyone had yet had from him, that cold iron argued and warm iron listened. It took them the better part of two days. Emeric checked the work behind them with a waxed linen thread of Abloh's pulled taut from end to end and a candle held low on the far side, looking for light under the thread, and on the first day he found it in nine places and on the second day he found it nowhere at all.

Vantic's ruling came down the hill on the first Monday in March, on a half-sheet, under the House's form and the date. Ferra had laid the question before her already answered, as she laid everything now: a shop that existed only to keep the powerhouse running was part of the powerhouse, and the powerhouse had stood on the day of signing. Vantic had read it once and written under it in her small grey hand.

A shop for the machine is the machine's.



The yard boys had been told to keep out of the lean-to while Vesk worked, and so they stood at the door of it, which was not the same thing, and Orrin, who had a weakness for any audience at all, gave them something to look at.

He held up his right hand to them, palm out, as a man holds his hand up to stop a cart.

A ring stood up in the air in front of it, faint and quick, the mark in its middle there and gone before Emeric could read it. Then there was nothing. Except that the light from the door bent very slightly over Orrin's palm, the way it bends over a hot stove, and there was a pane in the air a hand's width from his skin that had not been there before, and was not there to look at, and was.

"Go on," Orrin said to the boys. "Something hot."

They looked at Emeric. Everybody in Kaldo knew what the prince did with fire. Emeric, who had not meant to be part of this, found that he was, and drew a thumb of flame out of nothing over his own palm, the expensive way, out of himself and not the lamp, and threw it at Orrin's hand.

It struck the pane and went out across it like water thrown on glass. Orrin did not blink. He did not move his hand. He said, "That's a prince's fire, that is," to the boys, as if he were selling it to them, and they laughed, and one of them, the smallest, looked down at the bench beside the door where the roof bolts lay in a box, and took out a nut, and threw it.

It was not a hard throw. The nut struck the pane and stopped dead and dropped, and Orrin went white to the lips and shut his eyes, and his hand came down. He stood there breathing through his nose while the boys stared.

"That one," he said, when he could, "I felt."

"More than the fire?"

"A good deal more than the fire." Orrin opened his eyes and looked at Emeric with some resentment. "You paid ten for that flame, out of yourself. I paid one to stop it. He paid nothing for the nut, and I paid the whole of it." He shook his hand out. "Throw a rock at a mage, never a spell. Everybody on the coast knows that. You're not on the coast."

That night, behind the shutters, Emeric held his own hand up to the candle and tried it, and got the ring, and got the pane, very thin, for about the time it took to breathe out, and then had to sit down. He did not mind. It was a new thing, and it had a cost, and the cost was written plainly enough on his body that he could read it. He put it on the list with the others.



The rule lived in the mill's old tally room.

It was the coolest room in the mill yard, stone on three sides and the bank on the fourth, with one small window, and Harl had kept his weights in it for thirty years, the big iron weights and the little brass ones, and the beam he weighed the farmers' sacks on, and nothing else. The weights were still there, on their shelf. Emeric had not had the heart to move them and found, once the rule was in the room, that he did not want to. They belonged in a room like that.

The rule was a bar of crane iron as long as his forearm, forged square, filed flat on one face until Bram said there was nothing left to file, and laid on a strip of felt on a stone shelf away from the window. On its flat face, cut with the finest point Bram owned, there were thirteen lines. Twelve spaces. Each space was an inch.

He had set the first space himself. There had been nothing else to set it by. The crown's standard was a stone in the capital that three clerks read three ways, and Bram's thumb was Bram's, and Sela's was Sela's. He had a measure of his own in his hands from the other life, from years at a drawing board and a bench, an inch he knew as surely as he knew his own name written down, and he had marked it on the iron and checked it against his hands and his eye and the width of a rule he could see on a desk in a room that no longer existed, and then he had stopped checking, because it did not matter whether it was exactly the inch he remembered. It only mattered that there was one, and that it was here, and that everything in Kaldo was measured against it, and it was measured against nothing.

The first copies went to the masons.

They had begun on the aqueduct's arch in the last week of February, out at the fallen span where the timber trough had stood on its trestles for a year, cutting the stones that would carry the water over the gap. An arch was a set of wedges, every one of them leaning on the two beside it, and every one cut by a different man with a different rod his master had cut for him, and the masons had been arguing about whose rod was right since before the plague. Bram made them each a new rod, filed to the copy, and Emeric took the rods out to the span himself and laid them in the masons' hands one at a time. The old master among them, a grey man with stone dust in the creases of his face, held his new rod against his old one and looked at the difference, which was the width of a thumbnail over three feet, and then looked at Emeric for some time.

"Which one's wrong?" he said.

"Neither," said Emeric. "Now there's only one."



Tess was still in the ward. Imani had said the end of March, and Imani's end of March did not mean the twentieth.

He brought her the first gauge on the fourth of March, in his pocket, wrapped in a bit of linen. It was a block of iron, square, and nothing to look at, and Vesk had shaped it and Bram had lapped it on a stone until the two ends were flat enough that when you pressed it against the face of the rule they clung. He put it in her hand. She closed her fingers on it, and opened them, and turned it over, and then she put it down on the board across her chair and looked at it without saying anything, which was how he knew she understood what it was before he told her.

"An inch," he said. "This one's the same as the rule in the tally room. The masons have their rods. Bram has a block like this. You have this one."

Tess opened her bench book at the back, where the cover was coming away from the boards, and took something out from under the loose end of the cover. A piece of paper folded very small, soft as cloth from being folded. She unfolded it on the board. It was the margin of an alphabet sheet, torn off, from the school's first autumn, and in a careful forge hand there were two numbers on it. Thirty-one. Twenty-eight.

He had forgotten. She had not.

"Why isn't a thumb in here the same as a thumb at the forge," she said. Not as a question. As a thing she was reading back to him.

"Because nobody had made one."

She looked at the block. "And now somebody has."

"Now somebody has."

She folded the paper up again and put it back where it had been, under the cover, and he did not ask why she kept it. Then she looked up at him with the face she had when she was about to ask a question that would take the rest of the afternoon.

"How do you know the rails are true?"

"I looked for light under a thread."

"How do you know the thread's true?"

"It's pulled tight. A tight thread is straight."

"Then why didn't you make the rails with a thread? Why Vesk?"

He pulled the stool over and sat down.

He told her how a true lathe was made where there was no Vesk. That the first one was made by hand, and it was a bad one, and it wobbled, and you used it anyway, slowly, carefully, to cut one thing on it as well as it could be cut, which was a screw. A screw cut on a bad lathe was better than the lathe, because the cutting averaged out the wobble over every turn. And then you built a second lathe, and put that screw in it, to move the tool, and the second lathe was better than the first, because its screw was better. And it cut a better screw. And that went into a third. Each one a little truer than the one that made it, a generation at a time, the way a family gets a little taller over a hundred years if it eats.

"There's a name for the first one," he said. "The mother lathe."

"How many generations?"

"As many as it takes. A lifetime, sometimes. Flat is the same. You can make a true flat with no master to copy at all. Three plates. You rub the first on the second, and the second on the third, and the third on the first, and round again, and scrape the high spots off each, and after a few hundred turns they can't help being flat, because if any one was curved it couldn't fit both the others." He watched her see it. "It takes weeks. Vesk did it in two days."

"Then why does it matter?"

Bram had come in. He was standing at the end of the bed with his cap in his hands and grease to the wrists, and he had heard the last of it, and he answered before Emeric could.

"He wants me to make a bad one," Bram said. "Next to the good one. By hand. For the school." He said it as a man reports a lunacy he has agreed to take part in. "Three plates and a screw and the rest of it."

"Why?" said Tess.

Emeric looked at the block of iron on her board.

"In case the magic isn't there," he said. "One day there won't be a Vesk in the room. Somebody should know where it comes from."

Imani, at the next bed, with her back to them and a sheet in her hands, said nothing at all, and Tess took her pen out and wrote mother lathe at the top of a new page, and underlined it twice.



He went into the room the next afternoon, with Ward in the chair by the door and the shutters closed, because Ferra's rules said never alone and never at night, and in two and a half years he had found no reason good enough to break them.

The room was as it always was. The bench, the light, the shelf. A candle on the bench where he had put it.

He laid his hands flat on the wood on either side of it, and looked at the wick, and did not move them.

It took. Not a breath on a spoon of soup. A flame, a proper one, a finger tall, standing up off the wick as if somebody had leaned in with a taper, and the ring and the mark up over the bench where his hand would have been if his hand had been anywhere but flat on the wood. He looked at it. He did not quite believe it. He put it out and tried again, and it did not take, and he tried a third time and a fourth, and it did not take again, and when he came out Ward was sitting in the chair by the door exactly as he had been, and the light through the shutters had moved about the width of a finger.

That night in his own chamber, behind the cloak and the shutters, with the stool and the candle, he tried it once more. A breath on a spoon of soup. The wick glowed and thought about it and went dark.

So it was in him somewhere. It had happened in the room, where things went faster. His body had not been told yet.



Ferra came down to the tally room on the evening of the sixth to price it, because she priced everything, and because, she said, a room with a lock on it in the barony's mill yard was a room she would be asked about.

The light was going. He had the door open for it, and the lamp not lit yet, and she stood inside the door with her book under her arm and her shawl back off her hair, which the wind off the river had got into, and looked at the shelf.

"What does it cost?"

"Nothing. A bar of crane iron. An afternoon of Bram. A file."

"Then why is it behind a lock?"

"Because everything in Kaldo will be measured against it." He nodded at it on its felt. "The masons' rods. Bram's gauges. Tess's block. Every one of them is a copy of that. If it's knocked, or bent, or a boy borrows it to measure his brother, every copy is wrong and nobody knows."

She went over to the shelf and looked down at the bar. Then, as she would have put her hand on a page to hold it while she read, she laid her palm flat on the iron.

He had her wrist before he had decided anything. He lifted her hand off the rule and held it up a little way above it, in the air, the way you lift a child's hand off a stove.

"You're warming it," he said. "It grows."

Ferra looked at his hand round her wrist. He looked at it too. He let go.

"By how much?" she said.

"Less than a tenth of a hair."

"A tenth of a hair." She was still looking at the rule, not at him. "Then why does it matter?"

"Because the next one is made from this one," he said. "And the one after that from that."

She was quiet. She turned her hand over and looked at the palm of it, as if it might have something written on it, and then she opened her book and wrote, standing up, without a table, and he did not try to read it. Outside, above them, up the bank, the wall lamps came on, white, all of them at once along the stretch over the bridge, the way they did every evening now that the river was high.

Ferra closed the book and went to the door. She stopped in it, with the river behind her going dark and the wall above her going white, and looked up.

"Eight," she said, and went up the path toward the water gate.

He stood in the door of the tally room and watched her go, until she was through the gate. It was the sixth of March.
