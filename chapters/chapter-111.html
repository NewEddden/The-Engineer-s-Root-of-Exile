Chapter 111 - Kaldo From Above

Imani lost the argument about how high, and won the one about who.

"Not Armand," she said.

Armand had asked to go. He had asked at the table, in front of the six, which was not a thing he did, and he had given his reason, which was that he had once told Emeric across a bench that mages who crossed the northern passes came to the top of the road with more in them than they started with, and that the tower had always said it was the thin air going to their heads, and that nobody had ever gone up with a bench to see. He would like, he said, to be the first man from the tower who went up to see.

Imani let him finish.

"You'll be above the passes," she said. "Above any pass anybody has ever crossed. It'll be cold enough to stop a hand, and the air will be thin enough to stop a heart that's been working as long as yours has. And you hold this barony when he isn't in it." She did not raise her voice. "You signed a sheet in the spring that says you catch nothing. I'm not going to write a second one that says you don't fly. I'm asking you not to make me."

Armand looked at her for a long time. Then he looked at his hands, folded on the table, where anybody could see them.

"Then somebody must go who can feel it," he said. "Or you will have only his word for what is up there. And his word is very good, but it is one word."

That was true, and Emeric had already thought it, and had already decided who.

He asked Vesk at the etching floor, at the end of the day, when the twelve had gone home and Vesk was at the long table under the north window with the sieve and the pails. He asked him plainly: to come up in the ship, with the medium open, and say what he felt, and have it written down beside what Emeric felt, so that there were two readings and not one.

Vesk put the sieve down.

"Why not Orrin?"

"Orrin would talk the whole way up."

Vesk considered that, and seemed to find it sufficient. He had been five years in Kaldo, and a hired mage under a dead baron before that, and a captured one for a short while between the two, and in all that time Emeric had never once asked him for anything that was not work the terms allowed. He looked at Emeric across the long table for some time, with his hands flat either side of the sieve, and Emeric had the strong sense of a man adding a column he had been keeping for a long while and finding it come out to something he had not expected.

"I'll come," he said.

So it was four again. Emeric, Ward at the levers, Fletcher at the helm, and Vesk.

Ferra did not go, because the two keys were not going into the air together twice, and she said so herself before Saban could. She wrote one condition in the margin of the plan, as she had in the summer, and it was one line. Over water, the whole way.

That meant the lake.

Fletcher brought her up the river under steam, over three days, which was what she had been drawn for before anybody had drawn anything else into her: the river first, and then the coast. She took the bends slowly and the shallows more slowly, with a leadsman in her bow, and at every hamlet on the banks people came down to the water to watch her go by, because the story had come up the river ahead of her on the salt boats and they knew what she was. On the third evening she came up the western branch past the island, under the walls, with the whole town on them, and out into the lake above it, black and long and smoking, and anchored a quarter of a mile above the island's head. The whole of Kaldo came down to the quays and stood there in the dusk and looked at her, and nobody went home to their supper for an hour.

They went up on the fourth morning, at first light, with the steamboat under them on the lake.

Emeric had brought two things aboard that were not in any plan he had shown the table.

The first was a lamp stone on a cord, a plain one out of Saban's box, charged by Orrin to warm, which he hung over the rail on six feet of line so that it swung free in the air below her side. It was the simplest gauge he could think of. A charged stone in open air, with no casing and no neck and no cap, would give to the air whatever the air would take and take from it whatever the air would give, and it would glow brighter or dimmer with the difference. Every other stone on the ship was sealed, or rode in iron, or sat with its neck in a rail. The one on the cord would tell him what the sky was.

The second was a stick. It was a rod of ash as long as his arm with the twelve divisions of the master foot scratched along it, and he had worked out on it, the night before, in the long room, what the island would measure against the stick held at arm's length at every height he could think of. He knew how wide the island was. Ferra had walked it with a chain in the first year. A thing a known size, seen from above at arm's length against a known rule, told a man how far above it he was, and the stick told him that to the width of a scratch. He had marked on it the height at which he meant to turn back: the height at which the island would cover three divisions and a half, which was higher than any mountain in four baronies, and lower, he hoped, than it needed to be.

Ward moved the lift lever forward.

She came up out of the lake the way she had come up out of the sea, with the water running off her in sheets, and hung, and Ward did not stop her. He brought her up past the height of a mast, and past the height of the keep's tower, and the island went down under her port rail and got smaller, and the quays full of people got smaller, and the steamboat on the lake below became a dark mark with a thread of smoke, circling.

Emeric had seen the island from the keep's tower. He had never seen it from here.

It lay in its river like a thing laid on a table. The lake spread above it to the north, and the lake's water came down out of it and split round the island's head in two branches, one either side of the wall, and joined again below the town, and the wall went round the island in its ring, and the one bridge went out from the western gate across the branch to the shore. The old town was inside the wall in its streets, all of them at once, every roof. He could see the church. He could see the keep, and the yard, and the well in the yard where Ward had left him alive every morning for ten years. He could see the foundry on its bank below the island, and round it the part of the town that had not fitted inside the wall, two streets of pale new roofs and the start of a third, with no wall round them at all. He could see the shelf where the section had fallen, and the long roof of the etching floor, and the new walls of Imani's hospital, a pale square at the top of the long street with the trenches still open round it. And going north from the foundry yard, beside the river road, past the island's head and on along the shore of the lake, a thin new line, pale where the ballast was fresh and darker down its middle where the iron lay. It ran straight where the road bent. A little under four miles out it stopped in a field, like a line on a page where somebody had lifted the pen, and the road went on north without it toward the hills. Beyond the bridge the farms went out west and southwest in their strips, and the dark of the Whispering Woods along the road, and in the far south, where the river went, a haze that was the lower water, and beyond it, very faint, a line that was the sea.

He fixed it. He did it the way he had once fixed a propeller in the delve, eleven slices through it, and he took the whole of it into himself at once, every roof and every street and the shape of every one of them, and held it, to take down into the room when he came down.

"Cold," said Fletcher, at the helm.

It was. It had been coming on since the height of the tower and it came on faster now, a cold that went through a coat as if the coat were not there, and the wind of her own going came across the deck and took the breath out of a man's mouth before he had finished with it. Fletcher had put his hands inside his sleeves. Vesk had his arms wrapped round himself at the rail, and was looking not at the town but at the lamp stone on its cord, swinging below them in the wind, and at nothing.

Emeric held the stick out at arm's length and sighted the island along it. Five divisions.

At four and a half, his head began to ache. It came on behind the eyes, dull, and did not go, and when he looked at the others he could see it on them, Fletcher with his eyes half shut against it and Ward breathing through his mouth in a way Ward never did. At four, Emeric found that he was breathing hard, standing still, as though he had run up the keep stair, and he watched Ward's lips.

He watched all their lips. He did it without thinking about it, the way a man who has been taught a thing in another life does it before he knows he has remembered it. There had been a life, once, in which he had known what thin air did, and what it looked like when it was doing it, and in that life a man's lips went blue before anything else did, and a man whose lips had gone blue had very little time left in which to decide anything. Nobody in this world had ever been high enough to learn it. Nobody at this rail knew he was watching for it. Ward's lips were pale. They were not blue.

"Three and a half," he said. That was the turn.

He opened the medium to tell Ward to take her down, and the medium was different.

It had been thinning as they climbed. He had felt it go, a little at every height, the way the air had gone, and he had put it down to the same thing. Now it was not thinning. It had stopped. And then, as he held it, it was thickening, very slowly, from above, as though they were rising up through the bottom of something into the body of it.

On the cord, the lamp stone had begun to glow.

It was faint at first, in the grey light, so faint he thought he had imagined it. Then it was not faint. It was brighter than Orrin had left it. It was brighter than a charged lamp stone had any business being, and brightening as he watched, swinging on its cord in the wind with a light in it like a lamp in a window.

"Look at the rack," he said.

Vesk was already looking. He had gone to the hatch and was kneeling at it, looking down into her hold at the rack of cells with their splinters in their caps. Every cell she carried had been thinning since she left the lake. The splinters had gone from bright to less than bright, evenly, all together, the way they always went.

They had stopped going.

As Emeric watched, over Vesk's shoulder, the splinters began to come back.

He knew why before he had finished seeing it. The cells rode with their necks open in the rails, and the rails were stone, and the stone lay in the air. In the air of the lake the medium was a thin rain, and the rails took nothing from it worth taking. Here the medium was not a rain. He could feel it on every side of the ship, dense and deep and full, the way he had never felt it anywhere, and the rails were drinking it, and the necks were drinking it from the rails, and the cells were filling.

"It's like stepping out of the drizzle," he said, "into a river."

Vesk did not answer. He was kneeling by the hatch with both hands flat on the deck, and his face in the grey light was the face of a man who has been hungry his whole life without knowing it and has just been given bread.

"Vesk."

"I'm full," Vesk said. He said it as though he were reporting a thing he did not have a word for. "I've never been full. I didn't know there was full." He looked up. "It's coming in. I'm not doing anything. It's coming in."

Mages who crossed the northern passes came to the top of the road with more in them than they started with. The tower had said it was the air going to their heads. Armand had said that across a bench in the spring, and Emeric had put it on the shelf with the other things the tower had never looked at, and here it was, under his hands, two miles above a lake: a band of the medium lying over the world like a second sea, above the height of any pass, and every stone and every mage who entered it filled.

He looked at the rack again. The splinters were brighter than when they had left the lake. The ship was carrying more than she had come up with.

The data said it was filling. The data said the higher they went, the thicker it got. Every number he had, the probe stone and the splinters and the medium itself and Vesk kneeling at the hatch with his face alight, said go on.

"Up," he said. "A little. Slowly."

Ward looked at him for a moment, with his lips pale and his breath coming short, and moved the lever.

She went up through the band. It thickened. The lamp stone on its cord was so bright now that it threw a shadow of the rail across the deck in the grey morning. Emeric held the stick up with a hand he could hardly feel and sighted the island. Three divisions. Two and three quarters.

He felt it go before it went.

It went from above, the way it had come, and it went all at once. One breath the medium was a river round them, dense and full on every side. The next it was thinning, fast, from the top down, as if the ship's upper works had put their heads out through the top of the river into the air above it. And above it there was nothing.

Not less. Nothing. He reached into the medium above the deck and found no medium there at all, the way he had found no stone in the cup in the forge the night he emptied one, the lining of an empty pocket, a place where the world should be and was not.

On the cord, the lamp stone went out.

It did not dim. It went out in a breath, all of it, the way a candle goes out in a door, and hung swinging in the wind below her side, a dull grey stone. Everything Orrin had put in it and everything the band had put in it after him had gone out of it into nothing, in the time it took to see it happen.

Vesk made a sound. It was a small sound, not a word, the sound of a man who has missed a step in the dark, and he put both hands to his chest.

"It's going," he said. "It's going out of me."

And under their feet, silently, the feed died.

Emeric felt it. The rails were stone and the stone lay in the air, and the air was nothing now, and what the cells sent up the rails to the deciding stone went out of the rails into the nothing on every side as fast as it went in. The necks held. They were thin, and they let out what they let out and no faster, and the cells in their casings in the dark kept almost all of what they had. But the stations got nothing. The deciding stone had nothing to send them. There was no sound. There was no lurch. There was simply, for the space of a breath, sixteen dense strange hot places in two rows in her frames with nobody feeding them, and a ship at a height above any mountain in four baronies with nothing under her at all.

She dropped.

He felt his stomach go up under his ribs and the deck go away from his feet, and Fletcher's hands leave the wheel, and Ward's hand go to the lever and find it doing nothing, and he reached for the reserve.

He had done it once before, at the section, on a shelf of rock with a river under it and a count of fifteen to spare. He did it now with no count at all. Eight threads at once, to eight stones under the deck beams, each given the same number, the number he had carried in his head since the beach, a hair under her weight. He felt each stone take its work, and the whole of the ship come onto them from below, and he let go of all eight in the same instant and drew nothing back.

It cost him everything the section had cost him and the same again. The deck tilted under his feet and did not come back, and then it did.

She stopped falling.

She did not stop all at once. She came onto the reserve the way a man comes onto a rope he has been falling past, hard, with a jolt that went through every plate in her and every tooth in his head, and then she was hanging. Level. Not quite still. Coming down, slowly, a hair at a time, the way the drawing on Fletcher's stocks had said she would come down, from stones above her middle, like a lamp from a hook.

Emeric was on his knees. He did not remember going down onto them. Ward had him by the back of the coat with one hand and the lever post with the other, and Ward's face was very close to his, and Ward's lips were blue.

"Down," Emeric said. "She's coming down. Let her."

Ward let her.

She came down through the empty sky on eight stones spending themselves, and Emeric could feel them spending, the way the stones under the section had spent in the thaw, faster than anything he had ever set, because they carried more than anything he had ever set. And then the bottom of the empty sky was the top of the band, and she came down into it the way she had come down into the sea, and the medium closed over her.

The feed woke.

It woke as silently as it had died. The rails took the band's charge and the cells' and gave it to the deciding stone, and the deciding stone had something to send again and sent it, and the stations took it, and the ship, which had been hanging from her reserve like a lamp from a hook, was being carried from below again, and did not seem to notice the difference. On the cord, below her side, the lamp stone began to glow.

The reserve went on spending. There was nothing to take it back by. It had been set, and it would spend itself on her whether she needed it or not, all eight together, as it had been built to, and by the time Ward had brought her down out of the band and through the cold and the thin air and the ache behind the eyes, and the island had grown back to the size of an island under her port rail, every stone in the reserve was dead.

Vesk was sitting on the deck with his back to the rail. He had not moved since the band closed over them. He was breathing slowly, with his eyes shut, and his hands were still on his chest.

"Back," he said, when Emeric crouched beside him. "It came back. When we came down into it." He opened his eyes. "It was all of it. Up there. It wasn't going out of me into anything. There wasn't anything for it to go into. It was just going."

She came down onto the lake off the island at a little after noon, into water, as the margin of the plan had said she would, and the steamboat came alongside, and Imani came up the ladder first, before anybody could stop her, with the canvas roll open in her hands.

She looked at all four of them in turn. She looked longest at Ward's lips, which were pale again, and at Emeric, who was sitting on the hatch cover because he could not yet stand. Then she knelt by Vesk.

The ship rode at her anchor off the island with her cells fuller than when she had left it.

Every splinter in the rack was brighter than it had been at first light. She had gone up into the sky and been paid for going, and come down richer than she went, by a margin Armand could not have read on any bench in the world. And under her deck beams, where the reserve had been, there were eight grey stones in eight housings, dead.

Armand wrote them up that evening in the strongroom ledger, under the heading he had made at the lower water. The reserve. He did not write lent. He wrote spent, and the date, and the weight, and beside the weight, because the ledger had a column for it and he did not leave columns empty, the Crown's price of what had been spent. It came to fourteen Platinum Rand.

"Replaced together," he said, and did not look up. "Every one of them. Off one face, read together, fitted together." He closed the book. "It is a very good rule. It was worth a great deal in the thaw." He put his pen in the tray. "It is now worth fourteen Platinum Rand a time, and I would like it written down somewhere that I said so."

"It's written down," said Ferra, from the door. She had her book open. "I wrote it before you did."

Emeric took the island down into the room that night.

The six came with him, as they always did, into the boardroom over the town, where Kaldo lay spread across the great table in relief the way he had first built it out of maps he had read and streets he had walked, with the parts he had not walked vague at the edges and Saban's buildings standing in it here and there where Saban had put them. He had fixed the town from two miles up. He laid it over the room, all of it at once, every roof, and the room took it.

Streets moved. A lane on the east side that he had drawn as running straight turned out to bend. The yard behind the tannery was twice the size. Two streets the room had never held came up whole on the bank round the foundry, where the room still showed a yard and a stack of pig iron, because the room was older than the summer Ferra's count went past four thousand and the island ran out of houses. And Saban's buildings, the ones Saban had placed on the first day, confidently and in detail, from a memory of the town that was years old, went.

Not all of them. A roof seen from above is only a roof. Where Saban had put a chandler's and there was now open ground, or a wall, or somebody else's roof entirely, the room let his building go, and it was gone. But where he had put a chandler's and there was still a roof the same size in the same place, there was no way to tell from two miles up whether it was still a chandler's or had been a school for six years, and the room kept what Saban had given it, and it stood there as confidently as it always had, and quite possibly as wrong.

Saban walked round the table, slowly, looking at the gaps where his buildings had been.

"Seven," he said. "I've lost seven."

"You've kept eleven," said Ferra.

"I've kept eleven that nobody can prove," Saban said, with great dignity. "That's not the same as being right. But I'll take it."

Emeric looked at the eleven for some time after the others had gone.

He had wanted the room to be true. It was truer than it had been. It was also, now, a room that anybody could look at and believe, because it had been checked against the sky, and eleven of the buildings in it had not been checked against anything at all. He did not like that. He did not know yet what to do about it. He put it in the column for things he could not answer, and went up to bed, and slept for eleven hours, and dreamed about nothing, which was the first time in ten years that he could remember doing so.
