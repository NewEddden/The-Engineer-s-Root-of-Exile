Chapter 111 - Tuyeres

Bram had stood in the third bay of the old works in the first summer, inside the old pegs, at about the place a man would stand to feed a furnace that did not exist, and looked at the clean footing at his feet. Emeric had seen him do it. He had not said anything, then, because there had been nothing to say; there had been no coke, and no money, and no ore but what Grayrock chose to sell, and Bram had been the only smith in the barony for twenty years and had never once needed two of anything.

In March of the fourth year there was a furnace on it.

It stood in the third bay with the roof taken off over it and a new one built higher, a square tower of the tithe houses' brick lined inside with firebrick that had cost the barony real silver out of the fifth, twice the height of a man and a half again, narrowing at the top to a mouth and swelling in the middle like a bottle and narrowing again at the bottom to a hearth no wider than a cart wheel. A ramp of planks ran up from the yard to a platform at the top, where the boys would tip the charge in by the barrow: coke, then ore, then a scatter of broken limestone from the kiln, then coke again, round and round, day and night, for as long as the furnace was in blast. At the bottom on the river side there was a hole in the brick at the height of a man's knee, stopped with clay, where the iron would come out. And on two sides, a little higher, there were two more holes, small ones, each with a pipe going into it.

The tuyeres.

It was a stupid word. It was the word in the book, a metallurgy text with a red cover that he had read for an examination and never opened again in his first life, and in his second it had come back to him whole, every page, and the word with it, and he had said it aloud in the bay in January and Bram had made him say it again and then refused to say it at all. Bram called them the nozzles. The yard boys called them the twyers, which was nearer.

The blast came from the wheel.

The works' old wheel on the river side had turned the forge bellows since before anyone could remember, when it turned at all. Bram had taken the bellows off it in January and put on instead a pair of tubs, two great iron cylinders on end, each with a piston in it on a rod, and the wheel's crank drove the rods up and down by turns, so that one tub was always pushing air while the other drew it. Blowing tubs. The air went out of them into a long box of boards to even it, and out of the box down the pipes into the tuyeres, and into the bottom of the furnace, under the burning coke, in a steady roar that could be heard from the east bridge.

That was the whole of it. Burn coke in a tall tower with a great deal of air blown into the bottom. The coke burned so hot, with the air forced into it, that the ore above it gave up its iron, and the iron soaked up some of the coke as it went down, and melted, and ran down through the burning heap into the hearth in drops. The limestone took up the rock out of the ore, the earth and sand and stone that was not iron, and that melted too, into a glassy scum that floated on the iron in the hearth. Slag. When there was enough iron in the hearth, you knocked the clay out of the hole and it came out.

He had explained it to Bram in a paragraph, in January, standing in the bay. Bram had listened to the whole paragraph and then said it was a forge stood on end, which was true, and gone to look at the footing.



The first blast lasted nine hours. Then the tuyeres burned through.

They had been made of iron, two thick tubes Bram had forged in February with a flare at the end, and they had sat in the brick with their mouths at the edge of the fire, and for nine hours they had held, glowing red and then orange and then a yellow that hurt to look at. In the tenth hour the left one began to sag at the mouth. Bram saw it from the platform. By the time he had come down the ramp the end of it was dripping, and the air was going into the furnace not in a hard jet under the coke but in a loose spray out of the side of a melted pipe, and the heat at that side of the hearth was falling off.

They let it down. There was nothing else to do. They shut off the blast and let the furnace go quiet, and for two days it stood in the bay ticking and cooling, with a hearth full of half-made iron and slag gone hard in the bottom of it like a stone in a dog's belly, and Bram and the boys broke it out with bars on the third day, swearing.

The second time Bram made the tuyeres of clay. Fireclay, the same as the furnace lining, thick-walled, fired in the kiln. Clay would not melt at that heat. It lasted thirty hours, and then the right-hand one cracked across, and the left one wore away at the mouth, slowly, the blast eating it like the sea eats a cliff, until the jet was a spray again.

"Then we make them thick," Emeric said, on the morning after, standing in the bay with Bram looking at the cracked one on the floor. "And we make twenty. And we change them, every day, while she's in blast. You take the blast off for a few minutes and knock the old one out and drive the new one in. The book says,"

"The book's never changed a nozzle in a furnace that's in blast," said Bram.

"No."

"Nor have you."

"No."

Bram looked at the cracked tuyere at his feet.

"Ask Vesk," he said, after a while. He said it as though it were being pulled out of him with tongs. "He could shape one. Out of the same clay. Without a seam. Without a crack in it. He'd do it in an afternoon. He'd do twenty in a week."

"And when he goes home?"

"He's not going home. He likes the beer." Bram scowled. "I know what you're going to say. I said it to you myself, with the boiler. If Vesk makes it, Vesk has to mend it." He kicked the tuyere. "I'm saying it anyway. I'm saying there's a mage in my yard who could have this furnace in blast by Friday and we're standing here with a cracked pot."

"I'd use him," said Emeric, "if we had nothing else. I'd use him tomorrow to get us through to the summer. But I'd want the other way written down first. The way a smith does it with no mage in the yard. Because this is the one thing in Kaldo that has to work when the stones don't." He looked at the furnace. "Every stone in the barony can go dead in an afternoon if somebody cracks one in the wrong place. You've seen it. The furnace has to keep going through that. So does everything it makes."

Bram did not answer. He looked at the furnace for some time.

It was Tess who found the other way.

She had been at the works most days since January, in her chair, at the end of the bay, out of the boys' road, with a blanket over her knees and her slate, because the dynamo house was quiet in the spring with the river high and the engine idle and she had nothing to mend. She had watched both blasts from start to finish. She had watched the iron tuyere sag and the clay one crack. On the morning after the second she wheeled herself over to where Bram and Emeric were standing over the broken pipe, and looked at it, and then at the engine house, across the yard.

"The engine doesn't melt," she said.

They both looked at her.

"The cylinder." She pointed. "It's iron. It's got steam in it, all day, hot enough to scald you through a glove. And the firebox. It's got coal burning in it, all day, white, and the plates of it don't melt, because there's water on the other side of them. That's what the boiler is. It's a fire with water all round it." She looked at the tuyere on the floor. "So put water round that."

Bram opened his mouth, and shut it.

"Two pipes," said Tess. "One inside the other. The air goes down the middle one. And between them, water. From the race. In at the back, out at the back, all the time, running. The fire can be as hot as it likes on the end of it. It's never going to get the iron hotter than the water's carrying away." She tapped her slate on the arm of the chair. "Copper, not iron. Copper carries heat better. You said so. In the book you gave me. On the page about wires."

Emeric stood very still.

It was in the red book. A water-cooled tuyere, a jacket of copper with a stream running through it, and he had read the page, and he had known it, and he had stood in the bay for three days talking about changing clay pots every morning. He had been so sure of the order things came in, so sure the clay came first and the water came after, the way it had on his other world, that he had not once thought to look past the clay.

"Yes," he said.

"Did you know?" said Tess.

"Yes."

"Then why didn't you say?"

"Because I didn't think of it," Emeric said. "I knew it, and I didn't think of it. Those are different."

Tess considered this. Then she wrote something on her slate and turned it so he could not see what.

Bram had already gone. He was at the forge across the yard, pulling down the sheet of copper that had come up from the coast in January for the boat's rivets and that Fletcher had not yet asked for, and Emeric heard him start to swear at it, steadily, in the way that meant he was happy.



The third blast went in on the fourteenth of March, and on the morning of the sixteenth they tapped her.

Half Kaldo came across the bridge to watch. Nobody had asked them to. Word had gone round the market, as it went round the market about everything, and by the middle of the morning there were people standing along the bank outside the works and on the bridge and sitting on the wall of the coke shed, dock quarter women with their baskets and the cooper's boy and a knot of the levy who were supposed to be on the walk and were not, and Hollis with his oxen, who had stopped in the road and would not move on.

In front of the tap hole, on the floor of the bay, Bram's boys had made a bed of sand. Damp sand, from the river bar, raked flat and then cut into with a board: one long straight trench running away from the tap hole, and off it on each side a row of short trenches, side by side, like the teeth of a comb.

Tess looked at it from her chair.

"It looks like a sow," she said. "Lying down. With her litter."

"That's what it's called," said Emeric. "The long one's the sow. The little ones are the pigs." He watched her face. "Pig iron. That's what it's called. Because of that."

Tess stared at him. Then at the sand. Then she put her face in her hands and laughed until she had to hold the arms of the chair, and Bram, who was standing at the tap hole with the bar, looked round at her and then at Emeric and did not ask.

Haddon gave the word. It was his furnace as much as Bram's, by then. He had lived in the oven shed for two months and he had fed the furnace's charge for two days without sleeping, and he knew by the colour of the slag at the cinder notch, and the sound of the blast, and something else Emeric could not see, when the hearth was full.

"Now," he said.

Bram put the bar to the clay in the tap hole, and drove it, twice, three times, and on the fourth the clay gave and the bar went in and he pulled it back and stepped aside.

It came out white.

Not red. Not orange. White, like the lamps on the wall at night, a thick slow stream of it coming out of the hole in the brick and running down a little clay lip into the sand, and the heat of it came across the bay like a door opening on a summer noon, and the people on the bank all made a sound together, the same sound, which was not a word.

It ran down the sow. It ran into the pigs, one after another, filling each one and then spilling over into the next, the sand smoking and spitting as it went, a long white comb of fire laid out on the floor, and the air over it shook so that the far wall of the bay swam. And behind it, when the iron had run, the slag came, slower, thick and orange and glassy, and Bram's boys turned it off down a second channel to a pit outside the bay wall where it lay and crackled and went dark.

Nobody said anything for some time. The iron went from white to yellow to orange in the sand, and then to red, and then to a dull cherry, and the boys stood round it with their hands up against the heat.

"Iron," said Bram, eventually. He said it as though there had been some doubt about it.

"Iron," said Emeric.

"Our iron." Bram looked at it. "Out of Grayrock's ore. Grayrock's coal. Kaldo's fire." He did not look at Emeric. "Not bought. Not off a boat. Not off a barge at a price somebody else set." He put the bar down. "Made."

He walked out of the bay. Emeric let him go. He saw him go across the yard and stand by the race in the sun with his back to the works and his hands hanging at his sides, and nobody went near him.



Imani came across the bridge at noon, when the crowd had gone and the pigs had cooled enough to be broken off the sow with sledges. She came straight to the pit outside the bay wall, where the slag lay in a great dark glittering heap, still smoking at the bottom, and stood at the edge of it with her basket on her arm.

She did not come into the bay. Emeric went out to her.

It was the first time they had been alone since the night at the ward. He had seen her at the table on the Monday, where he had told the six that the Secretary of Health had refused the Chancellor and the holder both, and won, and Ferra had written it down without comment, and Imani had looked at the table and not at him. He had seen her in the market, at a distance. He had not been to the ward. He had told himself it was because nobody in the castle had been ill.

She looked at the slag.

"In the first summer," she said, "I stood in that bay with my foot on that footing and asked you what comes out of the bottom of the thing, and where does it go." She did not look at him. "You said you'd think about it."

"I did think about it."

"Did you." She nudged the edge of the heap with her boot. A piece of it broke off and rolled, ringing, like coke. "Where does it go?"

"Not in the river," he said. "That was the first thing I thought of. It's rock, mostly. Melted and gone hard. There's nothing in it that'll poison the water, not the way the tannery did, but you'd fill the river with it inside a year at this rate and the bar would come up and Fletcher's slip would be on dry land." He looked at the heap. "So it goes under the roads. Broken small, under the gravel, the way they do it on my other..." He stopped. "The way it's done. It drains. It packs hard. The high street's mud from November to April. In two years it won't be."

Imani looked at the heap for a while.

"And the rest?"

"Into the cement. When the kiln's lit. Ground up with the lime." He knew it was right as he said it. "It sets harder with it in."

She nodded, slowly. She did not write it down. She did not need to; she would remember, and in two years she would walk the high street in November and look at her boots.

"All right," she said.

She shifted the basket on her arm. Neither of them said anything else, and it was not easy, the not saying it, and he did not know whether it was harder for her or for him. He could see the place on her neck where the curl had come loose, though it was tied up now, hard, under the cloth, and not a hair out of it.

"The sewer," she said at last. "Ferra says the brick's coming out of the tithe houses for it this week."

"Yes."

"I'll walk it before a bucket goes down it." She turned to go. "Same as the drain."

"Same as the drain," he said.

She went back across the bridge with her basket, and he watched her go, and she did not look back, and he did not expect her to.



The sewer went up the lower ward in the last fortnight of March, in brick, at nothing.

It ran from the mouth of the old drain under the east wall, the one they had dug in the first autumn for the ward's water, and went on uphill under the main lane of the lower ward toward the inner ward: a trench as deep as a man and as wide as a door, and in the bottom of it an arch of tithe-house brick built over a wooden form, curved like the inside of an egg stood on its end, narrow at the bottom so that a little water ran fast and wide at the top so that a great deal of water had room. The book had drawn it that way. It was in a book about a city on his other world that had been dying of its own filth until somebody built exactly this, a long time ago, and stopped it. He had not needed to change a line.

Every house on the lane would run into it. Not yet. The houses would come later, one by one, as the barony could pay for their pipes. But the trunk was the thing that could not be added later without tearing up the lane twice, so the trunk went in now, while the brick was free and the ground was soft from the thaw.

Imani walked it on the last day of the month, with a lamp, bent double, from the river end to the head of the lane. It took her an hour. She came out at the top with brick dust in her hair and her skirt grey to the knee and told the mason that the joint at the turn by the cooper's was rough enough to catch a rag and that he would point it again before anything went down it, and he did.



The first plate came out of the forge at the end of the month.

It was Bram's, all of it. He had been drawing armour on the back of the boiler drawings since the war, in the evenings, in the lean-to, with a stub of chalk, and nobody had asked him to and he had not told anyone, and Emeric had only found out by turning one of the drawings over. Forged plate where the blows landed: the breast, the helm, the outside of the arms, beaten out of the new iron after it had been through the finery and come out soft and tough, the way a smith wants it. Cast plate everywhere else, the back and the thighs and the parts a man takes a blow on only when he is already losing, poured into sand moulds straight from the furnace and then dressed. Three sizes, because men came in three sizes, near enough, and a man who was between two could be padded.

No stones. Not one. Not on the breast, not at the wrist, not anywhere.

And no crest. No device, no colour, no badge. The suit was plain black iron, oiled to keep the rust off, the colour of a stove.

"They'll take us for hired men," said Osric, when he saw it on the stand in the guardroom.

"Yes," said Ward.

"Sellswords. Off a lord who can't afford colours."

"Yes," said Ward.

"Nobody ransoms a sellsword." Osric looked at the plain black breast. "If they take you in that, they don't hold you for coin. They cut your throat in the ditch."

"Yes," said Ward. He looked round the guardroom at the twenty men in it. "That is the point of it. Kaldo looks like a broke lord with hired men. A man who comes at Kaldo comes at a broke lord. He brings what a broke lord needs." He looked back at the suit. "And he finds us."

There was a silence in the guardroom.

"I'll have one," said Declan.

"And me," said a levy man at the back, whose name Emeric did not know, and then the others, one after another, not loudly, until it was all of them, and Osric last, grumbling, who said that if he was going to have his throat cut in a ditch he wanted the large size, and the guardroom laughed.

Bram meant to make forty. It would take him the rest of the year and most of the next.



The four knights' suits were different, and those were Emeric's.

The crown's plate was mediocre steel round a very good stone. Ward's bracer, which he had worn since before Emeric could remember, had one crystal set in it at the wrist: a battery. A knight drained it into himself to keep his own strength going longer than his body could carry it, and the workings carved on its face gave him a short hard burst of more, speed or force, when he called for it. Every knight in the kingdom had one like it. The signs on them had been carved by hand on the outside of the stone the same way for four hundred years, and they worked, and they were bloated, every one of them, like a letter written by a clerk who was paid by the word.

He had first looked properly at Ward's on the road south, by firelight, and seen the mess on its face. He had not touched it then. It had been Ward's, and it had kept Ward alive for twenty years, and he had not known enough.

He knew enough now.

He did them one a night, in the last week of March, in his room, behind the shutters: Ward's, then Rylan's, then Declan's, then Osric's. Each knight brought his own stone up the keep stair, prised out of its setting by Bram, and left it on the table and went down again, and Emeric sat on the rug with his mother's stone in one hand and the knight's in the other and went in.

In the room, the bracer stone lay open on the bench, and he went into it.

The old workings were all there, cut on the outside of it, a tangle of lines that went round and round on themselves, doing in forty strokes what wanted eight. He did not cut them away. He left them where they were, on the face, so that any armourer in the kingdom who looked at the stone would see what he expected to see. He went in under them, into the stone, where nobody had ever cut anything, and cut it there, small and clean, the way he had cut the watch stones of the wall: the same draw and the same burst, said once, in the fewest lines it could be said in. When he had finished he went back to the face and cut one fine line across the old workings, deep, so that the charge went round them and not through them. Then he came out.

Ward tried his on the morning of the twenty-eighth, in the yard, in front of nobody but Emeric and Bram.

The stone was set in a new bracer now, black Kaldo iron, cut by Bram so that the weight of it lay along the bone of the forearm and not out on the wrist where it dragged. On the inside of the setting, where the stone met the iron, Bram had put a thing of his own: a ring of soft copper with a narrow neck in it, between the stone and the knight's skin. A threshold breaker. If a man ever drew on the stone harder than it could give, too hard and too fast, the way a stone is drawn on when somebody means to crack it, the copper neck would take the current first, and part, and the draw would stop before the stone burst. Bram had made it after the war. He had made it from Emeric's description of a fuse on his other world, with no help after the first sentence, and he had tested it twelve times on spent lamp stones before he would let it near a knight's.

Ward drew on the stone and called the burst.

He crossed the yard. He crossed it the way Emeric had seen him cross the gate yard in the war with the ladders coming up, in three steps, too fast to follow, and stopped at the far wall with his hand on it, and stood there.

He did not come back at once.

"Well?" said Bram.

Ward turned round. He looked at the bracer on his arm. Then he looked at Emeric across the yard.

"It cost me nothing," he said.

"It cost you something."

"It cost me what it costs to walk to the gate." Ward looked down at the stone. "That burst has cost me half my stone every time I have called it since my rite. Twice in a fight and I had nothing left for the third." He was quiet. "I could call that four more times. Five."

"Five," said Emeric. "About."

Ward came back across the yard, at a walk.

"Do the other three," he said.

"I'm doing them."

"Do them first," said Ward. "Before the plate. Before anything." He put his other hand over the bracer, flat, the way a man puts his hand over a purse in a crowd. "And tell nobody what you did to it. Not the garrison. Not the levy. Not Bram's boys." He did not look at Bram. "If a crown armourer ever takes this off my arm and looks at it, I want him to see the same mess he would see on any knight's in the kingdom, and scratch his head at why it works."

"That's why I left the old lines on the face."

Ward looked at him for some time.

"Yes," he said. "I thought it might be."

Bram, who had been standing by the forge door with his arms folded through all of it, unfolded them.

"Twelve," he said. "I tested that breaker twelve times. And he never once needed it." He sounded disappointed.
