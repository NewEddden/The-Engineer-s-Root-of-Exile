Chapter 378 - Shards

The K Endeavor came down on the Isle of Shards at two in the afternoon on the eighth of January, a Thursday, in full daylight, with her running lamps lit as if anyone could have missed her.

For six years she'd come at night. Once a month, dark, no lamps, settling over the cove below the west cliffs with her necks shut and her crew talking in whispers, while the bell tank on the seabed gave up what the pumpjack under the dune had pushed down the buried pipe to it. Six years of that. Emeric had stood on her deck for some of those nights and looked at the black water and thought about what the book would say about it later, and had known what it ought to say. It was theft from the crown. It didn't matter that the crown had been Corbin's or that Kesrath was doing the same at its end. A thing could be the right call and still be theft.

Now the Claim was his and the treaty had made the Isle two crowns' and nobody's, and the K Endeavor came down in the afternoon over the pitch lake with half the island standing on the dunes to watch her, and settled on the flat beyond the new tank farm with a sound like a long breath let out.

Waylon was waiting at the foot of the ramp. He'd gone brown since the autumn, and he had a scarf round his head against the sand the way the Island men wore it, and he looked, Emeric thought, happier than he'd looked in the whole of his life at the palace.

"You're early," said Waylon.

"I'm on time."

"You're early for here. Nobody's on time here." He took Emeric's hand and held it a moment longer than a brother needed to. "They're in the cook tent. They've been in the cook tent since yesterday. Don't go in there with drawings."

"I've brought drawings."

"I know you have. Leave them with Isa." Waylon nodded past him at the ramp, where Isa from the Motion Shop was coming down with a tube of sheets under each arm and two of Dagny's old Wayford hands behind her with the crates. "Go in with your hands empty. Sit where I put you. Eat what they give you. It's goat. It's always goat."

"And then?"

"And then nothing. And then you go and look at the bores with the Duvrenne captain, and you leave me in the tent." Waylon looked at him, and there was something in his face Emeric hadn't seen there before, a kind of stubbornness that didn't come from fear. "They don't want you, Emmy. They want to know who's going to be here in March when you're not. That's me. So let it be me."

The Island families were older than either crown's claim. They'd been on the Isle when Verandel planted his flag on the north end and when Kesrath built its tower on the south, and they'd watched two kingdoms bleed over a desert for longer than anyone had been writing it down. They fished, and herded goats on the scrub behind the dunes, and dived the warm water off the east shore for shell, and sold the soldiers of both sides charms made of the dead grey flakes that littered the west beaches. Nobody had ever asked them anything. When the war came to the Claim in year thirteen they'd gone into the hills behind the pitch lake with their goats and waited for it to stop.

The cook tent was the size of a barn, black goat hair on poles, open on the lee side. Inside it was dim and smelled of smoke and fat. There were perhaps forty people in it, on rugs, round three fires, and they had the air of people who had been arguing for a day and a half and had about another day left in them.

Waylon put him beside an old woman with a face like a walnut and a silver ring in one ear, whom Waylon called Mother Sabbe, and who looked at Emeric's empty hands and seemed, very slightly, approving. Someone gave him a bowl. It was goat.

He ate it. He listened. He understood about one word in three, because the Island speech was Randillian worn smooth by four hundred years of salt and somebody else's grammar, and Waylon, sitting across the fire with his scarf off and his elbows on his knees, understood nearly all of it.

What they wanted was simple enough once he'd listened for an hour. The oil was under their goats. The shard seam was under their fishing water. The two crowns had signed a paper in Kaldo that said who owned the island, and the paper didn't have the families' names on it anywhere. A Kaldo ship had been coming in the dark for years and taking something out of the cove, and the old men had known, and had said nothing, because the last time anybody on the Isle had said something to a crown about a crown's business their grandfathers had been hanged on the Claim's gate.

And now the ships came in daylight and there was going to be a works, and wages, and a school, and a hospital ward, and somebody was going to decide where the new holes went in the ground.

"Who decides," said Mother Sabbe. In Randillian, slowly, for him. "The holes. Who."

Emeric opened his mouth. Waylon looked at him across the fire, and he shut it again.

"You do," said Waylon. "Not alone. You and the captain of Kesrath's works, and me, at one table, and the bore doesn't go in until all three have put a hand on the map." He said it again in the Island speech, worse, and somebody laughed at his grammar, and he laughed too. "If the families say no to a place, the bore goes somewhere else. If they say no to every place, then I sit here and eat goat until we find one."

"You'll get fat," said a man by the second fire.

"I'm already fat. Look at me. Six months of your wives' bread." Waylon patted his stomach, which was not fat, and there was more laughter, and the argument started again, but differently.

Emeric sat through two more hours of it. Nobody asked him anything else. At the end of it Mother Sabbe put her hand on his wrist, quite lightly, the way you'd touch a horse you didn't know, and said something to Waylon in the Island speech, and got up, and went out of the tent with three of her grandsons.

"What did she say?"

"She said you eat like a man who's been told to." Waylon was grinning. "And that I'm to stay."

The Duvrenne captain was a narrow grey man called Oskel who had been a sapper in Kesrath's tower for twenty years and had watched Kaldo's knights take the Claim's wells in the war, and who didn't like Emeric, and didn't pretend to. He walked him along the line of the old bores in the evening light with his hands behind his back.

The Kesrath end had been drilling too. Everybody had known that. They'd been doing it the way anyone would who'd never seen it done: a hole, a pipe pushed down it, and a lot of hoping.

"This one blew in the spring," said Oskel, at a black scar on the sand with a ring of fused glass round it the size of a threshing floor. "Gas. Nobody knew there'd be gas. It came up the hole and found the lamp. Six men."

"There's always gas with oil," said Emeric.

"Now he tells us."

He didn't say anything to that, because there wasn't anything to say. He knelt by the scar and took out the one sheet he'd kept back from Isa and laid it on his knee.

It was a simple enough drawing. A bore in section, with steel pipe down it, and a second pipe inside the first, and cement between them and the rock. Casing, he called it, which was the only word he had. At the head of it a thing like a great iron stopcock, three gates that could be shut on the pipe from outside, hand wheels, and a fourth that shut on nothing at all, an empty hole, and sheared whatever was in it. Below that a branch that took the gas off the oil and sent it down a separate line, not up into the air.

"You case every hole," he said. "All the way. So what's in the rock stays in the rock till you want it. And this goes on the head of every bore before it's a foot deep, so if it kicks you shut it. Here, by hand. Not with a stone." He tapped the wheels. "A stone helps. You can put a stone in it that shuts the gate faster than a man can turn a wheel. But the wheel's there first. If the stones die the wheel still turns."

"And the gas?"

"You keep it. It's worth more than you think. It'll light the works and heat the still and run the pumps." He looked at the scar. "You don't vent it and you don't flare it if you can help it. You pipe it. Kaldo's been burning it under the still for six years because it couldn't ship it in secret. It can now."

Oskel looked at the drawing for a long time.

"How many of ours died learning that?" he said. "That you knew?"

"Some." Emeric folded the sheet. "I could have sent it across under the treaty in the autumn. I didn't. I was busy." It sat badly in his mouth and he let it. "It goes on every head from Monday. Yours and ours. And your men get the same lamps ours get, in the galleries and on the rigs, and the same fans in the stone galleries, and a rescue station with two of the mine frames when Grayrock's done with the first one. And they're paid what Kaldo pays."

"Kaldo pays too much."

"Kaldo pays what it costs to keep a man alive long enough to get good at his job." He stood up. "You can tell your king I said that."

Oskel almost smiled. "I'll tell him you said it standing next to a hole that killed six of his men. He'll like it better."

Alina found him on the morning of the ninth, out past the tank farm, where Isa had set up her table in the lee of a dune and was doing sums with one hand and holding down her sheets with the other.

She was dressed for the Isle, plainly, a linen coat and a hat with a brim, and nobody would have taken her for a princess of Duvrenne except that the two men standing thirty yards off pretending to look at the sea had swords. She'd brought a book with her. She always brought a book.

"Waylon says you're going to make the oil lawful and the shards lawful and pay everyone a great deal of money," she said, "and then leave."

"In April."

"And when the oil's gone?"

He looked at her.

"It goes," said Alina. "Everything that comes out of the ground goes. My father's mines at Kesrath were the richest in the Core when his grandfather was a boy. Now they're holes with water in them, and we sell what's left of the crystal to the Church at the Church's price because nobody else will buy it." She opened her book, though she didn't look at it. "You stopped me drowning my country in your cloth last spring. You remember. You'd have sold it cheaper than my weavers could make it and called it a gift." She turned a page. "I'm not taking my power from a Kaldo wire either. What can the Isle make for itself? Not dig. Make."

It was a good question. It was, he thought, the best question anyone had asked him in a month. He looked at the sky.

It was January, and the sun on the Isle was the sun of a Kaldo May, hard and white and out of a sky with nothing in it.

"Isa," he said. "How many of Ivo's plates did we bring?"

"Twelve. For the rigs' lamps. Why?"

"Silver one."

It took Isa twenty minutes, with the kit she had, and when it was done it was a square of glass the size of a tea tray with a mirror's back on it, and Emeric took it out onto the open sand and turned it in his hands until a spot of white light lay on the side of the nearest crate. Then he held it there.

The crate's paint blistered. After a minute it began to smoke.

Alina watched it with her book shut.

"That's one mirror," he said. "A thousand of them, each one turning by itself on a clock to follow the sun, all aimed at the top of a tower. A tank in the tower, and something in it to hold the heat. Salt. Not table salt. The crust you've got lying on the desert behind the lake, melted, it holds heat for hours after the sun's down. Then steam, a turbine, and a wire. Your wire."

"A thousand mirrors," said Alina.

"About a thousand square yards of them. It wouldn't light Kesrath. It would light the works, and run the pumps and the ward and the school, all day, and most of the night off the salt." He put the mirror down face first on the sand. "And when there's cloud, fire stones in the tank. But the mirrors turn on clocks and gears, not stones. If a dead zone comes, the sun still comes up."

"And what goes wrong?"

He'd been going to say. Isa said it first, without looking up from her sums. "You point a thousand mirrors at the wrong thing and it burns."

"Yes," said Emeric.

"A village. A ship. A man." Alina looked at the smoking crate. "Waylon."

"So they can't point low. Stops on every mount, iron ones. And if the power goes they spring off the tower, all of them, so the tank can't melt." He looked at her. "Tess's rule. Anything that can hurt someone when it fails has to fail safe."

Alina opened her book again, and this time she looked at it. It was full of her small square hand. She found an empty page and wrote something on it and showed him.

The Isle's power to be made on the Isle. The plant owned by the Compact, not by either crown. The mirrors not to point below the horizon, in iron.

"That's a clause," she said. "For Vantic's paper. Put it in her hand when you get home, or I'll put it there myself."

"Whose site?"

"Waylon's," said Alina, as if it were obvious. "He builds things badly and mends them well. You'll want somebody who mends them." She shut the book. "And your Tess's people to build it, the way you're doing the suits. Equipment for a quota. Kaldo keeps it running and we pay in power until we've paid. I've read Ferra's terms for the suits. I'm not stupid."

"Nobody's ever thought you were stupid."

"My father has," said Alina, and walked back across the sand to her swordsmen with her hat in her hand.

The suits had been arranged in the autumn and were the easy part. The Isle's divers had been going down off the east shore for shell since before anybody wrote anything; they could hold their breath for three minutes and they knew the water off the west cliffs better than any Kaldo hand. What they didn't have was air. So the works would have suits from Kaldo, the cave suits, black and close, with Kaldo's bottles and Kaldo's training, and Kaldo would keep them mended and charged, and the works would pay in shard stone off the live seam, a quota by weight, every month.

"Not a discount," Ferra had said in October, at the long table, writing it. "A discount's a present. Presents get resented. A quota's a bargain. You can defend a bargain to your own people when somebody stands up in your parliament and asks why you're giving the Randillians your stone."

Finn came out on the K Endeavor with the first six suits and went straight into the water with the Island divers on the Friday afternoon, off the cove, and came up after an hour laughing, with a cut on his forehead where an Island boy of fifteen had kicked him in the face going past.

"He went down on one breath," said Finn, on the beach, with seawater running out of his collar. "To where I was. On the bottle. Forty feet. And then he looked at me like I was the slow one."

"You were the slow one," said Waylon.

"I was the one with the air." Finn sat down on the sand. "They'll be better than us in a month. Better than me." He didn't sound unhappy about it.

The Compact was argued in the long room on the twelfth, the Monday, with the lamps lit at four because the winter dark came early, and Waylon and Alina down from the Isle on the K Endeavor's return, still with sand in the turn-ups of their trousers.

Vantic had brought it in a leather folder with the House's mark on it. She'd been writing it since November. It was thirty-one pages in a copying clerk's beautiful hand, and she laid it on the table in front of Ferra as if she were laying down a card she knew was going to win, and sat back with her glass.

Ferra read it. It took her forty minutes. Nobody talked while she did it, except Saban, who murmured something to Alina about the weather on the Isle, and Alina, who murmured back that it had been sunny, which it always was.

The thing was simple at its centre and very complicated everywhere else. The works on the Isle were to be one works, not two, held by three owners: Randillia's crown, Duvrenne's crown, and the Island families, by a share each that couldn't be sold except to one of the other two. House Ducatis to hold the accounts and the money between them, the escrow, so that every mark the oil and the stones earned went into the House first, and out of it again in thirds on a published day, and no crown's treasury ever touched the money in passing. Neither king could skim it. Neither king could borrow against it. And all of it written, clause by clause, so that the Empire couldn't buy a share through a man in a hat.

"That's the part I like best," said Vantic, when Ferra turned the last page. "The man in a hat."

"Page nineteen," said Ferra.

"Page nineteen." Vantic turned her glass a quarter on the table. "A share can't go to anyone but the other two holders. Not by sale, not by gift, not by marriage, not as security for a loan, not by any person acting on behalf of any person. And any holder who lets it be done loses the share to the other two." She smiled slightly. "They funded both sides of your war, Majesty. Corbin's men and Kesrath's boats. They'll try to fund the peace the same way. The usual way's a merchant from Fairhythe nobody's heard of, who buys a tenth of somebody's share for a great deal of money and is very grateful to someone in the Core afterwards."

"Page twenty-three," said Ferra.

Vantic's smile changed. It wasn't less of a smile.

"Page twenty-three is the escrow," said Ferra. "The House holds the money between the day it's earned and the published day. Thirty days. Thirty days of the Isle's whole earning in your vault." She put her finger on the clause. "What does the House do with it for thirty days?"

"Keeps it."

"Keeps it where? In a box?"

"In the House's book."

"Lent out," said Ferra. "For thirty days. At the House's rates. And the House keeps what it earns on it, because that's what a bank's for, and you've written it so that the holders don't get a copper of the interest and can't ask." She looked up. "That's not an escrow. That's a free loan of the whole Isle to House Ducatis twelve times a year."

Saban made a small noise into his hand.

"It's a fee," said Vantic. "For holding it."

"Then write it as a fee. Write down what it is and I'll tell you whether it's too much." Ferra took her pen. "Or write that the House holds the money in its own vault, not in its book, untouched, and charges a fee, published. One or the other. Not both."

"The first one makes the House rich," said Vantic. "The second one makes the House a strongbox."

"The second one makes the House honest."

"Honesty's very expensive, Ferra. Somebody's got to pay for the guards on the strongbox."

"Then put the guards in the fee."

They went on like that for an hour. Emeric watched them. He'd seen Ferra argue with a lot of people in seventeen years, and he'd seen her win almost all of it, and most of the time she won the way a tide wins, without any expression at all. This wasn't that. She was leaning forward. Vantic was leaning forward. There was a colour in both their faces he didn't often see in either. At one point Vantic said something about the third clause of the second schedule and Ferra laughed, out loud, one short bark, and then said no to it anyway.

They were enjoying themselves. He hadn't known it could be like this for either of them.

"Fee," said Vantic at last. "Published. Three parts in a thousand. The money in the vault, untouched, with the Assembly's clerk and the Island families' clerk on the vault's key and mine." She wrote it in the margin, herself, in her own hand, which was hard and quick and not at all like the copying clerk's. "There. I've written myself out of a fortune and charged you for the pen."

"Three in a thousand's too much."

"Three in a thousand's what it costs."

"Two."

"Two and a half," said Vantic, "and I'll have Alina's mirrors in it for nothing, because they're interesting and I want to see them."

Alina, at the far end, took a folded paper out of her book and passed it up the table hand to hand until it reached Vantic. Vantic read it.

"The mirrors not to point below the horizon," she said. "In iron." She looked down the table at Alina with something like delight. "Your Highness. Who taught you to write a clause?"

"I read yours," said Alina.

"Clause forty," said Vantic, and wrote it in.

It was Ferra who turned it, in the end, and Emeric didn't see her do it until it was done.

"While you've got the pen," she said. "The House holds the Isle's money and publishes what it holds, monthly, and the Assembly's clerk signs it. And the House holds the crown's money, the same way, since the crown's book went to you. And Kaldo's notes, the House's paper the counting house takes for coin." She was looking at the Compact, not at Vantic. "Six years ago, in this room. Saban's three guards. A bank the king doesn't own. An open audit. And a written limit."

"I remember."

"We've got two of them. The bank and the audit. Nobody's ever written the limit." Ferra turned the folder round on the table so the margin faced her. "How much paper the House can put out. Against what. Who says no when someone wants more. It's on the charter sheet with the king's name blank, and it's been blank for six years, and I sail in April."

Vantic was quiet.

"You've just written a clause that says the Empire can't buy a share of the Isle through a man in a hat," said Ferra. "Write me one that says the next king can't print himself a war. Or the next House."

"The next House," said Vantic.

"You won't live for ever. Neither will I."

Vantic sat back. She looked, for a moment, very tired, and older than she was, and then not.

"That's not a clause," she said. "That's an article. The kind you write in a constitution and then spend forty years fighting over."

"Then we'd better start. I've got three months."

"You've got three months to start it. You'll not finish it. And there'll be no paper under it till you're back, and the king's back, because I'm not launching a currency with the auditor a thousand miles out and the empire closing its books on us." Vantic picked up her glass and found it empty and put it down. "Thursday. My office. Bring the charter sheet. Bring Bram's grandmother's tea caddy, if he's still got it."

"Bram's grandmother's tea caddy," said Ferra, "is the reason we're doing this."

"I know it is," said Vantic. "Bring it anyway."

And then, because she'd said the word, she said the rest.

"The empire's closing its books on us," she said again, to the table this time, and the room changed. "You'll have heard it in pieces. I'll give it you whole." She opened the folder at the back, where a letter lay loose, on thin foreign paper, folded small. "In the autumn the Core stopped one credit. A line in Duvrenne's name nobody drew on. They spelled Her Highness's name wrong on the notice."

"Twice," said Alina.

"Twice. That was a door shut. This is all the doors." Vantic unfolded the letter. "My correspondent at Ostrel writes on the first of the month. On the first of January the six imperial banking houses closed their books to Randillia and to Duvrenne. All of them. No new credit to either crown, or to any house in either, or to any merchant trading under either flag. What's lent stands. Nothing more is lent. The Core's mints won't change our coin. Their factors won't take our paper." She folded it again. "Not by decree. The emperor's signed nothing. There's no paper anybody could point to. Six banks all happened to decide the same thing on the same day."

Nobody said anything.

"Because of the Isle," said Waylon at last.

"Because of the Isle. Because he can't take it. An emperor can't take a subject's land or every king in the Core starts counting his own. So he does the other thing. He starves you." Vantic looked at Emeric. "And because you sent his envoy home with his gold, Majesty. Politely. They remember polite."

"And the House?"

"The House is seated in Kaldo. It's the crown's bank. It didn't follow." Her voice didn't change. "That's a risk for the House. My uncle Tullio would tell you so, if he were speaking to me, which he isn't. Every house in the Core that has paper with ours now has to decide whether it's worth keeping." She turned her glass again, though there was nothing in it. "Some of them have decided it is."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning that in the same three weeks I've had nine letters," said Vantic. "Quiet ones. From merchants in the Core's own cities, two in Ostrel itself, and from the free crowns, four from Solreth and one from Velmoor. They've seen Kaldo's glass. They've seen the soap, the lamps, the bulbs. A bicycle went to Solreth in a crate in October and the whole of the Solreth exchange has apparently been out to look at it." She almost smiled. "They want to buy. Through the House, not through the Core's factors. And they'll pay above the price. A good deal above."

"To break the empire's block," said Ferra.

"To get round it. They'd say it's not the same thing. It's a little bit the same thing."

"What do they want?"

"Everything." Vantic slid the nine letters across the table to her. "You'll want to argue the list."

"I'll want to write the list," said Ferra, and took them. "Soap. Glass. Lamps and bulbs. Paper and the printed books. Cloth, Alina, the cloth that's allowed. Bicycles. Gramophones. The medicines on the open tier." She was writing as she said it. "No stones. No radio. Nothing that flies. No weapons, no guns, no rounds, no plate. Nothing magitech. Nothing on the shut tier, at any premium, to anybody."

"Saban," said Emeric.

Saban had already taken the letters from Ferra's elbow, and was reading the names at the bottom of each, one by one, very slowly.

"A man who'll break an emperor's block for a bicycle," he said, without looking up, "will break something else for the Church, for less." He laid them out on the table in a row. "These four I've heard of. These two I'll find out about. This one's dead, so somebody else is writing his letters." He touched the ninth. "And this one's the Church."

"You can tell?"

"I can tell. The bishop at Velmoor has a cousin in the cloth trade with a very bad hand." Saban squared the letters into a stack and gave them back to Vantic. "Sell to the four. Make the two wait. Write to the dead man very politely and see who answers. And sell the cousin a bicycle."

"Sell him one?"

"One. At the premium. With a note in the crate saying it's the first of many." Saban put his hat on the table. "He'll take it apart. They all will. Let them. There's nothing in a bicycle we mind them having, and I'd like to know which workshop in the Core he sends it to."

Vantic was writing. Ferra was writing. Alina had her book open and was writing too, though nobody had asked her to.

Emeric looked down the long table at the lamps and the papers and the people round them, his brother with sand in his turn-ups and a princess with a book and two women arguing about an escrow and enjoying it and an old spy reading a bishop's cousin's handwriting, and realised that he hadn't said anything for twenty minutes. Nobody had needed him to.

It was the first time, he thought later, that he noticed it. It wasn't the last.

"It's the first sign," said Saban, when the others had gone and it was only the two of them in the long room with the lamps going down. "You know that."

"Of what?"

"You stood on that relay in October and looked at all of it at once. The Core, and the free crowns, and the coast going off past the end of the map. And you thought, they're all down there, and none of them knows who we are." Saban put his hat on. "They know now. That's what this is. The banks closing and the bicycles wanted. It's the same thing from two sides." He went to the door. "They're watching. They're not sure what they're watching yet. I'd sooner they stayed not sure until you're home."

"So would I."

"Then don't make anything else they'll want for a bit," said Saban, and went out.
