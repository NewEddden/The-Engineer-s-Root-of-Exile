Chapter 150 - Public Works

The first snow came in the night and was gone by noon, and Imani came up to the keep in the slush with her register under her arm and asked for a quarter of an hour.

She did not sit. She opened the register on his table at a page near the back and turned it round so that he could read it, and stood with her hands folded while he did.

He read the dose. He read the three lines under it. He read It does not give anything back twice, and then he read the page before it, the one in her plainest hand about the ward's water and whose fault it was, and he looked up at her, and she looked back at him and did not help him.

"Armand told me about the water," he said.

"I thought he would."

"He didn't tell me you'd written that."

"No," said Imani. "I didn't expect him to." She turned the register back toward herself a little, not closing it. "I've been giving it to the six on purpose since the morning I found it. Morning and night. Knowing, this time, and telling them, and asking. Five said yes. Hild said yes, which I didn't expect, when I told her it was a cup and not a stone. Senna drinks it when I give it to her." She paused. "Their columns haven't moved. Not down. Not up. And I've been giving it to myself, and to the staff who asked for it after I told them, and Armand has read us all with his glass every week, and I've written down what he read."

"And?"

"And we've moved. A little. All of us. The same way the men on the etching floor have moved, he says, but faster, and nobody's hurt, and nobody's marked." She put her finger on the dose. "That's what I came to show you. It's safe, as far as I can see. It's the gentlest thing I've ever seen work. I wanted you to see it written down before anybody did anything with it."

Emeric looked at the page for a long time.

He thought about Doran's book. He had sat with it once for four hours on a night ten years ago and put every line of twenty-six years of it on a map, and seen the wells come up out of the dead like a shape out of fog. He thought about what the book would have looked like if every name in it had drunk from this for twenty years. He thought about Wat, thirty-one, in Doran's hand, and about Armand at the window with his hands out, and about his mother, who had not been old.

"Not a cup," he said.

Imani waited.

"If it's a cup, it's a thing somebody hands you," he said. "It's a thing you come to the ward for. It's a thing people talk about, and the ones who talk about it wrong will say it's a potion, and the ones who talk about it right will say it's charge, and within a year there'll be a man in Milford selling water out of a barrel that he swears stood next to a stone. And the ones who need it most won't come, because they never come. They didn't come for the wells either. The wells came to them." He put his hand flat on the table beside the register. "It should be in the water. The public water. The wells. The thing everybody drinks anyway, every day, whether they've heard of you or not. A little. Every day. In a thing they do anyway." He looked at her. "You said it first."

"I said it about a ward," said Imani.

"You said it about bodies."

She looked at him. He saw her think it all the way through, the way she thought through a wound before she touched it. He saw her come to the end of it.

"Yes," she said.

It was fast. It was much faster than Imani said yes to anything. He noticed it, and he saw her notice him notice it, and neither of them said so.

The towers were the part he could draw.

He drew them at the long table that night with the six round him and Vantic gone home, on the good paper, the way he drew anything, starting with what had to work if everything else failed.

"A tower over each well," he said. "The market well first. Then the island's other four, and the one by the Western Gate. Timber on a stone footing, because it's winter and mortar won't cure, and I don't want to wait for spring. An iron tank at the top, lined, covered, with a roof on it so nothing gets in. Here." He drew it. "The water goes up from the well into the tank. Everything from the tank comes down one pipe to the taps at the bottom, four taps round the base of each tower, so nobody has to wind a bucket and nobody has to lean over a well-head in the ice. It's cleaner. It's quicker. Every house on the island gets a tap within two streets of its door. That's all it is, to anybody who asks, and all of it's true."

"And what puts the water in the tank?" said Bram.

"Wind." He drew the vanes. "A wind pump on the top. A wheel of vanes, a crank, a rod down the middle of the tower to a pump at the well. When the wind blows, it fills. When it doesn't, there's a hand pump at the base, here, the same rod, a handle a boy can work. If there's no wind and no boy, the well's still there, and anybody can put a bucket down it the way they always have." He looked round the table. "Nothing in the tower needs a stone to work. Nothing. If every stone in Kaldo went dead tomorrow, the water still comes out of the taps."

"And the stone," said Ferra.

"Here." He drew a box inside the tank, low down, against the side. "An iron cage. The stone goes in the cage, in the water. A small fire stone beside it, in the same cage, to keep the tank from freezing in the winter, which is a thing it needs anyway, and to warm the water round the other stone enough for it to take the charge. Imani found it takes it better warm." He drew a hatch in the tank's side, over the cage. "A hatch. Locked. The cage can only be reached through it, and the hatch opens with a key, and there are two keys."

"Whose?" said Ferra.

"Yours," he said, "and Imani's."

She did not say anything to that for a moment. He saw her not expect it.

"Not yours," she said.

"No. I'll never need to open it. Imani sets the dose and changes the stone. You hold the other key because you're the one who'd need to be able to take it out."

Ward, who had not said anything, said, "It's a sanitation works."

"It's a sanitation works," Emeric said. "That's what it is on the paper. That's what it is in the town. The same as the wells were. Clean water, on a tap, that doesn't freeze." He put the pen down. "It's also the other thing. Everybody at this table knows it's the other thing. Nobody outside this table needs to."

Nobody argued. He watched them not argue. Bram was looking at the wind pump and already thinking about the crank. Saban was looking at the cost. Armand was looking at the cage, and his hands were folded on the head of his stick. Ward was looking at Emeric.

Imani was looking at the dose in her register, which lay open beside the drawing, and she did not look up.

Ferra's conditions came the next morning, in writing, on a single sheet, ruled, in her own hand. He had expected them. He had not expected how few.

One. The dose in each tower is no stronger, by the gallon drunk, than the Secretary of Health's figure for the ward. The Secretary of Health sets it and signs it. It is not raised by anybody else, for any reason.

Two. The market tower goes first and alone, and runs a full season before any other tower gets a stone. The Secretary of Health reads the water and whoever consents to be read, every week, and the Secretary of the Arcane reads them with her. If anything in the reading goes the wrong way, the stone comes out that day.

Three. Every stone that goes into a cage is entered in the fund's book, with the day, the tower, the weight of the stone, and the name of the one who put it in. Every stone that comes out, the same.

Four. The towers are built so that the stone can come out of every one of them in an hour, and the town would never know it had been in.

That was all. There was no fifth.

He read it twice, and signed it, and sent it back, and she came up an hour later and stood at his table with it in her hand.

"You signed it without arguing," she said.

"They're right."

"They're not the whole of it." She did not sit. "I wrote down what I could write down. The rate, the order, the book, and the way out. That's what a form can do. It can't do the rest."

"What's the rest?"

Ferra looked at him for a long moment. Then she said it, the thing he had known she would say since the night Imani came up the stair with her register, the thing she had said at the long table with a stone in her hand on the first night the six sat down together.

"Is it a leash?"

He did not answer at once.

"You gave us stones," Ferra said. "At the table. You told us what they were, and what the bond was, and you told us you didn't know whether it was a leash or a gift, and you put it in our hands and let each of us choose. And I chose. And every one of us chose." She put the sheet down on the table between them. "Nobody in that town is going to choose. Every man, woman and child on the island is going to drink from those taps, because they're clean and they're close and they don't freeze, and they're going to change, a little, every day, for the rest of their lives, and not one of them is going to know. They'll live longer, maybe. They'll hold more. Their children will hold more. Armand will read them every autumn, and they'll have moved, all of them, and they'll never be told why." She did not raise her voice. "I'm not asking whether it's good for them. I think it probably is. I'm asking what it is. Whether you've given four thousand people a gift, or put a leash on four thousand people, and whether you know which."

"I don't know which," Emeric said.

"No," said Ferra. "I didn't think you did. I wanted it said."

He said it again, at the table, that night, because she asked him to. He said it the way she had said it, and then he looked round the table and asked whether anybody wanted to answer it.

Armand said that it was the same total at a rate the body could take, and that the rate was the only thing that had ever made it a crime, and that he did not know whether that made it a gift. Saban said that he had sold things to people for twenty years without telling them what was in them, and that it had never once been as good for them as this, and that he was not proud of the comparison. Ward said nothing. Bram said, "It's water," and then, after a moment, "I know it's not."

Imani said nothing at all.

She sat with her register closed under her hands. She did not look at Ferra and she did not look at Emeric. Ferra waited, and the table waited with her, and Imani did not say anything, and after a while Ferra wrote in her book, and Emeric saw from where he sat that what she wrote was short.

He thought he knew why Imani would not answer. She had given it to the ferryman and the Tarrow girl and her own staff for seven weeks without asking, and written that it was her fault, and then she had watched it work, and had come up the stair with it written down in her plainest hand. She had already answered the question once, for a ward, by accident. She did not want to answer it again, for a town, on purpose, in front of the woman who had first asked it. And she did not want to take her yes back.

He did not know that. He was guessing. He did not ask her.

The bands came out of the forge in the second week of winter.

He did the six stones first, one at a time, on six successive mornings, with each of the six standing at the end of the bench watching their own. None of them needed migrating. There was nothing in them that was theirs alone. They had all gone down into the delve through him, through his door, since the first night at the long table, and whatever they had built there was built in his room and in the shared room, not in the stones. So the stones went into the cup as they were, and the forge emptied and warmed and lifted them, and he fed in the shape, and each one came up out of its softening with the lattice climbing back over every new face, a bead a little smaller than a knuckle, and he handed it to its owner warm.

Bram took his and turned it in his fingers under the lamp for a long time, looking at the lattice on the curve. "Grows," he said. "Like frost."

"Like frost."

"I'd have bet you a Gold it was cut." He put it in his apron. "I'd have lost."

His own went last, and took two days, because his own was the only one with anything in it. He carried what was in it through his door and into the root, which lay in the strongbox as a bead now with the net folded away in the dark of it, and set it down there beside the net in a room of its own, and shut it, and felt very strange doing it, the way a man feels walking out of his own house and leaving the door open behind him. Then the forge, and the softening, and the lattice going and coming back. Then the long careful carrying of it all out of the root again and home. At the end of it he sat on the stool at the end of the bench with the bead in his palm for a quarter of an hour, and went down, and came up, and went down again, before he would believe that he was all there.

Bram set the six beads into the six bands. He had made the bands through the autumn from Emeric's drawings: gold barrels for the gravity beads, dark metal caps, a short iron bar across the band to keep the grey stone apart from the others, the charged beads faceted blue between the barrels. They looked like jewellery, which was the point. Nobody in the kingdom would look twice at a counsellor of a barony wearing a bracelet. Every one of them would look twice at a counsellor carrying a grey stone in his fist.

The six put them on at the long table, on a supper night, after Vantic had gone. Nobody said anything much. Ward turned his wrist over and looked at it and put it back on the table. Saban said it was the most expensive thing he had ever worn and he was going to find it very hard not to mention it to people. Imani put hers on and then took it off and looked at the inside of it, at the caps and the separator bar, and put it back on.

Ferra put hers on last. She fastened it without looking at it, and then she did look at it, for a moment, on her wrist beside her pen hand, and he saw her think about the pen he had given her, and the stone he had given her, and the leash. She did not say anything.

Then Bram came to the keep in the morning with his tools and took the breast door off Emeric's suit, the little iron door over the seat where the grey stone had sat on the flight to Emberfell and over Wayford, and reworked the seat inside it to take a bead instead of a flat stone, and put the door back on and tried it twenty times. And he took the silver star off Emeric's coat, the one the stone went into when the suit was off, and reworked its catch the same way, so that the bead sat in the star's heart and could not come out unless it was meant to.

"There," Bram said, and gave it back. "Now you can stop taking the whole star off to put it in the suit. You've been doing that for a year. I watched you."

"You could have said."

"You'd have told me it wasn't the stone's shape that mattered," Bram said, and packed up his tools.

The market tower went up in nine days.

It went up in the snow, which Emeric had been told it would not, and in the frost, which he had been told it could not. Bram's men built the frame on the foundry bank in sections and brought it across the bridge on carts in the dark and raised it over the market well in the morning with blocks and a crowd watching, a square timber tower three times the height of a man with a stone footing round the well-head and an iron tank at the top under a pitched roof. The vanes went up on the fourth day, and turned, and the rod went down through the middle to the pump, and on the fifth day the tank began to fill.

The town came to look at it. The town looked at everything now. Children stood at the base in the snow with their heads back and watched the vanes. Two of the old men from the ferry stood at the edge of the square every afternoon with their hands in their sleeves and told each other it would freeze, and on the sixth night it went down to a hard frost and the water in the well-head buckets froze solid and the tank did not, and the next afternoon the old men stood in the same place and told each other that it was the height of the thing, that water high up didn't freeze, everybody knew that.

The taps went on on the eighth day. Four brass taps round the base, one on each side, at the height of a woman's waist, with a stone trough under each to catch what spilled. Imani came to the square in the morning with a jug, and drew from each tap in turn, and took the jugs back to the ward, and she and Armand read them, and she came back in the afternoon and said the water was clean.

On the ninth morning, before light, Imani climbed the ladder inside the tower with a lantern and a small leather bag, and Ferra climbed it behind her.

Emeric was not there. He had said he would not be, and he was not. He was on the wall above the market, in the dark, with Ward, where he could see the tower's roof against the snow and nothing else. He saw the lantern light go up the inside of the frame, rung by rung. He saw it stop at the top. He could not see the hatch, or the keys, or the cage, or the stone going in, or the fire stone beside it, or Imani's hand. He could not see Ferra writing it down, though he knew she was, on her knee, by the light of the lantern, with the day and the tower and the weight of the stone and the name.

He could feel it. He had not meant to reach for it, and he did not reach far. But the sense went out across the snow of the square of its own accord, the way it went out now, and found the tower, and found the tank at the top of it, and the dark water in the tank, and in the water, low down against the iron side, a small bright place, very faint, humming, where a stone of the common cut, full, sat in a cage next to a warm one.

He pulled the sense back. He did not want to feel it any more than that.

"Done?" said Ward.

"Done."

Ward looked at the tower for a while in the dark. "Four thousand people," he said.

"Four thousand, two hundred and some."

"And they'll never know."

"No."

Ward was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, in a different voice, "I felt it in my teeth, in that cellar. You knew I'd go to Imani."

"I thought you might."

"She wrote it down. My words. She didn't write what she thought." Ward did not look at him. "I want you to know I'd have told you. If you'd asked."

"I didn't ask."

"No," said Ward. "You didn't." He pushed himself off the parapet. "Come in. It's cold."

The square filled up in the morning the way it always did, with the market setting out its trestles in the snow and the carts coming over the bridge and the smoke going up straight from every chimney on the island into a white sky.

A woman came to the south tap with two buckets and filled them, and stood a moment looking up at the vanes, and went away. A man from the tannery filled a jug. Two of the ferry old men came and looked at the taps, and one of them turned one on and off again, twice, and shook his head at it, and the other one said something, and they went off to stand at the edge of the square.

A child came across the square alone. A small boy of five or six, in a coat too big for him, with his hands in the sleeves, who had been sent for water with a tin cup, or had not been sent and had come anyway to see the thing everybody was looking at. He went to the east tap and stood in front of it and looked at it for a while, the brass handle and the spout and the stone trough underneath with a skin of ice on it. Then he reached up with both hands and turned the handle, and the water came, and he jumped back, and then came forward again.

He held the tin cup under the spout until it ran over his fingers, and turned the handle off, the wrong way first and then the right way. He stood by the trough in the snow and drank the whole cup, with his eyes shut, in long swallows, because it was cold and clean and his, and then he wiped his mouth on his sleeve and filled it again.

Nobody was watching him. The woman with the buckets had gone. The old men were looking at the vanes. Emeric was in the keep, at his table, writing a letter to Keller about something else. Imani was in the end room, reading Senna's column. Ferra had the fund's book open at a new line.

The boy drank the second cup too, slower, and then he put the empty cup in his coat and went away across the square toward the bridge, kicking the snow, and the tap dripped once behind him into the trough, and was still.
