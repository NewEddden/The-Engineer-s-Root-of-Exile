Chapter 150 - Iron on Iron

The new engine used its steam twice, and Bram did not trust it for a fortnight.

It stood in its own house beside the foundry, built that summer for it, with its own boiler: a water-tube boiler like the one in Somebody Came, only bigger, and with a steam drum on top of it that had not been riveted up out of Grayrock iron plate the way every other drum in Kaldo had been. It had been hammered, in one piece, from the converter's first good steel, round and seamless, on the big hammer over four days, with Bram at the tongs and Tess calling the heats. It would take three times the pressure of the drum that had opened at the works and killed Haddon. They had tested it with water, twice, at half again what it would ever see, with Vantic's lead on the valve, before there was a fire under it.

The engine on the boiler had two cylinders. A small one and a big one, side by side. The steam came off the drum hot and hard and went into the small cylinder first, and pushed the piston, and came out the other side still hot and still pushing, only not as hard; and instead of going away to the condenser, it went into the big cylinder, and pushed that piston too. Twice. The same steam. On Earth they had called it a compound engine, and it had taken them half a century to think of it, and when they had thought of it it had very nearly halved the coal it took to turn a wheel.

Bram had not believed that either. He believed it at the end of the fortnight, when Ferra brought him the coal book with the two engines' columns side by side, the old engine's and the new, and put her finger on the bottom of each. He looked at the two numbers for some while.

"Twice," he said.

"Near enough," said Ferra.

"The same steam."

"I don't know about the steam," said Ferra. "I know about the coal."



The engine turned the rolls.

That was what it was for. Two great cylinders of cast iron, one above the other in a frame of iron as tall as a man, turning toward each other, close, with a gap between them no wider than a finger. They had been cast in the foundry in June and turned true on the lathe, the lathe with the true screw that the forge's master had cut, and the turning had taken Bram and two of his journeymen three weeks, and every day of it Bram had laid the forge's gauge blocks against them and the forge's flat against their faces until the rolls were as round and as true along their length as the forge's own inch. And under the rolls, in their bearings at either end, where for every other shaft in Kaldo there had always been a sleeve of brass and a pot of tallow, there were balls.

Steel balls. Perfectly round, perfectly alike, a ring of them in each bearing between an inner race and an outer, in a little cage of brass that kept them apart.

Bram had been given one, a year and a half ago, in a room inside a stone, cut in half on a bench of glass so that he could see how it went. He had sat down on the floor of the room and taken it to pieces. Then he had come out and spent a year and a half making the races and the cages, out here, by hand, while the balls waited, because nobody could make the balls. You needed a steel that was hard enough. You needed a way to make a thousand of them round to a hair and exactly alike.

The steel had come out of the pear in June. The way had come out of the forge in July: one night, a run of master balls in five sizes, each ball a hair from perfect, and two plates, flat and heavy, with grooves cut in their faces in rings, that the forge had made as true as it made everything. That was all the forge did. The rest was Bram's shop. A boy put a handful of rough balls, roughly forged from the pear's steel, between the two grooved plates, with a paste of grit and oil; the top plate turned on the bottom one, slowly, on a belt off the engine, and the balls ran round and round in the grooves between them for a day, and two days, and three, grinding against the plates and each other, until every ball in the grooves was as round as every other and as round as the master that Bram held up beside it in his calliper. The master did not touch them. It only told Bram when they were done.

The first bearings went into the lathe that turned the rolls. Then into the rolls themselves.

When the engine turned them for the first time, on the third of July, with no load in them, the rolls ran so smoothly and so quietly in their new bearings that the men watching from the door could hear, over the beat of the engine next door, a sparrow in the rafters. Bram stood beside the frame with his hand flat on the top of it, feeling it, the way he had felt every machine he had ever trusted. Then he took his hand away and told the boys to bring the first bar.

It came out of the furnace yellow-hot on a pair of tongs, a short thick bar of the pear's steel, and they fed it in between the rolls, and the rolls took it, with a long groaning crunch, and drew it through, and it came out on the other side longer and thinner and flatter, and the boy on the far side caught it in his tongs and turned it and fed it back. And again. And again, with the gap between the rolls closed a little each time on the screw at the top. And at the fourth pass what came out on the far side was not a bar any more. It was a plate. A sheet of steel the length of a man and the thickness of a finger, glowing dull red, flat and even from end to end, lying on the iron floor plates of the mill hissing at the water the boys threw on it.

They rolled plate all the next week. Then Bram changed the rolls for the second pair, the ones with the grooves turned in them, each groove a little different from the last, so that a bar fed through them in order came out of the last groove in the shape of a rail: a flat broad foot, a narrow neck, a rounded head. The first rail came out of the mill on the fourteenth, and was nine yards long, and nine boys carried it out into the yard on their shoulders like a coffin, slowly, in step, and laid it on trestles, and the whole works came out to look at it.

It was a plain thing. A long bar of grey steel, of a strange shape, lying on trestles in the sun. Odo Pike walked the length of it with his stick, slowly, and back, and then he put his hand flat on the head of it and kept it there.

"Iron on iron," he said, to nobody. "Like water on glass."



Odo laid them at Grayrock from the shaft head to the river landing, a mile and three quarters, all of it downhill.

That was the beauty of it, he said. That was what he had known under that hill with his oak rails forty years ago. The ore came out of the mine at the top of the hill and wanted to go to the river at the bottom of the hill, and the hill wanted it to go there too. You did not need to pull a full wagon down a hill. You needed to stop it. So on Odo's line the loaded wagons ran down to the landing on their own, in a string, with a man riding the last one on a brake; and at the landing the horse that would pull them back up empty got down out of a little wagon of its own at the end of the string that it had ridden down in, like a passenger, with its nose in a bag of oats, and was hitched up to the empties and walked them home.

A dandy cart. That was its name. Emeric had told Odo the name, and why, and Odo had laughed so hard he had to sit down on a sleeper.

The rails went down on oak sleepers bedded in broken stone, the sleepers a yard apart, and Odo spiked the rails to them himself, every spike, with his stick hooked over his arm and his bad back bent over the hammer and his crew of six watching him do it wrong and not daring to say so. The first half mile was straight. Then the line turned, round the shoulder of the hill, on a long curve, to come down to the landing on the gentle side.

His first wagon came off the rail on the curve.

It was a good wagon. It had wheels the way his tubs had had them under the hill forty years ago: a lip round the outside edge of each wheel, so that the lip ran down the outside of each rail and held the wagon on. On the straight it ran beautifully. On the curve it climbed. The outside wheels' lips rode up on the outside rail as the wagon tried to go straight and the rail went round, and the wheel climbed it, and was over, and the wagon went off into the broken stone on its side with its load of ore spilling out across the slope and the brakeman jumping clear.

Emeric saw it go. He was standing at the shoulder of the hill with Ward and Bram and he saw the outside wheel start to climb, and he knew exactly what was happening and why, and he knew what would fix it, and he put his hands in his pockets.

Odo re-railed it, and put new lips on it a little deeper, and sent it down again on the second day.

It came off again in the same place.

This time it came off toward the men. The wagon jumped the outside rail on the curve and slewed, and its back end came round across the line, and there was a boy from the crew on the bank below the curve with a shovel who did not look up in time. Emeric was moving before he knew he had decided to. He was thirty feet up the slope. He was beside the boy in four strides, which were longer strides than a man has, and quicker, and had his hand in the back of the boy's collar and the boy off his feet and out of the way, and the back corner of the wagon went past through the place where the boy had been and buried itself in the bank in a spray of stones.

He put the boy down.

Nobody said anything about the four strides. The crew was looking at the wagon. Ward was looking at him, from the shoulder of the hill, without expression; Ward had seen the strides, and Ward knew what the warm low push into the legs felt like from the outside, because he had been doing it himself all his life. Ward looked at him for exactly as long as it took to see that nobody else had seen, and then looked at the wagon too.

Emeric's legs were shaking a little. Low draw. It had been very low. He stood beside the boy and breathed and put his hands back in his pockets.

Odo found it that night.

He found it by sitting at the curve with a lantern until long after dark, on a sleeper, with the wrecked wagon on its side in the stone below him and a wheel off it in his lap, turning it in his hands. Emeric watched him from the shaft head for a while, and then went to bed, and in the morning Odo was at the forge at Grayrock beating the lips off all four wheels.

He put them back on the inside.

The lip on the inside of each wheel, running down the inside face of each rail, so that the two lips sat between the two rails, close, with a finger's width of play. And then he took the spikes out of the whole of the curve, and moved the inside rail in, a little, and a little more, testing it with a wagon pushed by hand, and a little more, until the wagon went round the curve with its inside lips just kissing the inside of the rails and not binding, and not climbing, and not coming off. And he measured the distance between the rails where it was right, with a stick, and cut a notch in the stick, and went back to the shaft head and moved every rail on the line to the notch.

He brought the stick to Emeric at noon.

"That's the width," said Odo. "Between the rails. Every rail on every line. That and no other." He held it out. "Iron on iron. Inside, not out. And the same width every yard of the way, or it all comes off."

A flange, Emeric told him. That was the lip's name. And the width between the rails was the gauge. On Earth they had found both of them exactly as Odo had, by building it and watching it come off, and some of them had found it a good deal more slowly.

Odo looked at the stick in his hand with the notch cut in it.

"You knew," he said.

"I knew."

"And let me put a wagon in the stones twice."

"Yes."

Odo thought about it. He was an old man with a bad back who had drawn a railway on a tavern wall forty years ago and watched them paint it over.

"Good," said Odo Pike. "I'd not have believed you." And he took the stick back to the shaft head and nailed it to the wall of the winding house, over the door, where it hung from then on, and every rail anybody ever laid in the four baronies was laid to it.



The rail joints were welded.

Odo had wanted fishplates, iron bars bolted across the joint on either side, which was how Emeric had drawn them, and there were fishplates; but Tess had come up the river on the steamboat with a cart of copper cable and two of the powerhouse's carbon holders and a small dynamo off the works line, and set it up on a flat wagon, and joined the ends of the first two rails in the yard at Grayrock with her arc in front of Odo while Odo watched with his arms folded. Then she had told him to try and break it. He had tried, with the biggest hammer at Grayrock. Then he had let her do the rest of the line.

On the fourth day of it a man on the rail gang went blind.

He was one of Odo's crew, a carter's son, who had been told not to look at the arc and had looked anyway, the way a man looks at a thing he has been told not to look at, for the length of a whole joint, because it was beautiful. That night his eyes swelled shut and burned like sand, and he lay on his face in the bunkhouse and screamed. Tess sent for Imani down the line and up the river. Imani came the next evening on Somebody Came with her basket, and by then the boy could see light again, a little, through the swelling. He saw it fully in a week. He did not go back to the rail gang.

Imani's rule went up in Grayrock's winding house the day she arrived, in her own hand, under Odo's stick: the same words as on the powerhouse door, and a line under them. Nobody looks at the arc without the glass. Nobody. And a line under that, in a different ink, added after she had seen the boy: I am not going to say this again.

The glass was Ivo's, the darkest he made, the square he had made for Tess in the spring. But a square of glass in a man's hand was a square he put down the moment he needed both hands, and a welder always needed both hands. So Abloh made a hood of thick leather, waxed, that went over the head and the neck and came down over the shoulders, and in the front of it, where the eyes were, Ivo's dark glass in a brass frame that hinged up and down. Up to see the work. Down to strike the arc. A welder's mask. Emeric named it for them, and Abloh said that was a very dull name for a very good hood, and made a dozen.



The port went in beside the yard while the line went down at Grayrock.

The quays first: stone, faced with the new cement, along the river bank below the shed under the north wall, where the old wooden landing had been rotting since before Emeric came. Then the first two warehouses behind them, brick, with tile roofs, by order of the table. Then, alongside the shed, a second covered slip, longer than the first, for a hull nobody had drawn yet.

Penn set the footings. He went down in the diving dress on lead every morning for a month, with a boy on his rope and two men on the pump, and walked the river bottom where the quay walls would go, and felt for rock with an iron bar, and came up and told the masons where it was and where it was not. Where it was not, they drove piles. He went down again to see the piles were straight. Twice he came up and told them a pile was not straight, and the masons, who had been driving piles in Kaldo since before Penn was born, said a good deal about men in copper hats; and both times they pulled the pile and found he was right.

By the end of August the masons had stopped saying anything about copper hats. One of them asked Penn if he could go down.



The wire met in the middle on the twenty-sixth of August, at noon, somewhere on the capital road between a posting house and a ford, with nobody there to see it but a linesman from Kaldo and a linesman from Wayford, who shook hands on a pole and then climbed down and argued about which of them had got there first.

The first message came through to Kaldo at a quarter past two.

The telegraph room was in the gate tower now, at the top of the stair, with the line to Grayrock on one sounder and the line to the capital road on the other, and a clerk at each, and on that afternoon a good many people who were not clerks standing at the back of the room very quietly. The capital road's sounder had been silent since the poles started in March. At a quarter past two it clicked.

It clicked for some time. The clerk wrote. He wrote slowly, the way the clerks did when they did not want to lose a letter, and when the clicking stopped he looked down at what he had written and then up at the room, and then he got up from the bench and took the slip across the room to Ferra, who was at the back with her book, and gave it to her instead of reading it out.

She read it. Then she read it out.

"Wayford to Kaldo. Line through at noon, twenty-sixth August. Three hundred and seventy-one miles of wire by my count, which will want checking." She paused. "Square swept. Weighhouse open. Accounts follow by post. Aster."

Somebody at the back laughed. Ferra did not. She looked at the slip in her hand, at the words accounts follow by post, and then she folded it in half and put it in her book at the page for the day, and wrote something under the date, and Emeric, reading it upside down across the room, saw that it was a number, and that it was three hundred and seventy-one, and that she had already put a small mark beside it that meant she would check it.



Imani had come up to Grayrock for the boy's eyes and stayed for the mine.

The shaft had a sick room now, of a kind, a hut by the winding house with two beds and a shelf, and she looked at it and said it was a hut, and spent four days making it into something she would put a man in. She went down the shaft once, in a cage, against everybody's advice, to see where the men worked and what they breathed, and came up with her face set. She sat with Hask for an evening over his book of injuries and went through every line of it. She did not say anything to Emeric about any of it. She wrote it down, and he knew that it would come to the table in a fortnight as a list, and that the list would cost money, and that Ferra would pay it.

On her last afternoon Odo ran the line.

The whole line, all of it, from the shaft head to the landing, for the first time with a full string: ten wagons of ore and the dandy cart behind them with the horse in it, eating, and Odo himself on the brake on the last wagon. The whole of Grayrock came out to watch. And at the top of the line, at the shaft head, there was one wagon left over at the front of the string, empty, that had been meant to go down after the others and had not been loaded yet.

Imani looked at it.

She looked at it the way she had looked at the ether in the dyehouse yard, and at the room the first time he showed it to her, and at the chair in the crate. She looked at the line going down the hill away from it, and at the curve round the shoulder where the wagon had gone into the stones twice and the boy had very nearly gone under it, and at the river glittering at the bottom of the hill.

"Does it go down on its own?" she said.

"All the way to the river."

"And stops at the bottom?"

"Odo stops it. With the brake."

"Odo's at the back of the string."

"The front wagon has a brake too."

Imani looked at the front wagon. Then she put her basket down on the ground by the winding house, and gathered her skirts in one hand, and climbed in.

She sat down on the bottom of the empty wagon, on the ore dust, with her back against the front of it and her knees up, and looked up at him over the side.

"Well?"

He got in. There was not a great deal of room. He took the brake lever, which came up out of the floor of the wagon at the front, a long iron bar with a wooden handle, and looked back along the string at Odo on the last wagon, a long way back. Odo had seen. Odo raised his stick in the air and waved it in a great circle over his head, and then, because Odo was Odo, he let his own brake off.

The string began to move.

Slowly at first. A creak, and a long iron groan from every wheel at once, and then the rumble, low, rising, the sound of iron on iron coming up through the floor of the wagon into the bones of them both. The shaft head went by. The winding house, with Odo's stick over its door. The crowd at the top of the line, cheering. Then they were out on the open hill and going faster, and faster, with the rails running away ahead of them shining in the sun, and the wind of their going in their faces, and the whole long string of wagons behind them roaring down the hill like a river in flood.

Imani held the side of the wagon with both hands. Then the straight ended, and the curve came up at them round the shoulder of the hill, the curve, Odo's curve, where the wagon had gone into the stones twice, and she let go of the side and took hold of his arm instead, his left arm, above the elbow, with both her hands, hard.

They went round it.

The wheels' flanges found the inside of the rails, and kissed them, all the way round, with a long rising iron shriek, and the wagon leaned, and held, and came round, and did not climb, and did not come off, and they were through and out of the curve and on the long straight down to the river with the landing coming up at them and the water flashing.

And Imani was laughing.

She had laughed in front of him before. Short laughs, dry ones, a breath through her nose, the corner of her mouth. This was not that. She had her head back against the front of the wagon and her hair coming down out of its cloth and both her hands still on his arm and she was laughing out loud, the whole length of the straight, helplessly, the way a child laughs on a swing, with her eyes shut.

He brought them in on the brake. It took most of the straight. They came into the landing at a walking pace, and then a crawl, with the brake shoes smoking and the string groaning to a halt behind them one wagon at a time, and stopped, with the front of their wagon a yard from the stop block at the end of the line and the river beyond it.

Imani opened her eyes. She let go of his arm. She sat in the bottom of the wagon in the ore dust with her hair down round her shoulders and her face red, and looked at the stop block, and at the river, and then back up the hill at the long line of rails climbing away into the afternoon to the shaft head, so small now that the crowd at the top of it was a line of specks.

"That," she said, "was the stupidest thing I have ever done in my life."

She put her hair back up with both hands, briskly, and tied the cloth round it, and gathered her skirts, and climbed out of the wagon onto the landing.

Then she turned round and looked at the dandy cart at the far end of the string, where the horse was getting down, and at the empties, and at him.

"When does the next one go?"
