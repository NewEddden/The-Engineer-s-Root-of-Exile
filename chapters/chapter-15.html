Chapter 15 - What the Stone Was For

He was small, and his mother's hands were over his on a practice slate.

That was the whole of the dream and there was nothing remarkable in it. He had had it perhaps a dozen times since the exile, in various broken forms, and it never went anywhere. Her thumb on the back of his hand, correcting the angle of the stylus. Grey wool at her wrist. The slate cool. Her voice above and behind him saying something he had never once been able to hear.

What was different was underneath it.

Structure, layered under the dream the way a second exposure sits on one photograph. Sharp where it mattered and vague everywhere else. Not a place he was looking at. A place he was standing in while looking at something else. Plain walls that went soft where they met. A light that came from nowhere in particular. Shelves along one side, mostly empty, their front edges worn pale where a hand had gone to them often.

There was no threshold. There was no falling. Nothing announced itself.

And under all of it, the part that frightened him later rather than then.

He knew this room.

Not from the dream. From before. She used to come here. The recognition arrived whole, with no memory attached to it anywhere, no scene, no occasion, nothing he could have described to another person, which was exactly the texture of everything else behind that wall.

He came awake without remembering the transition.

It took him a full minute lying flat in the dark to work out that it had not been a dream.

The stone was warm in his palm because his hand had closed on it in sleep, as it did most nights, and had been doing for weeks before he noticed, and had been doing tonight when something finished adding up.

That was the important part and he found it before he found anything else.

He had done nothing different. No trigger. No technique. No ritual, no intent, no word said over it. He had been carrying this stone since he was a child and again every day for three months, and last night the sum came out.

He did not go back in.

He sat up against the cold wall with it in his hand and stayed there until the window went grey, which was about four hours, and he did not sleep and did not try to.

Somewhere in the second hour he started a list of what he did not know, because a list of what you do not know is the only honest instrument available at the beginning of anything.

He did not know whether it took something from him. He did not know whether it took something from the stone. He did not know whether the stone could be used up, or whether he could, or whether a thing that had waited fifteen years in a drawer had a reason for waiting. He did not know whether the room was a place or a picture of a place. He did not know whether anything in it was true.

And he did not know whether it could be gone into and not come back out of, which was the only item on the list that mattered and the one he could not test without spending the thing he was testing for.

The most dangerous thing a man can do with an unknown system is use it twice before he has any idea what it costs. He knew that in the abstract, the way everyone knows it. He also knew it the other way.

He had once put his name at the bottom of a consent packet that had been written to protect a company rather than a person, in a room with a chair that reclined farther than any chair needs to, because the line on a graph had reached March.

That got out of him at about the third hour and it cost him the rest of the night.



In the morning he went and found Ward.

"I'd like you to watch me sleep."

"No."

"It's an experiment."

"It's still no."

He heard how it sounded roughly half a second after it left him and did not recover well.

"Observe me."

"Worse."

"Supervise."

"Worse than that."

So he explained the whole night instead, standing in a corridor, badly, out of order, twice going back to correct himself.

Ward listened to the end without interrupting once, which he had done perhaps four times in fifteen years and never for a reason Emeric had liked.

Then he said he would do it.

Then, because Ward spent his humor the way a surgeon spends a blade, once and where it counts, he asked whether the prince would like him to sing.

Emeric laughed considerably harder than the joke deserved and the tension went out of both of them.

That night he lay down with the stone in his hand and Ward in a chair by the bed, and getting to sleep with a knight watching took longer than anything else in the experiment.

The test was the standard one, which Ward knew because every knight knows it, and he gave it afterward in the flat order of a man making a report.

Breathing normal. Temperature normal. Pulse slow and even and entirely uninteresting. Nothing wrong with him by any measure a knight is taught, and a knight is taught the body and nothing past it. Only the stone, warm in his hand. Warmer than a hand accounted for, warm enough that Ward had noticed it from the chair.

The rest Emeric read for himself when he woke, with a sense he had spent three months getting back the way a man gets back the use of a leg.

The stone gave off nothing at all. No signature. No draw. Not dormant, which has a feel to it. Simply absent, the way a rock in a field is absent.

And his own mana settling, and then drawn down. Steadily. The way it does when something is running off it.

He told Ward, and neither of them stated the conclusion in full, because it did not need stating.

The crystal had no signature and the man did.

Every test ever devised for these stones, for four hundred years, in every hall of the Circle in the kingdom, had been pointed at the stone.



The next night he went in deliberately, with Ward in the chair again, and did not let his attention drift.

Show me what you are.

He found a branch. He could not have said what it was made of or what it looked like, and when he tried afterward to write it down the words came out as nonsense, but he knew the shape of it the way a man knows a fork in a road in the dark.

He pressed on it. It held. Not resisting, exactly. Simply not answering force, the way a variable does not answer being shouted at.

He named it and the name arrived without any effort. Conditional logic.

There was a second shape further along that repeated, and repeated, and he looked at it for some time and deliberately did not touch it.

Probably a loop.

He studied both from a distance and used neither.

That was harder than the first night had been. The first night he had been frightened. This one he was not, and wanting to put his hand on the second shape was a physical thing with weight in it, and he sat in front of it for what he later counted as most of an hour and did not.

He told himself the reason out loud, in a room with nobody else in it, in a voice he did not much care for. You do not run a system you cannot read the output of. You do not touch the loop before you understand the branch. And you do not do either of those things with one knight in a chair between your body and a house where four of the men on the doors answer to somebody else.

He lost track of time entirely and surfaced to the bedroom. Ward was where he had left him, and the candle had barely moved, and Emeric lay there looking at it.

Then he took the notebook and renamed the thing, on paper, as a working label and nothing more. He struck out Hollow, the word he had been carrying in his head since the banquet, and wrote beside it:

Control stone.

Not because he was certain of anything. Hollow was a conclusion somebody else had reached with the wrong instrument, and had then written into every catalogue in the kingdom, and he was not going to keep using their word for it while he worked.

He tried it sitting up, in daylight, at the desk, because he wanted to know whether it needed sleep, and because he wanted a count he could trust, which a candle is not.

It did not need sleep.

He came back to Ward standing over him.

Five minutes. Not moving. Eyes open and forward and nobody whatsoever behind them, and one side of the face gone slack. Ward said, flat, that if he had not been told what this was he would have had Imani in the room inside two of those minutes, and would not have waited for permission.

Emeric's own count was somewhere past an hour.

That went into the notebook first, ahead of anything he had found in there.

Time runs wrong. He is completely defenseless for the duration, in a house full of people he does not trust, and to anybody who walks in on it he looks like a man in the middle of a stroke.



The other thing moved inside that week, and he told nobody about that either.

He had not cast anything in front of another person since he was a boy, for the excellent reason that he was bad at it and that being bad at it in public had been off the table for fourteen years. The mask had never left room for practice. So for three months he had made the room himself, behind shutters, with Ward's cloak hung over the gap where the shutters did not meet, because a glyph glows and a shutter leaks: the coin lifted to hand height and held, the candle flame bent and held, the stylus drawn dry across the slate. A handful of slow minutes a night, and his hand wandering through most of them.

Inside, there was nobody. Nothing glowed through anything, because there was nothing for it to glow through. He could hold a thing wrong forty times and drop it forty times, and nobody in the barony would ever know he had touched it.

So he practised there, in the hour that cost Ward five minutes in a chair, and went on practising there.

He lifted the coin and set it down and lifted it again, a hundred times over, until the shape of the lift was something his hand knew without being asked. He bent the flame and held it. He drew the same stroke until it came out the same every time.

He was no stronger for any of it. The pool he drew on was the same size coming out as going in, only lower. But every repetition took a little waste off the motion, the way a file takes the burr off an edge, and for three months the work had been slower than his patience. That first long session it was the other way round.

He did not tell Bram. He did not tell Imani. He wrote to nobody at all, and there was nobody to write to.



What he did instead was sit down and work out what he was holding.

A stone every noble house in Randillia gave away by the handful as a joke. A stone the Circle had classed as defective for four centuries and stopped testing three centuries ago. A stone that did something nobody had ever thought to look for, because looking for it would have required pointing the instrument at the man instead.

His brother had handed him nine of them at a banquet to make a point about being unchanging.

He got the case out and put all nine on the desk in a row, with his mother's in front of them, and looked at the ten of them for a while by candle.

They were identical to any eye he had. Same dull grey. Same fine repeating lattice over every face. His mother's had gone soft at the edges from being carried and the other nine had not, and that was the entire visible difference between a stone that had opened a room and nine stones that had sat in a case since April.

He tried one of the nine that night, deliberately, holding it the way he held the other, and got nothing at all. He tried two more and got the same nothing, a pebble from a riverbed sitting in his palm, and was not certain whether that meant the nine were dead or whether it meant years of handling was the price of admission and he had only ever paid it once.

He wrote both possibilities down and did not have a method for choosing between them.

He sat with that for quite a while.

Then he filed one more item, and it was the one that kept him up.

His mother had put the first one in his hands before he was seven. His memory of everything around that was a wall. And those two facts were now sitting next to each other on the same page for the first time in his life, and he could not make them do anything, and he could not make himself stop looking at them either.

There was one more thing to check.

He turned to the back of Armand's notebook. Not the very back, where the pages had been blank in April and were now full of Kaldo in his own hand, but the last of Armand's pages before them, where the old man's writing went small and cramped. He had skimmed it in the carriage. He read it properly now.

Eleven tests over nine years. Heat. Cold. A full mana stone pressed face to face against it for a day and a night. Water. Light through a lens. A tower mage's best sense held on it for an hour. Eleven methods, each one careful, and under every one the same word or near enough. Nothing. No response. Inert.

Armand had been the best crystal theorist the tower had, and he had looked correctly, at length, with real patience, and every one of his eleven instruments had been pointed at the stone. Then the entries stopped. No conclusion, and no last line that he could read. Under the final entry a strip of the page had been scraped back with a knife, neatly, the way a clerk takes out a wrong figure, and the paper there was rough under his thumb. A man who had spent nine years on a question had turned the page and gone on to something else, which is what a good engineer does with a dead end.

That was one old man. It was not four hundred years.

Four centuries of these things in pockets and on mantels and in velvet cases handed over as jokes, and in all that time nobody else had fallen asleep holding one long enough? He did not believe it. Somebody had stumbled into the room before him. And it had never got out. It was not in Armand's thirty years. It was not in the tower, or the Circle, or anywhere in the capital he had ever heard of.

He had no evidence of any of it. He had a feeling, the kind his old life had taught him to distrust and his father's whisper had taught him to keep, and the feeling was that a thing this useful does not stay unknown that long by accident. Somebody kept knowledge like this buried, and had been keeping it buried a long time.

He was a disgraced third son in a dying barony with four guards and a prisoner. Whoever they were, he was not ready to meet them, and being found out was the surest way to.

So almost nobody would know. Ward knew.

And he was not going to learn this alone either. He knew that inside three days. Both things were true and he could not make them agree, so he kept the number as small as it would go.

What he had was a town full of problems and a blacksmith who owed him nothing and had told him so.



So he went down to the forge with a different question than usual.

Bram, who had been fed nothing but latrines and wells and the price of ash for two months, heard about forty seconds of him working round to it and put the hammer down.

"Come to the house."

"It's not a house question."

"My daughter's been asking what you actually talk about." He went back to the fire. "I've been guessing for two months and I'm tired of it. Come Thursday."
