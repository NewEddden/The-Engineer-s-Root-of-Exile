Chapter 15 - A Fair Offer

Baron Paxton of Milford arrived on a good horse at the worst possible hour, which was the middle of the morning of the day they were cutting the fourth mile of the ring, and Emeric received the news standing thigh-deep in a trench with clay to his elbows.

"He's at the gate," Rylan said. "Twelve men. Says he'll wait."

"How long has he been waiting?"

"Long enough to have said he'll wait."

Emeric climbed out. He looked at himself, and then at Ward, who had come down the line at the first sound of a horn and was standing with his arms folded in the particular way that meant a decision was about to be made badly.

"Don't," Ward said.

"I haven't said anything."

"You're going to tell me you'll meet a baron in that."

"I'm going to tell you that he rode a day to look at what I'm building, and that walking him past a hundred and forty men digging a channel is the whole of my argument, and that turning up scrubbed and changed tells him I think the digging is something to be ashamed of." He wiped his hands on his thighs, which accomplished nothing. "Also I have precisely one good coat and it's a court coat, and if I put it on he'll spend the entire day talking to the coat."

Ward considered that with visible reluctance.

"Wash your face," he said.

Paxton was sixty or near it, built like a man who had spent his life getting on and off horses, with the deep weathering across the nose and cheekbones that came from forty years of open country. He looked at Emeric's clay to the elbow, and at the trench, and at the hundred and forty men, and did not say any of the four things a courtier would have said.

"You're grading it," he said.

"I'm grading it."

"So it runs itself."

"One in nineteen. Water walks down it and I never pay a man to lift a bucket."

"Hm." Paxton turned all the way round, taking in the run of the line, the stakes, the twine, the nine divisions pegged out across two thousand acres of floodplain. He was quiet for long enough that Emeric began composing the explanation, and then he said, "Where does it go when you've too much of it?"

Which was the question. It was not the question anyone had asked him yet, including Ferra, including himself until the third week, and it was the one that separated a man who understood water from a man who was being polite about a ditch.

"Back to the river," Emeric said. "Two gates, low end, both sides of the loop. In a wet spring I'm not irrigating, I'm draining, and the same channel does both if you build the fall right and don't get clever about it."

Paxton grunted. It was not a courtly noise. It was the noise a man makes when a thing has come out better than he expected and he does not intend to say so out loud.

They walked the ring for two hours, and Emeric learned more about his own farm in those two hours than he had in the preceding month, because Paxton was a stockman and read ground the way Ferra read a column. He wanted to know why the western arm and not the eastern. He wanted to know what Emeric intended to do with the clover in the resting division, and when Emeric said feed it under he said that was a waste of a hay crop and a waste of sheep, and had opinions about the second part that lasted a quarter of a mile.

He also did not once ask about the baron on the wall, or the crown, or the execution, or why a fifth son was standing in a ditch in a barony nobody wanted. Emeric noticed that, and could not decide whether it was courtesy or strategy, and eventually concluded that it was a man not wasting a morning on things that were not his business.

They ate in the long room, which had benches now, and Ferra sat at the end of the table with her book because Emeric had asked her to and because she would have anyway.

"I'll say it plain and you can think about it as long as you like," Paxton said, pushing his plate back. "Milford wants your grain."

"Milford has better land than this."

"Milford has better pasture than this. There's a difference, and it's cost me nineteen years of explaining it to men who ought to know." He set his forearms on the table. "We run wool and hides and beef, and beef eats, and my people have grown by a third since the plague years because everyone who ran from your water came to my grass. I've a growing town and a shrinking margin and I buy bread grain the same as you do, off the same road, at whatever Grayrock says it costs this season."

"He says it costs a great deal."

"He says whatever he likes. That's what one road buys a man." Paxton's mouth did something dry. "My father bought Kaldo grain. Forty years back, before the plague, before the levy, when this floodplain fed half the country between here and the coast. I came out here half expecting to find you'd sown a rumor. You've not. You've sown two thousand acres and you're digging water to four thousand six hundred, and either you're the biggest fool in the region or in three years I'm buying bread grain from Kaldo again, and I'd sooner be early than right."

The offer, when it came, was clean and specific, and Emeric found he had to make an effort to keep his hands still while he heard it.

Milford would contract now, this season, for the surplus of the coming harvest. A fixed price per bushel, set on the day of signing and not moved after, whatever the region did. A third of the money paid on the mark, before a stalk of it came out of the ground. The remaining two thirds on delivery. If the harvest came in short, Milford took what there was and the advance stood against it. If it came in long, Milford took the surplus at the same price and did not complain about it.

"And you'd cart it yourself?" Ferra said, without looking up.

"Half. You cart to the ford, I cart from it. I'm not carrying your wagons for you and I'm not asking you to carry mine."

Ferra wrote that down. Emeric watched her write it and knew she was already three columns further along than he was.

"Why fix the price," he said. "You lose if the region goes short."

"I lose if the region goes short and I've no contract at all, because then I'm bidding against every other hungry lord in a bad year with a purse I haven't got." Paxton shrugged. "Fixed means I know. I've forty years of not knowing and I'd pay something to be done with it."

"That's a strange thing to hear out loud from a man across a table."

"You'll hear the rest of it out loud too, and then we'll see if you still want to sign." Paxton looked at him steadily. He had pale eyes and did nothing decorative with them. "You're going to grow, and men are going to notice, and some of them are going to mind. Grayrock will mind. Wayford will mind more, because Wayford lives off a road and you're about to become a reason to use a different one. If it comes to a hard word between you and them, don't look to me. Milford is the smallest of the three of us and it's open pasture with a road in from every side and it has a wall you could ride a cart through. Whatever the two of them decide, I go with, because the alternative is my people paying for my principles."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"You've just told me you'd march with them," Emeric said.

"I've told you I'd not stand against them. There's a difference and one day it may matter, and I'd rather have said it now, sober, at your table, than have you find it out." Paxton reached for his cup. "I'll deal honest with you until the day I can't. That's the whole of what I have. Most men will tell you more than that and mean less."

Emeric turned that over. The unpleasant part was how much he liked it.

"I'll want a day," he said.

"Take three. I'll want an answer by the sowing either way, because if it's no I've got to go and be pleasant to Grayrock about grain, and I'd like to get the pleasantness over with."

"Say the thing," Emeric said to Ferra, when the man had gone off to be housed.

"The price is fair."

"I know the price is fair."

"No. Listen to it properly." She turned the book round. "It's fair for a normal year in a normal region. It's a little better than fair, because he's paying a third in advance and taking the short-harvest risk in the same breath, and a merchant would not do either. He's paying for certainty, and he's paying us for the trouble of giving it to him, and there is nothing in this offer I would call a trick."

"Then what's your face for?"

"My face is for the shape." Ferra tapped the page twice. "One road out of this barony that goes anywhere. One buyer at the end of it. One price, set by a contract we can't reopen. If we sign this for three years, the whole of Kaldo's income is a single line running from this floodplain to one man's granary, and if that line breaks anywhere along it, the barony has no other way to turn grain into coin." She sat back. "You've spent a season telling me a tree with one trunk is a bad design. That's a tree with one trunk."

Emeric was quiet for a while.

"Say the rest of it," he said.

"There isn't a rest of it. That's the problem. There's no second buyer to compare him to, because we haven't got one, and there's no better price, because nobody else has offered any price at all. A single point of failure that you refuse is not replaced by a better arrangement. It's replaced by nothing." She closed the book. "You're right about the shape and you're going to sign it anyway, and I want it written down that we both knew."

"Write it down."

She did, and dated it, and that was the moment Emeric realised he was not going to be signing anything.

He sat with it for a while and then said, slowly, "Sixteen parts in twenty."

Ferra stopped writing.

"It isn't my grain," he said.

He had written the terms himself. He had read them out in front of thirty-one people in this room. He had put the register in a candle shop on purpose so that no future baron could pretend the arrangement had never happened, and he had specifically, deliberately, with a straight face, told Dunnel that the count belonged to the field.

Four parts in twenty of the coming harvest were his to sell to Baron Paxton of Milford. The other sixteen belonged to thirty-seven households, by name, in the chandler's book, on the fifth page after the weir.

"You did that on purpose," Ferra said.

"I did that on purpose about four months ago for entirely different reasons and I'd like it on the record that I'm being held to it by a version of myself who had no idea this was coming." He rubbed his face and got clay on it. "Get me Halloway."

The meeting was on the fourth day, which meant sending word to Paxton that a day had become four, and the reason had to be given plainly because Emeric could not think of a lie he was willing to tell.

"You have to ask them," Paxton said. He did not sound annoyed. He sounded like a man who had found an unexpected step on a stair.

"I have to ask them."

"You're the baron in all but the word."

"I'm the man who wrote down that I wasn't, in a book in a shop, in front of the whole town." Emeric shrugged. "It seemed clever at the time. It was clever at the time. It's extremely inconvenient this morning and it's still clever."

Paxton looked at him for a long moment with an expression Emeric could not read at all.

"I'll wait," he said.

They filled the long room. Twenty-nine of the thirty-seven came, which was better than Emeric had expected, and Ferra read the terms out because she had read the last ones out and it was becoming the way things were done.

Emeric made the case himself, and he made it as well as he could, and he made it about risk because that was the true reason and he had decided some time ago that he would rather lose an argument honestly than win one sideways.

A fixed price meant the field knew, in the second week of spring, what its harvest was worth. A third in advance meant coin in the barony before a stalk came up, which paid the dig crews' grain wage without eating the seed store. And a short harvest, which was the thing that could kill them, became Milford's problem instead of theirs.

"That last part is the whole of it," he said. "Every one of you has had a bad year. Most of you have had a bad year that took something you couldn't get back. This is the first year any of you has had a chance to buy your way out of the next one, and the price of it is that if the harvest comes in enormous you'll have sold it cheap and you'll be annoyed for a season. I'd take that trade with both hands. I want to be clear that I'd take it, and that I can't take it for you."

Then Dunnel stood up, and Emeric understood inside four sentences that he had lost.

"You told us four thousand six hundred acres," Dunnel said.

"I did."

"You put it on a board in this room and you put the dots up for thirty years of my ground and you told us the slide stops and turns round." He was not angry. That was the difficulty. He was working, carefully, in front of everybody. "I've bought grain off Grayrock's road every winter of my life at whatever price the man felt like naming, because we were short and he knew we were short. That's what a shortage costs a man. I have paid it for thirty years." He looked around the room. "Now you're telling me we're about to be the ones with grain in a region that's short of it, and the first thing we should do is sign a paper fixing what we'll take for it, three years out, to the first baron who rode up the road and asked nicely."

"The price is fair," Emeric said.

"It's fair for a normal year. You've spent four months telling us there isn't going to be a normal year." Dunnel spread his hands. "I believe you. That's the trouble. I believe you, so I'll not sell it cheap."

Maren spoke against him, which Emeric had not expected and should have. She had eleven acres and four grown sons and no reserve of any kind, and she said, in about nine words, that coin in spring fed her house and coin in three autumns did not, and sat down.

Then Halloway asked the question that decided it, and he asked it flatly, as a matter of procedure, without any apparent understanding that he was pulling a floor out.

"How are we counting this? Heads or parts?"

The room went quiet, and Emeric stood there and felt the shape of what he had done four months ago come round and find him.

He had written who owned what. He had written how the harvest was cut, and how the parts passed, and who held the tallies, and where the register lived. He had written the clause that cost him money to prove he could be checked.

He had not written a single word about how the field decided anything.

Both answers were available to him. Both were defensible. And he could see, standing there, exactly which way each of them fell: heads gave it to the smallholders, who were many and poor and wanted the coin, and parts gave it to the ground-part holders, who were few and comfortable and wanted the upside. He wanted the sale. Heads would give him the sale.

"Parts," he said.

Ferra's pen stopped.

"A share of a thing that votes by heads isn't a share, it's a membership, and I'd be telling you the parts mean something on the day they pay out and nothing on the day they decide." He heard his own voice being reasonable and disliked it. "Parts. And for the avoidance of any doubt about what I'm doing here, ruling it the other way hands me the outcome I came in wanting, and everybody in this room can see that, and that's exactly why I'm not doing it."

They counted parts. It went against him, eleven and a fraction to eight and change, and the fraction was Widow Corbell's, who had inherited a half part and would not have sold it to God.

Halloway abstained, which Emeric thought about for a long time afterward.

He sold his own four parts in twenty to Baron Paxton of Milford that evening, at Paxton's price, with a third paid on the mark, because those four were his to sell and because coin now was coin now.

Paxton took it without visible disappointment and with what Emeric was fairly sure was amusement.

"A fifth of a harvest," he said. "I rode a day for a fifth of a harvest."

"You rode a day and found out that the man in the ditch can't sell you the field. I'd have thought that was worth the trip on its own."

"It was worth more than the grain." Paxton signed, and blotted it, and looked at his own name on the page. "I've never in my life had a lord tell me to my face that he'd have to go and ask the farmers. Half my barons would say the tenants had no business in it. The other half would say it and then have the man who asked flogged." He handed the pen back. "I don't know yet whether what you've built is clever or whether you've simply given away the only thing a lord actually holds. I'd not bet against you. I'd not bet on you either."

"That's about where I am on it."

"Then we understand each other, and I'll say the other thing once more before I go, so it's twice and not once." He put his hat on. "Whatever Grayrock and Wayford decide, I go with. Don't be surprised, and don't be hurt. Being hurt about it would be the only genuinely stupid thing I've seen you do."

Maren caught him at the gate the next morning, on her way out to the resting division with a hoe over her shoulder, and said it without preamble, because she was fifty-four and had stopped wasting words some time in the last decade.

"I'd have taken the coin."

"I know. I heard you say so."

"I said so and it counted for eleven acres." She adjusted the hoe. "Halloway said nothing at all and that counted for a hundred and forty."

She went out to the field. Emeric stood at the gate for a while after that.

He got the list out that night, under the empty space, and wrote the thing down without any softening, because the softened version would be no use to him in a year.

I wrote down what they own and I never wrote down how they choose. Fix it before the harvest, and fix it in a way that costs me something, or it will read as me changing the rules the first time the rules bit me.

Then, lower, because it was the other half and he had been avoiding it since Paxton's second telling:

One road. One buyer. And the buyer told me twice, at my own table, exactly when he'd stop being one.
