Chapter 15 - The Crown's Judgment

Petram took the news about Cobb's account the way a man takes the weather. He listened, nodded, and sat looking at the stone wall of the cell.

Then he said, "So the baron paid."

"Somebody in this keep paid. Through the baron's books."

"And the boy on my door is dead." Petram turned his head. "And the man who hired me is a courier nobody can find. And Garrin's in the trees." He worked it through out loud, slowly, as if he were counting coins he didn't trust. "So there's me."

Emeric didn't answer that, because it was true.

"I'm the only one of us left you can stand up in front of anybody." Petram looked at the door, at the stair beyond it, at the lamp. "That's not safe, is it. Being the only one."

"No."

Petram nodded again. And then, for two days, he talked.



They were two days in that cell, with no window and one lamp and the smell of old stone, and Ferra on a stool in the corner with her book on her knee.

She had come down the stair the first morning with Rylan in front of her and Ward behind, and Petram had looked at her once and asked nothing, and nobody told him her name. She wrote down everything he said. Everything. The signals Garrin used on the road, a hand on the back of the neck, a boot scraped twice. The two places they'd met in the capital, a stable yard and the back room of a cookshop. The coin, which Garrin had never once counted out in front of him, only handed over in a bag already tied. How Garrin had disliked him from the first day and never troubled to hide it.

"He was told to take a household man," Petram said. "Not his own second. He didn't like that either."

"Told by whom?"

"He didn't say. He said he'd been told."

Every so often Ferra stopped him with a question nobody else would have thought to ask. Which day of the week was that. Was it before the rain or after. How many bells had rung. When he told the same thing twice and it came out a little different the second time, she didn't correct him. She wrote both versions down, one under the other, and drew a line beside them.

"Why both?" Emeric asked her on the stair, at the end of the first day.

"Because he's telling the truth." She didn't slow down. "A man who tells it the same way every time has learned his lines."

On the second afternoon, almost as an afterthought, Petram mentioned that he'd seen Cobb with Garrin once.

Once. In the stable yard in the capital, before any of it began. Not long, a minute and a half, perhaps. They'd been talking, and Petram had come in on them, and Cobb had said something, and Garrin had stood up.

"Stood up," Petram said again, and frowned at the wall, as though the memory were a stone in his boot. "Garrin doesn't stand for anybody. I've seen him sit through a captain walking in. He stood for a courier."

Emeric heard it. He set it beside the rest and went on to the next question, and didn't turn it over, and didn't ask himself why not.

When they rose to go on the second evening, Petram said, without looking up, "Will Corliss be all right."

"I don't know," Emeric said.



He came up out of the cell that evening and asked Hale where the baron was.

Hale was in the steward's room with the winter grain figures. He put the pen down and told him at once, as if he'd been waiting to be asked.

"He rode yesterday, Your Highness. First light. Two horses and his man. For the capital." A pause. "He heard about the books three days before that. I don't know from whom. He's gone to petition the crown before you can."

Emeric stood in the doorway and did the arithmetic. A day and a half's start on fresh horses, and a petition at the top of a magistrate's pile before anyone else had a word in.

"Thank you," he said.

Hale didn't pick his pen up again. He got to his feet, and within the hour there was food for the road packed in the yard, enough for a week, and a good saddle on the second horse that Emeric had never seen before, and a folded sheet in Hale's hand that turned out to be instructions for the winter grain. Which barns, which order, what to hold back, what to sell if the spring came late. They read like the work of a man expecting to run the barony on his own for some time.

Emeric read them twice and didn't know what to make of them, and put them in his coat.

Six hours after he'd come up the cell stair, they rode out of the South Gate in the dark before dawn: Emeric, Ferra, Ward, and Petram in irons under a guard's cloak on a led horse, with Declan riding behind as far as Wayford, where Ferra had sent ahead for an escort from the magistrate's house. Two days behind Wexler, near enough.

Ward rode up beside Emeric as the sky started to grey.

"Better direction this time," he said.

Emeric looked at the road running north.

"We're going toward it," he said.



Ferra had never been further from Kaldo than the kiln. She rode badly, and said so, and did it anyway.

She had written ahead before they left: to the waypoint registries along the south road, where every courier and every carter with a crown pass signed in and out, and to the toll clerks at Wayford. She paid the riders who took her letters out of her own advance, and wrote down what she'd paid, and when Emeric offered to cover it she said it was the barony's business, not his, and that she'd present it at the right time.

The forest south of Wayford took the whole of the second afternoon. Emeric rode it with his eyes on the treeline. Nothing came out of it.

At Wayford they stayed a night longer than they'd meant to, because of the tolls.

Every cart bringing goods down the south road paid a toll at Wayford by weight, and the clerks kept the returns in a long room above the stables. Ferra spent most of a day in that room. Emeric held the lamp.

She wanted eleven years of returns. The clerks said nine was what they had to hand and the rest were in a cellar and nobody went in the cellar. She argued with them, politely, for some time, and got the nine.

"The grain," she said that night, at the inn, with the returns spread across the table. "Wexler's contract. Eleven years of grain, bought at that price, delivered to Kaldo." She put her finger on the contract figure and then on the toll books. "Grain coming south on this road pays here. By the cartload. If it was ever bought, it came through Wayford and somebody wrote it down."

"And?"

"And nobody did. Not once in nine years." She sat back. "It never came. There was never any grain. He made it up, and he paid himself for it." She looked at the returns. "And Wexler never touched a line of this. These are the crown's books, kept by the crown's clerks, two days from his desk."

Emeric thought it was beautiful, and nearly said so, and didn't.

She hadn't withdrawn her objection. It was in her book, dated, and not crossed out. What she was building, page by page, was a theft case and a treason case, each made to stand without the other. It wasn't quite the case he believed. Once, on the road beyond Wayford, she corrected him when he said that Wexler had hired Petram. Paid, she said. We can show he paid. We can't show he hired. He heard it as care. He was glad of her care.



At a waystation on the fourth day, in the late afternoon, while the horses were watered and Ferra was in the registry arguing with another clerk, Emeric sat on a bench in the yard with Ward beside him and the stone in his hand, and went in.

He went to his own room, the plain one with the soft corners and the shelves, where he'd lifted the coin a hundred times in July. The room he shared with Ferra at Bram's table was a different place. He hadn't been in it alone since the Thursday at Bram's. Near a month.

It was exactly as he'd left it.

The coin was where he'd last set it down. The candle. The slate with the last stroke on it, the one he'd drawn before he stopped. Nothing had faded. Nothing had moved.

He stood there a while.

Nobody had been holding this. Not for a month. Not Ward, not him, not anybody.

He came out. Ward looked at him.

"Fine," Emeric said.



They came over the last rise into Randillia City at dusk on the seventh day.

The rings climbed from the river to the castle as they always had, lamp after lamp coming on. Above the inner rings the small sky-ships were going home, slow and low, each with a man standing in it pouring himself away to keep it there.

Ferra stopped her horse and looked up at them. Emeric watched her look, and thought he'd give a good deal to know what she was counting.

He made a list on the way in, without meaning to, as he had in the audience hall in April. His father. Corbin. Aldane. Augustine. Waylon. People who were somewhere in that castle on the hill, who knew he was coming or would by morning.

He went down the list once, testing each name like a tooth. Only one of them hurt, and it was the brother who'd decided he wasn't worth staying sober for.

Wexler had been inside the walls two days already, by the gate book. Talking.



The magistrate's chamber was a small working room on the ground floor of the judiciary, with a long table and windows set too high in the wall to see anything out of. A clerk at a side desk. A water jug. Nothing on the walls.

Magistrate Keller was grey at the temples and somewhere past fifty, and he didn't stand when a prince of the blood came in. He looked up from the papers in front of him, nodded, and went back to them, and Emeric decided he liked him.

Petram wasn't there. He was being held three streets away, in the judiciary's own cells. A confessed conspirator's word, Keller said, without looking up, was a liability before a magistrate, not evidence. It could be bought and it could be frightened. He'd hear Petram if he had to. He'd rather not have to.

Wexler sat at the far end of the table in a clean coat. He looked older than he had in April, and very steady.

Wexler spoke first. He'd petitioned, he said, so that rumor wouldn't become record. There were things being said in Kaldo about his books, by a prince with a grievance and no experience of a barony's accounts, and he'd thought it right that the crown hear them from him first.

"I don't want to hear them from anyone," Keller said. "I want to see them. Volume isn't evidence."

So Ferra put them on the table.

She did it in order and slowly, sliding each sheet across so Keller could read it before she gave him the next.

Three pages from the baron's own ledger, scraped and written over, and under the new ink, held to the window, the old: a debt discharged, routed through a waypoint marker on the south road that turned out to sit on the dispatch route of a courier called Cobb. Cobb's payments, rebuilt from the waypoint registries and from the marks on the coin, quarter by quarter, eleven years of them. Coin leaving the barony's treasury under Wexler's own seal within a week of the road. The drawbridge chain, paid for in full twice, and replaced with rope. The grain contract, and nine years of Wayford tolls that showed no grain had ever come down the road. Sixteen names on the garrison's pay roll that belonged to nobody.

And three petitions, from the healer of Kaldo, entered in the castle's register of outgoing letters in the first year of the plague, each with its date, and each with no mark beside it to say it had ever been sent.

Emeric had not known about the petitions. He looked at them on the table, at Imani's name written three times in a clerk's hand, and at Ferra, who didn't look at him.

Wexler said a baron didn't control every payment that went out under his seal. When Keller asked whose hand had entered them, he said the pages were forged.

Ferra laid the scraped page beside a page from twenty years before, both in the baron's hand, and showed Keller how Wexler closed his g, and his d, and how he crossed a seven, and how all three did the same on both pages, under the scraping and over it.

Keller looked at them for some time.

"And you are?" he said to her.

"The barony's clerk," Ferra said.

Keller wrote that down. Nothing else. The barony's clerk.

Then he turned to Emeric, and asked whether he had anything to say.

"I've got a grievance," Emeric said. "I won't pretend I haven't. He paid for a road I nearly died on, and a boy I should have protected is dead." He looked at the papers. "So don't rule on me. Rule on those."

Keller looked at him a moment longer than he needed to. Then he did.

Treasonous conspiracy. Financing an attempt on the life of a prince of the blood. Death, by crown order.

"This is the crown's judgment," Keller said, to Emeric, and only to Emeric. "Not yours."

He went on, in the same voice, to the consequences in law. The baron was attainted. There was no heir resident in Kaldo, and the founder's line had ended four hundred years ago with the founder. A barony that fell vacant by attainder passed to the highest-ranked noble holding title within it. He looked up.

"Which is the prince," Keller said, "whose title is that place."

Emeric sat very still.

He found, sitting there, that he'd wanted to do it himself, and if he was honest, and the chamber seemed to require it, it had nothing to do with Tam. He'd wanted it for the fire on the fifth night and the man he'd been on the road who had trusted two strangers because he was too busy suspecting his father.

He was relieved that he wasn't. And he was disgusted that he was relieved. He sat with both of them until the clerk had finished writing.



Wexler was waiting by the door when they came out. Two guards stood a pace back from him. He looked at Emeric, and Emeric stopped.

"In the first season of the plague," Wexler said, "I rode to this city. Six days. I sold a horse at Wayford to pay for the second half." His voice was flat. "I waited two days in a corridor upstairs. On the third day a clerk came out and told me the matter had been noted." He let that sit. "Noted. So I went home, and I stopped writing letters, and I buried a third of my town."

Emeric said nothing.

"Then, this spring, a rider came down the south road with one line. The crown was giving four generations of my family's work to its embarrassment." Something moved at the corner of Wexler's mouth. "So I paid for it."

"So you never saw me," Emeric said. "You paid for what you were told was coming."

"I paid for a drunk."

They stood there.

"Then I watched four months of what actually came," Wexler said. "Wells. A kiln. That woman's hands." He looked down the corridor. "More than I managed in thirty years. That isn't a defense. I'm telling you because nobody else will."

His wife had died before the plague, he said, when Emeric didn't move. His son had taken a clerkship with a Wayford merchant eleven years ago, and his letters home had got shorter every year, and then stopped mentioning home at all.

That evening Emeric wrote to the son. He didn't know what to say, so he kept it short, and said it plainly, and sent it by a rider he paid himself.

Petram got labor, not death. Keller explained that too, without being asked: a court that hanged its cooperating witnesses never got another one. Emeric asked that the sentence be served in Kaldo. He'd seen men in irons hand-cutting a road on his second day out of this city, under two guards who didn't care whether they lived. If Petram was going to break stone, Emeric wanted to see how it was done. Keller granted it.

"Cobb's still loose," Ward said, in the yard, low. "And Wexler's garrison answers to nobody now."

"After the farm," Emeric said. "The farm eats first. The garrison keeps."

Ward didn't argue. He didn't agree, either.



Armand was standing in the courtyard of the judiciary when they came out into the last of the light.

He was older than Emeric remembered, and thinner, and had the look of a man who'd been standing there a while. He looked at Emeric's face and then at his hands, the way he had when Emeric was a boy and had come to a lesson with ink on them.

"You read it," Armand said. "The notebook."

"To the back page." Emeric stopped in front of him. "Eleven tests. Nine years."

Armand's face did something complicated.

"You were looking correctly," Emeric said. "You stopped too soon."

The old man didn't ask what that meant. Emeric thought he probably knew it wasn't a thing to ask in a courtyard.

"I have obligations in the tower," Armand said at last. "When they're settled, I'll come south." He paused. "If I'd be welcome."

"You would."

Armand nodded once, and went back across the courtyard toward the tower, and didn't look round.

Emeric stood in the dusk with Ferra and Ward and watched the sky-ships going home over the inner rings, each one a man holding it up, and above the tower the dark bulk of the Circle's gallery.

Four hundred years, twenty men a day, to hold up one gallery.

And a room in a grey stone had kept its shape for a month with nobody holding it at all.

He didn't say it.
