Chapter 15 - What the Stone Was For

He was small, and his mother's hands were over his on a practice slate.

That was the whole of the dream and there was nothing remarkable in it. He had had it perhaps a dozen times since the gallery, in various broken forms, and it never went anywhere. Her thumb on the back of his hand, correcting the angle of the stylus. The slate cool. Her voice above and behind him saying something he had never once been able to hear.

What was different was underneath it.

Structure, layered under the dream the way a second exposure sits on one photograph. Sharp where it mattered and vague everywhere else. Not a place he was looking at. A place he was standing in while looking at something else.

There was no threshold. There was no falling. Nothing announced itself.

And under all of it, the part that frightened him later rather than then.

He knew this room.

Not from the dream. From before. She used to come here. The recognition arrived whole, with no memory attached to it anywhere, no scene, no occasion, nothing he could have described to another person, which was exactly the texture of everything else behind that wall.

He came awake without remembering the transition.

It took him a full minute lying flat in the dark to work out that it had not been a dream.

The stone was warm in his palm because his hand had closed on it in sleep, as it did most nights, and had been doing for weeks before he noticed, and had been doing tonight when something finished adding up.

That was the important part and he found it before he found anything else.

He had done nothing different. No trigger. No technique. No ritual, no intent, no word said over it. He had been carrying this stone since he was a child and again every day for three months, and last night the sum came out.

He did not go back in.

He sat up against the cold wall with it in his hand and stayed there until the window went grey, which was about four hours, and he did not sleep and did not try to.

Somewhere in the second hour he started a list of what he did not know, because a list of what you do not know is the only honest instrument available at the beginning of anything.

He did not know whether it took something from him. He did not know whether it took something from the stone. He did not know whether the stone could be used up, or whether he could, or whether a thing that had waited fifteen years in a drawer had a reason for waiting. He did not know whether the room was a place or a picture of a place. He did not know whether anything in it was true.

And he did not know whether it could be gone into and not come back out of, which was the only item on the list that mattered and the one he could not test without spending the thing he was testing for.

The most dangerous thing a man can do with an unknown system is use it twice before he has any idea what it costs. He knew that in the abstract, the way everyone knows it. He also knew it the other way.

He had once put his name at the bottom of forty pages that had been written to protect a company rather than a person, in a room with a chair that reclined farther than any chair needs to, because the line on a graph had reached March.

That got out of him at about the third hour and it cost him the rest of the night.

In the morning he went and found Ward.

"I'd like you to watch me sleep."

"No."

"It's an experiment."

"It's still no."

He heard how it sounded roughly half a second after it left him and did not recover well.

"Observe me."

"Worse."

"Supervise."

"Worse than that."

So he explained the whole night instead, standing in a corridor, badly, out of order, twice going back to correct himself.

Ward listened to the end without interrupting once, which he had done perhaps four times in fifteen years and never for a reason Emeric had liked.

Then he said he would do it.

Then, because Ward spent his humor the way a surgeon spends a blade, once and where it counts, he asked whether the prince would like him to sing.

Emeric laughed considerably harder than the joke deserved and the tension went out of both of them.

The test was the standard one, which Ward knew because every knight knows it.

Breathing. Temperature. Pulse. And the coarse read on whether a man's mana is moving, and in which direction, which is the part they teach last and the part that takes.

Breathing normal. Temperature normal. Pulse slow and even and entirely uninteresting.

The stone warm. Warmer than a hand accounted for, and giving off nothing at all that a knight's read could find. No signature. No draw. Not dormant, which has a feel to it. Simply absent, the way a rock in a field is absent.

And Emeric's own mana settling, and then drawing down. Steadily. The way it does when something is running off it.

Neither of them stated the conclusion in full, because it did not need stating.

The crystal had no signature and the man did.

Every test ever devised for these stones, for four hundred years, in every hall of the Circle in the kingdom, had been pointed at the stone.

That night he went in deliberately and did not let his attention drift.

Show me what you are.

He found a branch. He could not have said what it was made of or what it looked like, and when he tried afterward to write it down the words came out as nonsense, but he knew the shape of it the way a man knows a fork in a road in the dark.

He pressed on it. It held. Not resisting, exactly. Simply not answering force, the way a variable does not answer being shouted at.

He named it and the name arrived without any effort. Conditional logic.

There was a second shape further along that repeated, and repeated, and he looked at it for some time and deliberately did not touch it.

Probably a loop.

He studied both from a distance and used neither.

That was harder than the first night had been. The first night he had been frightened. This one he was not, and wanting to put his hand on the second shape was a physical thing with weight in it, and he sat in front of it for what he later counted as most of an hour and did not.

He told himself the reason out loud, in an empty room, in a voice he did not much care for. You do not run a system you cannot read the output of. You do not touch the loop before you understand the branch. And you do not do either of those things alone in a house where four of the men on the doors answer to somebody else.

He lost track of time entirely and surfaced at morning.

Then he took the notebook and renamed the thing, on paper, as a working label and nothing more.

Control Rune.

Not because he was certain of anything. Hollow was a conclusion somebody else had reached with the wrong instrument, and had then written into every catalogue in the kingdom, and he was not going to keep using their word for it while he worked.

He tried it sitting up, in daylight, at the desk, because he wanted to know whether it needed sleep.

It did not.

He came back to Ward standing over him.

Five minutes. Not moving. Eyes open and forward and nobody whatsoever behind them. Ward said, flat, that if he had not been told what this was he would have had Imani in the room inside two of those minutes, and would not have waited for permission.

Emeric's own count was somewhere past an hour.

That went into the notebook first, ahead of anything he had found in there.

Time runs wrong. He is completely defenseless for the duration, in a house full of people he does not trust, and to anybody who walks in on it he looks like a man in the middle of a stroke.

The other thing began that week and he told nobody about it either.

He had not cast anything in front of another person since he was fourteen, for the excellent reason that he was bad at it and that being bad at it in public had been off the table for fifteen years. The mask had never left room for practice.

Inside, there was nobody. He could hold a thing wrong forty times and drop it forty times, and nobody in the barony would ever know he had touched it.

So he practised there, in the hour that cost five minutes, and went on practising there, and it is the reason his control came out tight and wasteless years before any living person had watched him train.

He did not tell Bram. He did not tell Imani. He wrote to nobody at all, and there was nobody to write to.

What he did instead was sit down and work out what he was holding.

A stone every noble house in Randillia gave away by the handful as a joke. A stone the Circle had classed as defective for four centuries and stopped testing three centuries ago. A stone that did something nobody had ever thought to look for, because looking for it would have required pointing the instrument at the man instead.

His brother had handed him seven of them at a banquet to make a point about being unchanging.

He got the case out and put all seven on the desk in a row, with his mother's in front of them, and looked at the eight of them for a while by candle.

They were identical to any eye he had. Same dull grey. Same fine repeating lattice over every face. His mother's had gone soft at the edges from being carried and the other seven had not, and that was the entire visible difference between a stone that had opened a room and seven stones that had sat in a case since April.

He tried one of the seven that night, deliberately, holding it the way he held the other, and got nothing at all, and was not certain whether that meant the seven were dead or whether it meant fifteen years of handling was the price of admission and he had only ever paid it once.

He wrote both possibilities down and did not have a method for choosing between them.

He sat with that for quite a while.

Then he filed one more item, and it was the one that kept him up.

His mother had put the first one in his hands before he was seven. His memory of everything around that was a wall. And those two facts were now sitting next to each other on the same page for the first time in his life, and he could not make them do anything, and he could not make himself stop looking at them either.

He was not going to learn this alone. He knew that inside three days.

What he had was a town full of problems and a blacksmith who owed him nothing and had told him so.

So he went down to the forge with a different question than usual.

Bram, who had been fed nothing but latrines and wells and the price of ash for two months, heard about forty seconds of it and put the hammer down.

"Come to the house."

"It's not a house question."

"My daughter's been asking what you actually talk about." He went back to the fire. "I've been guessing for two months and I'm tired of it. Come Thursday."
