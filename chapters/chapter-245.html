Chapter 245 - What Ward Never Said

Fletcher took her up into the westerlies an hour out from the island, because they ran strong that week, high up, three miles over the sea, and a ship that rode them came home in four hours instead of five.

It was cold up there, even sealed, even with the heater humming under the deck. The sun was well up by then and lay along the top of the cloud below them like light on a field of snow, and the sky over the cloud was the deep hard blue it only ever was at that height, and nothing moved in it but the ship. Everybody aboard was asleep but Fletcher at the stick and Emeric at the chart table with his engine, and Ward at the after port.

Ward had not slept. Ward did not sleep in ships. He stood at the after port with his hands behind his back and watched the cloud go by under them, as he had stood at the island's going in the dawn, and at the gully's lip through two patrols, and at every door Emeric had ever slept behind for nine years.

Emeric put his pencil down after the third sheet and went aft.

He had not planned to. He had been drawing the injector, the small hard nozzle at the top of the cylinder that would squirt the oil into air hot enough to light it; and the pencil had stopped on the paper of its own accord, and he had sat looking at the half-drawn nozzle and found that he was not thinking about it at all. He was thinking about the investiture. About a heavy man with a square grey beard coming up the dais steps in May among the peers to bend his knee to the crown's will, as his own sheet had said he must; and looking at Ward, who stood a pace behind Emeric's left shoulder in plain black; and something going through that man's note, quick and hot and old, and put down again so fast that only a man with his sense open would have felt it go. And Aldane looking away first.

He had thought at the time it was a duke's contempt for a guard. He had known even then that it was not, and had put it down, because there had been a hall full of people and a crown's will to bend to and a dinner after; and then there had been the war's end and the winter and the Band, and he had not picked it up again.

He had not picked up a great many things. He knew that about himself.

He stood beside Ward at the after port and looked at the cloud.

"What happened," he said, "between my mother and Aldane?"



Ward did not move.

He did not turn his head, or take his hands from behind his back, or change his face; but Emeric had his sense open, as he always had it open, and Ward's note, which was the steadiest thing he had ever felt in a living man, went still. Not quiet. Still, as a pond goes still in the instant before a stone hits it.

"You have not asked that," said Ward. "In nine years."

"No."

"In twenty-five."

"I'm asking now."

Ward looked at the cloud for some time. Then he looked forward, down the length of the hold, at Fletcher's back at the stick and the sleepers along the bulkheads, Linus and Paskin and Fennick and his two men; and then back at the cloud.

"She bound me," he said. "While she lived. And after, I bound myself, because she had asked it." He was quiet. "She is dead twenty-five years. And you have asked." He took one hand from behind his back and put it flat on the cold rim of the port. "I will tell you all of it. You will not like it. I have not liked it for twenty-five years."



"You were five," said Ward. "Six. In the summer. She taught you herself, in the mornings, in the study on the family stair, the one with the window on the lime walk. At your slate. Your letters, and your numbers, and the runes off the lamps." He looked at the cloud. "She did not wear the crown for it. She did not wear anything a queen wears. She wore a plain grey gown she had brought with her from wherever she came from, and her hair down her back in a plait like a farm wife's, and she sat on the floor with you. The household thought it was strange. The household thought everything she did was strange."

"Where were you?"

"At the end of the passage. Where I always was. Armand was in the study, at the table, with his books; he had you in the afternoons for the runes, and he came early in those days to sit in on her mornings, because he said she taught better than the tower did." Ward's hand did not move on the port's rim. "That morning she came out of the study to fetch a book from her rooms. She left the door open a hand's width, so she could hear you. She went along the passage toward the stair. And Aldane came up it."

"Why was he there?"

"His sister was queen. The first queen. He had the right of the family wing; he had come to see her, or the boy, Corbin. He rarely came to court. He had seen your mother crowned, from the back of the hall, and not since." Ward's voice did not change at all. "He saw a woman in a plain grey gown with her hair in a plait coming along the family passage toward him with no one with her. He took her for a tutor. A nurse. Somebody's woman. Something beneath him." He was quiet. "He put his hand on her. And then the other. And he pushed her against the wall at the top of the stair and said what he meant to do."

The cloud went by under the ship.

Emeric did not say anything. He found that he could not. He stood at the port and looked at the white floor of the cloud with his hands at his sides, and his own note, which he could feel as clearly as he could feel Ward's, had gone somewhere very cold and very far down, and was waiting.

"I was at the end of the passage," said Ward. "Thirty paces. I had my sword out before he had said the second word. I ran." He stopped. "I was too slow."

"Too slow."

"She had finished before I was halfway." Ward turned his head, at last, and looked at him. "She did not cast. She did not raise her hand. There was no glyph, no light, nothing anyone in that palace could have felt or seen. She took his wrist off her, like this," and Ward's hand came off the port's rim and turned in the air, a short hard twist that made Emeric's own wrist ache to watch it, "and went under his arm, and put her shoulder into him, and he went over her hip onto the floor of the passage on his back, so hard I heard the breath go out of him from fifteen paces. And she went down with him. She had his arm across her knee before he had stopped sliding. Straight. Locked at the elbow, the wrong way. One more ounce and it would have broken." Ward put his hand back on the port. "She held him there. On his back, on the floor, with his arm across her knee and her other hand on his throat. Not squeezing. Resting. So he would know where it was."

"And you."

"I stood over them with my sword at his eye." Ward's voice was quite flat. "I would have killed him. I meant to. I had the point at his eye and I meant to put it in, and I looked at her, for leave, because she was the queen and it was hers to give." He was quiet. "She shook her head."

"And Armand?"

"In the study door. He had heard, and come out. He stood in the door with his hand on the frame." Ward looked at the cloud. "He did not move. I asked him after why he did not move. He was a High mage; he could have taken the duke apart where he lay. He said he did not move because he could see she did not need him. And because if he had moved, he would have cast, and she had not; and she had not for a reason, and he did not know the reason, and he would not undo it."

"What reason?"

"She never cast in the palace," said Ward. "Never once, in all the years. Not a light. Not a warm hand. I never knew why. I asked her, once. She said a palace was full of people listening." He shook his head, very slightly. "That is all she ever said."

Emeric stood at the port.

He did not remember any of it. He knew that before he looked for it, and he looked anyway, the way a man puts his hand into a pocket he knows is empty: the study on the family stair, the window on the lime walk, a slate, a woman in grey on the floor beside him, a door left a hand's width open. There was nothing. There was the wall, where there had always been the wall, smooth and blank and high, and beyond it the dark; and somewhere on the far side of it, a boy at a slate, alone, who had heard a man fall in the passage outside and had not got up to look, or had, and had forgotten.

"Where was I?" he said.

"In the study. At your slate. The door a hand's width open." Ward did not look at him. "You did not come out. I looked, after. You were sitting at your slate with your chalk, doing your sums. You looked up at me and asked if it was time for bread and butter." He was quiet. "You did not see it. I am almost sure you did not see it."

"Almost."

"Almost," said Ward.



The ship hummed under them. Forward, Paskin snored.

"She let him up," said Ward. "When he had stopped struggling. She let go of his arm and took her hand off his throat and stood up, and smoothed her gown, and said to him, quite quietly, in the voice she used to you over your slate, that she was the queen, and that he would go back down the stair now, and that she would not speak of it if he did not. And he got up off the floor and looked at her, and at me, with my sword still at his face. And he went." Ward breathed out through his nose. "I have seen men beaten in the yard, and on the field, and in the street, many times. I have never seen a man's face do what his did on that stair. He had been put on the floor by a woman, in front of two men, in his sister's house, and been wrong about who she was while he lay there. That is not a thing a man like that forgets. It is a thing he eats every day after, for the rest of his life, and it goes bad in him."

"She never told my father."

"Never." Ward shook his head. "I asked her to. That night. I said the king should know. She said, and what would the king do. Charge the first queen's brother? In open court? Set his own first wife's house against him, and half the north with it, over a thing that was finished, that she had finished herself, on the stair, with no harm done but to the duke's pride?" Ward looked at the cloud. "And a court case puts a queen in front of every eye in the kingdom. Who she is. Where she came from. What she is. She did not want any eye in the kingdom on where she came from. She never did. She said the matter was settled, and she would not have it unsettled, and she asked me to say nothing, and Armand to say nothing, while she lived." He was quiet. "So we said nothing."

"While she lived."

"While she lived." Ward's hand tightened on the port's rim, the first thing it had done. "She lived another year. A little more. Then the carriage."

Emeric did not say anything.

"After the carriage," said Ward, "I told your father. All of it. I stood in his room with the blood not dry on my coat and I told him what had happened on the stair, a year before, and who it was, and that I thought he had done it. That he had waited his year, and paid men, and done it." His voice did not rise. "Your father heard me out. Then he did what a king does. He looked at Aldane first. Before anyone. Harder than anyone. He had his own men in Aldane's house for a season, and in his steward's books, and in his stables." Ward shook his head. "And there was nothing. And he said to me what he said to the court after, when the court asked: that no duke in the north could have done what was done in that channel. Twenty of the best knights in the kingdom, and a queen who was a mage, gone in an afternoon, with nothing left of whoever did it but the dead. Not a man. Not a house." Ward was silent a while. "He was sure."

"And you?"

"I have not been sure of anything about it for twenty-five years," said Ward. "I have suspected Aldane every day of them. Every day. I suspected him at your father's table, at the banquet, when he gave you a box of wooden soldiers and laughed. I suspected him on the dais in May, when he came up the steps and saw me behind you, and looked away first." He turned his head and looked at Emeric, full, with his flat grey eyes. "I do not know that he did it. Your father does not believe it. I do not know what else to believe. A man who would do what he did on that stair, and hate as he hated after, over a thing he had done himself." He looked away. "I have never been able to stop."

The cloud went by under them, white, unbroken, lit along its top by the sun.

"She could put a man on the floor," said Emeric.

He had not meant to say it. It was the only thing in his head that was not cold.

Ward looked at him for a long breath.

"Her parents raised her running," he said. "From one place to another. All her childhood. She told me that much and no more. And they taught her to look after herself without a stone or a glyph, because a stone could be taken and a glyph could be seen." He looked back at the port. "She could put any man I ever saw on the floor. She put me on it twice in the first month, in the yard, in front of the guard, to see what I would do." Something happened at the corner of his mouth that Emeric had seen perhaps a dozen times in nine years. "I got up. She said that was why she had kept me."



Fletcher brought her down over the lake at a little before ten, out of the high cold into the grey February morning, and into the reeds at the secret dock; and Emeric went up the hill with oil under his nails and three sheets of an engine in his coat, and Ward at his shoulder, and neither of them said anything all the way to the keep.

He went looking for them before he had washed.

He did not know he was going to until he was on the stair. He had meant to go to his room and bathe and sleep the four days off, and he found that his feet had gone past his own door and on up the east stair to the counting room, where the door was open and the lamp was lit at ten in the morning against the grey; and Ferra was at her table with the household book open in front of her, and the ship's gauge sheet beside it, which Fletcher had sent up ahead by a runner, and a new page ruled at the top in her square capitals.

ISLE. OIL.

She was entering the five hundredweight. He could see her doing it from the door: the gauge's figure, and the date, and against them the figure she had written in the ring a week ago, so that the one could be checked against the other. Her pencil moving. The line between her brows. The lamp on her hair.

He came round the table and put his arms round her from behind, over the back of the chair, and put his face in the side of her neck, under her ear, and breathed.

Ferra's pencil stopped.

"You smell like a tar pit," she said.

"I've been in one."

"I know where you've been. I wrote it down." She did not move her head. "You're back a quarter of an hour inside your time. That's good." Her pencil moved again, very slightly. "Five hundredweight and a little over. That's inside my ring. That's good too."

"Come to bed."

"It's ten in the morning."

"Come to bed anyway."

"Work first," said Ferra. She said it to the page; she said it the way she said a total. Then she put her free hand up over her shoulder, without looking, and found his face, and held it against her neck where it was, her palm along his jaw. "Then fun. I've two columns to finish and a runner from the dock to answer and a page for the drum to rule, and you've a bath to take before you put anything of yours on anything of mine. Go and take it."

He did not go. He stood behind her chair with his face in her neck and her hand on his jaw and breathed her, ink and tallow and soap and her, and she let him, and went on writing with the other hand, and finished the first column.

"Something's happened," she said, without looking up.

"Yes."

"Not the oil."

"Not the oil."

Ferra's pencil stopped again. She did not ask. She sat still, with her hand on his face, and waited, as she waited for a column to come right; and when it did not come, and he did not say anything, she turned her head a very little and put her lips against his temple, once, and turned back to her book.

"Bath," she said. "Then tell me. Then fun. In that order, or I'll lose my place."



He took the bath. He did not tell her then, because he went to his room after it to put on a clean shirt and lay down on the bed for a breath with the shirt in his hand and woke four hours later with the light gone grey in the shutter.

He went down to the long room in the clean shirt and sat at the far end of the table with the engine's sheets, because he could not think of anything else to do with his hands, and began on the injector again.

Imani found him there at four.

She came in from the ward with her coat still on and snow on its shoulders, a thin wet snow that had started at noon, and the stethoscope round her neck, which she had worn every day since the night in the dispensary as if it had grown there; and she saw him at the end of the table, and the sheets in front of him, and his face, and stopped in the door.

Then she came down the table and took the pencil out of his hand.

"No," he said.

"Yes." She put the pencil down on the far side of the table, out of his reach, and sat down on the bench beside him, close, her hip against his, her coat still wet. "You've been back six hours and you've slept four and you've been drawing the same small bit of something for an hour. Ferra says something's happened and it isn't the oil." She took his wrist, two fingers inside it below the band. "So stop."

"I have to finish this."

"You don't."

"Work first," said Emeric.

It came out of him before he could stop it, in Ferra's voice, as Ferra had said it to him that morning at her table; and Imani looked at him, with her fingers on his wrist, and her mouth did the thing at the corner.

"Then fun," she said. "That's how it goes. I've heard it from both of you now. Somebody always says it to somebody." She did not let go of his wrist. "Say the rest of it."

"Then fun."

"Good," said Imani. "You've said it. You've done the work: it's a nozzle, it's drawn, I can see it from here, it's a very nice nozzle." She stood up and pulled him up off the bench by his wrist, which she did not have to do, and which she did anyway, because she could. "Now come and have the fun, and tell me and Ferra whatever it is, and eat something, and stop drawing nozzles at people."

He let her pull him. He let her pull him all the way up the east stair to the counting room, where Ferra had finished her columns and answered her runner and ruled her page for the drum, and was waiting, with the lamp lit and the door open; and Imani put him in Ferra's chair, and sat on the table, and Ferra sat on the arm of the chair, and the two of them made him tell it.

He told it. All of it. The stair, and the passage, and the woman in grey, and the twist of the wrist that Ward had shown him in the air, and the duke on his back on the floor with his arm across her knee. The door a hand's width open. Bread and butter. And the year after, and the carriage, and Ward in his father's room with the blood not dry on his coat.

When he had finished Ferra had her arm round his shoulders and Imani had both his hands in hers, and neither of them said anything for some time.

"She put him on the floor," said Imani at last.

"On his back. With his arm the wrong way across her knee."

Imani looked at his hands in hers.

"Good," she said, quite quietly, with great satisfaction, and with no softness in it at all; and Ferra, on the arm of the chair, said nothing, and her arm tightened round his shoulders; and he found, sitting between them in the lamplight, that he was laughing. Not much. A breath of it. The first thing that had come up through the cold since the cloud.



Armand confirmed it the next morning, in two lines, without being asked twice.

Emeric went up to his room after breakfast and sat on the stool by his fire, and said only, "Ward told me about the stair," and Armand, in his chair with the blue book on his knees, looked at him over his spectacles for a long while and then took them off and rubbed his eyes.

"She put him on the floor," he said. "I stood in the door and did not move, because I could see she did not need me, and because she had not cast, and I would not be the one who did." He put his spectacles back on. "I have been sorry every day since that I did not break his arm for her anyway. I think she knew. She brought me a cup of something hot that night and sat with me while I drank it, which she did not do for anyone." He looked at the fire. "She was the most frightening woman I ever taught. And I taught her very little."

That was all he said, and it was enough.

Emeric sat on the stool for a while longer, in the warmth of the old man's fire, not talking; and then went down to the yard, where Ward was drilling the new class in the thin snow, and stood at the edge of it and watched.

Ward did not look round. He took Arthur's blade off him with a short hard twist of the wrist that made the boy's hand open on its own, and gave it back, and said, "Again." Arthur came in again. Ward took the blade again. It was the same twist. Emeric had seen Ward do it in the yard a thousand times in nine years and had never once thought to ask where Ward had learned it.

He did not ask now.



The stewards came down for Ilsa's quarterly sitting on the tenth, on the morning train from Grayrock, Bryda with her daughter, and Paxton from Milford on the grain train, and Aster, who had come the long way from Wayford by the posting road to the railhead and down the line, a day and a half of it, as he came every quarter, without complaint.

They met at Ferra's table, as they had every quarter since Ilsa first came down to keep Grayrock's books under Ferra's hand: the year's reckoning on Ferra's forms, the holdings' figures set beside Kaldo's and checked against them, the levies and the payrolls and the grain and the ore. It was the first full sitting since the settlement. It was Ferra's sitting, and Ferra's room, and her table, with the stewards along one side of it and her book open at the head. Saban sat at the end. Ilsa sat at the side table under the window with Grayrock's books, where she always sat, with her pen and her ink and her back very straight.

Ward was not in the room. Declan had the door.

Emeric had told Ward that morning in the yard, plainly, that he would not need him at the sitting; and Ward had looked at him for some time and then said, "No," and gone back to the new class, and had not asked why. Emeric did not know how to have told him why. He could not have sat at that table with Ward at the door behind him, and Bryda Renner across it, and said what he meant to say; not that week.

So he came in alone, and sat down at the foot of Ferra's table, opposite her, and listened to the figures.

It took the morning. It always took the morning. Grayrock's ore was up by a fifth on the year. Milford's grain had fed four baronies through the war and the smallpox winter and still filled the reserve granaries to Ferra's rule. Wayford's posting trade had doubled since the line reached the railhead, and its barns were full. Every payroll in every holding had gone out on its day, every quarter, for five years. Ilsa read Grayrock's figures out from the side table in her clear dry voice, and Ferra checked them against her own, and they agreed, to the copper.

Then, a little after noon, with the last figure agreed and the book about to close, Bryda Renner put her hand flat on the table.



"I'll say something," said Bryda. "Before we eat. And then I'll eat, and we can all pretend I didn't."

Ferra did not close her book.

"We came down on the train," said Bryda. "Ilsa and I. Down the line, into the new station at the foot of the town, and up through it. On foot, because Ilsa wanted to see the trams' rails they're laying in the high street, and I wanted to see what my daughter wanted to see." She looked at Emeric down the length of the table. "Lamps on every corner. In the lanes, too, the little ones, not only the high street. The lamp-walker going round at dusk with his pole. Taps. In the terraces. Water in every kitchen; I saw a woman fill a kettle at her own sink with the door open on the lane. The bath house. The hospital, with its windows lit. A school with a bell." She did not raise her voice. "Grayrock digs your ore. Has dug it for nine years. Every hundredweight of iron in every pipe in this town came up out of my hill on my men's backs. And Grayrock has its rows."

Nobody said anything.

"Three of them," said Bryda. "Back to back. One pump. Twelve to a cottage. You know them; you had the smallpox through them last winter. Eight days late with the needles, they were, because the rows were last. Because the rows are always last." She took her hand off the table. "And now your wages are emptying them. Not the rows. The young. The young men go down the line to Kaldo, where there's a tap in the kitchen and a lamp in the lane and a wage on a Friday, and they don't come back up it, and I don't blame them. I'd not come back up it." She looked at him. "Grayrock digs your ore and gets nothing back. That's what I wanted to say. Now I'll eat."

She did not eat. Nobody moved to eat.

Paxton cleared his throat.

He was a big slow man, Paxton, who had been a baron once and was a steward now and had found, he had told Ferra once over a quarter's figures, that he liked it better; and he did not like to speak at tables. He spoke now, looking at his own big hands.

"Milford feeds four baronies," he said. "The grain comes down the line every week. The beef. The cheese. Your Maren's ducks." He turned his hands over. "And the young people go down with it. On the grain trains. Sitting on the sacks. You see them at Milford halt with their bundles on a Monday, waiting for the wagons." He looked up. "We've the new pump houses. We've the line. We've the granaries to your rule; we had them in the war, when Wayford had nothing. That's not nothing." He looked down again. "It's not a tap in a kitchen, either."

Aster said nothing for a while. He sat very straight in his mended brown coat at the end of the stewards' side, as he had sat at the posting house in Wayford five years ago with his hat in his hands, and when he spoke he spoke carefully, as a clerk speaks who has kept another man's books for fifteen years and has learned to say only what he can show.

"Wayford's on the capital road," he said. "Everything from the south that goes north goes through Wayford. And everything from the north that comes to see what Kaldo's doing comes through Wayford, too, first. They stop at our posting house. They eat our bread. They look at our street, which is mud to the axle from November to March, and our lamps, which are tallow, and our water, which comes out of a standpipe at the corner, which is more than it was and less than yours." He folded his hands. "Then they go on south, and come back a month later, and they stop at the posting house again, and they ask me. Every quarter, somebody asks me. If Kaldo's so rich, why's Wayford still like this? What's he doing down there that he doesn't want the road to see?" He looked at Emeric. "I don't have an answer for them. I'd like one."



Emeric did not answer at once.

He sat at the foot of Ferra's table and looked at the three of them along its side, and at Ilsa at the side table with her pen still and her face very carefully not doing anything, and at Saban at the end, who was looking at the ceiling; and at Ferra, at the head, with her book open, not writing, waiting.

"You're right," he said.

Bryda's eyebrows went up.

"All three of you. You're right." He put his hands flat on the table. "I'll tell you why it happened, and then I'll tell you what I'm going to do, and when, and then I'll tell you something else you'll not like, and you can decide what you think of me after."

Nobody said anything.

"You did your jobs," he said. "That's why. Every quarter, for five years, the figures came down the line on time and they agreed to the copper. The ore came down. The grain came down. The payrolls went out on the day. Nobody wrote to me to complain. Nobody came down to shout. So I never had to sit with you." He looked at Bryda. "I gave Wayford standpipes and Milford pump houses and all three of you the line and the granaries, and I thought that was a great deal, and I looked at your figures every quarter and they were good, and I took the figures for the place. And they weren't the place. The figures were you doing your work. The place was the rows." He looked down the table. "If the money comes in every week, the man who owns the business never has to go and look at it. He never learns it's bleeding. That's what I did."

"That's not all of it," said Saban, from the end, mildly.

"No. The rest of it's true, but it's not an excuse." He did not look at Saban. "Everything Kaldo knows, it knows in a few heads. Mine. Ferra's. Bram's. Imani's. Ward's. Saban's. Armand's. A school full of children who are learning it but don't have it yet. Every one of those heads has been teaching, all day, every day, for nine years. There was nobody left over to send up the line to Grayrock to put a tap in a kitchen." He turned his hands over. "So it stayed here. And I let it, because it was easier, and because some of what Kaldo has can't leave the walls, and I let that be a reason for none of it leaving. Saban let me." He looked at Saban now. "I'm not blaming him. I'm saying who was in the room."

Saban inclined his head, very slightly, and went back to the ceiling.

Ferra turned back.

She did not have to look for it. She took the leaves of the book between her finger and thumb and turned them back by the handful, through the war and the smallpox and the Wayford mail, to a harvest three and a half years gone, and put her finger on a line, and read it out in the voice she read every line in.

"The twenty-ninth of September, year six. Supper in the long room. Grayrock asks a mason who knows drains, and a nurse. Wayford asks a teacher." She looked up. "Deferred."

Aster looked at the table. Paxton looked at Bryda. Bryda did not look at anybody; she had the face of a woman who had been right for three and a half years and had not enjoyed a day of it.

"A year or two," said Bryda.

"That's mine," said Emeric. "I said it." He did not look away from her. "It was true that autumn. Every child off Sela's bench was a year into his trade and there was nobody up the line to finish teaching him. It was true the year after." He made himself count it, out loud, because she deserved the count. "Six Commencements. Bram's floor has more of Sela's children on it now than men who learned their trade anywhere else. Imani has nurses who've trained nurses. Ferra's clerks run the gate tower without her. It stopped being true two years ago, before the war, and I never turned back to the page." He looked at Ferra's finger, still on the line. "She wrote it down so somebody would. It should have been me."

Ferra took her finger off the page and left the book open there.

"So," said Emeric. "What I'll do."

He told them. He told it the way he told Ferra anything, with dates, because they were figures people and a promise without a date was a figure without a column.

Water first, because water was what killed people, and because it was the thing Kaldo already knew how to build and could teach in a season. Filter beds and a tower and pipes in every holding's main street by the autumn, and in Grayrock's rows before that, by midsummer, because the rows were last and would not be last this time; Bram's men to lay the first of it and the holdings' own men beside them, learning to lay the rest.

Trainers, both ways. Kaldo's people sent up the line: not the six, who could not go, but the ones the six had made. Every lead at the table to name a team out of the Commencement years, by the end of February, to go up for a year at a time and work beside the holdings' own: Bram's masons and pipe men for the rows and the water, Tess's line men for the powerhouse when it came, Imani's nurses for a clinic in every holding, Ferra's clerks to sit with Ilsa, two of Ward's yard for each holding's watch, and a teacher from Sela's room for Wayford, three and a half years late. And the holdings' people brought down. Every steward to make a list, by the end of February, of anybody they could spare who could learn: masons and smiths and carpenters and clerks and nurses and teachers and men to run a pump house. Paid for their term, housed in the new terraces, put into Bram's shops and Imani's ward and Ferra's counting room and Sela's school beside Kaldo's own, a season for a trade and a year for a teacher or a nurse or a clerk; and then sent home to teach the next ones, who would teach the ones after. The first of them down by April.

"And Ilsa," he said, and looked at the side table. "If she'll do it. Grayrock's clerks first. She's kept Grayrock's books under Ferra's hand for five years; there's nobody alive outside this room who knows Ferra's methods better. Grayrock should have a school for its clerks, and she should run it."

Ilsa's pen, at the side table, went down on the blotter, very carefully.

Bryda turned in her chair and looked at her daughter, and her daughter did not look back, and Bryda turned round again with her face doing something complicated.

"Power next," said Emeric. "Grayrock first, because Grayrock has the coal and the hill and the need: a powerhouse at the pithead, by the summer, to light the pit and the rows and the street. Milford and Wayford after, by the line, as it comes." He looked at Aster. "And the line comes to Wayford this year. The whole way. No posting road from the railhead. When it does, Wayford stops being where the capital road looks at Kaldo's mud. It starts being where it looks at Kaldo." He put his hands flat again. "That's the plan. Ferra has the dates. You'll each have a copy before you go home."



"And the thing we'll not like," said Bryda.

"Yes."

He had thought about how to say it all the way down from the westerlies. He had thought about it in the bath and on the bed with his shirt in his hand and on the stool by Armand's fire, and in the end there was no way to say it but to say it.

"There's a great deal I can't tell you," he said. "About Kaldo. About what's here, and how it's done, and what it's for. Some of it for safety. Most of it because I don't trust you with it." He held Bryda's eyes. "Not yet. I don't know if ever."

The room was very quiet.

"I'll tell you why," he said. "Because you deserve that much. I killed your husband, Bryda. At my South Gate, at dawn, nearly eight years ago, with his men coming at my town. Aster, your father hired men to kill me before I'd ever seen Kaldo, and died for it. Paxton, your barony was given to me by a court; you had no say in it at all, and nor did I." He looked along the three of them. "And I like you. All three of you. I've liked you for years. You do the work. Every one of you does the work, every quarter, to the copper, and none of you has ever shown me a moment's trouble."

He stopped. Then he said the rest of it.

"That's what I can't get past. Three people with every reason in the world to wish me under the ground, and not one of you has ever moved against me. Not once. Not even last year, with Aldane's host on the stream ground and every lord in the north waiting to see which way the holdings would go." He shook his head. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry I think it. I've thought it for years and I've never said it, and I'm saying it now because it's the first honest thing I've said to you in five years and I'd rather say it to your faces than have you work it out. It's suspicious. Nobody is that good."



Nobody said anything for so long that Saban took his eyes off the ceiling.

Then Bryda laughed.

It was short and hard and not unkind, and it went up into the counting room's beams, and she put her hand over her eyes for a breath while it went, and took it away.

"Nobody's that good," she said. "No. You're right about that too." She looked at him across the table with her hard grey eyes. "I have a daughter," she said.

She did not look at the side table. She did not have to. Ilsa, at the side table, sat perfectly still.

"I have a daughter, and my husband was in the wrong. I knew he was in the wrong when he did it. I knew it when he hid the shaft, and when he let the duke help him hide it, and when he took his men down the road to your gate. I told him so. He went anyway." She folded her hands on the table. "You killed him at your gate, doing what he'd gone there to do. If you'd been the one in the wrong I'd have hated you till I died. You weren't. So I don't." She was quiet. Then, flatter, almost dry: "And you pay on time."

Something went through the table like a breath.

"Every quarter," said Bryda. "To the copper. You said so yourself, just now, as if it were our credit. It's yours. My husband never paid anybody on time in his life." She looked at him. "That's my reason. Make what you like of it."

Paxton had his big hands round each other on the table, turning one in the other, slowly.

"I had no say," he said. "You're right. The court gave Milford to you because I went with Grayrock against your town, and they were right to, and I knew it at the time. So I thought, well. Better the lord I know than one I don't. You'd sat at my table. You'd bought my grain at my price. You'd not hanged me when you could have." He looked at his hands. "And I've done better as your steward than I ever did as a baron. Milford's in profit. The herds are up. The new fields are in. I eat better now than I did when the barony was mine." His face went slowly red, from the collar up. "I'm a bit ashamed of that. That a man should be better off for losing his title. But it's true, and you asked, and it's my reason."

Aster did not answer at once. When he did he looked at the table, not at Emeric.

"My father tried to have you killed," he said. "On the road. Before you'd done anything to anybody. Because you'd find his books." He was quiet. "You never held it against me. Not once. You took my books at the posting house, all fifteen years of them, and read them, and gave me Wayford to keep. You've never mentioned him to me." He looked up, at last. "That's why. I keep waiting to be punished. I've been waiting five years. I think I'd feel better if you did." He looked down again. "You never will. So I do the work. To the copper. Every quarter. Because somebody in my family should."

Nobody said anything after that.

Emeric sat at the foot of Ferra's table and looked at the three of them, and found that he had nothing to say that was better than what they had said, and so did not say it. He looked at Ferra instead, at the head of the table, and Ferra looked back at him, and her face did not change at all; and she picked up her pencil.

"Then you'll meet," said Ferra.

The stewards looked at her.

"The three of you. With my office. Here, every quarter, at this table, on the day after the reckoning. Not the figures. The figures we do already. The rest." She wrote it as she said it. "What your holdings need, and haven't got, and when. What the trainees are doing, and whether they're any good. Whether the water's in by midsummer, and if it isn't, why not. Whether the rows are still last." She looked up. "If you've something to say, you'll say it there, every quarter, to his face, and not save it up for five years and say it at a reckoning before lunch."

"A council," said Saban, from the end.

"The Stewards' Council," said Ferra, and wrote that too, at the top of a fresh page, in her square capitals, with the date under it and her initial; and turned the book round so that all three of them could see it, and Ilsa, who had risen from the side table without being asked and come to look over her mother's shoulder.

"Now we'll eat," said Bryda.



Saban caught him on the stair afterward, going down, with the stewards gone ahead to the long room and Maren's soup.

"You told three people you don't trust," said Saban, "that you don't trust them."

"Yes."

"To their faces. At a table. Before lunch." Saban shook his head slowly, with something that in another man would have been admiration and in Saban was very nearly the same thing. "I've seen a great many things done at tables. I've never seen that done on purpose."

"Better they hear it from me than work it out."

"Oh, they'd worked it out," said Saban. "Years ago. Bryda the first week." He went on down a step, and stopped, and looked back up. "What they hadn't worked out was that you'd say so. That's the part that'll keep them." He went on down. "Pity. I'd have charged for it."

Ferra came down the stair behind him a quarter of an hour later with her book under her arm and went past him on the landing without a word; and that night, when he went up to the counting room, the book was open on her table at the household page for the year, and under the heading for works, in her small square hand, there was a new line. WATER, THE HOLDINGS. And against it a figure, large, in the column for the spring; and against the figure, nothing at all. No query. No ring. No note.

He looked at the line for some time.

It was the first figure that size she had ever entered for him without a word in its margin, and he knew exactly what it meant.
