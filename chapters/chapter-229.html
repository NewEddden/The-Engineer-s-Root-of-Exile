Chapter 229 - Split Days

"Then don't let it pool," said Emeric.

He said it in Ward's yard on the first of July, at seven in the morning, to Ward and Rylan and Imani, standing by the rail with a sheet of paper in his hand that he had written at four, because he had woken at three with it and not been able to lie still.

Ward looked at the paper and did not take it. Rylan took it. Imani took it from Rylan.

"Armand's result says rate kills," said Emeric. "It does. Fill a body faster than it can grow and it tears. But the room fills a man who's lying still on a stone floor in the dark, with nothing to do with what's going into him but hold it. It pools. It has nowhere to go." He put his hand on the rail. "A man who takes it in hard while he's spending it hard isn't a jar. He's a pipe. It goes in and it goes out. He's stretched, not torn. And whatever's stretched and not torn grows."

"Like the bar," said Rylan.

"Like the bar. Like a smith's arm. You don't work the same arm every day. You work the arms one day and the legs the next, and the arms grow on the day you're working the legs." He looked at the paper in Imani's hand. "So it's two days. Not one. A filling day and a spending day, together. Fill them up past what they'd carry sitting down, and then make them spend it, the same hour, until they're back to level. And then rest. And the next week, a little more."

Imani was reading. She read the way she read a dosing card, all the way down, and then back to the top.

"Fill them with what?"

"Mana food."

"There's no mana food in Kaldo. There's never been any to spare." She did not look up. "You'd want something a body can't overeat by accident. Something you can weigh."

"I'll get you something you can weigh."

"And spend it on what?"

"Stones. Spent ones. The works throws out forty a week that have gone flat and need Orrin or Vesk to wake them. Lay them out on trestles. They put their hands on them and push until a gauge says the stone's full or they're empty, whichever's first. I'll code the gauge." He looked at Ward. "The works gets its stones back full. The class gets stretched. Nobody's wasted anything."

Ward looked at him for a long breath with no expression.

"Never alone," said Ward.

"Never alone."

"And she stops it." Ward looked at Imani. "Not you. Not Rylan. Not me. Her."

Imani put the paper in her pocket.

"First sign of blood at the nose, they stop eating and go to the stones," she said. "Second sign, they sit out the day, and I'll not argue about it with anybody's pride. A headache that won't lift by the stones, they go home. Nobody under sixteen eats more than I've weighed for them. And it's a week before the first of them, because I want every one of them read before they start, by me, and every week after." She looked at Emeric. "And I want the something you can weigh before I agree to any of it. I want to see it and hold it and eat a piece of it myself."

"You'll have it by Friday," said Emeric.



He took Armand up on the Thursday.

They went at ten at night, from the shed, with the doors open on the lake and Ward on the gangway, and Fletcher's men sent home, and nobody else in the yard; four of them aboard, because Ward would not hear of three, and Imani would not hear of Ward being one of the three instead of her, and in the end it was four. Emeric at the top helm. Imani and Armand in the saloon. Ward wherever Ward was, which was everywhere.

And in the hold, lashed to a ring bolt, because Imani had asked for it and Maren had packed it without asking anything: a crate of oatcakes, Maren's hard ones that kept for a month, forty of them, each one weighed on Ferra's letter scale before it went in and the weight written on it in pencil.

"If the Band gets into everything," Imani had said on the slip, "it'll get into those. And I can weigh those."

Armand came down to the shed on Ward's arm, in a fur coat that had been Wexler's and had hung in a press in the keep since the first year, with his stick in his other hand and his face perfectly composed; and at the foot of the gangway he stopped, and looked up at her, the whole length of her, dark against the lake, with the brass of the irises catching the slip lamp; and he did not say anything for a while.

"I watched them build her," he said at last. "From the gallery. Every night for a year." He looked at the gangway. "I never came aboard. I told myself it was the stairs."

"There are no stairs," said Ward. "It is a plank."

"Then I have no excuse," said Armand, and went up it.



They climbed slowly. Emeric took her out over the sea as he had taken her a month ago with the two women on the stair below him, and up through the cloud into the stars, and on up, and he did not hurry it; the sealed hull's pump ran the whole way and the breath candles sat in their racks at every seat, and Imani had Armand in a chair in the saloon with a blanket over his knees and her fingers on his wrist and her eyes on his face, and every half mile she sent it up the sealed stair to him, what she had, and every half mile it was the same.

"Out of step. Seventy, eighty. Dropping."

At three miles the cold came through the shell. The irises frosted at their rims. Imani put a second blanket over Armand. At three and a half the probe on its pole above the top cockpit, which had been a faint dull pearl in the dark since they left the cloud, began to brighten, very slightly, as if somebody far off had turned up a lamp.

At four it went bright all at once.

Emeric felt it come, as he had felt it the first time on the first voyage, as a swimmer feels it who comes up out of cold water into a warm current: the thin cold air outside the shell, which had been nearly empty, was full, suddenly, from one side of the sky to the other, thick, warm to his sense as a bath is warm, and pressing in through the riveted seams and the wood and the leather and the felt, and through his own skin, everywhere at once. Every stone aboard her opened its throat and drank. He could feel them drinking. His own head swam with it.

He set her at the notch that held her there, at the Band's foot, and went down the sealed stair to the saloon.

Armand was sitting up.

He had pushed the blankets off. He was sitting in the chair by the forward iris with his two hands on the brass rail that ran under the ports, and he was looking out, at the dark, at nothing, at the black above the Band where the stars were very hard and very many; and his face was the face of a man listening to music in another room.

Imani had her fingers at his wrist. She was not counting aloud. She looked up at Emeric when he came in, and her face had nothing on it at all, and then, slowly, it had something.

"Sixty," she said. "Sixty. Even."

Emeric looked at Armand's hands on the rail.

They were still. Both of them. Lying on the cold brass, old and brown-spotted and very thin, with the veins standing up on the backs of them, and quite still, as he had not seen them in a month and had not, he realised, seen them still in a good while before that without noticing that he had not. They did not move at all.

"It's quiet," said Armand. He did not look round. "The thing. Under the breastbone." He was quiet himself for a breath. "I had forgotten what quiet was. I had thought it was always like that, at my age, and that everybody had it and nobody spoke of it."

"May I look?" said Emeric.

"You may always look," said Armand. "You need not ask again."

"I'll ask every time."

Armand turned his head, then, and looked at him; and nodded.

So he put his hand on the old man's chest, and let himself in, slowly, at the old man's note, as he had on the floor of the long room; and it was easier, here, and harder at once: easier because the old man's body was full, lit all through by the Band from outside, every vessel and every branch of the lungs bright as a lamp, so that he barely had to sink anything into it to see; and harder because his own head was swimming with the Band too, and it would have been very easy, very pleasant, to go out into the old man and not come back. He held the edge of the iris rail in his other hand until his knuckles hurt, and came back to the hurt, and looked.

The top of the heart was beating. Not shivering: beating, cleanly, once and then once again, and the big chambers below were following it as they should, in step, in time, as if they had never done anything else.

And the dead place in the wall was still dead.

It lay there in the bright beating fist of light exactly as it had lain on the floor of the long room, the size of the end of his thumb, holding nothing, as a knot holds nothing in a plank; and round it the muscle was brighter than it had been, and moved more easily, working round it; but it was there. The Band had lit everything in the old man's chest and had not lit that. It had put the beat back. It had not touched the scar.

He drew back out, slowly, and took his hand away.

"In step," he said. "All of it. Not the old place. The old place is still dead."

"Will it mend?" said Imani.

"I don't think so. I don't think anything we've got up here mends that." He looked at Armand's hands, still on the rail. "But it's working round it. Better than it was."

Imani had her book open on her knee. "I've written that down twice now," she said, "as what he did. What do I call it?"

He thought about it. In his own head it had only a name from another world, which he was not going to say aloud in a saloon four miles up.

"A hollow view," he said. "It shows me the hollows in a man. The heart, the vessels, the lungs, anything with something running through it. The solid parts hardly at all."

Imani wrote it, and underlined it once.

Armand had not been listening. He was looking out of the iris at the dark, with his hands still on the rail.

They held her there an hour, at Imani's order, not a breath longer. At the end of the hour Imani's own nose had begun to ache, she said, and Ward's had not, and Emeric's head was a bell, and Armand looked exactly as he had looked at the start of it, still and quiet and listening; and Imani looked at the saloon clock and said, "Down," and Emeric went up the stair and took her down.

At three miles, going down, with the probe going dull on its pole and the Band falling away above them, Armand spoke for the first time since the iris.

"I should like to go back," he said.

He said it to nobody. He said it to the iris, quite quietly, in the teacher's voice, as a man states a fact he has just been given.

Imani looked at him across the saloon for a long while. Then she looked at his hands on the rail, which had begun, very slightly, as the Band fell away above them, to move; and she wrote something in her book, and did not show it to anybody.



The oatcakes came down different.

They looked the same. They smelled the same, of oats and Maren's lard. But Imani took one out of the crate on the slip at two in the morning by the lamp and held it in her palm, and Emeric, standing beside her, felt it there without trying, small and warm and bright, as he felt a charged stone a few paces off; and Imani, who had no faculty and could feel nothing of the kind, held it a while longer, and then broke a corner off it and put it in her mouth.

She ate the corner slowly. She stood on the slip in the lamplight with her eyes shut and chewed it. Then she opened her eyes.

"Full," she said. "Like a meal. From a corner." She looked at the rest of the oatcake in her hand. "It's not taste. It's being full. As if I'd eaten the whole crate." She touched her nose with the back of her wrist, and looked at the wrist, and there was nothing on it. "I'll weigh it. I'll weigh a corner and eat it every morning for a week and tell you what it does to me before it goes anywhere near a child."

"Imani..."

"Before it goes anywhere near a child," said Imani, and put the crate's lid back on, and wrote the date on it in pencil.

Armand walked up the keep stair the next morning without stopping.

Emeric saw it from the counting room door. All of it, from the bottom to the top, sixty steps, with his stick in one hand and the other hand on the rail and nowhere else, not inside his coat, not once; and at the top he stood on the landing and was not out of breath, and looked down the stair he had just come up as if somebody had moved it while he was not looking, and went in to his room and shut the door.

His hands were still for four days. On the fifth, at the long table, Emeric saw the right one move, very slightly, on the edge of a sheet. Armand saw him see it, and put it in his lap.

He went up again on the Tuesday after, and on the Saturday, and on the Tuesday after that; and by the middle of the month it had become a thing that happened, as the noon tape happened and the bread came up from Maren's at eight: on Tuesday and Saturday nights the ship went out over the sea with the lamps dark and four aboard, and came back before two, and in the morning Armand came up the keep stair without stopping. Imani went every time. She would not let anybody else call the time. Ferra opened a column for it in her book without being asked, at the back, under a heading in her small square capitals, A. ALOFT, with the date and the hour up and the hour down and the minutes in the Band; and Emeric saw her rule it, one evening, and did not say anything, and she did not either.



The first split day was the thirteenth of July.

Imani had eaten her corner every morning for nine days and written down what it did, which was nothing that a night's sleep did not undo, and a headache on the sixth day when she ate two corners on purpose, and a nosebleed on the eighth when she ate three. She had read every one of the sixty-one, herself, in the school hall, with Armand beside her because she had no faculty and he did; she had weighed out sixty-one portions on Ferra's letter scale, every one different, by the reading and the size of the body, and wrapped each in a twist of paper with the name on it; and she stood in the yard at seven in the morning beside the trestles with the twists in a basket, like a woman about to sell eggs.

The trestles ran the length of the yard, three rows of them, and on the trestles in rows lay the works' spent stones, sixty-one of them, grey and dull and flat as river pebbles, each one with a little brass cap screwed over its end and a needle on the cap, lying at the bottom of its dial; Emeric had sat up with the caps for three nights, coding the gauge stone in each one to read its own stone back to level and no further.

"You eat what's in your paper," said Imani. "All of it. Not more. Then you sit on the bench until I tell you. Then you go to your stone and you put your hands on it and you push, the way you pushed the rail, and you keep pushing until the needle's at the top or you've nothing left. If your nose bleeds, you tell me. If your head aches so you can't see, you tell me. If you feel sick, you tell me." She looked along them. "If you don't tell me, I'll know, and you'll go home. Ward said so."

Ward, at the well, did not say anything, which said so.

"Me first," said Rylan.

He had a twist of paper with his name on it, and it was bigger than any of the class's, because he was a knight and carried more; and he unwrapped it at the trestle's end, in front of all of them, and it was a quarter of one of Maren's oatcakes; and he ate it, in three bites, chewing slowly, looking at the sixty-one of them while he did it, the way a man eats something in front of a dog to show it is safe.

Then he sat down on the bench.

Emeric watched his face. He saw it come: the flush up from the collar, and the eyes going bright, and Rylan's breath going deeper, as a man's breath goes after a long run; and then a look of the most enormous surprise, quite naked, on the face of a man who did not show surprise, as the Band came up in him from inside.

"Oh," said Rylan, quite quietly. "Oh, that's a lot."

"Sit," said Imani.

He sat. For as long as it took Imani to count a hundred under her breath, he sat on the bench with his hands on his knees and the sweat coming out on his forehead and his eyes very bright, and the class watched him in total silence. Then Imani nodded, and he got up and went to the first stone on the first trestle and put his two hands flat on it, and pushed.

The needle went up the dial like a cat up a tree.

It went to the top in less time than it took to say it, and stayed there, and the stone under his hands went from grey to the faint warm shine of a charged stone; and Rylan took his hands off it and looked at them, and then at the barrow of spares the works had sent up at the row's end, and then at Imani.

"I've more," he said.

"Then do another. And another. Until you haven't."

He did five. Five spent stones from the barrow, one after another, laid on the end of the first trestle, the needles going up and up, and the class watching him go down the row with their twists of paper in their hands; and at the sixth he put his hands on it and pushed, and the needle went halfway, and stopped, and Rylan stood there with his hands on the stone and his head down, breathing like a man at the end of a race.

"Level," said Imani, with her fingers on his wrist. "That's level. Sit down."

He sat down. His face was wet. He looked at the five charged stones and the half one in the row along the trestle, and then at the class.

"Your turn," he said. "And you'll not do five. Not this year. Do one. Do one properly."

They ate. They sat. They went to the stones.

It took all morning. It was not a show; it was sixty-one young people in a yard, one at a time, sitting very still with a twist of paper in their laps and the flush coming up, and then standing at a trestle with their hands on a stone and their faces screwed up, pushing; and the needles going up, a little, mostly, and stopping. Imani went up and down the rows with her basket and her fingers and her book. Ottoline sat at her trestle with the cards and punched each figure as it came: how far the needle went, and how long, and what the healer said.

Arthur filled his stone and looked round for another one, and Imani said no, and he sat down. Mira Holt filled hers, very slowly, with her teeth set and her plait over her shoulder, and when the needle touched the top she kept her hands on the stone a breath longer as if she did not quite believe it, and then took them off and wiped them on her skirt. Bren Tallis filled his and looked across at Mira's, and saw it was full too, and did not look again.

Three of them bled. A Commencement girl first, a little, from one side, and she said so at once, as she had been told, and Imani put a cloth in her hand and sent her to the stones, and she pushed and the bleeding stopped. Then Kit Bastow, who had not said so, and Imani saw it from four trestles off and came down the row like a hawk, and he went home, and he went home laughing, because he had been told he would and that was what Kit did with things. Then a levy man, at the end, who said so, and was sent to the stones, and filled his to the top, and sat down with a bloody lip and a look of great satisfaction.

Noll Finch ate his twist of paper, which was the smallest in the basket, and sat on the bench for his count of a hundred, and went to his stone and put his hands on it and pushed; and the needle moved, a little; and moved; and stopped, about a third of the way up the dial. And stayed there. He pushed until his arms shook and his face went grey and Imani came and put her fingers on his wrist and said, "Level. Off," and he took his hands off.

A third. He looked at it.

"Three weeks of Maren's noon dinners," said Imani, writing in her book. "And that." She closed the book. "Next week you'll do a third and a hair. I'll see the hair. Go and sit in the shade."



The works got fifty-two stones back that afternoon in a barrow, a few of them full and most a third or a half, every one of them warm, with Ottoline's slip on top saying who had filled which; and Orrin, who had been filling the works' fire stones by hand every day for seven years and had come up from the works to collect them because he did not believe it, stood in the yard with his hands in the barrow, turning them over one at a time, holding each to his own sense, and said nothing whatever until he had held them all.

"Children," he said at last.

"Children and some levy men," said Emeric.

Orrin put the last stone back in the barrow. He was a hedge mage, grey at the temples, with ink on his fingers from the school where he still taught letters in the mornings, and stone dust in the creases of his hands from seven years at the works; and he looked at Emeric over the barrow with an expression Emeric could not read.

"I'll be glad of a morning," he said. "I'll not pretend I won't." He picked up the barrow's handles. "I'd like to know what I'm to do with it."

"Teach," said Emeric.

Orrin looked at him a while longer. Then he went off down the yard with the barrow toward the works, and Emeric watched him go, and could not tell whether he had made the man glad or taken something from him, and suspected both.

Ferra put the crate on the books that night.

She did it in the counting room after the lamps, with the barrow of stones already entered and Orrin's wage beside it, which was the same wage it had been yesterday and would be the same tomorrow and was, as of that afternoon, she said, a wage for teaching letters and not for filling stones, and she would need to know by the quarter day whether that was what the Holder wanted, because it was not what the Holder had written. Then she turned the page.

"The oatcakes," she said. "Forty went up. How many are left?"

"Twenty-nine. Imani's got them."

"Where?"

"In the dispensary. In the press with the poisons."

Ferra wrote it down. Then she sat with her pen over the page and did not write anything else for a breath, which was not a thing Ferra did.

"A corner fills a woman like a meal," she said. "She told me. Three corners, she bled." She looked up at him. "There are six hundred people on the plant floor and the still crew and the stone shed who've heard about the split day by now. Every one of them can add. A man who wants what Rylan's got, and has been told that a quarter of a biscuit got a girl from the west side up a wall, will want the whole biscuit." She put her pen down, square. "Who else knows where the crate is?"

"Imani. Me. Ward. You."

"Keep it that way. And lock the press. With a key, not a bar." She picked the pen up again. "I'll not have a column in this book for children who ate the Holder's biscuits because they wanted to be knights by Lantern Night." She wrote something small at the bottom of the page and did not show him. "Not this year."

He came round the table and stood behind her chair and put his hands on her shoulders, which were tight as wire, and worked his thumbs into them, slowly, and she let her head go forward, and then back against him, and shut her eyes.

"That's not on the list," she said.

"Which list?"

"The one I'm keeping. That I don't keep." Her mouth moved. "It's nine. Second bell's in two hours. You've two hours of being useful first."

"Useful how?"

"The Wayford tape," said Ferra, and pushed it across the table to him without opening her eyes. "All of it. Out loud. Slowly."

He read her the Wayford tape. All of it. Out loud. Slowly. By the end of it she had stopped writing and was leaning back against him with her eyes shut and her hand up over her shoulder holding his wrist; and when the second bell went she opened her eyes and stood up and put her book under her arm and went out without a word, and he waited a quarter of an hour by the counting room clock, exactly, and then went down.

The first lane of the lower ward was opened that week, under Linus's rule: the lane behind the old corn exchange, the whole length of it dug once from end to end, and everything put into the one trench before it closed, the sewer and the storm drain and the water and the ducts for the wires, and concrete over all of it, and the lamp posts set in the concrete while it was wet; so that the lane would be dug once in a hundred years and not, as every street on Earth that Emeric remembered, once a season by a different gang each time. The families on the lane stood in their doors and watched it, all week, and said it was a great deal of hole for one lane. On the Saturday Linus walked the length of it with his levels and found it a quarter of an inch out at the bottom end, and had the gang take up forty feet of it and lay it again, and the families said that was a great deal of hole twice.



Rylan taught them the jump in the afternoon.

He did it himself. Not Ward; Ward stood at the well and watched, and did not say anything, which was Ward giving it to him. He stood the class in a long line in front of the stable wall, the old wall with the slates along its top that was twice a man's height, and he stood in front of them with his back to it.

"Knights jump," he said. "You've seen it. Ward goes over a wall like that as if it were a stile. So does Osric, badly. So do I." He did not turn round to look at the wall. "The mages will tell you we cast it. We don't. A mage casts. He tells his mana what to be and where to go, outside him, and it costs him, and he gets tired. We don't tell it anything. We see it."

They looked at him.

"You see yourself," said Rylan, "up on the wall. Before you're there." He waited. "That's all. You don't push. You don't try. You look at the top of the wall and you see yourself on it, standing, with your feet on the slates, as clear as you see me. And it comes."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"That's it?" said a Commencement boy at last.

"That's it."

"That's not a lesson. That's a..." The boy did not know what it was. "How do we see it?"

"The mages say the rite puts a shape in your head, and the shape does it, and without the rite there's no shape." Rylan shrugged. "I've reason to think they're wrong. Don't look for a shape. Look at the wall."

They looked at the wall.

They looked at it all afternoon. They stood in their long line in front of it in the heat with the fill still in them from the morning, from Maren's oatcakes, and looked at the top of the stable wall, and jumped; and fell back down onto the cobbles, every one of them, every time, a foot up, two, three at the most, and landed in a heap. Rylan explained it again. Slower. It was worse, the second time; he could hear that it was worse, and so could they; he was not a teacher and had never been, and he said "you see it" four times in one breath and stopped and looked at the cobbles. Then he explained it a third time, slower still, and differently, and it was better.

"Don't look at the wall," he said. "That's wrong. I said it wrong. Look at the top of it, where your feet will be. Look at the slates. One slate. Pick one. That one there, with the chip out of it." He pointed. "See your boot on it. Your own boot. With the mud on. See the mud." He looked along them. "Not you jumping. You've never jumped that. You can't see a thing you've never done. See your boot on the slate. That's all. Afterward. Already there."

Arthur tried it, and went up three feet, and came down, and looked at the slate with his brows together. He was trying too hard, Emeric thought from the stair. He had always tried hard; it was what had got him through the falling and the track and the rail and a night on a cart in a rope; and this was the first thing he had met that trying spoiled.

Bren Tallis tried it, and went up four feet, and came down, and swore, and was told by Ward not to.

Mira Holt stood in front of the wall with her plait over her shoulder for a long while without jumping at all. She stood there so long that the line behind her began to shift, and Rylan held up his hand to stop it, and they waited. She was looking at the slate with the chip out of it. She was looking at it, Emeric thought, as a carter's daughter on the west side looks at a house on the river street she will never be let into, with her chin up, knowing exactly what it would be like to stand in the doorway, and that nobody would ever ask her to.

Then she went.

Nobody would have called it a jump. It was nothing like what Rylan did, or Ward. She went up, all at once, as if something had picked her up by the back of the dress, and her hands came down on the edge of the slates, the very edge, and her boots scrabbled at the stone a yard below the top, and she hung there by her hands, a full man's height off the ground, with her plait swinging, and her face turned to the yard in absolute astonishment.

Then she let go, and came down, and fell in a heap, and sat on the cobbles, and burst out laughing.

Nobody else laughed. The whole line was looking at her.

"Again," said Rylan.

She did it again. Not as high. Her hands missed the slates by a foot. But she went up nearly as far, from a standing start, on the cobbles, with nothing under her, and the line saw it; and Bren Tallis, at the end of it, went white and then red, and turned to the wall, and stood in front of it, looking at the slate, as long as she had; and then went, and came up short of the slates by a hand, and hung in the air for the space of a breath at the top of it, which nobody in that yard had ever seen a man do who had not had the rite, and came down on his feet. On his feet. And stood there.

By dusk two of them could almost do it.

The rest of them sat along the bench against the armoury wall in the last of the light, grey, done, with their elbows bleeding and the fill gone out of them, looking at the wall as if it had insulted them personally. Noll Finch, at the end of the bench, had not come off the ground more than a foot all afternoon, and had fallen every time, and got up every time, and was looking at the slate with the chip out of it as if he meant to remember exactly what it looked like for the rest of his life.

Rylan sat down on the end of the bench beside him. He looked very tired. He had explained the same thing a dozen ways all afternoon and only two had heard it, and Emeric, from the stair, saw him put his elbows on his knees and his face in his hands, and stay like that for a breath, as a man does who has found out that the thing he can do is not the thing he can teach.

Then he took his face out of his hands.

"Two," he said, to Ward at the well. "In a day. From nothing."

"Two," said Ward.

"It took me a year to see the wall after the rite. With the shape." Rylan looked at Mira, sitting on the cobbles at the far end with her back against the wall and her eyes shut and her hands, which were raw from the slates, palms up on her knees. "She did it in an afternoon on one of Maren's biscuits. With no shape at all."

"Yes," said Ward.

Rylan was quiet a while, looking at her, and then at his own forearms, at the old raised silver going up them; and did not say what he was thinking, and did not need to, because Emeric, on the stair, was thinking it too, and so, by the look of him, was Ward.
