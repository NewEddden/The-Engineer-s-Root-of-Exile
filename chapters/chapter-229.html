Chapter 229 - Tempered

The first forty came up the palace stair in the last week of the summer, in crates packed in straw, and every one of them was a triangle he had drawn in his first evening in the east range.

That was the thing that paid. He had not known, drawing them, that it would. He had drawn four kinds of triangle because four kinds, laid edge to edge on a frame of six lengths of strut, would bend round into a dome, and that was all he had been thinking about: the shape. He had not thought about what it would mean, later, that there were only four kinds. That a pane that went could be replaced by any other pane of its kind, from a crate, without measuring, without a template, without anybody climbing up to the hole with a rule. That Orrin, making the new ones, needed only four templates, and could make them forty at a time.

That the whole of the dome could be taken off its frame, pane by pane, and put back, pane by pane, in new glass, while the frame stood where it was and the tree stood under it.

They did it a quarter at a time. They drew the dome down into the stones on the gallery each morning, until it was ordinary air, and Bram's men went up the ladders on the inside with their spanners and their hot irons and took out the wired panes in one quarter of it, the eastern quarter first, one by one, and handed them down, and handed up the new ones, the clear ones, the tempered ones, that nobody had touched since they came out of the air except with a cloth. They set them in their sockets. They did not tap them in. Bram had put a rule on the socket, in the fitting shop, in iron: a thin ring of island rubber round every seat, so that a pane sat in its node on rubber and not on iron, and the iron could grow in the sun and shrink in the night as much as it liked and never touch the glass. They bedded them in pitch. They came down. And in the evening Bram put cartridges in the collar and brought the dome back up to Ferra's red line, and the tree stood in it, and drank.

Four days, four quarters. On the fifth morning there was no wired glass left in the dome at all. It lay in a stack on the gallery, three hundred and some panes of it with their fine iron nets, and Ferra entered it as returned to stock, because she did not throw anything away that might one day be glass for somebody's window.

And the black iron triangle on the west side, the one that had closed the hole on the night of the quarter day, came out last. Bram took it down himself and held it for a moment, a sheet of plain black iron with pitch on its edges, and then carried it down the gallery and set it against the wall by the pass-box, where it stayed, afterwards, for years, leaning there, and nobody ever moved it.

They filled it that night to the line, and he stood at the gauge and listened.

There was nothing to hear. That was what he was listening for. On the night of the quarter day there had been a slither and a crack. On the night of the first fill there had been a tick, the pane above the collar, a hair of a line across it. On every night since, lying in his room above the gallery, he had heard the dome in his sleep: not a sound, quite, but the waiting for one. The steady push of the band on three hundred panes, never stopping, and in any one of them, any night, an old bruise at a corner, worked at.

Now there was nothing. The band leaned on the glass as it always had, and the glass held it, and made no sound.

Bram went round the dome from the gallery with the small mallet, the one from the tears, and tapped every pane he could reach through the pass-box sleeves and every one he could reach from the ladders on the outside, lightly, one tap each, the way a wheelwright taps the spokes of a wheel to hear if one is cracked. Every pane rang. The same note, more or less, high and clear, a little different for each of the four kinds. He went all the way round, and came back to the gauge, and put the mallet in his belt.

"That's the only noise it'll make now," he said. "When somebody taps it."

The tree had come through its first flush of leaves.

That was Imani's word. She had watched it at the pass-box every morning of the summer with her hands in the sleeves, watering her beds and round it, and she said it the way she said it of her willow slips: a first flush of leaves, and then a pause, and then the second. It was the height of a man's forearm now, from the earth to its tip. The stem had thickened and gone from white to a pale grey, and in the grey, when the light came down on it at noon, there was a faint pattern, fine lines crossing each other, like the net on the old broken face of the stone it had come from. Its leaves went up the stem in their spiral, long and narrow and dark, twelve of them now, and every one of them lay flat and open to the light.

The three burned ones at the top of the first flush were still there, curled and bronze-edged, a little way down the stem now, with the good leaves going on above them. Imani would not let anybody take them off. She said a plant kept what it had been through, and that the three burned leaves were the first entry in its book, and it was not her business or anybody's to tear a page out.

And at the foot of the stem, just above the earth, the two grey half-shells were still held out on either side, like hands. They had not fallen. Emeric had thought they would; a bean's seed-leaves fell when the plant had no more need of them. These had not. They had gone a little darker, and a little harder, and they stood out from the stem on either side as if they had been carved there, cupped, open to the light, holding nothing now.

At the line it was flat and dark and open, every leaf. It drank. He could feel it, every morning from the gallery, the steady thread of the band going into it. At night, behind the canvas, it lit along every vein.

The helmets were harder than the dome, because the dome was flat.

Every pane of the dome was a flat triangle, cut flat, ground flat, lying flat on its cradle in the hot box, and the air came down on both its flat faces at once. A helmet's front could not be flat. It had to go round the front of a man's face, from ear to ear, from brow to chin, in one piece, curved, a great deal of glass between a man's eyes and whatever was on the other side of it, with no joint in it anywhere, because a joint was a seam and a seam was a leak.

"Shape it first," the prince said, at the glassworks, to Orrin and Bram. "Then temper it. Never the other way."

Orrin shaped it. He had made bowls all his life; a helmet's front was a very deep bowl with a very even wall. He blew it, and cut it, and laid the warm glass over a mould of iron that Bram had turned on the lathe to the shape of a man's face and a hand's width clear of it all round, and let it slump down over the mould in the oven until it lay on it everywhere, and ground its edges, and drilled the holes at its rim where it would bolt to the helmet's collar, all of it, everything, before it went near the hot box.

Then they tempered it. It was a different frame of pipes: not two flat plates but a hood and a bowl, the hood over the outside of the glass and the bowl inside it, both drilled with holes, so that when the curved glass came out of the hot box on its mould and between them, the air came down on both its faces at the same moment from every side, and it roared, and the glass took it.

The first two burst on the rack. The third held. Bram put it across two padded blocks, face down, and hit it in the middle with the small mallet, and then with the large one, and then, after a long look at the prince, with a hammer, not hard, and it rang, and held.

"Four times," the prince said. "Five. Than the same glass, not tempered. At least."

Bram put the hammer down.

"And when it goes?" he said.

He said it quietly, with his hand on the helmet's glass, and he did not mean the dome. He was looking at the mould, at the shape of it, the hand's width clear of a man's face all round. He had been looking at it all afternoon, while Orrin shaped, and not saying anything.

"When it goes," the prince said, "it goes all at once. Into pieces the size of peas, with no edges. Like the pane you broke with the hammer. It doesn't crack across and hold a knife in front of a man's eye. It's there, and then it isn't."

"And the man?"

"The man has a mouthpiece. Inside the helmet, in his teeth, on a hose from what he breathes. Not the helmet's air. His own." The prince did not look away from Bram. "If the glass goes, the helmet's full of whatever's outside it. The mouthpiece stays in his teeth. He breathes on it, and he has the line in his hand, and he goes home on the line." He put his hand on the glass beside Bram's. "Nothing in front of his face is the thing he lives by. The glass is so he can see. The mouthpiece is so he can breathe. They're not the same thing, and they can't fail together."

Bram was quiet for a while.

"Breathe what?" he said. "Home from where?"

The prince did not answer that. Not there, at the glassworks, with Orrin at the pot and the boys at the benches. He looked at the helmet's glass on its padded blocks, whole, having taken a hammer, and then at Bram, and Bram looked back at him, and after a moment Bram nodded, once, slowly, as a man nods at a sum he has worked out for himself and does not like the answer to, and picked up the helmet's glass in both hands, with a cloth, and wrapped it.

He went down to the gallery that night, late, after the house was asleep, as he did most nights now, and stood at the pass-box with the gallery's lamps out and looked through the glass at the tree.

It was lit. Every vein of every leaf, and the stem, faintly, all the way down to the two grey hands; a drawing of itself in cold blue-white light, standing in the middle of the dark round room behind the canvas, with Imani's beds round it and the new glass over it holding the band in. It was very still. It was growing. He could feel it drink.

He had never seen anything like it. Nobody had. Nobody alive in the world had ever seen one of these grow, or known that the grey stones in the dealers' boxes were anything but grey stones. Somebody had, once. Somebody had called them hollow. Somebody had drawn this, in a book, with the sun over it and the wavy lines of thick air all round it and pods on its branches, eight to a pod, as if it were a thing you might see any day, in any field. As if there had been fields of them.

And there were none. Not one, anywhere, that anybody knew of. There were grey stones in dealers' boxes, dead, a few; there were a handful in drawers and treasuries, like his mother's had been; there were the eight in Corbin's gift and the ones he had bought one at a time through three different hands. And there was this, in a glass bowl in the middle of his house, that he had made from half a book and a dead woman's seed. And that was all.

Where had they gone?

Not the stones. The trees. A tree that made eight seeds to a pod, and had been drawn standing in the sun as if it were common, did not stop being common by accident. It did not leave its seeds lying in dealers' boxes, dead, a few, and nothing else, without something happening to it. A great deal of something. Everywhere.

He knew what it needed. He had learned it this summer, the hard way, with three burned leaves. It needed the band. Thick air, held, at a number Armand could read. And there was no air like that on the ground anywhere in the world. There was only the band, three miles up, where no tree could stand.

Unless the ground had been thicker, once. He had thought that before, in the winter of the seed, and put it away, because there was no way to know. Armand's books had bones in them that nobody could explain. The crowd in the king's hall had lain in the medium thick and slow. Life gave off the medium; a great deal of life gave off a great deal of it. If there had once been a great deal more life, of a great deal larger kind.

He did not finish the thought. He had never been able to finish it. There was nothing to finish it with.

Except a hollow under a ridge, on an island, in the dark.

He had felt it on the first night at Ashendeep: the rock going down under his feet, and then not going down, and a space under it, long, going down and in under the ridge, with the medium lying in it differently. Thicker. Not much. Enough. He had not been able to feel the bottom of it. And on two of the drilling nights in the winter, with the bit going down in the dunes a mile to the west and nothing for him to do but turn the windlass, he had gone to the ridge in the last hour of the dark with Ward, and stood on the spoil below Kesrath's lowest bench, and put the sense down again, as far as it would go, and further than it had gone the first time, because he had been a year stronger and a great deal more patient.

And under the first dark space there had been water. Still water, standing in the rock, filling a passage that went on down past it. And under the water, very far down, at the very edge of what he could feel, something that was thicker than the space above it. Thicker than anything on the ground. Not the band. But more like the band than anything he had ever felt that was not three miles up.

And the signs go down with it, and I will follow them in the morning.

The scholar had gone down. He had never come back up. And his book had the tree in it, and the pods, and the wavy lines of thick air, and he had found all of it somewhere, and written it down, and gone down under that ridge to find the rest.

He stood at the pass-box with his hand on the glass and looked at his mother's tree lit in the dark, the only one in the world, growing, and knew that he was going to go down after him.

He had known it, he thought, since the helmet held under Bram's hammer. Perhaps since the tear. Perhaps since the three burned leaves. He had not said it to himself until now, because a thing said was a thing that went in a book, and this was going to be a thing that went in a book with a great many people's names in it, and some of them were going to be very far down, in the dark, under water, breathing on a mouthpiece, with a line in their hands.

He would want to know how it died. Everywhere. That was the question. And the only place in the world that might have the answer was at the bottom of a flooded hole under a ridge on an island that two kings claimed and a third was digging, with a dead man's book for a map.

He went back up the gallery to his room. He did not light the lamp. He sat at his desk in the dark with the window open onto the gallery and the dome below it dark behind its screens, and took a sheet of paper, and wrote at the top of it by feel, in the dark, the way he had once written four words against his chest in a doorway in Wayford:

Under water. Air carried. A line.

Then he sat there until the grey came, and did not sleep, and did not try to.
