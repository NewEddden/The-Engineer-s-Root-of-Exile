Chapter 293 - Honest Paper

Keller's copy of the charge came south on the fourth of December, on the night express from the capital's railhead, in a satchel that never left a young clerk's knees.

Emeric felt him come up the castle hill a little before ten: a thin note, cold at the edges the way every note from the north was cold now beside Kaldo's, tired and anxious and very determined. It argued with the gate, and came on. By the time the porter knocked at the study door Emeric had put his pen down.

He was perhaps twenty, in a black coat not warm enough for the night express in December, with chilblains on both hands. He looked at Emeric's eyes, went a little pink, and unbuckled the satchel.

"From Master Keller, Your Grace. Into your hand. He said I was to wait for a receipt."

"You'll have one," said Ferra, and got up.

It was a packet the thickness of a man's thumb, sewn in oiled cloth and sealed at the seam with Keller's plain seal, the one he used on the crown's evidence. Inside, on top, a single sheet in Keller's own small square hand; under it, in a clerk's hand, black ink and red, forty-four pages, numbered at the corner and tied with tape. Ferra counted them twice by the corner numbers, wrote the receipt, and sent him down to Maren for soup and the bed over the guardroom.

Keller's sheet was short. What followed was a true copy of the matters laid before His Majesty's council on the first of December, touching the crown's oath as it concerned the duchy of Kaldo, against the hearing in the spring. He had checked every line of it he could check from the capital, and found every one true. He should be obliged if the Chancellor of Kaldo would tell him which were not, and send him the books for those that were; because a charge true in every particular could not be answered on its particulars, only on the law, and he would sooner know now which kind of answer he was writing.

And below his name, a line more.

The notes in the margins are in a second hand on the paper as laid. My clerk has copied them in red, where they stand. I know the hand. It has written to my court on His Highness's behalf for twenty years. I make no remark on it. K.

Ferra reached past him and untied the tape.



She read all night.

It called itself a schedule of matters, in twenty-six articles, and every article had at its head the same three lines in the close's language, which Keller's clerk had copied down to the capitals: that capacity in a man is given once, by the Light, in the chamber; that power which did not come through the chamber was not given; and that what is held and was not given was taken. And under that, in each article, what had been taken, and where it could be read.

That was the thing. Where it could be read. Not a witness, not an informer, not a priest's account of what a priest had seen: a page. The Kaldo Almanac, year eleven, page nineteen. The duchy's company roll, as returned to the chancery. A Healer's Handbook, page forty. The open record of the court of Kaldo, which any person may read.

Any person may read. They had copied it off the rule on the records hall's door, word for word, as if it were a joke they had not noticed.

By two in the morning she had a slip for every article by the lamp, its number and the book it named and the page, and beside each, in her own hand, a mark. He came and stood behind her chair at four. She had the twenty-second open beside Ottoline's register.

"Go to bed," she said, without looking round.

"Are any of them wrong?"

She ran her finger down the register to the line she wanted, and read it against the clerk's copy, and made her mark on the slip.

"Not yet," she said.



The six came to the wine store at seven, because the porter had gone round at six with a slip that said only Seven. The table. All. F.; and nobody in Kaldo had ever needed more than that from her to get out of bed.

She had not been to bed. She sat at her end in yesterday's grey with a pen through her hair and the slips squared in front of her, and two of Ottoline's clerks at the wall with their arms full of books. Bram was still pulling his chair out when she began.

"It's what was laid against the king on the first. Keller wants to know which lines are false." She put her hand flat on the slips. "None of them are. I've been through every article against our own books. Every figure, every name, every quotation. I found one place where their clerk wrote a seven like a one, and I checked, and Ambry's compositor set it badly in the Almanac. It's a one on our page too." She turned the top slip over. "So it's right."

"Read it," said Ward.

She read it as she read the accounts to the Assembly, heading and matter and source; and after each source she touched the slip, and one of Ottoline's clerks laid the book open where the person it concerned could see it.

The company rolls first. Every quarter's, every man under arms by name and parish, three hundred to the man and not one over; they had counted, and written that down too, with a kind of fairness. What they had found was the column. Ferra's office had always kept a column on the roll for what a man was: pikeman, gunner, gate, rack hand, cook. And against some of the names the column said knight. Arthur, son of Sela, of the north well. Mira. Bren. Others, men and women, written knight in the duchy's own hand, who had never gone up the stair to the floor in their lives.

"It's what they are," said Ward.

"I know what they are. I wrote it." She touched the slip. "Third quarter, line two hundred and six. Arthur. It's my hand."

The burial counts, from the register Doran sent north every Michaelmas in a fair copy, as every parish priest in the realm did; and Doran kept the best book in the south and wrote the cause beside every burial, plainly, as he had since the plague. In May of year ten, three boys of the dock quarter, dead of the duke's bread eaten past the rule. And the article under it: that the duke's food carried a power not of the chamber, that the duke had known it could kill, and had fed it to his town.

Imani's hands were folded on the table. They stayed folded.

"He wrote it true," she said. "That's what it was."

Then Armand's letter.

It was copied whole, a page and a half, from an original the article said had been given into the tower's keeping by its addressee. At the top, My dear, and a space where the name had been cut out with a knife, which the clerk had marked in red with a little bracket. It was the letter Armand had written in the second winter to his old students. Emeric had never read it. Fifteen people had come down the Wayford road because of it, and dug his drains and held a river for a coach and turned a swarm for an hour; and he had never once asked to see what had brought them.

It said that the writer was old and had been wrong about a good many things, and was writing to the people he had been least wrong about, to say that there was work in the south. That there was a young man in Kaldo who held more than any student Armand had ever had and did not know what he held, which the writer had seen once before and had not done right by; and this time he meant to. That the tower had taught them to hold, and if they came south he would teach them what holding was for. That the pay was poor and the town smelled, and they would not be bored for an hour of their lives again. And that they should not tell the Circle why they were going, because the Circle would ask what they meant to learn that the tower would not teach them, and he did not want them to have to lie.

The next article was the Circle's own roll for the year after: fifteen posts given up in a single spring, the date written against each in the Circle's clerk's hand, every one of them the spring Armand's people came down the road.

Armand sat at the fire end with the rug on his knees and listened to his own letter in Ferra's flat voice. When she had finished he held out a hand, and she slid the copy down the table, and he read it again himself, close, with his face nearly on the page.

"I was rather pleased with that," he said at last, in his lecture voice. "At the time." He did not give it back. "Go on, my dear. I'm listening."

She went on. The school list, from the duchy's printed Commencement lists: every child in the four baronies by name and parish, carters' and lightermen's and miners' and freed men's, girls the same as boys, the same lessons. The census, from the Almanac's tables: the number in each barony who could read, which Ferra had counted every year since it was ninety-three and printed every year because she was proud of it. The Seers' Table, from the school's printed list of classes: children taught by the duchy to see what was hidden from other men.

"A claim to prophecy," Ferra read. "Made by the duchy, of its own children, in print."

"I told them it was the worst name I'd ever heard," said Emeric.

"It's in the school's minutes. In Sela's hand." She touched the slip. "They didn't ask for the minutes."

Bram made a noise in his chest that might have been a laugh on a better morning.

Nobody laughed at the next. Its source was the Grayrock priest's letter to his bishop about a mother in the pit rows who had kept the needle from her children because the prince was blessed and his blessing lay on the rows, and whose youngest had died of the speckled sickness, a girl of three. The article said that the duke's people paid him a worship that had not come through the altar, and that it had killed a child.

Emeric had sat in that kitchen under the tip for an hour, after Doran told him, and told her whose hands it had been, every one by name; and at the end of it she had asked him to go. It was not a thing to say at a table.

The Hollow Night notice was in it entire, from one of the four's eleven sacks: a feast of the dead, kept at the end of October, with masks. And under that, because the clerk who drew it was honest, the notice's own words, which said that it was a night for stories, and said nothing else at all. Ferra read it out and touched the slip and did not look at him.

And Imani's ward, in three articles: the cartridges let into her rooms through iron collars, from the ward's printed rules; the town's water dosed with the stones, from the Almanac's table of the waterworks, with the dose; and the tank, in which the healer of Kaldo laid the sick naked in a stone bath and saw into their bodies by an art the chamber did not know, as her own handbook said on page forty.

"They're not naked," said Imani. "They've a sheet."

"It doesn't say a sheet. On page forty."

"It should." Imani unfolded her hands and looked at them. "I'll put it in the next printing." She was quiet. "They've got the dose right."



Gideon Marl's paper was the eighteenth article.

Ferra stopped before she read it, the length of a breath, with the slip under her finger.

A letter, the source said, from a fair copy in the hand of the Chancellor of Kaldo given to each man whose mark was on a petition of the masons, in October of the twelfth year. To Gideon Marl, mason, of Kaldo. Emeric knew it before she was a line in. He had read it over her shoulder the night she wrote it.

The gatehouse's faces would be dressed by hand, as the masons asked, and the bastions' after them. The masons would do what a printer could not, the openings and lintels and carved doors and dressed stone, at the wage written below, and be paid to teach, a morning a month, any boy or girl who asked. And there had been no guild in Kaldo since the plague, so the masons' trade in Kaldo would be what the masons and this office wrote down together; here was what the office wrote, and the masons might write back.

And the article: that the Chancellor of Kaldo, in her own hand, declared the old trades of the realm void in the duke's lands and the duke's office the maker of them; and that walls were raised in Kaldo without hands, by an art the chamber did not know, as the duke's own officer admitted in writing to the men whose hands it had replaced.

Ferra put her finger on the heading.

"That's mine. That's my hand they've copied; I can see where their clerk didn't know my g." She was very still. "I ran off eighty-one. I wrote every man's name at the top myself, so he'd know it was his." She took her finger away. "That's Gideon's."

"Where's Gideon?" said Ward.

Saban unfolded his hands. "Gone north. The nineteenth of November, the morning express. Told his gang there was work on the close for a man who could dress stone by hand. A letter had come for him. Nobody saw it." He looked at his hat. "And you'll remember my man's docket from October. The quiet one of the four, at the river inn, standing an old mason a jug and listening to him for an hour about the plague and the graves and the trough he set. I wrote nothing down then. There was nothing to write."

"And now there is," said Imani.

"Now there's a guess. Somebody offered a bitter man a wall. He took his letter north because it had his name on it, and somebody asked to see it." Saban folded his hands again. "I'd put money on it. I wouldn't put my name to it."

"Poor stupid man," said Imani.

"He's not stupid," said Ferra. "He asked me in writing what his trade was to be. I told him. In writing." She turned the slip over. "I'd write it again. Every line."

Emeric looked at the heading in another man's copy of Ferra's hand and thought of a square grey man on his knees at the corner of the Port Town's third street, saying to the doorstep he was setting that nobody had asked him to sit at the machine; and of the same man twelve years before, setting the feet of a timber trough for eleven days in the cold so that a town could have water. Both were true. He did not know what to do with their both being true, and did not try.



The twenty-first was Doran.

That on the last night of October of the eleventh year, in the market square of Kaldo, before many witnesses, at the feast the duchy calls Hollow Night, the priest of the parish, being asked nothing, had called out to the duke's Chancellor and the duke's healer together and asked when the three of them would let him marry them; and said that his chapel was there any day they liked, and he had had the banns written since the spring. That a priest of the Light had offered, in the hearing of the duke's town, to make one marriage of a man and two women. That the register of the parish of Kaldo, as returned at Michaelmas of the twelfth year, had been searched entire, and carried no such marriage. That the chamber laid the offer, and left the register to the council.

The source was a man's hearing, written down that night at an inn on the river road; and the register.

Ferra touched the slip.

"Article twenty-two," she said, and read the heading.

Her voice did not change. Her pen lay by the slips where it had lain all morning, and she did not put her hand near it; she turned the next slip and read the next heading in the same clear flat voice as the last, and the one before that. Imani, beside her, was looking at her own hands and did not lift her eyes. Saban looked at his hat. Ward looked at the wall.

Emeric looked at Ferra.

He had stood on the keep leads a year and five weeks ago, between the two of them, with the turnips burning down all over the town, and heard them say not yet in one breath before he could ask; and heard Ferra say that a wedding was paper. A page in Doran's register with the duke's name on it and hers and Imani's and the date and Doran's hand. That she would not hand them that page. Not while there was a portfolio. Not for anything.

There was no page. The council had the register, entire, and it was empty, and all they had was a good old man calling across a square on a feast night because he loved them.

She did not say so. She read the twenty-second and the twenty-third and touched the slips; and he sat with his hands on the table, because he did not trust them anywhere else, and watched her not say it the whole length of two articles, and understood that she was never going to say it, to him or to anybody, and that this was the most she had ever told him about anything.



The twenty-third came from the crown: the only one of the twenty-six whose source was not Kaldo's own. A report made under his mark to his captain at the river garrison by a sergeant of the king's soldiers, posted south to the Duke of Kaldo's base in the summer of the eleventh year under the king's seal and returned north with his men at first snow; written for him by a garrison clerk, because he wrote slowly. That the duke's captain, a knight of the rite, drew glyphs in his yard at dawn like a mage, with his sleeves down, and lifted stones with them. That the captain's second did the same, less well. That another of the duke's knights had set his own sleeve alight at it and been laughed at for a week. That the sergeant had seen it eleven mornings and had not been told he was not to.

"He had a rug in his pack," said Ward.

Everybody looked at him.

"Abloh put it there at first snow. Four pounds. He was cold every night from July and said so every morning." Ward's face did not change. "He was a good sergeant. He saw everything. That's what a sergeant's for. You can't tell a man not to see a thing in a yard he's standing in."

The twenty-fourth set the floor's numbers out in two columns, from the tower's own books, for ten seasons: the king's soldiers who had gone to the floor through service, how many had gone up and how many stood, the one in ten that had always been the floor's, season after season, to within a man. And then the last season's: thirty-four of the king's own soldiers sent to the floor from the Duke of Kaldo's base, where the king's seal had posted them; and of the thirty-four, thirteen stood.

The article did not say why.

It said that the chamber knew of no cause. That nothing in the rite had been altered; the floor was the floor and the liturgy the liturgy, as for four hundred years. That men who had lived a summer in the duke's lands had stood up from the chamber at more than three times the Light's own rate, and the chamber could find in the duke's papers no account of how. And that power over the chamber's own choosing, worked by no means the chamber could name, the chamber could only call by its name, which was sorcery.

Ward's hands were flat on the oak. Emeric saw them go a little flatter, and felt his note go very steady, as it had the night Saban told them where the king was sleeping.

"They've written down that they don't know," said Saban quietly. "That's not a thing the close writes down often."

"They counted, and it came out wrong for them." Ferra touched the slip. "Thirteen of thirty-four. That's Imani's sheet and the king's list. I checked it at three."

There was red in the margin beside the number. Ferra turned the page round so that they could all see it: in the clerk's careful copy of another man's hand, one mark. A question mark, small and firmly made, and nothing else.

"He doesn't know either," said Ferra.

"No," said Ward. "Not from that."



The margins were the last thing she read, apart from the articles, because Keller had sent them apart.

Everywhere but three places the second hand had only ticked or underlined, the way a man reading closely underlines what he already thought. In three places it had written.

Beside the school list: All of them. Every child. Girls the same. In ten years none of them will need a priest or a lord to read them a paper.

Beside the census, where the readers went up year by year from ninety-three: He counts who can read. He prints it. It goes up every year, and he prints that it goes up.

And the hangings. The notice was the fifteenth article, from one of the two thousand Ambry had set in the biggest type he had: that five lords be hanged at Wayford by the order of the Holder of Wayford, and not by writ. The second hand had underlined by his order and not by writ twice, the second line darker, and written beside it, smaller than the others, as if to himself:

Five lords. By his word. He was right about every one of them, which is the whole difficulty. When he is right about all of us, what will he print?

Nobody spoke for a while.

Emeric had thought at four in the morning that it was the truest thing in the forty-four pages, and he thought it again in daylight. The close had built its schedule to say that power had been taken. The man in the margins did not care much about the chamber or the Light. He had read the school list and the census and the hangings and seen what they were for. Ninety-three who could read, the first year; Hob Tanner's girl reading her father's line aloud at the Assembly door. Five lords on a beam. A town that could read a paper, and a duke who would one day give it one.

He had told the six at this table, three years ago, what he meant to build. Corbin's old clerk had not needed telling. He had worked it out from the Almanac.

"He's read it properly," said Armand, into the quiet. "That one. He's the only man who has."

"He agrees with it," said Imani.

"With the bottom of it. Not the top. The top's the chamber, and he doesn't care about the chamber at all." Armand's hand was still flat on his own letter. "He's a careful man. He'll want to be sure."

Emeric looked up. He had said those words himself, in August, with his hand on a laundry list.

"There's a thing worth saying," said Saban, in his commercial voice, the voice for a price that was going to be disputed, "and I'd like to say it before anybody's angry with me. We print a great deal. Every one of those twenty-six is a thing we chose to write down and could have chosen not to. I don't say stop. I say that next year somebody in a black coat will buy one of everything at the works again, and we might think about what's in it."

"No," said Ferra.

She was squaring the slips. She finished squaring them and laid her hand on top.

"Every line on that schedule is true because I wrote it true. If I'd written the rolls to be safe, they'd have had to make up the knights, and Keller could beat a made-up knight in an afternoon. They didn't have to make anything up. It was all in the books." She looked at Saban. "That's the trouble with it, and it's the best thing in it. Every article on this table is a thing we did and said we did, in print, at the time, with our names on it. Nobody can say we hid it. Nobody can say we're ashamed of it." She took her hand away. "I'm not. I'll not have the books changed. Not a line. Not next year. I'm going to write to Keller that none of them are false and send him fair copies, certified, of every page they name. The originals stay where they are."

"Except the sheet," said Imani. "They should have had a sheet."

"Keep the books," said Ward.

It was the king's line, off the cipher sheet, and he said it in the same flat voice he had read it in, as if passing an order down. Ferra wrote it at the top of a clean page under the date and underlined it.

Then she put her head down on her arms on top of the slips and was asleep before Bram had finished getting up to fetch her a cushion.



Armand sent for him that evening, through Imani, who came up at eight with her ward coat still on and said that he was not to keep the old man up past ten, because she had counted him at six and did not like it.

"How bad?"

"Ninety-six at the chest, sixty-four at the wrist. It was ninety-four on the ninth of last month." She pulled her gloves off finger by finger. "The pills aren't for that. They're so a clot doesn't go to his head. He's been sitting with that letter all day."

Armand's room was at the end of the east range, square, with a fire and too many books and a bed he slept very little in. He was in the chair by the fire with the rug over his knees and the two pages of his letter on the rug under his hand.

"Sit where I can see you; Imani says I'm to stop turning my head." He waited. "I wrote forty of these, that winter. At the table in the long room, by one lamp, because Ferra wouldn't let me have two. I wrote this one best." He touched the red bracket round the cut-out name. "One of the ones who didn't come. He kept it twenty years in a drawer, and somebody asked him for it nicely, and he had a career." He was quiet. "I'd like to blame him. I'm too old to waste the time."

"Armand."

"Let me finish. It's an old man's privilege and I've earned it twice." He took his hand off the letter. "Half that schedule is mine. The letter. The Circle's posts; those are my people, I could tell you what every one of them eats for breakfast. The knights who cast, which nobody would have tried if I hadn't said in that room one night that the body could learn what the hand did. And the thirteen." He looked into the fire. "The thirteen are mine as much as anybody's."

"They're Ward's."

"Ward's, and mine, and Imani's, and the king's." He turned his head after all, slowly, and looked at Emeric. "They want somebody to answer for it. That's what a hearing is for. A body at the bar." He folded his hands. "Send me. I wrote the letter. I'm a master of the tower. I'm eighty-seven, and I would very much like, before I go, to stand up in front of the lords spiritual and say, Yes. All of it. On purpose. And I have the numbers. Let them try a mage for a mage's work, in the king's place. It's mine more than his. It's mine more than yours."

Emeric looked at the old man in the chair: the swollen knuckles on the rug, the long kind horse's face that had sat at the end of the table every night for twelve years and been right about nearly everything. He did not need to think about it at all, and was sorry that he did not.

"No," he said. "Three reasons." He held them up, because Armand had taught him at six to hold an argument where it could be counted. "One. You'd go north on the express, which is warm, and then they'd hold you in the close until the spring, which is not; a man who isn't the king and comes to the Church of his own will goes into the Church's keeping. A stone room, in midwinter, in the north, with a beat that won't keep step for a minute together. You'd not see the hearing. Imani would tell you so in fewer words."

"She'd tell me in two."

"Two. The hearing isn't about who did what. Ferra's sending them the books for that. It's about whether the king kept his oath, and only the king can answer it. A tribunal won't take a substitute for the crown. Not from a duke, not from a tower master, not from anybody. They'd thank you, and try him anyway, with you in a cell to show for it." He lowered his hand. "And three. If you died in their keeping, and you would, they'd write it down. They're careful people. The master of the tower who wrote the letter came north of his own will, gave himself up, and died in the Church's keeping before the hearing; and every man in the capital who read it would read a confession. Nobody would read an old heart in a cold room. They'd read a man who knew he was guilty and went to God before they could make him say so." He was quiet. "You'd hand them that page with your own name on it. I won't let you."

Armand looked at him a long time.

Then he looked down at the letter on his knee and smoothed it, once, with the flat of his hand.

"Yes," he said. "You're quite right."

Emeric had braced for the argument. Armand had argued with him about everything for thirty years, from the order of the elements to the proper way to hold a teacup, and had never once said quite right in that tone and stopped. He waited for the rest of it. There was no rest of it.

"Will the glaziers be done before the frost?" said Armand.

"The dome? Not till the spring. They've stopped for the cold. The last panes are in crates."

"Pity. I'd have liked to see it whole." He held out the letter. "Give this back to Ferra. Tell her it's a good letter and I'm not sorry for a line of it either; she'll like that. And put another log on before you go. I'll sit a while."

Emeric put a log on and turned the lamp down to a bead. At the door he looked back. Armand was sitting with his eyes closed and his hands folded on the rug, and his face was calm, the way it was calm at the end of a lecture that had gone well.

He went down the stair glad that it had been so easy.



He took Vantic her articles himself, on the seventh, at dusk, because it was not a thing to send by a porter.

There were two. The twelfth: that a usurer's house had been set up in the old tithe house on the market square of Kaldo, by license of the duke and with his money in it, taking coin in from the duke's people and lending it out again for gain, which the Light had forbidden since the Light was first preached. And the thirteenth: that in the summer of the locusts the same house had lent seed corn to three hundred parishes of the realm, every one listed, against the next harvest, on terms the house set; and that a parish lent its bread by a duke's banker in a hungry year is a parish bought, and the chamber laid it as bribery of the realm's people, three hundred times over.

The House's tower stood on the civic ring, twelve storeys of glass going gold in the last light; but the counter was still in the old tithe house on the square, because the town would go through a door on the square to put a coin on a counter and would not go up twelve storeys in a cage to do it. The shutters were up. There was a lamp lit at the far end, and he could feel her before he knocked: a still note, very quiet, held close, the note he had felt in the east tower since the first day she came and never mentioned to her because of what it would have meant to say he could.

The clerk let him in and went out by the side door with his coat over his arm.

She was at her desk behind her own rail, with nothing on it but a pen and a book and her two gloved hands, as it had been the morning Maren came across the square with her jar. She did not get up.

"Your Grace."

"Livia."

She looked at him at that, because he did not often use it and never in the tithe house. Then she held out her hand for the pages, and read them.

She read the way she read a borrower's book, every line and then every line again, and he did not try to read her face, because there had never been any point; it did what she told it, and tonight it was doing nothing at all. She read the list of the three hundred down two columns and onto a third, and he saw her lips move once, very slightly, as if she were counting. Then she laid both pages on the desk and put her gloved hand flat on them.

"They've spelled Brackenfold with one l," she said. "It's the only error. I've checked it against my own. They copied it from the ledgers my clerks carried north with the seed. I told my clerks to show any man who asked." She was quiet. "I thought it would be farmers." She looked at the pages. "A bribe is paid before. To make a man do a thing he would not otherwise do. This was lent after, to a man who had already been eaten. It is a loan, on terms, which the parish will repay out of its next harvest; or if the harvest fails I will wait, and if it fails again I will wait again, because a dead parish repays nothing. That is what they object to. Not that I lent. That I'll be paid. That every one of the three hundred will remember who lent it and that they paid it back, and be proud of it." She folded her hands. "I said a hundred years, at your table. I may have been modest."

He waited.

"A usurer's house, in the tithe house." She looked down the dark hall at the counter and the shuttered windows. "They chose this building and not the tower. They chose the tithe house because of the word." For the first time something moved in her face: the corner of the mouth, not much. "It's well done. I'd have done it."

"The House has never lent at interest. On paper."

"Never a copper, and the Church knows it, and nobody ever asked to see the paper. Everybody knows what a bill at two prices is. Nobody can find the word in it." She looked at him. "Don't answer it."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were. You came at dusk with the pages in your coat and your face set, to offer the duke's answer in the duke's print under the duke's name." She shook her head, slowly. "If the duke answers for the House, the House is the duke's. Then it's exactly what they say: a usurer's house in the duke's tithe house, with the duke's money in it, defended by the duke. I'll answer it myself, in my own time and the House's name. They want to know what the House's contracts say. I'm going to show them."

"All of them?"

"Every contract the House has written in Kaldo. Every share and rent charge and bill. Plainly, on cheap paper, so the priests can afford it." The corner of the mouth again. "Let them find the word."

He laughed. It came out of him louder than he meant in the dark hall, and Vantic did not laugh but looked at him as if she had been waiting for it and was satisfied.

Then she looked at the pages again, and was quiet a while.

"I refused my House's match in writing," she said, "seven years ago. Saban told your table why; I knew he would. I'd not hand them the explanation, ready-made, for the rest of my life: that the woman who made the House's best book in fifty years had made it on her back." She touched the twelfth article with one gloved finger. "And here they are, seven years on. A woman in a tithe house who made her book on her knees, at the duke's feet, for gain." She took the finger away. "One can't win the argument about how a woman got her book. I learned that at twenty. One can only keep the book."

"You'd have made a very bad duchess," said Emeric.

"I'd have made an excellent duchess." She did not smile. "I'd have hated it and been excellent at it, and you'd have known I hated it every day, and so would they. Both of them." She was quiet. "It would have been a very well run marriage. I'd have priced it beautifully."

He did not answer that. It was true, and had been true for seven years, and neither of them had said it aloud before because neither had needed to. He found, standing at her rail in the shuttered tithe house, that he liked her more than he had in a long time, and that there was nothing in it that would have made Ferra lift her head from her book; and that Vantic knew that too, and had said it at dusk in the tithe house precisely because it was safe to now.

"Thank you," he said. "For not letting me answer it."

"I'd not have let you whether you thanked me or not." She squared the pages and held them out over the rail. "Give these to Ferra. Tell her the list is right except for the l, and I'll want her press for a week in January, and her best compositor, and as much of the cheap grey as the works can spare." She paused with the pages still in her hand. "And tell her she was right about Doran's page. The twenty-first. She'll know what I mean." She let go. "Don't tell her I said it in so many words. She'd hate it. Good night, Your Grace."



Hob Tanner beat a Kaldo foreman in open court on the tenth.

It was in the records hall with the doors open, as the rule said, on the coldest morning of the month, and by nine on a working day half of ward 3 had found a reason to be on the steps.

The foreman was a man named Garrod: Kaldo-born, of the lower ward, forty, broad and red and decent, who had been on the printer since ward 3's first terrace and had run the long printer's crew in the Port Town since the spring. Bram had made him foreman.

In the last three weeks of November, with the frost coming and four families from up the line in the marsh huts waiting on the fourth street's last terrace, Garrod had kept his crew at the printer from first light to the lamps, fifty and fifty-four and fifty-six hours; written forty in the crew book against every name, every week; and paid the hours past forty at the straight rate, in coin, from the crew's float, on the Saturday, by hand. To the men from up the line, who did not know the law and were afraid of anything that looked like it, he had said it was the harvest exception. Two of them were Tarrowmede grain men, who had been told what a harvest was by a steward every autumn of their lives and had never once won the argument.

The four families were on the Works Office's list by name. One was a copyist's from Withey, five children and a wife, whose abbey had laid off half its copying hall since cheap paper and print began coming up the abbey road, and him in the first half. One was a widower out of Cinderhale with two boys, who had told the clerk at the counter that the castle at home was bigger than Kaldo's keep and nobody had been warm in it since his grandfather's day, and that he would take a marsh hut, thank you, if it had a roof. The other two had come off the Wayford line with nothing to say about where they had been.

The harvest exception was the farm delegates' own motion, and it said farms, and it said time and a half.

The court was three of the register, drawn that morning by Ottoline's tabulator in front of witnesses: a lighterman from the port, Fairhythe-born, who had worked the barges off its quays until he was thirty and still called Kaldo's river the creek; the woman who taught the second class at Sela's school; and a carter's widow from the Milford road with a voice like a gate.

Emeric stood at the back by the door. He had not sat for Hob Tanner the first time and did not sit now. Ferra was on the end of the front bench with her book shut on her knee; the rule was hers, and you did not sit in judgment on your own rule.

Hob Tanner spoke for himself, badly and briefly, with his cap in his two hands: he had worked the hours and been paid for forty and a bit; he had heard at the Assembly door that forty was the law and the rest was half again; he had asked, and the foreman had said harvest, and he did not think a terrace was a harvest. Then he went red to the neck and said his girl had it written down, because he could not.

Nell stood up. She was twelve, taller than at the Assembly door, with a sheet of the school's grey paper folded in four, and she read it in the high clear voice that had carried to the back of that queue. Every night in November she had asked her father when he had started and when the lamps came on at the printer, and written it down, because Sela's school taught that; and added it up on the Saturday. Fifty and a half. Fifty-four. Fifty-six and a quarter, the week of the frost. And the coin, counted on the kitchen table.

"That's us," she said at the end, and sat down.

The crew book said forty, forty, forty, in Garrod's hand.

Garrod did not lie. It would have been easier for everybody. He said the girl's figures were right, near enough; that he had written forty because forty was what the Works Office's sheet had a column for, and the frost was coming, and there were four families in the marsh. That the Kaldo men on his crew had asked for their half again and been paid it, and the up-the-line men hadn't asked.

"Did you think they'd sooner have it," said the schoolteacher, "or did you think they wouldn't know?"

Garrod looked at her a long time.

"Both," he said. "I don't know. Both." He looked at the floor. "I'd four families in the marsh, missus. I've had them in the marsh before."

The crews' delegate stood up from the third row, unasked: the young man with the voice like a crane chain who had asked the Assembly the summer before last to think about forty. He was on Garrod's crew. He had been paid his half again every Saturday, he said, and the up-the-line men beside him at the same printer had not, and he had seen it and said nothing because Garrod was a good foreman and the houses wanted finishing; and he would like it written down that he was ashamed of that.

The widow wrote it down.

It took them a quarter of an hour in the back room. The lighterman read it, because the schoolteacher would not and the widow said she had done her share. A terrace was not a farm. Twelve men on Garrod's crew were owed every hour past forty at time and a half, by Nell Tanner's sheet and their own word against the crew book; the Works Office would pay it in coin by the Saturday, and strike the crew book and write it again in the open with the true hours. Garrod would work a year as a crew hand on a crew hand's wage, and pay into the float what the law said a foreman paid who wrote a false book.

"And the girl's sheet goes in the record," said the lighterman, "with her name on it. Because it's the only true book anybody brought."

The hall did not cheer. A good many on the benches, Emeric saw, were looking at the Kaldo men from the crew along the back wall, and the Kaldo men were looking at the floor.

Bram came across from the side wall and leaned both fists on the counter in front of the lighterman. "My office set the date. My office pays. By Friday, not Saturday." Then he went and stood over Garrod, enormous, and put a hand on his shoulder, briefly. "You're a good foreman. You'll be one again next year. Write the book true. Even when it's a bad week. Especially then."

He went out.

Saban found Emeric on the steps afterward, in the cold, with the crowd going away down the square and Hob Tanner and his girl and Bet and the baby among them.

"It goes in the record," said Saban. "The four copied last year's every page. A foreman of the duke's own Works cheating the men from up the line out of their wages, in the duke's own court's hand. It'll be in the next schedule."

"Yes," said Ferra, coming out behind them with her book under her arm. "And the next article after it will be the court that made him pay it back by Friday, with a twelve-year-old's sheet in the record because it was the only true book in the room." She went down the steps past them. "Let them copy that too."



She wrote Keller's answer over four nights at the study table, and he sat across from her for most of it and did the parts she gave him, which were few.

It did not argue. Against each of the twenty-six, in the large clear hand she kept for things meant to be read by somebody who was not her, she wrote the article's number and its source, and one word, and the book and page where the magistrate would find it; and under that, where there was something more that was also true, a line. Against the tank: the patient is covered by a sheet; the next printing will say so. Against the hangings: the Chancellor objected at the time, and wrote a stay, and the stay is enclosed. Emeric had not known she meant to send that. Against Gideon's: the letter is the Chancellor's and every word of it is meant; the petition it answered, with all eighty-one marks, is enclosed, so that the council may see what was asked.

Ottoline's clerks copied for four days, eleven of them in the long room, every page the schedule named, and Ferra read every copy against its page and certified it at the foot, and they went into three crates under the duchy's seal. On the last night, on top of everything in the third crate, she put a copy of the open record of the court of Kaldo for the tenth of December, Hob Tanner against the Works Office's foreman, with Nell's sheet in it in Nell's own round hand, because Ferra had asked the girl to come up the hill and copy it herself.

"He didn't ask for that," said Emeric.

"Any person may read the record of it." Ferra chalked the crate's list on its lid. "Keller's a person."

The crates went north on the fourteenth on the morning express, in the guard's van under the young clerk's eye, and the clerk in the soup car in a pair of gloves Maren had found him. Ferra watched the van out of the long shed until it was round the curve. The Long Table was eleven days off. Maren had already begun on the geese.

He had watched her write the twenty-first, on the third night.

She came to it in order, between the twentieth and the twenty-second, as she came to everything. She wrote the number, and the source, the priest's offer in a man's hearing at an inn and the register as returned at Michaelmas; and then the one word, as she had written it against every other article, in the same hand, the same size.

True.

And then the register's book and page, where the magistrate would find that it was empty.

She did not add a line. She blotted it and wrote the number of the twenty-second, and went on; and across the table Imani, who had come up from the ward at nine and was sitting on the floor with her back against Ferra's chair and her shoes off and her eyes shut, put up one hand without opening them and found Ferra's ankle, and held it. Ferra let her, and wrote the next word, and the next.

He did not say anything. There was nothing to say that would not have been too much, and Ferra had already said it in the only way she ever would: in a book, in the right column, in her own hand, where anybody who wanted to could read it.
