Chapter 163 - A Liturgy

The answer came in the second week of autumn, by the ordinary post, in a packet with a grain factor's letters and two bills of lading, and it was one line long.

Armand knew it was the answer before he opened it. He knew it by the seal. He had seen that seal on every feast-day roll of the dead of the chamber for forty years, pressed into the red wax at the foot of the list of names before the list was read: a hand open, palm out, and a lamp in the palm, and round the edge the words of the office in the old tongue. The cardinal's seal. The seal of the office that kept the rite, and had kept it for nineteen years under the same man, who had blessed the third son of Harrold on his way south out of the capital.

He broke it at his table in the window of the keep's east room, with his hands braced on the wood so that the paper would not shake.

It was a good sheet, heavy, cream, the cathedral chancery's own paper, with the edges cut clean. The hand was a secretary's, not the cardinal's. The words were the cardinal's. There were only eleven of them, under the office's opening and above the office's close.

The chamber is a liturgy, not a procedure. It will not be altered.

That was all.

Under it, the seal again, smaller. Augustine's own, beside the office's.

He read it twice. Then he put it down on the table and sat looking at it for a long time, and the thing he felt was not what he had expected to feel. He had expected anger, or grief, or the old weary knowing. What he felt was very like relief. He had written the letter so that he could say he had written it. Now he could say it. And now there was nothing else he could say, to anybody, ever, in any form the Church would read, because the Church had told him in eleven words that the four hundred years of dead in its rolls were a liturgy, and a liturgy was not a thing that could be wrong.

He folded it and put it in the locked box in the cupboard with the other thing. The eleven words on top of the six years.

The Circle had not written back at all.

That told him more than the cardinal had. He had sent the letter to the second chair of the Circle, to be passed to the cardinal's office, and the second chair had passed it, promptly, as he was bound to. And had not written one line of his own. Not a note to say it had been received. Not a word to the man who had taught him for a winter forty years ago and had been his colleague in the tower for thirty. The second chair had read an old master's letter asking whether the oldest rite in the kingdom might be made safer, and passed it on without comment, and put nothing on paper that could ever be shown to anybody.

That was how the Circle said a thing. By not saying it. Armand had taught three generations of mages how to read what the Circle did not say.

He read it now. It said that he had become somebody it was not safe to write to.

The footing went round the circle that autumn, and it was the finest thing he had ever seen done with earth.

He watched it from a chair. Imani had forbidden him the flat for a month after the fire in the winter and he had gone anyway, and Ferra had entered it, and after that he had stopped arguing about the chair. They put it on a cart at the foot of the ridge every morning, a plain kitchen chair with a blanket over its back, and he sat in it with his stick across his knees and watched them.

There were forty-one of them by the middle of autumn. Thirty-one who had signed in the long room, and ten more who had come in the weeks after in twos and threes up the Kaldo road with his letters in their coats, and signed in the same room and been shown the same flask. Twenty-six earth mages. They worked in pairs along the footing line, a pair every two hundred paces round the whole circuit, which was a little over two miles and took a man most of a morning to walk.

And they raised it.

He had taught most of them how. He had never seen them do it at scale, because there had never been a scale. A mage stood at the edge of the trench, which had been dug down through the pasture and the clay to the grey rock beneath, the width of a cart, and put his hands flat on the ground at its lip, and drew. And the ground came up. Not fast. The clay and the stones and the gravel along the trench's sides and floor came up slowly into the trench, heaving, shifting, packing themselves together, going from loose to dense, the way a pile of sand goes dense when you beat it, but beaten from the inside by a man who could feel every grain of it. It came up out of the trench and stood along its line a yard wide and a yard high, packed hard as brick, and it did not come up any further, because it was not meant to.

Then the mage stood up and walked twenty paces on and knelt down again.

And behind him the men came. That was the other half of it. Two thousand men on the flat, and every one of them who was not at the quarry or the kilns or the sawmill was on the footing, behind the mages. They came with the ridge's own grey stone off the rail spur and the mortar from the kilns, and they laid the first courses of the footing's face along the packed earth where the mage had been, dressed stone on the rock, bedded in lime, and set it. Masons first. Labourers after, with barrows, with rammers, with the lime.

The earth magic lifted the ground into the line of the wall. The ordinary work set it. And the ordinary work stayed. If every mage on the flat had fallen down dead at noon, the footing behind them would have stood, because it was stone and lime on rock, laid by men with trowels, and it did not know there had ever been a mage there.

That was the prince's rule, and Armand had heard him say it at the table in the spring, and had thought it a small thing, a mason's rule. He sat in his chair on the cart at the foot of the ridge and watched it go round the circle, and understood that it was not small at all.

They were spent by noon. Every one of them.

That was what the work was. He had told them so in the long room. An earth mage raising footing at this rate, all morning, came to the end of himself by the time the sun was high. He could feel it happen along the line. He did not need to see them; he could feel it in the medium over the flat the way a man feels the weather change, the whole line of them drawing, drawing, and then the drawing thinning, pair by pair, like a fire going down.

And then the flasks.

They came out from the quarry sheds at noon on a handcart under a cloth, forty-one of them, one for each, in a rack, and Hobb or Dace walked the cart along the footing line and gave one to each mage and wrote the name and the flask's mark in a book. The mage sat down on the new stone of the footing where he had just raised it and held the flask against his chest and turned the lid.

And filled. In the time it takes to drink a cup of water. Armand felt each flask open along the line, each one a small sharp sigh in the medium, and felt each man fill, and knew exactly what each of them was feeling, because he had felt it himself on a landing above a lake in the winter and on the deck of the Hod in the spring. It was the most pleasant thing in the world, for as long as it lasted.

And then they stood up and worked the afternoon.

He had worked it out at the table with Ferra, before the first of them came. A mage spent at noon and sent to bed did half a day. A mage spent at noon and filled from a flask did a whole day. It was the difference between closing the footing before the frost and closing it next summer. It was two thousand men not standing idle on the flat for half of every day waiting for the mages to get up. It was the whole of it.

And it was a flask of what went into boys on the table in the fourth hour, in a man's hands, a lid's turn from his heart.

He counted Bastian's.

He had not meant to. He had not meant to count anybody's. He had told himself, when the prince stood up in the long room and said I'm not going to watch you, I can't, that he would not watch either. It was not his to watch. They were grown men and women, most of them past forty, and they had been told plainly what it did and what it cost, and he had sat beside the prince while they were told.

But he sat in his chair at the foot of the ridge every day with nothing to do but feel the medium, and on the fourth day he felt one flask open along the line, at noon, in the right place, with the rest. And then later, in the afternoon, near the fourth bell, when nobody should have been spent, he felt the same small sharp sigh in the same place along the line. Where Bastian Farrant was working.

He did not say anything.

He felt it again the next day. And the day after that. And on the tenth day he felt it three times: at noon, and in the middle of the afternoon, and at the end of the day, just before the carts came.

Bastian was not an earth mage. He was a water mage, and there was no water in the footing for him to lift. He had been set to the drains instead, the first quarter's mains in their long straight trenches, and he worked there alone, at the pace he chose, lifting the thaw water and the ground water out of the trenches ahead of the pipe layers so they could lay the clay pipes dry. He was very good at it. He did more than any two men with pumps. He did more every day than the day before, and he came to the carts at evening grinning and talking, big and fair and loud, with his sleeves rolled up, and said he felt twenty-five.

Armand counted for three weeks.

At the end of the three weeks he had Hobb's book brought to him on the cart, and read the column, and saw that Hobb had written a flask against Bastian's name three times on most days, and four on two, and that Hobb had written it the same way he wrote everything, without comment, because nobody had told Hobb to count.

He closed the book.

He sat in his chair for a long time with the book on his knees and his hands on the book. He thought about the prince in the long room, saying what you do with it is yours. He thought about the woman at the end of the table, the Chancellor, entering things. He thought about his own hand on a flask on the landing above the lake, turning the lid, knowing the rate. He had done it once. He had done it for the market corner and the people in the lofts. Bastian was doing it four times a day for the pleasure of feeling twenty-five.

He said nothing for three weeks.

On the evening of the twenty-second day he had the cart stop by the drains on the way back to the island. Bastian was standing on the edge of a trench with his hands on his hips, watching the pipe layers, and he turned and saw Armand in his chair and grinned and came over.

"Master."

"Show me your arms," said Armand.

Bastian's grin did not change. He held out his arms, both of them, big and brown from the summer, with the sleeves pushed up past the elbow. Armand looked at the inside of each forearm in the evening light, from the wrist to the crook of the elbow, slowly.

There was nothing. Smooth skin. Fair hair. A burn scar from some old cistern accident.

"You're taking four," said Armand.

"Some days."

"The prince said one. When you're spent."

"I'm spent four times," said Bastian cheerfully. "That's the thing, Master. I get spent, and I fill, and I go on, and I get spent again. I've never done so much in my life. Look at it." He waved his hand at the drains going away down the flat, dry, with the pipes in them. "The prince said stop when you feel full. I stop. I always stop. I know when I'm full."

"You know when you're full," said Armand, "at the fourth hour?"

Bastian looked at him.

"There is a room at the tower," said Armand, "that you've stood outside on feast days, as I have, while the names were read. The boys on the table in that room know when they're full, too. They feel it very clearly. And then they go on being filled, because nobody stops the pouring, and the fourth hour is when the full turns into the other thing." He did not raise his voice. "You're a grown man. You've been told. I'm not the prince and I'm not your master any more and you can take a flask every quarter hour if you like. But I'm telling you as the man who taught you to lift water: one. When you're spent. Not four."

Bastian was quiet for a moment. It was the first time Armand had seen him quiet since the carts came down the ridge.

Then he grinned again, not quite as wide.

"One," he said. "When I'm spent."

"When you're spent."

"I'll mind it," said Bastian, and went back to the trench.

Armand had the carter drive on. He did not look back. He knew he would feel the flasks open along the line tomorrow at noon, and he knew he would feel for the fourth bell, and he did not know which he was more afraid of: feeling it, or not.

He flew with the Hod again in the last week of autumn, against Ferra's no.

He had asked her first. He always asked her first. She had said no in the spring and she said no again now, at the table, in front of everybody, with the same flat reason as in the spring: he was near eighty, his heart had been read by Imani and found wanting, the Hod had no rail to speak of and went two miles up at night, and if she lost her charge at the top there was a reserve and then a flask and then a very long fall. And then she had said, as she had said in the spring, that her veto bound the prince and not him, and that he could go if he chose, and that she would enter it if he did.

He chose.

He went up with Ned Tallis and Garrick and the boy Wes off the far end of the lake after the last bell, on a night with no moon, the Hod lifting off the black water with her racks empty and going up in the dark with no light on her at all. It was the fourth time he had flown with them since the spring. He had flown every time his hands went bad enough that he could not hold a pen, which was not often, and every time Ferra had entered it.

The band took them at a little under three miles.

He felt it the way he always felt it. As if the air had turned to warm water, and he had stepped down into it, and it had closed over him. The medium up there was not like the medium on the ground. It was thick. It was everywhere. It pressed on the skin like a warm bath and went in through the skin and filled him up slowly and gently, not like the flask, not at the rate of a lid, but at the rate of breath. His hands went still on the rail. He watched them go still.

And the room opened.

He had not tried. He had not meant to. He was standing at the Hod's low rail in the dark with the racks filling behind him and Ned Tallis at the helm humming, and he was thinking about nothing, about the footing, about the line of it going round the circle two miles below him in the dark, and it opened round him the way a door opens that has been leaned on from the other side.

His room. Not the prince's. His.

He knew it the way he had known his own hands at twenty. It was a study. It was very like the study at the tower where he had worked for forty years: a long narrow room with a window at the end, and shelves, and a table under the window, and on the table a stone and a cup of water. The assessment table. The one where every boy had stood in front of him at fifteen with a stone in one hand and a cup in the other, waiting to be told.

He stood in it for what he knew was a very short time outside and a long time in.

It was his. Nobody had opened it for him. The prince was in Kaldo, asleep in the keep. It had opened because the medium up here was thick enough to lean a door open, and it had not needed the prince at all.

Then the Hod dropped a little in a cold pocket of air and Ned Tallis said something to Garrick, and he was standing at the rail again in the dark with his hands on it, still.

He tried again, at once. It opened again. He let it close. He tried again. It opened.

Every time. Up here, in the band, it opened every time he asked.

It would not open on the ground.

He tried the next morning in the keep's east room, with his hands still quiet from the night, at the table in the window with the door shut and Imani's girl outside it and nobody else. He sat with his hand on his band and thought of the study at the tower, the long room and the window and the table with the stone and the cup. He saw it perfectly. He had seen it every day of his life for forty years. He had just stood in it a dozen times, two miles up, in the dark.

Nothing.

He tried for an hour. He tried by seeing it, and by not seeing it, and by thinking of nothing, and by thinking of the footing. Nothing.

And Bram could.

He had watched Bram do it at the long-room table, at the end of the summer, when the table asked him to show it: Bram sitting at the end of the table with his hand on his band and his eyes shut, and his face going still, and then empty, for as long as it took Ferra to count to forty, and then coming back. A blacksmith. A man who had told the table himself that he could not picture a face, who could not draw, who had never been able to see a thing that was not there. Bram had sat at a bench in the dark and thought about a boiler, one stay at a time, and opened a room on the ground, alone, in the middle of the night.

And Armand, who had assessed a thousand boys, who had spent fifty years in the tower and six in a locked box proving that the tower was wrong, whom everybody at the table had expected to be the first of them to find his own way in, could not open his own room on the ground in his own study in broad day.

He was furious.

He had not been this angry in thirty years. He sat at the table in the window with his hands, which had started again now, braced on the wood, and he was so angry that he could feel it in his face, hot, the way it had been when he was a young man and a master at the tower had told him that his work on the lift stones was clever and of no use. He was angry at the room. He was angry at the band, for showing it to him and taking it back. He was angry at Bram, which was unjust and which he knew was unjust, and that made him angrier.

He was angry because it was the same thing. It was the same thing he had done to boys at fifteen for forty years. He had put a stone in a boy's hand and a cup of water in the other and told him, middling, and the boy had gone away believing it, because the grey robe had said it. And here was a blacksmith who had never been told anything by anybody about what a room was supposed to look like, and so had built one out of what he knew, and walked in. And here was the grey robe, who knew exactly what a room was supposed to look like, and whose knowing was the door that would not open.

Ferra came up at noon with her book.

She did not knock; Imani's girl let her in. She stood by the table in her grey coat and opened the book at a page near the back, a page he had not seen, and did not sit down.

"You flew," she said.

"I flew."

"Against my no."

"Yes."

"I've entered it." She looked at the page. "That's four since the spring. The eleventh of the late spring. The second week of summer. The third of the early autumn. Last night." She read them off in her flat clerk's voice, the dates and the hours up and down, off Ned Tallis's log, which he gave her every morning because she had asked him to. "Four flights, eleven hours and a quarter in the band in all, by the boy's glass. Each one against a no given at the table and entered."

She closed the book.

"I'm not going to stop you," she said. "I can't. I'm telling you the count, because somebody should know it besides me, and it ought to be you."

Armand looked at her. He was still angry. He knew his face showed it and he could not help it.

"Four," he said.

"Four."

"Did you enter why?"

"I entered what Ned said. That last night you stood at the rail and were very still for a long while, stiller than the other times, and that the boy thought you'd gone to sleep standing and Ned told him to leave you." She looked at him for a moment longer, and something in her face changed, not much, and went back. "I didn't enter anything else. I don't know anything else. Do you want me to?"

"No," said Armand.

She nodded, once, and went out, and shut the door behind her.

He sat in the east room all that afternoon and into the evening.

His hands shook on the table. The light went off the window and the girl came in with a lamp and set it down and went out again. The footing was nearly closed, two miles south, on the rock, stone and lime, with a gap of a few hundred paces left at the east side where the ground went soft by the marsh, and the frost was coming, and they would close it in a week. The eleven words were in the box in the cupboard. Bastian Farrant was on the flat, or in his lodging on the east bank, with his big smooth arms, taking one flask when he was spent, or four.

He put his hand on his band.

He thought of the study at the tower. The long room, the window, the table, the stone and the cup. He saw it. He had always been able to see it.

Nothing.

He took his hand off the band, and sat for a while, and put it back.

He tried again. And when that did not work either, he thought, for the first time, of the table. Not the room. Only the table. The wood of it, which he had rested his forearms on for forty years, and knew the grain of, and the ink stain at the left-hand corner where he had upset a pot in his first year as a master, and the burn by the right-hand corner from a lamp, and the four brass corners, one loose. He thought about the loose corner. He had meant to have it fixed for thirty years.

Nothing opened. But he stayed there, with his hand on the band and his eyes shut, thinking about a loose brass corner on a table in a room at the far end of the kingdom, and did not stop, and did not open his eyes, for a long time.
