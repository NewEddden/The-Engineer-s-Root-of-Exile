Chapter 163 - Voice on a Wire

The first telephone in Randillia hummed, and said nothing, and Tess took it apart on the line room bench and did not speak to anybody for two days.

She had built it out of what was there. That was how she built everything, and it was what she liked best about building: the line room's spare parts, the works' scrap, the powerhouse's broken things, and whatever Emeric had told her about the thing in the other life that he could not build himself and she could. He had told her about this one in July, at the bench, in a quarter of an hour, with a pencil. A voice was air shaking. Put a thin plate in front of a mouth, and the voice shakes the plate. Put something behind the plate that changes how well it lets a current through when it is squeezed, and the plate squeezes it as it shakes, and the current down the wire shakes too, in the shape of the voice. At the other end of the wire, a coil round an iron core under another thin plate, and the shaking current pulls the plate, and the plate shakes the air, and the air is a voice again. That was all. Two plates and a wire.

The thing behind the plate was the hard part. Emeric had told her what it had been in the other life and Tess had found it in a bin in the powerhouse in an afternoon: carbon. The arc lamps' carbons, the stubs of them, burned down to a finger's length and thrown away by the dozen every week. Ground fine, to grains like coarse black sugar, and packed loosely in a little brass cup behind the plate, so that when the plate pressed on them they pressed together, and let more current through, and when it eased off they eased apart, and let less. A carbon microphone. He gave her the name and she wrote it in the data book and underlined it, the way she underlined anything she meant to take apart.

The first one hummed. That was all. One instrument in the line room at the top of the keep and another at the works across the river, joined by a pair of new copper wires on the telegraph's poles, with a battery of the powerhouse's cells at the keep end; and when anybody spoke into either one, the other gave back a long low hum like a bee in a jar, and nothing else.

Tess took it apart. She sat at the line room bench for two days with its pieces laid out in front of her on a cloth, with her lamp pulled down low and her data book open, and tried every piece of it against every other with the galvanometer, and said nothing to Emeric or to anybody; and on the evening of the second day she poured the carbon grains out of their brass cup onto a sheet of white paper and looked at them through Ivo's glass, and said, aloud, to the paper: "Too fine."

She had ground them to dust. Dust packed solid, and did not press and ease; it sat. She ground the next lot coarser, and shook them through a sieve of the works' finest brass gauze and kept what stayed on it, and packed them looser. And she found the other thing, too, by herself, without asking: that the plate at the speaking end wanted to be thinner than she had made it, as thin as a leaf, and the plate at the hearing end wanted to be iron and not brass, because the coil could not pull brass.

The second one spoke.



It spoke on the fourth of September, at a little after four in the afternoon, and Emeric was not there for the first words. Tess had wanted it that way. She had built the thing, and she wanted to hear it first, alone, with nobody watching her face; so she sent Bram's boy across the river to the works with a note for the man at the works end, and sat at the line room bench with the hearing piece to her ear, and waited.

Emeric was on the keep stair when he heard her shout.

It was not a word. Tess did not shout. In six years he could not remember her raising her voice to anybody, and this was not raising it. It was a high wordless sound, half a laugh, that came out of the line room door and down the stair; and he went up the stair two at a time and stood in the line room doorway, and she was sitting at the bench with the hearing piece pressed to her ear with both hands and her eyes shut and her whole face lit, and she did not see him.

"Say it again," she said, into the speaking piece. "Say anything."

She listened.

"He's counting," she said, without opening her eyes. "The man at the works. He's counting to ten." Her mouth moved with it. "Six. Seven. He sounds like he's in a barrel." She laughed, short and helpless. "He sounds like he's in a barrel at the bottom of a well, and I can hear every word."

She opened her eyes and saw Emeric in the doorway.

She held the hearing piece out to him.

It was a cup of turned brass on a cord, with an iron plate across its open end as thin as a leaf, and it was warm from her hand. He put it to his ear. And out of it, small and far and hollow and humming, as if a man were talking to him from the bottom of a well with a bucket over his head, came a voice; and the voice said, eight, nine, ten, and then, after a pause, in the tone of a works man who has been told to say anything and cannot think of anything: is it doing it?

It was doing it.

He stood in the line room with the brass cup at his ear and a man's voice in it from across the river, from the works, half a mile off, through a wire on poles, and for the space of three breaths he was not in the line room at all. He was on a train in the other life, with a thing in his pocket that did this and a thousand other things, that he had used every hour of every day for twenty years and never once thought about, and thrown away when it broke, and bought another. He had never once in that life stood and listened to a voice come down a wire and felt it in his chest.

"Who do you want?" said Tess.

He looked at her.

"On the other end," said Tess. "It's built. It works. There's a man at the works who's been counting to ten for a quarter of an hour, and he's bored, and he's not who you want." She had her data book open again already. "So who do you want?"

He opened his mouth. Then he shut it.

It was a real question. It was, he thought, standing there with the brass cup in his hand, a question that a man in his position ought to have had the answer to before he came up the stair, and he had not thought about it at all. There were two of them. The terms were four, and the first of the four was that nobody was first; and the first voice on the first telephone in the world was a first, whatever else it was.

Tess watched him not answer. Something went across her face that he could not read.

"Ferra's at the works," she said. "I asked her to go over at three. To read the meter on the battery at that end while I tried the line. Because she reads a meter better than anybody, and because she'd want to be the one who wrote down that it worked." She did not look up from the data book. "I didn't ask you who you wanted then. You weren't here." She turned a page. "I'll send the man for her."



It took the works man a quarter of an hour to find her. Emeric stood in the line room with the brass cup in his hand for all of it, and did not put it down.

Then the voice in the cup changed.

It was Ferra's. He knew it in the first word, small and far and humming in its barrel, and it said, quite briskly, in the voice she used for suppliers: "Is this the line room?"

He put the speaking piece to his mouth.

"Yes," he said.

A pause. Humming.

"Who is that?"

"It's me."

A longer pause.

"Oh," said Ferra's voice, in the barrel. It sounded different. He could not have said how; the barrel took most of it. "It's you."

Behind him, at the bench, Tess picked up the second hearing piece, the spare she had hung on the line to listen for faults, and put it to her ear, and opened the data book at a fresh page, and dipped her pen. He did not see her do it. He was looking at the wall.

"Can you hear me?" he said.

"I can hear you. You sound like you're in a barrel."

"So do you."

"That's not a very nice thing to say to a woman."

"You said it first."

"I said it about you. That's allowed." A pause. The hum. "Tess says it works. I'm to read the meter. It's reading," a pause, "a little low."

"Tess is here."

"I know Tess is there. Tess is always there." Another pause. "Why are you there?"

He did not know what to say to that. He had a great many things he could have said, and he had had, he realized, a whole quarter of an hour in which to choose one, standing in the line room with the cup in his hand while a works man went looking for her across the yard; and he had spent the quarter of an hour looking at the wall. He said the first thing that came into his head, which was what a man says when he has been asked a question by a woman he cannot see and has nothing ready.

"I wanted to hear your voice," he said.

The hum.

"What?" said Ferra.

"I said I wanted to hear your voice."

"The line's bad," said Ferra's voice, in its barrel, quite steadily. "There's a crackle. I didn't hear. Say it again."

There was no crackle. He could hear the line quite clearly; he could hear, behind her voice, the beat of the works engine across the yard, faint and far. He stood in the line room and understood, all at once, what she was doing, and that she knew he understood, and that she was going to make him say it again anyway.

He said it again. It was worse. It came out of him slower, and louder, and with a stop in the middle of it, as though he were reading it off a page he did not trust, and it filled the line room the way the first time had not, because he had not been listening to himself the first time and now he was.

"I wanted," he said, "to hear. Your voice."

A long pause. The hum. The far beat of the engine.

"Well," said Ferra. "Now you have."

And the line went quiet, all at once, the hum and the engine and everything, which was the sound, Tess told him afterward without looking up, of the hearing piece at the works end being put down on the bench very carefully by somebody whose hands were not quite steady.



Tess said nothing that evening. She said nothing at supper, where she sat at the end of the table in her chair and ate her stew and looked, Emeric thought, very faintly pleased about something, in the way she looked when a weld came out clean. Ferra came in from the works at seven with her book under her arm and her face entirely composed, and sat down, and ate, and talked to Bram about the coal for the second battery, and did not look at Emeric once, which was itself a thing he noticed every second of.

Tess said everything the next morning, at the long table, at the second bell, before the business.

She had the data book. She opened it at the place, and laid it flat, and put her finger on the page; and in her flat small voice, the voice she used to read a dial, she said that she would like to report on the first trial of the line between the keep and the works, for the record, as the table had asked her to report on every trial since the dynamo.

"Fault log," said Tess. "Line one. The fourth of September, from twenty-eight minutes past four." She read it. The battery's charge at the start. The meter at the works end, which had read a little low. The carbon cup's packing. A loose joint at the works end, found by the second listener, which had lost seven words in the first exchange; the seven words were noted as lost. "The rest of the exchange," said Tess, "as heard on the second piece, for the record of the line's quality."

And she read it.

All of it. Every word, his and Ferra's, from is this the line room to now you have, in order, without expression, exactly as she had written it in the data book at the bench with the spare piece at her ear. Including both times. Including the stop in the middle of the second time, which she had marked in the log, with a small careful dash, as a fault in the speaker and not the line.

The table listened to all of it.

Bram looked at the ceiling. Saban put his hat over his face. Armand, at the end, opened his eyes for the first time that morning and did not close them again. Ward did not do anything at all, which from Ward was a great deal. And Ferra sat at her place with her book open and her pen in her hand and her face very slowly going red from the collar up, a deep slow red that started at the white of her throat and went up and up past her freckles until it reached her hair and there was no telling where the one stopped and the other began.

"End of log," said Tess, and shut the data book. "The line works. I'll want copper for a second pair to the powerhouse, and cells. Ferra has the figures." She folded her hands on the data book. "That's all."

Nobody said anything.

Then Imani, across the table, who had listened to the whole of it with her chin on her hand and her eyes on Emeric, took her chin off her hand.

"When it reaches the hospital," she said, to Tess, very pleasantly, "I'll want my turn on it." She looked at Emeric. "And you'll do it better."

Ferra made a sound into her book that might have been a laugh and might have been something else entirely, and turned a page that did not need turning.



She found him on the river wall that evening, at dusk, where the lamps were coming on.

She stood beside him at the parapet with her hands in her cloak and looked at the river. She did not say anything about the line room, or the long table, or Tess's log; and he did not either, because he had decided at the long table that if he ever said anything about any of it again he would say it better, and he had not yet worked out how.

"It's a strange thing," said Ferra at last. "A voice. Coming down a wire. From somewhere you can't see." She was looking at the telegraph poles going away north over the fields, and the new pair of copper wires slung under the old ones, toward the works. "I've been thinking about it all day. In between being angry with Tess."

"Are you angry with Tess?"

"Not really." She was quiet. "She asked me to go over at three. She'd been planning it since July. I worked that out at about midnight." She looked at the wires. "I've been thinking that it's like the room. A little. When you take me in. You're there, and I can't see you, and your voice comes from nowhere, and it's you."

He did not say anything.

"Except that in the room you've got your hand on my arm," said Ferra. "You've always got to be touching. That's the rule. Contact." She turned her head and looked at him. "And this doesn't need anybody touching. Just a wire." She looked at him a little longer, in the way she looked at a column she thought was wrong and had not yet found where. "You could do it without the wire. Couldn't you."

He opened his mouth to say no.

He did not say it. He stood at the parapet and thought about it, honestly, the way she had asked it, and found that he did not know. The room needed a stone, his; that was the first thing anybody learned about it. And it needed contact. It always had. A hand on a forearm, a palm on a shoulder; he had never once taken anybody in without touching them, and never thought about why, because it was the rule, and it was his rule, and he had written it down in the first summer. Contact. Consent. Nothing forced.

But what was the touch for?

He had his sense out, now, half the time without thinking. Thin, on the wall at night. He could feel Ferra beside him at the parapet with it, the small close steady note of her, as plainly as he could feel the cold stone under his hands. He was touching her now, in a way. Not with his hand.

"I don't know," he said.

Ferra looked at him a little longer. Then she nodded, as though he had said something that agreed with a sum she had already done, and went back along the wall walk toward the keep, and down the water stair, with her cloak round her.



He tried it that night with Ward.

Not with Ferra. He thought about that on the wall after she had gone, and decided it before he had finished thinking it. She had had the first voice on the first wire that afternoon, in front of the whole table, read out of a fault log; and a man who had just been told across a table that he would do it better next time had no business doing a second first thing with the same woman before the week was out. And Ward was in the six, and had been in the room alone, and his note was the one Emeric knew better than any other in the world, slow and even, a wall.

Ward did not ask why. He stood in the keep yard at midnight with the gate shut and the lamp on the mounting block, where Emeric put him, and waited.

Emeric had his mother's stone in his left hand. He put his right hand on Ward's shoulder.

That was easy. That was the room, the ordinary way: the stone, and the touch, and Ward's note under his palm, and the door of the room there in the dark between them, ready to open. He did not open it. He did not want the room. He held the door the width of a crack, the way a man holds a door against a wind to speak to somebody on the step without letting them in, and through the crack, without moving his lips, he said, to Ward, one word.

Ward.

Ward did not move. But Emeric felt it arrive. And a breath before it arrived, he felt Ward feel it coming, a stillness in the note, like a room when somebody in it has just stopped talking.

"Yes," said Ward, aloud.

Emeric took his hand off Ward's shoulder. He held it a hand's width away. He tried again.

Nothing. The door was not there. Without the touch there was no door at all, only Ward standing in the lamplight a hand's width from his fingers and the cold of the yard between them, and the stone in his left hand doing nothing. He tried for a long breath, and then another, and it was like reaching for a cup in the dark that was not on the table.

"Nothing," said Ward.

"No."

He stood with his hand in the air a hand's width from Ward's shoulder and thought about the wall walk, and Ferra's note beside him at the parapet that he had felt as plainly as stone, and her voice coming down a wire from somewhere he could not see.

He let his sense out. Not wide. Thin, and short, the width of a hand, no further: out of his fingers and across the gap to Ward, the way it went out over the dark town from the wall, a breath on a cold morning. It touched Ward's note. The slow even wall of it, that he had known since before he had known there was such a thing as a note, that his body had known in the corridors of the palace when he was a boy and Ward was his knight and nothing else. It touched it. And he did not ask the door to open. He leaned on it, very gently, the way Paskin leaned on a bank of clay, and waited.

It gave. The width of a hair.

Through it, without moving his lips, he said: Ward.

He felt the stillness in Ward's note first, the room going quiet a breath before. And then he felt the word arrive, small and far, as if it had come down a very long wire, and Ward's head turned, very slightly, toward him, though Emeric's hand was not touching him and his mouth had not moved.

Ward stood in the lamplight and looked at Emeric's hand, a hand's width from his shoulder, not touching it.

"Again," said Ward.

Emeric leaned on the hair-width again. It was harder the second time, the way things were after the first, before they got easier; it cost, a dull pull behind the eyes, more than the sense alone ever cost him. But it gave.

Bed, he said, through it.

Ward looked at him for some while in the lamplight. Then he did something Emeric had never seen him do, in six years of dawns: he laughed. Once, short, out of his nose, like a horse.

"Yes," said Ward, aloud. And then, after a breath, with his mouth shut and his face perfectly still, and the stillness coming first, small and far, down the width of a hair from the other side:

Yes.
