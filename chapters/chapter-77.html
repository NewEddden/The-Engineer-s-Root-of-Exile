Chapter 77 - The Most Lawful Man

The chancery's southern desk was a counter in a corridor.

Emeric had expected a room. He had expected, if he was honest, the kind of room the crown kept for receiving things, with a clerk at a high desk and a seal on a chain. What there was instead was a long stone corridor on the ground floor of the chancery, cold as a cellar, with a counter let into one wall behind a grille, and a line of men waiting at it with papers in their hands, and a brazier at the far end that warmed nobody. Above the grille, painted on a board in letters that had been repainted so often they stood up from the wood, it said SOUTH.

He stood in the line. Ward stood behind him.

The man in front of him was a factor from somewhere on the coast with a complaint about a harbor due, and the man in front of the factor was a priest with a petition about a bell. It took the better part of an hour. Nobody in the line looked at Emeric twice, and he was not sure, standing there with the war in an oilcloth packet under his arm, whether that was the best thing that had happened to him in the capital or the worst.

The clerk behind the grille was young, and had ink to the second knuckle of his right hand, and a cold.

"Matter?"

"An account of a private war," Emeric said. "Fought on the land of the barony of Kaldo, last May, by Grayrock and Milford. For the crown's notice."

The clerk took the packet through the gap under the grille and opened it, and turned the pages over one at a time without reading them, counting them, and wrote something in a book on his left.

"Kaldo," he said, and sniffed. "Kaldo, Kaldo." He turned round on his stool to a set of pigeonholes on the wall behind him, ran his finger along a row, and stopped at one. "That'll go in with the others."

Emeric said nothing for long enough that the clerk looked back at him.

"The others?"

"Grayrock's widow sent one in." The clerk had his finger in the pigeonhole and was working a bundle out of it, tied with tape, not very thick. "In the spring. Lady Bryda. And there's Wayford's, came up in the summer, the baron's own hand, very fair." He laid the bundle on the counter on his side of the grille, put Emeric's packet on top of it, and began to tie the tape round all three. "Says it was Kaldo started it, Wayford's does. Grayrock's widow says it was her husband. Which it would have to be, wouldn't it, him being the one that died." He looked up, and appeared to hear himself, and the cold made his face go a deeper red than it was already. "Not that I've read them, sir. That's not for this desk."

"No," said Emeric. "Of course not."

"They're for the court."

"Which court?"

The clerk opened his mouth and closed it.

"Whoever brings it, sir," he said finally. "Nobody's brought it."

He tore a slip from a block, and wrote on it, and pressed a small round seal into the corner of it, and pushed it out under the grille. Received at the southern desk of the chancery, on such a day, one packet, an account of a private war, from the barony of Kaldo, so many leaves. The clerk's initials. The seal.

Emeric picked it up and looked at it.

It was the first piece of paper the crown had ever given him in exchange for one of his. It was the size of his palm. The seal was crooked. He held it as if it might get away.

"Ferra will want this," he said to Ward, outside, in the street, in the cold.

"Ferra will want it framed," said Ward.



The summons came to the inn that afternoon in a judiciary clerk's hand, and it said four o'clock, and it did not say the magistrate would be pleased.

The chamber was the same chamber. Long table, hard chairs, the clerk at his side desk with his pen already wet, and the windows set so high in the wall that there was nothing in them but a square of grey. It was colder than it had been in August. Somebody had lit a fire in a grate at the far end of the room and the room had not noticed.

Keller was at the table. He was a little older, or the winter made him look it, grey at the temples and a shade greyer above them. His papers were squared at his elbow. He did not stand. He looked at Emeric and at the satchel and at Ward, who had stopped against the wall by the door as if the wall had been put there for him, and then back at Emeric, and said, "The barony of Kaldo," in the tone of a man reading the heading of a page.

"Magistrate."

"Sit down."

Emeric sat. He opened the satchel and took it all out and laid it on the table one piece at a time, in the order Ferra had packed it, which was the order Ferra thought it ought to be read in: Hale's twenty-one lines in oilcloth, the knot sealed. Ferra's page, copied fair, with its single empty line and the 21 and the rule under it. The second page, the one with the other column, everything Kaldo had sent anywhere else in two years and every answer that had come back. Vantic's four lines. And last, because Ferra had said put it last and let him come to it, Saban's lamp-oil bill, turned over, with the dot and the two roads and the blot.

Keller read every sheet.

He read them the way he had read in August, all the way through and without looking up, turning each page face down on the table on his right as he finished it, so that the pile on his left went down and the pile on his right went up and nothing at all happened on his face. When he came to the end of Hale's twenty-one lines he went back to the start of them and read the dates again. When he came to Vantic's four lines he read the signature. He held Saban's bill a long time, and turned it over, and read the lamp oil, and turned it back.

The fire made a small noise at the far end of the room. The clerk's pen did not move.

Keller squared the right-hand pile and set it down.

"What do you want me to do?" he said.

"The crown's post on the south road is losing every letter this barony sends to the crown. Not some. All twenty-one. Through one house, at Wayford." Emeric put his finger on the blot. "I want it stopped. I want someone to find out who's doing it, and why, and I want it known that the barony reported its war, three times, by two roads, and that the crown didn't hear because somebody made sure it wouldn't."

"And you want that from me."

"You ruled on Wexler on the paper. Nobody else would have."

"I ruled on Wexler on the paper," said Keller, "because there was paper." He laid one hand flat on the right-hand pile. "This is not paper. This is a list of things that did not arrive. You have shown me, very clearly, that you sent twenty-one letters, because a steward who writes a fair hand says so in his own book. You have shown me that no answer came, because your clerk says so on a page she ruled herself and has kept on a shelf. You have shown me that letters you sent elsewhere arrived, because the people you sent them to answered. And you have shown me a merchant's drawing of a road on the back of an unpaid bill." He did not raise his voice at any point. "What you have not shown me is a single thing that happened to any one of those letters, on any day, in any place, done by any person. You have shown me a hole, prince. The edges of it are very well drawn. It is still a hole."

It was what Emeric had known he would say. He had known it in Hale's office with Saban's bill under his hand, and on the road, and in the line at the southern desk. Knowing it did not make it pleasant to hear in that voice in that room.

"You're persuaded," he said.

"I am persuaded." Keller's tone did not change. "I am not convinced. Those are different words, and I am paid to know which one I am using. Persuaded is what a man is when he goes home and does not sleep. Convinced is what a court is when it rules." He looked at the blot on Saban's bill. "A pattern is not evidence. It is the shape of the evidence you do not have."

"And your war," he went on, before Emeric could answer, "is in the corridor. I sent for it after your note came. There are three accounts of it in one pigeonhole at the southern desk, and they disagree, and the aggressor is dead, and nobody has brought it to a court, and nobody will rule on it until someone does. If you bring it, I will hear it. A widow who says her husband began it. A baron at Wayford who says you did, and who wrote within a month of the fighting. And you, who wrote nothing that arrived for seven months, and come up the south road in December to tell me it was not your fault." He let that sit for exactly as long as it needed. "I would ask you why. You would tell me about a house at Wayford. I would ask for the evidence. You have shown me what you have."

The clerk's pen moved, once, and stopped.

Emeric sat back in the hard chair.

He thought about it as he would have thought about a part that had failed in testing, which was the only way he could stand to think about it in that room. The war was not lost. The war was waiting on the post. Every road to the war went through the hole, and the hole could not be argued, it could only be filled.

"Then tell me what would be enough," he said.

Keller looked at him for a little longer than the question required.

Then he leaned back, very slightly, which was more than he had moved in the whole of the afternoon, and spoke to a point on the wall somewhere above Ward's head, in the voice of a man reading out a form he had not written and did not much like.

"Something dated," he said, "against a thing outside your own house. Not your steward's book. Something a man who does not belong to you wrote down on the day, with the day on it."

Emeric did not reach for a pen. He did not need one.

"Paired. Every item you send, you send twice, by two roads, so that when one of them goes into your hole the other one is somewhere it can be found. Not a copy made afterward. A twin, made on the day, that can be laid beside the first."

The clerk at the side desk had stopped pretending to write.

"Attested. By people with nothing to gain from the matter either way. Best of all, by people who do not know what they are attesting to, because then nobody can say they were bought." He paused. "And long. A year, at the least. A pattern of a month is bad luck, and a pattern of a season is a bad winter, and I have heard both argued by men I would not trust to hold my coat. A year is a pattern." He brought his eyes down off the wall. "Bring me a year and I will look at it. Bring me eleven months and I will send you away and tell you to come back in four weeks, and I will not be sorry."

"Dated, paired, attested, a year," said Emeric.

"I have said nothing." Keller turned to the clerk. "Strike that. All of it, from 'something dated.' The magistrate offered the barony no advice." He looked back at Emeric. "I will not say it again, and I will not write it down, and if you tell anyone in this city I said it I will say that you misheard a tired man in a cold room. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

"Good." Keller picked up his own pen, which was the end of the interview, and held it without dipping it. "You may take your papers."

Emeric gathered them up in the order Ferra had packed them, and put them in the satchel, and buttoned it, and stood. Keller did not stand. He did not look up from whatever he was not writing. It was, Emeric thought, exactly how he had been dismissed in August, and he had been grateful for it then too, though it had taken him most of the road home to know it.

He had his hand on the door when Keller spoke again.

"The drawing."

Emeric turned. Keller had not moved, but his eyes had gone to the satchel, to the corner of Saban's bill where it stuck out between Hale's oilcloth and the strap.

"That waypoint," Keller said. "Wayford. The house by the ferry." Something went across his face and was gone, too quickly to read, like a man remembering the name of a debt. "I know that waypoint."

He did not say anything else. He dipped his pen, and wrote, and the interview was over again, more finally this time, and Emeric went out with Ward into the corridor and down the stairs and out into a street where it had begun, very lightly, to snow.



They walked back to the outer ring because neither of them wanted a hired chair, and it was a long walk, and the snow did not settle.

Emeric said it over to himself on the way, in time with his feet, until it was worn smooth. Dated. Paired. Attested. A year. It was a specification. It was the most useful thing anyone in this city had ever said to him, and it had been said by a man who had then struck it from the record and threatened to call him a liar if he repeated it, and Emeric found that he liked Keller a great deal more for the second part than the first.

"He knew it," he said. "The house at Wayford. He knew it before I showed him."

"Yes," said Ward.

"How?"

Ward walked on for half a street.

"He is old," he said, "and he has been reading paper in that room a long while." He stepped round a carter's dog asleep in a doorway. "Old men know a great many waypoints."

Which was true. And was, Emeric noted, in exactly the voice Ward used for the horses being fine.

At the inn the woman at the hatch said there had been nobody asking for them. Emeric went upstairs and sat on the bed with the satchel on his knees and took out the chancery's slip and looked at it by the window: the crooked seal, the clerk's initials, the date. A thing written down on the day, by a man who did not belong to him.

He had come seven hundred miles to be told he had nothing, and he had been handed the first piece of it at a counter in a corridor before he ever reached the room.

He put the slip away very carefully, in the inside pocket of his coat, where Armand's letter was.
