Chapter 77 - The Second Column

The second column had been started three years ago as a kind of mercy, a place to put the things he was not allowed to do. It listed what each frozen project would need on the day the freeze lifted. It had never listed what any of them would cost against the others, because for three years there had been no others. Everything on it had been equally impossible, and there was a comfort in that which he had not noticed until it was gone.

He read it out at the table on the shortest day of the year, with the fire lit and the shutters half open on a sky the colour of pewter, and nobody interrupted him, which was worse.

The port. Fletcher's yard. The Grayrock road as far as it went, and a good deal further than it went. The glassworks, with the condition written against it in the back of Ferra's ledger in his own words. Imani's hospital. The sealed working. The Wayford leg of the relay, which was already being built and was already over its number. He read the lines in the order he had written them, which was the order he had thought of them, which was no order at all.

Then Ferra read hers, and hers was the only one that counted.

She did not stand. She had a single sheet, and on it was the year: what four baronies would bring in if the ore came down at the rate it was coming down and the grain went up at the rate it was going up and nothing burned, less what they already owed to the people they already owed it to, less the relay, less the finishing shop that had gone up in the autumn on an order book three years old. What was left she had written at the bottom, once, and underlined once.

"Two," she said. "Two things of any size this year. Three if one of them is small and nobody gets ill. Not seven."

"Two."

"You can have your seven. You can have them over four years. You can't have them this spring." She laid the sheet square to the edge of the table. "I'm not telling you which two. That's yours. I'm telling you the number, and I'm telling you it doesn't move because you look at it."

He looked at it anyway. It did not move.

Vantic sat at the far end, where she sat whenever an expenditure above the line was going to be argued over, a right she had held since the loan and now held for as long as her house cared to. She had a small book open and a quill in her hand and she had said nothing at all. When numbers were said aloud she wrote them down. She had written down Ferra's.

"The hospital," Imani said.

She had waited for Ferra, which was courtesy, and she had not waited a moment longer, which was Imani.

"I have fourteen beds in a building that was a grain factor's store until a year ago," she said. "It's dry and it has a door that shuts, and I was glad of it, and I said so. It is not a hospital. The water comes in by bucket. The fever beds are two yards from the lying-in beds because there is nowhere else to put them, and I scrub between them, and I lose women I would not lose if there were a wall." She did not look at her notes. She did not have any. "Last winter I lost six people in that building to things the building gave them. That's not a guess. I kept the register. I can show you which beds."

"I've read it," Emeric said.

"Then you know." She turned her hand over on the table. "And the other thing on that list the same size as a hospital is three streets at the bottom of the barony for a man who builds boats."

Nobody looked at anybody.

It was a fair way to put it, and it was also exactly true. In the autumn, with the covenant a line through a ledger and the notebook open for the first time in three years, he had ridden down to the lower water and walked the three streets of Fletcher's village end to end, which he had never once done in nine years of knowing precisely where Fletcher was, and he had stood on the beach and named them to the old man before the old man had to. They would be drained and bedded and dry by the time the yard needed carts on them. He had said so. He had not written it down anywhere, because it had not felt like the kind of thing a man writes down.

"It's a road contract," he said. "Forty men at the lower water have had no work since the boats stopped needing hands, and they'll have none this winter either unless somebody gives them some. The streets are work. The yard can't take a single timber in through that mud, so no streets, no yard, and no yard means the port stays what it is, which is a beach with a history."

"And a promise," Imani said.

"And a promise."

"A promise made privately to a shipwright," she said, "is not a reason to put off a building that stops people dying."

"No," Emeric said. "It isn't."

He let that stand for a moment, because it deserved to.

"Here's the reason. Nobody in Kaldo has ever built a hospital. Nobody in four baronies has. I don't know what one looks like on the inside."

"I do."

"Then it's in your head, and it has to be on paper before anybody lays a stone. Where the water comes in and where it goes out. Which rooms can't share air. How many beds, and which beds are never to be two yards from which. I can give you forty men and a season tomorrow and they'd build you a large, dry, expensive chandlery." He looked at her. "The streets have a plan. They've had one since the autumn. The hospital has a woman who knows what it has to do and hasn't had a year to write it down, because for three years there was no point."

Imani held his eyes for some time. It was the look she gave a wound when she was deciding whether the man attached to it was lying about how it had happened.

"You're right," she said. "I don't like it, and you're right."

She did not argue further. She had never once argued past the point where she lost, and it was the thing about her he found hardest to be in a room with.

"Then I want a date," she said. "Written. Not when there's room. A day."

"Ground broken at the thaw after next."

Ferra had it down before he finished. Imani reached across and took the sheet from her, read the line, and slid it down the table to him.

"In your hand," she said.

He signed it. She took it back and read his signature as if it were a dosage, and folded the sheet once, and put it inside her coat.

The glassworks took less time, because Bram had already done the work of failing at it.

He set a piece of glass on the table between the ink and the bread. It was the size of a hand and the colour of weak tea, and it had a crack running across one corner and a spray of tiny bubbles caught in it like frogspawn, and there was a long ropy streak through the middle where the melt had not mixed.

"That's the best of forty," Bram said.

He had built the trial furnace in the autumn, in the lee of the foundry wall, to Emeric's drawing and then to his own corrections of it: a squat bottle- shaped chamber of Colm's firebrick, a grate, a tall stack, charcoal and nothing else. No crystal, no stone, no mage pouring himself into anything. It had got to heat on the third day, which was more than anybody had expected. It had held that heat for an hour at a time and then gone wherever it liked. The melt had come out seedy and corded. And the forty trial pieces, which had been set to cool in a long brick tunnel with a fire at the hot end, had come out the next morning with thirty-one of them cracked straight across.

"The furnace is sound," Armand said. "I measured it on the fourth and the seventh day. The draw is good and the chamber holds." He said it with the air of a man who wished to be on record before anybody was blamed.

"The furnace is sound," Emeric agreed.

He turned the glass over. He knew, in the way he knew most useful things, without a source he could name, that glass had been made on wood fires for longer than anybody had been writing anything down. The fire was not the problem. The problem was that feeding a fire so that it sat at one colour for a day and a night was a trade, and so was taking a gather of melt off the end of an iron and turning it into anything but a lump, and nobody in four baronies had either trade, and neither of them was a drawing he could make.

"Read it," he said to Ferra.

She did not need to find the page. "A furnace that can hold a heat and let it fall, on charcoal and a good stack, with no stone in it. The day there is one, the works. Until then, the school glass is a one-off, and it stays a one-off."

"Hold and fall," Emeric said. "We can make it hot. We can't keep it anywhere, and we can't bring it down without breaking what's in it. That's not a furnace. That's two men I haven't got." He set the glass down. "It doesn't start."

Saban said that glassblowers did not come south, as a rule, in the voice of a man who had once tried to buy some.

"Then it waits until one does."

It was the first time a condition he had written himself had stood between him and something he wanted, in front of the whole table and a factor with a quill. He had failed in front of people before. He had once broken three machines in a week in front of half the barony, and had been cheerful about it, because finding where they broke had been the point. This was a different thing. This was his own sentence, in Ferra's ledger, in his own words, saying no. He found, a little to his surprise, that he was glad he had written it. Without it he would have built the glassworks this spring on crystals and two exhausted mages, and called it temporary, and it would have been temporary for twenty years.

Vantic wrote something down. He did not ask what.

"That's the streets," Ferra said. "And no glassworks, and the hospital at the thaw after next. You've one more."

"A lathe."

The word went round the table and came back to him without having attached itself to anything.

"A turner's lathe," Bram said, after a moment, carefully, the way a man repeats a word in a foreign market to check he has heard it. "Treadle and a pole. For chair legs."

"Iron, not wood. A bed that doesn't move and doesn't bend. A spindle that runs true in its bearings. And a rest that holds the cutting edge." Emeric put his two hands flat on the table a foot apart. "On a pole lathe the man holds the chisel, so what comes off is as round as the man is good. On this, the machine holds it. What comes off is round because the machine is round."

"What does it make?" Saban said.

"Nothing I can sell."

There was a small pause, of the kind that happens when a merchant has been given an answer he believes to be a joke and is waiting for the rest of it.

"Nothing," Ferra said.

"Parts."

"Parts of what?"

"Of everything else on that list." He turned the notebook round so the column faced her, though she did not need it. "The yard needs iron fittings that are the same from one hull to the next. The glassworks, when it comes, needs a furnace door that shuts true. The heads on the water were built fixed because a head that turns needs a bearing and we couldn't make one. Every engine anybody will ever build in this barony needs a round thing inside a round hole with nothing between them but oil. At the moment round is a property of Bram." He did not look at Bram. "Bram is very good. There's one of him. I want round to be a property of a machine, so that the man at the machine only has to be careful."

"You want me to put a thing that makes nothing on a line she'll read." Ferra did not so much as glance towards Vantic, which was how everybody at the table knew who she meant. "Ahead of the hospital."

"Put it under plant."

"I'll put it under what it is."

"It is plant."

"It's a promise that something will be plant eventually," Ferra said, "and I don't enter promises. I enter what's standing in the shed."

He found he was enjoying this, which was not a useful thing to be doing with money he had only just stopped not having, and he went on doing it for a sentence longer than the argument needed.

"Then enter it the day it stands," he said. "And enter what it cuts after that under whatever the thing it cut becomes. You'll find the lathe in every line of your book in two years and it'll never have made a thing on its own."

"That," said Ferra, "is exactly the sort of sentence I don't enter."

Imani had said nothing since the date. She said, now, to nobody, that it was going ahead of the hospital, and it was not a question, and he did not treat it as one.

Vantic closed her book on her quill to keep the page.

"What does it return, Your Highness?"

"Nothing, this year."

"And next year?"

"Everything, eventually. Nothing you can find in a single line."

She considered that with every sign of taking it seriously. "My house will ask what it is."

"Tell them it's a tool."

"They'll ask what for."

"Tell them I said everything."

She opened the book again and wrote that down, in full, apparently word for word. He had not expected her to, and he could not tell from her face whether it was a joke on him or on her house or on neither, and she did not offer to explain.

He had lost the room. He could feel it, the way he had felt the drain in the room above Kaldo a month ago, a slow steady going-out of attention from six people and a factor who had understood every word he had said and not one thing he had meant. He could draw a lathe. He could not draw why a machine that made nothing belonged ahead of a building that stopped women dying of their own children, because the reason was twenty years long and ran through things none of them had seen yet, and the only way he knew to show it to them was to build it and wait.

It was Bram who spoke, in the end, and not to the table.

"The thing you showed me," Bram said. "Up there. That turned."

"Yes."

"That comes off this?"

"Not off the first one. The first one cuts the second. That comes off the second."

Bram looked at his own hands on the table for a while. They were large and burned in several places and very steady, and they had put an axle through a hole and greased it and accepted what came back for thirty years.

"Then you'd better have it," he said.

It was not a vote. Nobody at that table took votes and nobody had ever pretended they did. But Ferra looked at her father for a long moment.

He had about the length of that look, and he used it.

He had found it nine days ago, going back over a sentence he had said out loud at that table and not listened to while he was saying it. It gave about the width of a hair. He had told six people about the third time he leaned on Ferra, and had filed the whole business as a failure for eight years, and somewhere on the stairs afterward it had occurred to him that a door which gives the width of a hair is not a door that failed to open. He had spent three evenings finding it again and two more finding out what would go through it, and the answer was nothing at all except sound. He had not tried it on a person. There had never been a moment worth discovering it did not work.

He reached for her and stopped at the first inch.

Nothing arrived. The room stayed exactly where it was, the fire in it, the bread nobody had eaten, Bram's hands flat on the wood. There was only more of it, in the direction, and the direction was the width of a hair, and nobody was going anywhere through that.

He shaped the words in his throat, behind his teeth, the way a man reads a hard line to himself.

Write the name. Don't write what it's for.

Ferra's hand did not move on the pen.

That's new.

Yes.

How new.

Nine days.

A reason isn't an entry anyway, she said, after a moment. I was never going to write one. Don't take that as you asking and me agreeing.

I'll take it however you like.

You'll take it as I said it. Then, in the same breath, without any change in it: I felt you come.

Before the sound?

A moment before. A pause the length of a pause. I've felt that for years. I took it for the room going quiet. So did the rest of them, I expect, and none of us had a name to put on it. Her chin came up perhaps half an inch, and she was looking at the sheet. Now I know what it is, I'll know every time. So will they, once somebody tells them. You haven't got a way of arriving unannounced.

Good, he said, and found he meant it, and found immediately afterward that it was also the single most useful limit on the thing and he had not been the one to notice it.

He let go. It shut the way a held breath goes.

At the far end of the table Vantic looked up from her book, at him, for slightly longer than a woman looks up at nothing. She did not write anything down. That was somehow worse.

And then she wrote the lathe on the sheet under the streets, in the column for the year, and drew her line beneath the two of them.

"Two," she said. "That's your two."

Imani stayed after the others had gone.

She did not do it on purpose, or not so as anybody could tell. She was slow collecting her things, and then she was standing at the window looking down at the frozen edge of the Eastern Branch, and then the room was empty except for the two of them and the fire and the smell of the bread nobody had eaten.

"The Order," she said, without turning round. "Health for four baronies. That's what it says I am."

"It is."

"Then everybody in four baronies is mine to worry about." She turned. "You haven't slept since before the fire was lit. You've the colour of that glass, and you signed my date like somebody was holding your wrist."

"I'm not your patient."

"You're on the roll." She picked up her bag. "You signed that too."

She went out. He heard her on the stair, unhurried, and then he did not hear her, and he found he was still looking at the door some time after there was nothing left to hear. He put it down to the hour and went back to the notebook.

The letter from Grayrock came down the river road that evening, with the ore tallies, in a hand he knew.

Bryda wrote the way she spoke, which was as though every sentence had been costed before it was allowed out. She did not open with greetings. She opened with the number.

The shallow workings had given a fifth less since harvest than they had given the harvest before, and the harvest before had been down on the one before that. She had walked the galleries herself, twice, with the captain, and the captain agreed with her, which she noted was a thing he had not always been willing to do. Renner had taken the easy seams for twenty years, fast and shallow, and had spent what came out of them on men rather than on the mine, and the men who had known the lower ground best had gone up Kaldo's road behind him and had not come back down it. She wrote that sentence exactly as plainly as the others. She did not make it a grievance. She did not make it anything. It was a line in a specification, in the place where it belonged, and he read it three times.

At the present rate, the shallow ground would carry the foundry through to next winter. Not to the one after. She had set out her figures below so that Ferra could check them, and she expected Ferra would.

She did not ask him for anything. She had never once asked him for anything that was not already hers.

He read it twice more, standing by the fire, and then sat down with it.

The foundry had outgrown the road on the day the second furnace was lit. He had known that since the autumn and had been pleased about it, because a road could be widened, and he had widened it. What he had not seen, and what Bryda had seen from the other end with her own feet in the galleries, was that the road had quietly outgrown the veins. He could move more ore down that road than Grayrock could now pull out of the ground to put on it.

He was not short of money. For the first time in nine years he was very nearly not short of money at all, and here was the same feeling anyway, the same tightness in the hand, the same arithmetic that would not come out. It was throughput. It felt exactly like being poor and it was nothing like it on paper, and he had spent the whole morning telling six people and a factor that a machine which made nothing was the most important thing on the list, and none of them would have been surprised to learn that he had not seen this coming.

There was other ore in that hill. He knew precisely where it was, and precisely how deep, and precisely what the air was like at the bottom of it.

He opened the notebook to the second column and wrote Grayrock at the foot of it, under the lathe and the streets and the hospital's date, and did not draw a line beneath it. It was the first thing in that column he had not wanted. It was simply going to happen, whether he wanted it or not, at the rate of a fifth a year.

Ward had come in without knocking, as Ward did, and was standing by the door, not reading the letter, which with Ward meant reading Emeric's face instead.

"You'll want to go up," Ward said.

"Not yet."

"When?"

Emeric looked at the column for a long moment, at the lathe and the streets and the empty space beneath them where the year's money had run out.

"When I've got something to go up with," he said.
