Chapter 356 - Trial Water

Zale heard the whale before he heard the reef.

He had been sleeping aboard his own ship on the marsh side since the end of January, moored to four pegs in the lee of the dunes where the castaways' hut had stood, because somebody had to be near the Catch when it went and Emeric had a city to run. Two nights in three he slept there. On the third he flew back over the hills to the bay and slept in a bed, and Tess told him in the morning that he smelled like the bottom of a net.

On the night of the fourth of February the wind was light out of the south and the tide was making. He was awake with the hatch open, because he never slept well at a mooring, and he heard the blow out on the water: a long hoarse breath and the slap of a fluke after it, a mile off or more, north-west, on the Pull's track. A big animal, going down the coast alone, the way they did in winter.

It blew again, nearer. He sat up.

The third time was wrong. It came too quick on the second, the breath of something that had been under longer than it meant to be and come up in the wrong place. Then the water past the reef began to break white in the dark, in one spot, over and over, and the sound came across the lagoon a little after the sight of it, a heavy, muddled smashing like a cart going over in a ford. It went on a long while. Zale stood on his deck in his shirt and listened to it, and there was nothing to be done, so he did nothing.

Some time before light it stopped.

At low water he walked out along the reef with a boathook for the pools. The whale lay across the broken wall of the old pen on its seaward side, half out of the water, longer than a fishing smack, grey and white and crusted with barnacles along the jaw. One eye was open. He had seen whales killed in the north by men with lances, and he had eaten whale, and none of it had ever troubled him much. This troubled him. It had been doing nothing but going south.

Emeric came over at ten on the Hod with Fletcher and the guest. The guest came down the ladder on her own feet, in the grey cloak with the hood up, and walked out along the reef behind Zale as carefully as a woman on ice. The thin water had her on legs and kept her there. She went past him to the whale, and down off the wall into the pen to her knees, and put her hand flat on its side under the eye. She held it there a long time. Zale didn't ask. Whatever she was doing, it wasn't his.

Emeric went the other way, to the upper end of the Catch where it broke the surface in the middle of the pen like the back of a sleeping animal, and stood with his palm on the wet stone.

"Dry," he said.

"All of it?"

"You couldn't pull a punt with what's left." He took his hand away. "It spent everything getting that onto the wall."

Zale looked at the whale, and at the woman beside it. "Six weeks, then."

"About six. I'll read it every week." Emeric wiped his hand on his coat. "She's not ready, Zale. The nose isn't seated. It hasn't been down."

"Then she's got five weeks to be ready in," said Zale, "and one to get home." He took his book out of his coat and wrote it on the day's page: Caught, 4th, night. Home by 15 March. He ruled a line under it, the way Tess drew boxes.

Fletcher had come out along the wall behind them with his hands in his pockets. He stood looking at the whale with the face Zale had seen on him in the yard when a good piece of oak came in.

"We'll have the oil off her," he said. "You don't waste a whale."

Nobody argued with him. The guest came up out of the pen with her cloak wet to the waist and stood on the wall beside Zale, and said one word in her own language, low, that he didn't know. He didn't ask that either.



The nose went down on the ninth with nobody behind it.

The outer ply had come out of the big kiln whole on the second, in its box in the night book, and Tess had the three of them laminated in the five days after. Celluloid went between the plies. Between the outer and the middle went the shard-dust layer, and between the middle and the inner a loop of fine wire laid round the rim in a groove, so that if either ply cracked through, the loop would part and a bell would ring. The stack sat in a seat ring on a test frame Fletcher's men had made from a spare length of the pressure hull: a steel drum the height of a man, blanked off at the far end with plate, the nose on its cone and gasket at the near end, and the berth's own air sealed inside it on the day they closed it.

Two gauges went down with it, Bourdon gauges, a curled tube that straightens as the pressure comes on and turns a hand. One was inside the drum, to say if anything came in. One was outside in a cage, to say how far down it went. Tess had given each a second hand that the first pushed round ahead of it and then left behind, so the gauge would remember the worst it had seen. The yard called it the tell-tale.

Zale's ship took it out slung under the cargo winch to the nearest deep water, east of the south headland, where Fletcher's soundings ran off the end of his line and the sea went from green to something nearer ink. Tess came in her chair, lashed to a ringbolt on the cargo deck. Bram came because it was his ring. Emeric came because he had drawn the thing thick and Tess had made it thin, and he had signed the corner.

The bell sat in a box at Tess's elbow, wired to a pair of lines taped down the length of the cable.

Fletcher's men lowered away a little after nine. The drum went in with a fat slap and lay on the swell, and then the weight took it, and it went down out of the light, the nose last, a pale eye going green and then gone. The deck crew called the paint marks on the cable as they ran over the sheave. Emeric opened his sense down the wire after it. He couldn't see the drum. He could hear the shard dust between the plies, very faintly, the way he could hear a dustcrete wall if he leaned on it: a note, not a sound, that the whole web held together.

At the fourth mark the bell rang.

"Stop," said Tess.

The winch stopped. Everybody on the deck looked at Emeric, because they knew he was listening and they didn't know what he heard.

The note was the same. Lower than at the surface, tighter, the way a rope sounds when it takes a load, but whole.

"It's in one piece," he said.

"The bell says it isn't."

"The bell's a wire," said Bram.

"So's the loop." Tess had her hand on the bell box as if she could hold it shut. "Can you hear one break before it breaks?"

Emeric thought about lying to her, and didn't. "I've never heard one break."

"Then I'll have the bell."

Zale had gone to the rail. He was looking down at the cable where it went into the water, with his head on one side.

"Your wire's turning," he said.

They all looked. It was, very slowly: the new cable unlaying itself under the dead weight, a quarter turn and back, a quarter turn and back, and the taped lines going round it in a spiral that got tighter at every mark.

"New wire off a drum always does, with a weight on it and nothing to stop it. It'll have twisted your lines till one went." Zale looked at Tess. "That's your bell telling you what it was told."

Tess said a word Emeric hadn't known she had. They brought it up. It took an hour, and when the drum came out of the water and swung in streaming over the deck, the inside hand was on its pin, the nose was clear, the loop between the plies tested whole, and one of the two lines was snapped clean through at the sixth mark down.

They went again the next morning with a bronze swivel off Zale's own mooring cable shackled in above the drum, and the lines taped on loose, with slack at every mark. Zale had the swivel off and the cable rove before Fletcher had finished his breakfast. He didn't say anything about it.

This time the bell stayed quiet. The marks went over the sheave and the crew called them, past the fourth, past the sixth, to the one Tess had painted black on the cable before they left the bay, and there Fletcher held it by his watch for an hour while the ship rode the swell above it. Emeric sat on a coil of rope with his eyes shut and listened to the note go down and down and then hold. Nobody talked much. Bram ate an apple, all of it, core and pips.

Somewhere in the second half of the hour Emeric thought about the ship he'd named her for, which had sounded the deep with a weighted line and a great deal of patience, a long way from here. He thought its crew would have liked Tess.

When the drum came up, Tess had them bring it alongside her chair before anyone touched it. She leaned in to the cage with her stick across her knees and read the outside gauge aloud, for Fletcher's book.

"Tell-tale's past the black. A hair past." She moved to the little window in the drum's end. "Inside hand's on the pin. Hasn't moved."

Bram put his thumb on the gasket where it met the nose's rim. It had squeezed out a little, all the way round, even.

"She went tighter," he said. "Like you said she would."

"Like the drawing said she would."

"Like you said," said Bram, who didn't give credit to drawings.

Emeric looked at the nose. It was wet and cold and perfectly clear, and he could see the inner face through it, dry as a window in a parlour.

"Thin," he said.

"Thin," said Tess, and wrote the hour.



The Cradle took her down on the morning of the twelfth.

The nose had gone into her seat ring the day before, off the test frame by the Cradle's sheerlegs, with every hand in the berth holding his breath and Fletcher talking the whole time in a low, even voice, the way a man talks to a horse. Now the Cradle's rim stones let the slab down into the bay, so slowly that Zale, on the berth's shore end with the guest, couldn't see it go. He could only see the water. It came up over the deck's edge and across the stone, round the keel blocks, up the Challenger's black flank to her lowest port, and then she stirred on the blocks and lifted off them, and the blocks were under her and she wasn't on them any more.

She floated. She floated down by the head.

Fletcher had worked out on paper that she would, and been right to the inch, which annoyed him, because he had hoped to be wrong. Three plies of glass and a bucket-steel seat ring sat in her bow, and every other heavy thing in her had been put where it fitted. His men pumped the forward trim tank dry and went aboard with pigs of iron and carried them aft one at a time, down the hatch and along the passage, and Ned Tallis stood on her deck with his bad knee braced on a cleat and watched the draught marks on her stem and her stern until they read the same.

"She sat like a shot duck," he said, coming ashore. "Now she sits like a boat."

Zale went aboard at noon. He had his place on the trial crew because he knew what the sea sounded like, Emeric had said, and nobody else in Kaldo did. Tess had built him a set to listen with: one of her telephone ears, a carbon microphone in a sealed bronze housing bolted to the hull outside, below the waterline, with its line run in through a gland to a headset at a little desk in the corner of the control room. A hydrophone, Emeric called it. Zale called it the ear, and so did everyone else by the end of the week.

Ned took her out of the bay on the diesel, on the surface, slow. Zale stood with him on the little bridge on top of her tower with the cold wind coming in round the headlands and the diesel thumping under their feet, and before they cleared the bay mouth he went below and had Noll Finch show him the stone-keeper's station.

Noll showed him. Every dial was down on its stop but one. The flasks of liquid mana were in their iron locker with the lid dogged.

"All cold," said Noll. "All but the latch."

"And it's spent," said Zale. "The Catch."

"Emeric read it."

"Spent's a thing he reads," said Zale. "Cold's a thing I can see." He looked at the latch dial, the one needle off its stop. "That one stays warm?"

"That one has to. If it goes cold, the weights go." Noll had read the stone-keeper's sheet four times, the way he read everything. "She keeps one stone warm, and it's the one that lets go."

They went round the south headland wide, by Zale's word, on a track that kept the whole bulk of the island between them and the Catch's line, and came into the headland's lee in the early afternoon. The swell fell away there. The water was long and grey and quiet, with the mountain standing over it and nothing beyond it in any direction but the sea. Nobody would see a boat here who didn't already know it was coming. The Hod hung overhead with Emeric and Tess aboard and the marker buoy's telephone line on a reel at her rail.



Ned's rule on the Hod had always been four aboard and never fewer. For this he took six. Himself at the helm and the planes. Bram aft at the motor and the battery, because it was his steel. Fletcher at the ballast manifold, because he had built her. Noll at the stones. Imani in the sick bay door with her bag, because she had looked at the cabin and said nobody was taking it under the sea without her in it, and nobody had found a way to stop her. Zale at the ear.

The hatch came down and Fletcher dogged it, and the air in the boat changed. It got close. Zale had been on a great many decks, but there was nothing over this one but steel.

Ned called down the boat, and every station answered him.

"Upper hatch."

"Shut and dogged," said Fletcher.

"Air intake."

"Shut."

"Diesel."

"Stopped," said Bram, from aft. "On the motor."

"Stones."

"Cold," said Noll. "All but the latch. Latch is warm."

"Bilge."

"Dry."

"Ports."

Fletcher went down the row of little gauges between the plugs with a lamp. "All on the pin."

"Ear."

Zale had the headset on. "The swell on the headland. Nothing else out there but us."

Ned looked round the control room once, at each of them, and his eyes stopped on Imani, who looked back at him with her bag in her lap.

"Open the vents," he said.

The air went out of the ballast tanks with a roar Zale felt in his back teeth, and the sea came in behind it. She went down by the bow, gently, and Fletcher bled the forward trim and she levelled, and Ned took her down to where the periscope just broke the surface and stood with his face against the eyepiece for a long while, turning it.

"Hod," he said. "Buoy. Headland." He stepped back. "Take her down."

The hand on the depth gauge began to move.

What Zale heard, mostly, was the boat. Tess had said it would be. The motor sang aft, a high steady whine that came through the hull and through the ear both. The fans ran. A pump knocked somewhere under the deck. Bram's boots went along the plates. And the hull talked: a tick, and a long low groan, and a tick, as the rings took the sea on them, and every time it groaned Fletcher's head came up like a man hearing a child cry upstairs.

"That's her settling," said Bram's voice, from aft. "That's all that is."

"I know what it is," said Fletcher.

Under the boat, out in the water, there was a dry crackle like fat in a pan that Zale had heard through a hull every winter of his life and never known the cause of. A seal somewhere, a long way off, complaining. The surf on the headland had gone to a slow breathing. He listened to all of it and found it all where it ought to be, and then, under the motor's whine, there was a hiss.

The sea didn't hiss like that. He lifted the headset off one ear and the hiss was still there, in the room, faint, behind him.

"That's us," he said. "Aft. Low."

Bram was already moving. Zale heard him go down the passage, and then heard him say something short and very ugly in the motor room, and then shout.

"Gland! The shaft gland!"

Where the propeller's shaft went out through the stern there was a packed gland, flax and tallow under a follower, the same as every stern tube Fletcher had ever built. At the surface it had wept a few drops a minute, the way they all did. Down here the sea had found it, and it wasn't weeping. It was spraying, a hard white fan off the turning shaft across the motor room, and the water was coming down off the frames into the bilge under the deck plates, under the battery.

The angle came on her. Zale felt it in his chair before he saw it: the stern going down, the bow lifting, the whole long boat beginning to tilt and slide backward into the dark the way a cart slides off a bank.

"Stop the motor," said Ned. "Blow aft. Full rise on the planes."

Fletcher's hands went across the manifold. Air roared into the after tank. It slowed her and didn't stop her, because the water that was making her heavy wasn't in a tank. It was in the boat.

Then Zale smelled it, and so did everyone else, because they had all heard the story of the fourth of January, and Imani had been there. It was sharp and greenish and it caught at the back of the throat like a hook. Salt water had got to the cells.

"Masks," said Imani.

She said it the way she'd have said it in a ward, not loud, and she was already up and moving down the room. The leather masks hung by every seat on their hooks. Zale got his on. Ned got his on at the helm without taking his other hand off the planes. Noll got his off the hook and dropped it, and went down after it, and came up with it on and coughing hard behind it, and Imani was there with her hand flat on his back until he stopped.

Aft, Bram and Fletcher were in the spray with spanners, taking up the gland's nuts a flat at a time, turn for turn, so the follower would go in square. Zale could hear it through the deck: the clank, and Bram swearing, and the clank. The spray came down to a fan, and then to a jet. It didn't stop. The spanner slipped once and Bram's knuckles went into the follower's edge, and he swore again and kept turning.

The depth gauge's hand was still creeping round. Slower. Not stopped. Under them the floor of the lee shelved away toward the deep water where the nose had gone down on its cable, and that drum had been a drum. This was a hull with a gland in it.

"Emergency blow," said Ned.

Fletcher threw it. Every flask in the boat dumped into every tank at once, and the noise of it filled the hull so completely that for a little while there was nothing else, not the motor, not the gland, not the ear. The hand on the gauge stopped.

It stopped, and stood, and then, very slowly, began to creep on.

Ned didn't look at anybody. "Weights," he said.

Noll had his hand on the latch stone's cord already. He lifted the stone up out of its socket, the way Bram had done it on the Cradle's deck in January, and Zale heard the bellows sigh even through the mask.

The catch let go with a bang that went through the whole boat from the keel up. Some tons of iron dropped away under her. She came up.

She came up hard and stern-heavy and wrong, with her bow cocked at the sky, and everything in the control room that wasn't fixed went aft along the deck in a slither: the night book, a lamp, Imani's bag, the ear's spare headset. Zale went into his desk with his ribs. Ned's bad knee hit the helm's column and he made no sound at all except to breathe in. Then she broke the surface stern first and lay over on her side, far over, so far that Zale had his hand out flat on the bulkhead beside him, and hung there, and came back, and rolled the other way, and came back, and lay rocking in the swell like a log.

Fletcher had the hatch open before she'd finished rolling. The air came down it cold and grey and full of the sea, and it was the best thing Zale had ever breathed.



Nobody died. Imani made sure of that before she let anybody up the ladder, going down the boat from Ned to Bram with her own mask pushed up on her forehead, looking in eyes and listening to chests. Noll would cough till the weekend. Bram's knuckles wanted four stitches and got them on the deck in the wind with the Hod's shadow going over them. Ned's knee swelled like a loaf and he wouldn't let her look at it until everyone else was done.

"It does that," he said.

"Then I'll look at what it does," said Imani, and did.

The motor room had sea in it to the deck plates. The battery had eleven cells poisoned with salt, and the rest needed draining and washing and filling again. The drop weights lay somewhere on the floor of the lee, under water nobody would ever see the bottom of, and the iron would have to be cast again at the island's forge. They came home on the diesel at walking pace with the after hatch open and the fans running, and the Cradle lifted her out at dusk, and Bram stood in the motor room in his wet boots with his bandaged hand and looked at the gland for a long time without touching it.

The fix came from three people in two days, and none of them was Emeric.

Noll said it first, on the Cradle's deck the next morning, still hoarse, with Tess's drawing of the ports open on his knee. He had been reading it, four times.

"Why isn't the shaft like the windows?" he said. "Two plugs, and a gauge between. If the outer one goes, you know, and the inner one holds."

Fletcher looked at him a long while. Then he went down into the boat and drew a second gland on the bulkhead in chalk, inboard of the first, with a little box between the two and a drain cock on the box, so the first gland could weep into it and the second would stay dry, and a man could see at a glance which was which. Bram drew a sill round the battery well, a hand high, and lead trays under every cell, so that water in the bilge would have to climb before it got to them. Tess put a float in the motor room bilge that rang a bell at the helm when it lifted, out of the same parts as the nose's loop, and Ned wrote a new line in his crew book under the old ones: Bilge bell, every dive.

Ferra came across on the run on the fourteenth and wrote the weights into her book. Zale saw the column. It was the dear one again.



They went down again on the twentieth, into shallow water this time, in the lee, with new iron in the cradle and the second gland in and dry and the bell silent at the helm. Ned took her down by the book and laid her on the sand where Zale said the sand was, and they sat there on the bottom with the motor stopped and the fans off, and did the two drills Tess had written on the board for this day and no other.

The first was the candles. Ned had Fletcher shut off the oxygen flasks, and Bram took the lime canisters out of the fan trunk, and they sat in the still air and let it go stale on purpose. It didn't take long. In an hour the lamps looked dimmer than they were and everybody had a headache, and Noll, who was still coughing, had started to yawn.

"Candles," said Ned.

Breath candles, Emeric's word. Each was a cake of grey stuff the size of a brick in an iron can. You struck the fuse on the lid, and the cake burned from the top down, slow and hot, and gave off clean air as it went. Bram lit the first in its tray. It hissed, and glowed orange through the vents of the can, and inside a quarter of an hour Zale's head had cleared. The tray under it went brown at the edges, then black, and Imani looked at it and then at the deck under the tray, and said that it was going on a stone slab next time, and that she would like to be told who had thought an iron tray was enough. Nobody told her.

The second was the trunk.

The escape trunk was a steel chamber the size of a wardrobe, under the after hatch, with a hatch below into the boat and a hatch above into the sea. It was meant for two. Folded into it with his knees against his chin and Noll's elbow in his ear, Zale thought it had been drawn for two people of about Noll's size.

"Breathe out," Imani said up through the lower hatch, before Bram shut it. "All the way up. Don't hold it. The air in you swells as you go up, and if you hold it, it'll burst you from the inside. Breathe out the whole way, and when you think you've nothing left, breathe out more."

"Aye," said Zale.

"I mean it."

"I know you do."

Bram shut the hatch. They flooded the trunk from its own valve, and the sea came up cold round Zale's boots, then his knees, then his chest, and the air left above it got tighter and tighter until his ears hurt, and he swallowed and they cleared, and he swallowed again. When the water was at his chin and the pressure inside the trunk matched the sea outside it, the top hatch would move. Noll put his shoulder to it and it went.

Zale went up the buoy line hand over hand into green water going grey, letting the air go out of him in a long string the whole way up, the way she'd told him. It went on much longer than he'd thought it would. He came out into the February air beside the marker buoy with the cold like a slap and the Hod hanging over him, and Noll came up a breath after with his hair in his eyes, coughing and laughing at once.

Imani had them out of the water and wrapped in blankets on the Hod's deck before either of them had stopped shaking, and listened to both chests, and said they'd do.



Emeric heard about the trunk that evening in the berth's drawing room, from Imani, who told it as a complaint.

"Up a rope," she said. "Off the bottom. On one breath, letting it out the whole way, with me shouting it through a hatch, because if they'd done it the way their bodies wanted to do it they'd have come up with their lungs torn." She sat down on the bench by the stove and put her wet boots up on the fender beside Tess's chair. "That's the way out of the boat. That's the plan. Hold your nerve and don't hold your breath."

"It's the plan on every boat like her," said Emeric.

"Then every boat like her has a bad plan."

Tess, who had drawn the trunk, didn't argue. She had the drill board on her knee and was rubbing out the second drill with her thumb, slowly, as if she wanted it to take a long time.

"The divers had flasks under the Isle," said Noll, from the other bench. He was still hoarse. "In the cave. Ward and Rylan and that. Why can't the trunk have flasks?"

"It could. A flask's twenty minutes, if you breathe it in and out into the sea. Less if you're frightened." Emeric looked at the stove. "And everybody's frightened, coming out of a boat on the bottom."

He laughed then, not much, and Ferra, at the table with the weights still open in front of her, looked up.

"What?"

"I didn't plan any of this." He waved a hand at the room, the board, the drawing of the Challenger pinned over the stove with her black flank and her glass eye. "Water. I never planned for water. I planned ships, and the ships were for the air, and the only thing I ever asked of them on the water was that they float. That was the mundane line. If a stone fails over land, you come down hard and maybe walk away. If it fails over the sea, and the hull doesn't float, you drown in your own cabin. So they float. That was all the water I thought I'd ever need." He rubbed his face. "And now I've got a boat whose whole job is to sink, and two men going up a rope out of her, and a guest at the end of the slab who breathes the stuff."

"You'll want a better rope," said Fletcher, from the door, and went out again.

Imani didn't laugh. She was looking at her own hand on her knee, the way she did when she was seeing something else.

"Her gills don't work the way you'd think," she said.

The room went quiet.

"I've had my sight in them every morning since November. You'd think they'd be big. They're not. They're small, for what she is. They're not doing it by being big." She turned her hand over and drew on her palm with one finger, two lines side by side. "The blood goes one way through them, and the water goes the other. Against each other. So the freshest water meets the bluest blood at one end, and the stalest water meets the reddest blood at the other, and the whole way along, the water's always got a little more to give than the blood's already got. It never evens out. It never stops taking." She looked up at Emeric. "A fish does it. I opened one in the first week to see. She does it better."

"Countercurrent," said Emeric.

"Is that what it's called?"

"It is now." He sat forward. "Say the rest."

"You know the rest. You've had that face for a minute." But she said it anyway, slowly, the way she said a diagnosis she wasn't sure of yet. "If a stone can be taught to want something, the way a cold stone wants the heat out of a room, then it can be taught to want what's in the water and not the water. Air, the good part. Pull it out of the sea through a plate, and the plate keeps the sea out, and you breathe what comes through." She frowned. "I don't know how thin the plate has to be. I don't know how much of it."

"A lot," said Emeric. "More than you'd think. The sea holds hardly any air. A man sitting still uses about a cup of the good part every minute, and a whole bucket of sea gives up barely a spoonful. You'd need five buckets through the plates every minute just to sit there. Ten if you're swimming. Twenty if you're frightened."

"You can't carry a pump that does twenty buckets a minute on your back," said Tess.

"No."

Noll had gone very still on his bench. Then he said, carefully, as if somebody might take it off him, "The pump-jet."

They looked at him.

"From the cave. The one you had in your hands. It sucks in at the front and pushes out the back. That's how it goes. It's already moving all that water." He swallowed, and coughed, and went on. "Put the plates in front of the mouth. Then it's not two things. When you swim, it breathes."

Tess looked at him for a long moment. Then she turned the drill board over to the clean side and took the pencil out of her hair.

"Sharks," said Emeric quietly, to nobody. "Same as sharks."

"What's a shark?" said Noll.

"A fish that drowns if it stops swimming."

Noll thought about that, and decided not to ask anything else.

Zale had come in at some point and was leaning on the wall by the door with his arms folded. "I can push bad air out of a room," he said. "I did it under the Isle. Why can't an air mage just make the good stuff, in the helm, and save the plates?"

"Because making's dear," said Imani, before Emeric could. "Anybody can tell you that. Making a cup of water out of nothing costs more than carrying a bucket up a hill. Making air to breathe, every minute, for an hour, you'd be on the floor of the sea with nothing left in you." She tapped the drawing on Tess's knee. "This isn't making. The air's already there in the water. The stone only moves it. Moving's cheap."

Zale considered that, and nodded, and stayed where he was.

Tess drew it before the stove had burned down. Emeric gave her the names and she wrote them in, small, along the edges, and argued with half of them.

The base of it was a thing that would work with no stone in it at all, because that was the rule, and everyone in the room knew the rule by now. A rebreather: a closed loop. You breathed out into a bag, and the bag's air went through a canister of lime, the same lime the Challenger's fan trunk used, which took the bad part out, and came back to you through a second bag. Nothing went into the sea. A small flask of the plant's oxygen sat at the bottom of the loop and bled in a little, to make up what your body had used. With no stone in it, a diver on that loop had hours, not minutes, limited by the lime and the flask.

The stone went on top. The mana gill: plates of pressed air-stone dust, thin as card and pale grey, thousands of them folded into two flat cartridges, one down each side of the pack, with the sea running through them one way and the loop's air running back through them the other, the way Imani had drawn on her palm. A small air stone behind a glass window at the top ran the plates. It told them how much to pull, and it told them when to stop.

"When to stop?" said Ferra. "Why would you stop? If the good part's good, more's better."

"It isn't," said Emeric. "Too much of the good part poisons you. Slowly at the surface. Fast, deep down. You go to fits." He saw Imani's head come up and nodded at her. "I'll write you what I know."

"You'll write me all of it," said Imani.

The pump-jet went at the bottom of the pack, with its mouth drawing up through the gill cartridges, so that swimming pulled the sea across the plates and the plates fed the loop. A chest panel with dials: the flask, the stone's charge, and a third that Tess left blank with a question mark, because nobody in Kaldo had ever built a gauge that told you how much good air was in a bag.

"I'll do the third one," said Tess. "Don't tell me. I want to work it out."

"Then I won't."

"You've got that face again."

"It's the same face," said Emeric. "I only have one."



He told them the rest in the watch room, after, with the door shut.

Ferra was undoing her braid with her fingers and Imani was lying across the end of the cot with her feet in his lap, which was how she had started telling him to rub them without saying so. He rubbed them. He'd been thinking about it since the drawing room, and it came out the way things did with them, without his deciding to say it.

"They had rebreathers," he said. "Where I'm from. Lime and a bag and a flask, almost exactly that. Old ones. Navies used them, and men who went into flooded mines. That part's easy. That part's a hundred years older than anything I ever touched."

"And the gill?" said Ferra.

"Never." He shook his head. "People wanted it for as long as there were people in the water. You'd see drawings. A man in the sea with a little mouthpiece and nothing else, breathing the ocean. Somebody was always selling one." He smiled a little. "None of them worked. They couldn't have worked. Everything I said in there about the buckets, that's true there too. To pull enough air out of the sea for one man you need a skin that lets the air through and stops the water, and it has to be so thin it's barely there, and so big that if you laid it out flat it'd cover this floor and the one under it. And then you have to push all that water across it, every minute, forever. They could make the skin, near enough, in a laboratory, a scrap of it. They could never make it small enough to wear, or strong enough to last a week in salt, or make the water move without a pump bigger than the man."

"So what's different?" said Imani, with her eyes shut.

"The plate doesn't have to be a sieve. It wants. You tell a stone to want the air and it reaches for it, so the plate can be a hundred times smaller than the skin would have to be." He moved his thumb up the arch of her foot and she made a small noise of approval. "And Noll's jet was already on the table. And you'd already been inside her gills for three months and seen how they're laid out. Any one of those, alone, and it doesn't fit on a man's back." He was quiet a moment. "On Earth they'd have needed all three, and they had none."

Ferra had stopped with her hair half undone. She was looking at him the way she looked at a column that came out exact.

"You're pleased," she said.

"I'm pleased."

"You didn't think of it."

"No," said Emeric. "Imani did. And Noll." He let go of the foot. Imani put the other one in his hand without opening her eyes. "That's why I'm pleased."



The first gill cartridge was a box.

It wasn't a pack and nobody wore it. On the twenty-second Fletcher's men stood it in the test drum on the Cradle's deck, the same one the nose had been tried on, with the blanked end off: a cartridge of forty plates pressed at the dust mill and sent out on the morning run, a hand pump in place of the jet, two leather bags on a loop of hose, and Tess's third gauge, a lead-and-silver cell in a brass cup that made a needle move when you gave it air, which she had built in a day and a half and refused to explain until it had worked. The bags were filled with stale air from the Challenger's fan trunk, the same bad air that had given everybody headaches on the bottom, and the needle sat low.

Noll pumped. Imani sat beside the box with the stone's window under her hand and her sight on the plates.

For a while nothing happened. Then the needle moved. Not much. A hair. Then another hair, and it went on going, slowly, steadily, up out of the red and toward the line Tess had painted on the dial for clean air at the surface, while Noll pumped the sea through the plates and back over the side.

It stopped at the line. Imani had told the stone to stop there. It stopped.

"Again," said Tess. "Empty the bags. Do it again." She was writing the times down the edge of the board in a column, and her pencil had gone through the paper in two places.

They did it again. It did it again.

The guest had come down the slab from the Cradle's end to watch, with her hood up and her bare feet in the wet at the rim, and when the needle stopped the second time she looked at the box, and at the plates through its open side, the pale grey leaves in their rows, and then at Imani. She touched two fingers to the slits under her own ear. Then she pointed at the box.

"Yes," said Imani. "Yours. I took it from you." She hesitated. "Is that all right?"

The guest looked at the box a while longer. Then she said one of her short words, and Zale, who was on the Hod's ladder with a coil of line over his shoulder, turned round and said, "She says it's ugly."

"It's a box," said Tess, without looking up.

"She says it's ugly, but it's right."

Tess wrote that down too.

Ferra came across on the next run with the plates priced. A wearable pack, she wrote, would take until the summer: the pressing, the shell, the jet married to the cartridges, the loop, the gauge made small enough for a chest, and the trials. Emeric read it on the Cradle's deck and handed it back.

"Summer's when she goes," he said.

"I know when she goes," said Ferra. "That's why it says summer."



On the twenty-third they took her out with her stones on.

Emeric came aboard for it, and Noll warmed the stones one by one at his station under Tess's sheet. The four pipes ran at the stern in place of the propeller, the Shell stood on its register, Imani's mark sat on the cabin dial where she'd set it, and the slip panels did their quiet work along her flanks. She went faster and quieter than she had on her motor alone, and she turned on her planes and her pipes exactly as well as she had turned before, and Tess, who had come aboard in her chair down a ramp Fletcher built for it in a day, sat in the control room with her stick across her knees and watched the gauges and said, at the end, that she was the same boat she'd been on the twentieth.

"Only quicker," said Emeric.

"Only quicker," said Tess. "That's what the stones are for."



The guest was on the Cradle's deck when they came in that evening.

She was standing at the slab's seaward edge in the long grey cloak with the hood up, on her legs, with her bare feet in the wet at the rim. She had been there, the deck crew said, since the middle of the afternoon. She'd watched the bay mouth the whole time, and she had turned her head toward the headland a long while before anyone ashore could see the Challenger round it.

Zale came up on the deck when they had her over the blocks and the Cradle began to rise. He was tired. His ribs still hurt from the desk. He went to the edge and stood beside the guest and looked at the boat with her, black and wet, coming up out of the bay as the slab lifted under her, water pouring off her flanks and out through the limber holes in her fairing.

She said something to him in her own language. Short, with the stop in the middle of it, and then a longer thing after.

He worked it out. "Loud," he said, in Kaldo, so she'd have it. "You heard her coming."

She nodded. She said the longer thing again, slower, for him, and put her hand out over the water toward the headland and drew it back in to her chest, and he understood that too. The seals had gone out of the bay. They'd gone out before the boat came round the head, the whole colony off the rocks under the bird cliffs, and they still hadn't come back.

Zale looked at the Challenger. Fletcher was already on her deck with his chalk. Bram was coming up out of the after hatch with his bandaged hand held up out of the way, like a man carrying a full cup through a crowd.

He thought about how to put it in her words, which didn't have most of the words he needed, and put it as best he could.

"She was built by people who've never had to be quiet under the water," he said.

The guest looked at him.

Then she laughed. Not the short sound through the nose that Zale had learned was her laugh. This came up out of her all at once and out loud, with her head back and her eyes shut, so that the hood fell off her hair, and it went on, and it was a much bigger sound than he would have thought she had in her. Two of Fletcher's men on the Cradle's deck turned round to look. Tess, at the berth door in her chair, turned round. Bram stopped halfway up the hatch with his hand in the air.

She stopped, eventually, and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, and said something to Zale, still smiling, that he didn't catch at all. The wind came off the water then, and something went over her skin, quick, light and dark from the throat down, and left a little blue at her neck where the gills were. Zale knew that one. She'd be cold. February, and her hood down.

"Aye," said Zale, as if she'd said something about the weather.

He went down the deck to help Fletcher with the gland.



The Catch was a quarter full by the first of March, by Emeric's reading, and a half by the eighth. By then the Challenger was back in her berth under the roof with her stones cold and her nose under its canvas, and her new weights in their cradle, and the bell at the helm, and Fletcher's second gland dry as a biscuit. The berth crews were saying she'd been out sounding the reef.

Zale crossed his line out in his book. Home by 15 March. She'd been home three weeks early.

On his last night on the marsh side he walked out along the reef at low water. Fletcher's men had had the oil off the whale, as Fletcher said they would, and the gulls and the crabs had had the rest. What lay across the broken wall of the pen now was the long white frame of it, the ribs standing up out of the water like the frames of a boat nobody had planked.

He stood there a while. Then he went back to his ship.
