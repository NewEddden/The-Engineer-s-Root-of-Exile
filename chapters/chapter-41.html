Chapter 41 - The Posted Rate

Ferra put the money in and then told him what it was.

"It is a large sum and it does not fix anything. I want to say that once, now, before anybody in this town starts spending it in their heads, including you."

"Say it."

"A payment that arrives once is not income. There is no harvest. There is no soap trade, and there will not be one until somebody decides to buy from us again, which is not a date I can put in a book. There is a foundry that wants ore and cannot get it from the only hill in the region that has any, and I would very much like to know who owns that hill this month, because I have written to the steward twice and had nothing."

She closed the book.

"It buys months. It does not buy a year."

The two mages had nowhere at all to go.

Orrin was about fifty, broad, and talked continuously. Vesk was younger, quieter, and did most of the actual thinking in the partnership, which Orrin cheerfully acknowledged whenever it came up and did not appear to mind in the slightest.

Their employer was dead. Their pay had died with him, because there had been no contract anywhere and there never had been. Three of the five they had come with were in the ground on the west bank. And every hall in the region now knew them by the only fact that was ever going to attach to them again, which was that they had been at Kaldo and had lost.

When Emeric asked why they had been there at all, the answer took four minutes and was not the one he expected.

Research costs money. That was the whole of it. They were unaffiliated, which in that kingdom means no patron, no stipend, no library, no bench and no standing of any kind, and the only work available to a man in that condition is work that somebody wants doing with a spell. Neither of them had a quarrel with Kaldo. Neither had heard of Kaldo before being told where the road was.

"We would have taken your coin just as fast," Vesk said. "If you had offered first. I want that understood before anything else gets said, because I am not going to sit in your hall and pretend there was a principle in it."

Emeric believed them.

Partly because it was unflattering, and men do not usually volunteer the unflattering version without a reason.

And partly because he recognised the shape of it exactly. He had known a great many people in another life whose entire moral position was a function of who was funding them that year, and he had been one of them, in a building with good coffee and a waiting room that smelled of new carpet.

The offer took a minute to make and neither of them believed it.

Wages at the barony's posted rate, which was public and written on a board in the market square that anybody could walk up and read. A roof. Injury pay on the same terms as the Works crews, which were also on the board.

And, which neither of them could have named at the time and both described years later in nearly the same words, the first professional respect either had been shown by anybody.

No oath. No binding. Nothing he could enforce.

There is no such thing in that world as a contract that holds a man by anything other than his own interest, whatever the ballads say about it, and Emeric did not invent one and did not pretend to one and did not ask for a promise he had no possible way to collect.

He said plainly that they could walk out of the gate on any morning they chose and that nobody would follow them or write to anybody about it.

That was the thing that actually landed.

Orrin asked him to say it again. Not sarcastically. He asked him to repeat the sentence, and Emeric repeated it, and Orrin sat back and looked at Vesk, and Vesk did not look back at him.

The job was charging stones. Not fighting, not guarding, not being pointed at anybody, ever.

Charge rate was the barony's bottleneck in about nine different places and had been for a year. The tower, the sections, the shop, the pattern floor, the drying shed, the lamps. Every one of them waited on somebody putting power into a rock, and the barony's entire supply of somebody had been one man who could not be seen doing it.

Two men who did nothing all day but fill batteries were worth a great deal more to Kaldo than they had ever been worth to Renner as weapons, and Emeric said so to their faces, and it was the first time in eleven years anybody had told either of them that the boring thing they could do was the valuable thing.

The other reason he kept to himself.

They were the only trained practitioners he had ever had his hands on. They knew what the Circle taught and in what order. They knew what a bench test measured and what it did not. They knew how ordinary etching was cut, and why, and how long it took, and what it cost a man to do it.

About a third of what they told him was wrong, because they were hedge-mages who had learned from other hedge-mages who had learned from a book somebody had copied badly, and he had no possible way of knowing which third.

He built on all of it anyway, because it was the only thing in the kingdom he had.

Orrin started teaching inside the year, in a room with eleven people in it, and some of what he taught was the wrong third, and nobody noticed anything at all.

Doran objected to them staying.

He came once, briefly, and made the case on the grounds of the men they had killed on the hill, and made it well and without heat and did not repeat himself.

Then he stopped objecting, about a week later, and did not explain why.

Emeric noticed the reversal, and noticed that it had a cause, and noticed further that whatever the cause was it had happened inside that church in the intervening week, which narrowed it to about four possibilities and none of them were his business.

He did not ask. That cost him something and he was aware of it.

Imani did not object at all. She put both of them to work carrying on the second day and neither of them had the nerve to refuse.

Vesk had been in Kaldo about a month when he started keeping a separate notebook.

It was a good place, which was the strange part. He had spent eleven years in bad ones. He had a bed with a window over the eastern branch and the water made a sound against the stones all night that he stopped noticing inside a week and then started noticing again every time it rained. He was paid on Fridays, the same amount every Friday, off a board with the rate written on it that anybody could walk up and read.

Nobody had asked him to kill anyone. That was new.

The first thing was containment.

The prince's mana sat tight to him. It did not drift, did not bleed, did not fill a room. Vesk had stood next to a great many people with the faculty and most of them leaked constantly and had no idea they were doing it, and nobles were the worst of the lot because nobody had ever had the standing to tell them.

Holding it that close is a discipline. The tower spends four years beating it into acolytes with a stick and most of them never get it clean.

And the man did not cast.

Which was odd, and would have been the end of it, except for the second half of it, which was that the man did not practise either.

Vesk had been there a month and had eyes. He had watched the prince run forty men through strength work and endurance work and mandatory rest days off a written schedule, with the weights and the distances recorded in a book and pinned up on a wall where the men could see their own lines. He had watched him stand over crossbow crews with a count in his mouth, timing reloads, making them do it again, and again, and then once more in the dark.

The entire barony was drilled to a degree Vesk had never encountered anywhere, by a man who plainly believed that nothing on earth improves except by repetition and had organised eighteen hundred people around that conviction.

And that man had never once been seen to raise a hand at a candle, a target, or a bowl of water.

Orrin's explanation, offered cheerfully over supper with his mouth full, was that noble blood tells and some men simply have it.

Vesk did not believe that, for the straightforward reason that he had met noble blood, and it leaked like everybody else's.

The second thing was the count, and the count was an accident.

Charging stones meant handling the barony's inventory, and handling the inventory meant that sooner or later somebody sent him out along the irrigation main with a list and a sack.

He stopped at the eleventh section latch because the spoil had slumped over the winter and the thing was sitting half out of the ground, and he picked it up and looked at it properly for the first time.

The working on it was shallow. Crude, very nearly. Hold a charge, let it go on a signal, and absolutely nothing else, cut the way a man cuts when he is deliberately keeping the cost down.

Vesk approved of that. It was what he would have done and it would not have occurred to him to be embarrassed about it.

Then he started counting, and he got past a hundred, and he stopped counting.

Because depth is only half the bill. The other half is volume. A hundred and more etchings, however shallow, is a season of work for a working mage, and it would leave him wrung out for most of that season, and a man who is wrung out for a season is a thing that gets noticed by every single person in a small town.

Nobody in Kaldo remembered anyone being wrung out. He asked three people in the most casual way he could manage and got three blank looks and one helpful suggestion that he might be thinking of the plague.

So he went to the carver, because there was a carver, and the carver was the obvious answer and Vesk wanted very much for him to be the answer.

Nevin was cutting a housing bracket in the shed by the pump-house, was perfectly happy to talk, and had no suspicion of anything whatsoever.

He had cut the control post. He said so with some pride and walked Vesk out to it, and Vesk looked at it and saw exactly what he had expected to see. Competent ordinary charge-and-hold work with eleven years of mine lamps behind it, and the hand of the man standing next to him all over it.

You could see where he had started. You could see where he had rested. There was a slight consistent wander at the bottom of the curve that Vesk could have picked out of a hundred.

"And the latches," Vesk said.

"Not mine."

Nevin said it without any interest at all and went back to the bracket, and that was the end of the most important conversation either of them ever had.

Vesk walked back out to the eleventh latch and held the two of them side by side in his head, and it took about four seconds.

There were no tool marks on the latch. Not faint ones. None. No entry, no exit, no wander, no place where a hand had stopped and started again. The working was simply in the stone, the way a vein is in a rock.

Two facts, and nothing joining them.

Either the prince was a great deal stronger than he permitted anybody to see, or he had a method that nobody in this kingdom had.

Vesk could not tell which from where he was standing, and he was not going to ask, because a man with no lineage learns early and permanently that noticing things out loud is how you get moved along.

He liked it here. He was paid on Fridays off a board. Nobody had asked him to kill anybody.

So he started a private count in a notebook he kept in his own coat, and added to it whenever something did not add up.

It was a short list for now.

Emeric asked them, a few days after that, what they had actually been working on before the money interrupted it.

Orrin talked for twenty minutes. Vesk corrected him four times, twice on substance, and both times Orrin said yes, that, and carried straight on.

The question was a good one and Emeric had not thought of it.

Why is there more mana in some places than in others. The capital is thick with it. The backcountry is thin. Some valleys are thick and the next valley over is not, and there is no visible reason for any of it, and nobody in four hundred years has produced an account better than the capital's, which is that great places attract great power.

"Which is not an explanation," Vesk said. "It is a flattering description of where the capital happens to be sitting."

Their hypothesis was that mana is not made by places. It moves. It collects where something holds it, and the something is a property of the ground or the stone or whatever else happens to be there, and the reason nobody had ever proved it was that they had no way to measure the thing they were talking about, no funds to spend a year not measuring it, and no patron anywhere who cared.

Emeric funded it in front of Ferra, out loud, as a line item.

In the same month she had told him the bill bought months and not a year. She wrote it down without a word, and he knew exactly what the silence cost her, and took it anyway.

Their wages continued. They got materials on request, in reason. They got time, which was the expensive part and which both of them understood was the expensive part. The barony asked for nothing back at all except that they write down what they found, including the parts that did not work.

Orrin asked twice whether he was serious. Vesk did not ask, and went away and sat on the wall over the eastern branch for about an hour.

Then Emeric gave them one sentence and stopped.

"Has either of you considered that it might behave less like water in a bucket, and more like heat in a room?"

And then he let them run with it.

They got to conduction by themselves inside a fortnight. They got to the idea that a thing empties into the thing beside it until the two of them match, and argued about that for three days at a volume that reached the kitchen, and got to gradients out of the argument, and from there to the notion that some materials take a long time to fill and a long time to empty and others do not.

He did not use the word equilibrium.

He did not tell them that it explained why a dead zone refills slowly. He did not tell them why a stone that has lain in a running branch for a century turns into a water stone. He did not tell them that when they finally worked out how to measure the thing, what they would be measuring would turn out to be a rate rather than a quantity, and that the whole of their difficulty was that they were looking for an amount.

He had all of that, and he held it.

The reasons were two, and only one of them was respectable.

The respectable one was that he had twice now handed this town a finished answer and watched it go nowhere. A given idea sits where you put it. A discovered one gets defended by the person who found it, and taught by them, and argued for in rooms he is not standing in, and he had come to believe that with some conviction and could point at the two occasions that had taught him.

The other one was that he wanted them to stay.

A man with a solved problem leaves. A man with a problem that keeps opening does not leave anywhere, because there is nowhere to take it, and Emeric had exactly one trained practitioner who could measure things and exactly one who could teach, and no second source of either in any direction for four hundred miles.

He knew precisely which of those two reasons was doing the work. He wrote both of them in the book in order of respectability, and then wrote a third line underneath noting which one was load-bearing, and did it anyway.

They worked it out, and did not mind.

Orrin said it cheerfully within the month, over supper, to Vesk, with his mouth full. A man cannot agree that confidently with an argument he has not already had. Their princely patron kept agreeing with the correct half of things he was supposedly hearing for the first time, and never once with the wrong half, and a man who is genuinely following along makes at least one mistake.

Vesk did not say it out loud. Vesk wrote it in the notebook, under the containment and under the count.

And neither of them ever raised it with him, for the simplest reason available to either of them, which Orrin put into words once and then never repeated.

Whatever the man knew and was not saying, he was the only person in either of their lives who had ever paid them to find something out. A man who hoards knowledge and funds your research is still infinitely better than a man who has neither.

The venture men got their accounting in the long room over the threshing floor.

The field outside was black for a mile and a half and had stopped smoking about four days earlier, and every one of them had walked past it to get there.

Emeric stood up and gave them the same numbers he had given them in the autumn, in the same order, because he had promised them in advance and the entire point of promising a thing in advance is that you say it again afterward when it has become unpleasant.

Everyone eats. Full ration, every hearth, through the winter and into the spring. The pauper allowance stands. The child wage stands.

Your seed is safe. All of it, sealed, inside the walls, where it has been since the spring.

And there is nothing to sell for a year.

He did not soften the last one and he did not put anything after it, which was the hardest part, because there was a sentence available about next year and he had no right to it.

Nobody thanked him for winning.

Not one man in that room mentioned the lane, or the sixty-one, or the baron who had ridden out through the Western Gate having settled an account.

Three of them, on the way out, thanked him for the cellars.
