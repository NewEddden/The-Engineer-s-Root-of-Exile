Chapter 41 - One Wire

The line itself was iron, because iron was what Kaldo had.

Emeric had drawn it on a slate for Bram in the forge in the last week of June: one wire, on poles, from the keep at the top of the town to the tower over the South Gate at the bottom of it. A battery at each end. A needle at each end. A key at each end, to send the current one way down the wire or the other, so that the needle at the far end swung left or right.

"One wire," Bram said. "Your jars want two. You showed me. Out of the copper, round, and back into the zinc."

"The ground's the other wire."

Bram looked at him.

"An iron plate in the wet ground at each end. The current goes down the line and comes back through the earth." Emeric had not quite believed it himself the first time, in the other life, and remembered not believing it. "It isn't a good wire. It doesn't need to be. It's very, very wide."

Bram looked at the slate, and then at the floor of his own forge, as if it had just been accused of something.

So the line was one wire, and the one wire was iron, drawn on Tess's plate from what the tithe houses still had in them once the grates had gone to the scythes, door bands and pot hooks and bed irons and a cartload of hinges, forged down to rod at the anvil and drawn on Harl's wheel through the first nine holes of the fourteen, which was as fine as a line needed to be. It came off the drum as thick as a bootlace, grey, stiff, ugly, and there was nearly half a mile of it by the time Tess's knotted cord had been along it twice and she had chalked the number on the mill wall.

The poles came out of the baron's woods under a second waiver, in the barony's form, in Hale's hand. Thirty-one of them, larch, stripped and charred at the foot, sunk in holes the gate crew dug, which Ward wrote on their board as a day's work and the gate crew did not agree with. The carpenter set them. The line went out from a bracket on the keep's south wall, down across the gate yard, down the high street on the poles, past the market and the end of Cooper's Row, and up the side of the South Gate tower to a window in its upper room. Where it crossed a pole it went over a wooden peg driven into the top, and was stapled down.

The iron plates went into the canal ring under the keep at the top and the moat under the South Gate at the bottom.

The town watched the poles go up the way it watched everything the keep did now, from doorways, with opinions. Children followed the carpenter from pole to pole. A man on the high street asked whether the wire was for hanging people, and was told no, and did not believe it. On the second day a woman whose house stood under the line found it a convenient height and hung a wet shirt on it to dry, and Tess, walking the line with her cord, came on the shirt and took it down and handed it back to her with great politeness.

"Nothing touches the wire," Tess said.

"It's a shirt."

"Nothing," Tess said, and the woman, who had known Tess since she was born, looked at her as though she had grown a second head, and took the shirt indoors.



The needles were the trouble.

A needle at the end of half a mile of iron, with thirty-one damp poles and two plates in the mud taking their share on the way, got very little current to work with, and a needle that had very little current to work with needed a great many turns of wire round it to swing at all. Not the sixty turns of thick copper round Abloh's compass. Hundreds of turns, of copper drawn much finer, as fine as a hair, so that a great many of them would fit round a needle's box.

They tried it on the wheel first.

Bram cut a second plate, a small one, with six holes in it finer than the fourteenth hole of the first, and Tess fed a copper wire into the first of them on the drum on a Saturday night, and it came through and broke. It came through the second and broke. Harl's wheel pulled as a river pulls, which was strongly and steadily over an hour and not at all steadily over a heartbeat: the water pushed a little harder, the drum snatched, and a wire as fine as a hair parted like a thread in a draft. Harl changed the belt three ways and slowed the drum to a crawl, and the wire still broke, every time, at the third hole or the fourth.

"Too fine for a wheel," Harl said, at the end of the night. He said it without regret, as he might have said that bread would not rise in a cold room. "A wheel's a strong thing. That wants a careful one."

It wanted a pull that never snatched. Emeric had one.



He did it in the dyehouse, at night, with the door barred, sacking nailed over the shutters and a single lamp on the bench, because it could not be done anywhere else.

Tess was there because she would not go home.

He had told her to. She had said that it was her plate, and that she had annealed every length of wire that had ever gone through it, and that if he was going to put her plate in a vise and do something to it in the dark she was going to watch, and she said all of this in the quiet flat voice she used when something mattered, and then stood by the door with her arms folded. He looked at her, and thought about how few people in Kaldo knew even about the room, and how many people a thing could be known by before it stopped being a secret, and did not send her home.

He clamped the small plate in Bram's vise on the bench. He took a length of copper drawn as fine as the wheel could manage, pointed the end of it with a file, and fed the point into the first hole far enough that it showed on the other side. He took hold of nothing with his hands.

He pointed at the wire's end, past the plate, and pulled.

It was not like lifting a coin, or himself. A coin came to his hand because nothing held it back but its own weight. The wire was held. The plate gripped it all round, and the hole was smaller than the wire, and every hair's width of copper that came through had to be squeezed down and stretched out against the whole resistance of the steel. He felt every bit of that resistance, in his hand and then in his arm and then behind his eyes, as if he were dragging the wire through the plate with his teeth. The copper came, slowly, a hand's length, two hands, steadily, without a snatch, because he was the thing pulling it and he did not snatch.

The glyph stood in the air at his pointing hand while he did it.

It was not the fire's. Tess had watched the fire's glyph for seven hours on the floor of Bram's hearth in June, and she saw at once that this one was another shape altogether. He saw her see it. She did not move from the door.

Between holes the copper went hard, as it always did, and he could not take it to a hearth in the middle of a pull. So he made a needle of fire instead, a thread of flame no thicker than a straw drawn off the lamp, and ran it along the wire from end to end until the copper glowed a dull red and went soft, and then let the lamp have its flame back, and pointed, and pulled again.

The first night he drew one length through all six holes, about as long as the dyehouse. At the end of it he sat down on the floor with his back against a vat, and there was blood on his upper lip, and he wiped it on his sleeve, and Tess brought him a cup of water without being asked and stood over him while he drank it.

"What was that?" she said.

"Fire work."

She looked at the bench, where the coil of hair-fine copper lay in loops beside the vise. She looked at his hand. She did not say anything.

A needle wanted four hundred turns of it, by his reckoning, and there were two needles. Tess ran her knotted cord along every length he drew and chalked it on the bench, and by the end of the first night she had worked out how many nights it was going to take at that rate, and told him, and he had wished she had not.

The second night he drew two lengths. The third night three, and the nosebleed did not come, though the headache did, and lasted until noon.

On the third night, when he had finished, Tess sat on her stool by the lamp with her bench book open on her knee, the book in which she wrote the measure of everything that went through the plate, and wrote something in it, and then turned the book round and held it out to him, as she did when she wanted him to know that she had seen a thing and was not going to be told she had not.

Coil wire. Third night. Three lengths. The wire moved toward his hand.

He read it twice.

"Fire work," he said again.

Tess took the book back and closed it and put it inside her shirt, where she had kept the torn margin of the alphabet sheet in September.

"All right," she said, in a voice that believed nothing.



Abloh covered the fine wire. It could not go bare round a needle any more than the thick had, and his waxed linen was too coarse for it, so he went back to the soap yard and came back with a skein of the finest thread he owned, silk, from some chest of his he had never mentioned, and waxed it, and sat on his stool by the dyehouse door winding it onto the hair-fine copper turn by turn, swearing steadily under his breath. When he had covered a length he held it up to the light, between finger and thumb, and looked at it with an expression of deep personal grievance and very great pride.

"That's the finest work I've done since the sails," he said. "And it's going round a compass."

Bram made the keys. Emeric drew one, a strip of springy brass on a block with a stud under each end, and Bram looked at the drawing and said it would wear through at the studs in a month, and made it his own way instead, with the studs faced in a scrap of silver off a broken buckle, because silver did not foul. He would not say where the buckle had come from. He made two, and set them side by side on the forge bench, and pressed each one down a dozen times with his thumb, listening to the click, and then pushed them across the bench at Emeric without a word.

The code was Ward's, in the end, though Emeric started it.

He brought Ward a list of twenty things a gate might want to tell a keep, each one a pattern of swings: left, right, left-left, left-right, and so on, written out in two columns on a sheet. Ward read the list from top to bottom, standing, and handed it back.

"The gate crew cannot read this."

"They can count."

"They can count to four, at night, in the rain, with somebody shouting." Ward took the pen out of Emeric's hand and drew a line through fourteen of the twenty. "Six. And no word on the board. A picture."

So there were six, and Abloh painted them, on a board for each end, in a row down the left side beside the swings: a horseman on the road, for riders coming. A boat, for a boat on the river. A flame, for fire. A bell, for come at once. A hand held up flat, for all well. And a hand held up with the fingers spread, which Ward said meant say that again, because somebody always would need it said again.

Ward chose the swings himself. The flame was three to the left, fast, because fire was the one a tired man must not mistake, and three of anything fast was hard to make by accident. The bell was three to the right. All well was a single slow swing, left, and back.

The wall-crew man who had read his name in Doran's class asked to learn it, and learned all six in an afternoon, standing in front of the board in the gate tower with the needle under its glass beside him, saying each one out loud to himself the way he had said his name.



They tried it on the last day of July, in the middle of the afternoon, in sun.

Emeric was in the room at the top of the keep where the line came in, with six jars from the dyehouse on a shelf under the window, the key on the table, the needle in its box of hair-fine copper, and Abloh's board on the wall. Rylan was with him, because Rylan would not have missed it for any money. At the other end, half a mile down the hill, Ward was in the gate tower with the other six jars and the other needle, and the wall-crew man, and Tess, who had walked the whole line that morning from the keep to the gate, peg by peg, and pronounced it sound.

Emeric put his hand on the key.

Bram's key: pressing one end sent the current down the line one way, and pressing the other sent it back. He pressed the left end, once, slowly, and let it up.

All well.

His own needle kicked as he pressed, as it should, and lay still when he let the key up. Rylan, who had been holding his breath, let it out, and said that he had expected something more.

Then the needle moved.

It swung to the left. Slowly. And back.

A single slow swing. All well. From the gate.

Rylan said a word that was not suitable for the room he was in.

Emeric sat with his hand still on the key and looked at the needle. Half a mile of iron wire on thirty-one larch poles, and the ground of Kaldo under all of it carrying the current back, and at the other end a man who in June could not read his own name had looked at a needle and known what it meant and answered it. It had taken seven months from the first length of copper coming off Harl's drum.

The needle moved again.

Three to the right. Fast.

The bell. Come at once.

Rylan was out of his chair. Emeric caught his arm.

The needle went on, before Rylan had got halfway to the door. A single slow swing to the left, and back. All well.

Come at once. All well. Emeric looked at the needle, and then at Rylan in the doorway with his hand on his sword, and then he understood, and he was laughing.

"That's Ward," he said. "That's Ward saying come down and look at it."
