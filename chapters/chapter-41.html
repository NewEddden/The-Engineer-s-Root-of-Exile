Chapter 41 - Grain for Stone

He asked Corliss for the list on the twenty-first of September, in the yard behind the mill, because he had decided that if he was going to ask for it at all he was going to ask for it standing on the ground it was under.

Corliss heard him out and did not put his tools down.

"Two copies," he said. "I wrote it twice the same week. Names where there was a name, and where there wasn't a name I put what he had on him and where he was lying, and I numbered the rows so a man could be found again without opening anything."

"Why two?"

"Because paper burns and I'd just watched a building do it." Corliss went back to the strap he was working. "It's in the vestry press under Doran's key and you can have it and I'll not pretend I'm giving it to you."

"I wasn't going to thank you."

"Good." He pulled the strap through. "I didn't do it for them and I didn't do it for you. I did it because it wanted doing and nobody was doing it, same as the boxes, same as the ground. Take it up the river if you like. Don't tell me it's a kindness. I buried a hundred and forty-nine men in two days in the last week of August and there's nothing kind anywhere in that."

Emeric took it and did not say anything else, which he had learned nine months ago was the only thing that worked with Corliss and had taken him rather longer than nine months to stop wanting to improve.

The room happened on the twenty-second, and Saban made his objection in it, and then made it again on the road, which was how Emeric knew it was a real one.

"Say the worst version," Emeric said.

"The worst version is that your furnace eats one hill." Saban had the sheet in front of him and did not look at it. "I walked the Duvrenne road twice and sounded that water once and I stood in a coastal counting house in February being laughed at, politely, and I have got you a factor down there who wrote to us in September offering credit we did not ask for. That's a year of my legs. Sign this and inside eighteen months there is not a man in this barony who will authorise a Duvrenne load at four times the carriage when there's a barge coming down for the price of loading it, and a road nobody uses is a road that shuts, and then one hill and one river and one widow's name is the whole of your iron."

"It binds her, not us. We can still buy anywhere."

"Can and will are different men," Saban said. "You know that better than I do. You've written it on a wall."

Ferra ruled a line and did not look up. "He is right and it is not free. Say what you want to do about it and I will price it."

"One load a year off Duvrenne, standing, whether we need it or not. Small. Paid for out of the four parts and shown on its own line so nobody can lose it in a total."

"A load of ore we do not need, at four times the carriage, every year, forever." Ferra wrote it. "So that a road stays open."

"So that a road stays open."

"I am putting it under the wells," she said, "because it is the same argument, and because if I put it under ore somebody will strike it out in a hard March, and if I put it under the wells they will have to come and find me first."

Vantic had been silent for the whole of it with her case shut, which she did when she wanted a room to finish arguing before she spoke into it.

"Two matters," she said. "The first is that a counterparty inspecting your ore line touches a pledged asset, and I am permitting it, narrowly, in writing, on the ore line only and not on the foundry books, and the reason I am permitting it is that it is an ordinary supplier's term and my house has written it a hundred times. Do not treat my saying yes as a general habit."

"And the second."

"The second is the standing load." Vantic opened the case after all. "I am recording it as a cost with no return, because that is what it is, and I am recording beside it that the borrower proposed it unprompted and that in my opinion it is the reason my house's security is worth what it is worth. My house will read the first sentence and not the second. I am writing both."

They rode out on the twenty-fourth, three of them and two riders behind, no banners, on the river road.

It was the first time he had gone out of Kaldo carrying anything but a request. In November he had ridden west to Milford with Dunnel and a page and a piece of paper asking to be allowed to owe a debt in grain, and he had come home with the paper signed and had understood on the road exactly what had been done for him and what it had cost the man who did it. This time there was a written offer in Saban's case with a price on it, and a granary behind him with a lid on it, and the difference between those two rides was eleven months and about four hundred acres, and he thought about that for the first hour and then made himself stop, because a man who spends a whole day admiring his own arithmetic arrives at the other end of it soft.

The road climbed after the split and stopped pretending to be flat. Below them the water ran fast and shallow and loud and all in one direction, which was the entire economy of two baronies said out loud by a river: everything Grayrock had ever sold had gone down it for nothing, and everything Grayrock had ever bought had come up the road on wheels at whatever the road cost, and that had been true before either of them was born and would be true after.

Head frames stood on the shoulders of the hills with nothing turning. Two of them had water in, which Saban had said in February and Emeric had written down in February and had now seen, and seeing it was not the same and he made a note of that too.

"Say your piece about the wounded," Emeric said, "and then don't."

Ward did not look round. "Two hundred and twelve men off that hill were carried up into your town and put on a stone floor, and a hundred and forty-nine were put in the ground properly by a man who hated doing it, and there's not a house up here that doesn't know somebody who came home because of it." He shrugged. "That's a card."

"It's not a card."

"It's a card whether you spend it or not."

"Then it's a card I'm not spending, because the day I put it on a table it stops being a thing we did and starts being a thing we bought, and I'd rather have done it." Emeric watched the empty head frames go past. "Also she'll have counted it herself and she'll despise me for saying it, and I'd like at least one person in this kingdom who despises me for the right reasons."

"That was almost a joke," Ward said.

"It was a whole joke."

"It was almost a joke."

The hall at Grayrock was stone and cold and had not been heated, in September, in a hill country, which told him more about the treasury than the ledgers did.

Bryda Renner did not stand up when he came in.

He could not tell what it was. Insult was one reading and it was the loudest one and he distrusted it immediately, because grief did it too, and so did being tired in the particular way of a person who has been the only one working for a year, and he had walked in with his certainty turned all the way down on purpose and it went down further.

She waited until he had a hand on the chair.

"You shot my husband off a horse," she said.

He took the hand off the chair.

"A little over two hundred paces, off my own marks, from the gate tower." He said it in the flat voice he used for numbers, because dressing it would be worse. "He was inside a piece of ground where nothing could be cast at him and he knew it and he was using it to turn men round who had stopped, and he was the only man on that field whose falling would stop it. So I stopped it. I'd not have got a second shot and if I had I'd have spent it on Wayford."

"You are not going to say you are sorry."

"No."

"Why not."

"Because you'd price it," Emeric said, "and I'd have paid you in something that costs me nothing, and you'd hold it against me for the rest of whatever this is, and you'd be right to."

Then he stopped talking.

He had decided on the road that he would, and it was much harder than he had expected, and the silence went on long enough that he counted the courses of stone in the far wall twice and started a third time.

"Sit down," she said. "Say what you came to say."

He said it in order because Saban had trained him to. Kaldo had a surplus and would have one again. Kaldo had a foundry that could eat more stone than it could get and had spent a year buying inferior ore at eleven days' distance. Grayrock had veins and no treasury and fourteen hundred people and a winter coming. Grain against ore, priced at standing, fixed for five years, re-read in the third against the published prices at the capital, both sides' books on the ore line open to the other's man.

"And exclusive," she said.

"And exclusive."

"There is a writ on that table." She did not look at it and did not offer it to him. "It came up the road on the second of September. It makes me holder in trust for my daughter until Ilsa is twenty-one or married, whichever the crown notices first. So I can put my name on five years. In April I could not have put it on five weeks, and your merchant stood in my yard in February being sent from one man to another until he gave up, and I could not tell him why because I did not know myself yet."

"Then I'll ask the question I'd ask anyway. Why should you take exclusive."

"I was going to raise it," Bryda said, "and I would rather raise it than be handed it." She turned her hand over on the table, once. "Exclusive is what a man writes when he means to set the price himself later on. It takes away the only thing a seller has, which is another buyer in the room. My husband would have taken it because it came with a large number at the front and he did not read past the front."

"And?"

"And I am going to take it." She said it without any weight at all. "Because you have written that Kaldo buys the whole of the grade in a year Kaldo does not want it. There is not a house in this kingdom that would write that except one that wants the hill more than it wants the stone, and I would rather be wanted for five years by somebody I have watched than be free every spring to sell to whoever has not heard yet that Grayrock has no baron. It is a bad deal against a good year and it is the best deal on the table against the years I am actually going to have."

She looked at him for the first time properly.

"I watched that wall hold from behind it," she said. "I paid two hundred and fifty men to teach me that, and a hundred and forty-nine of them are still down there. I am not going to pay twice for the same lesson."

Saban and the mine captain did the rest of it in an afternoon at the end of the hall with the shutters open for light, and it went quickly because both of them had been ready for a season and neither of them had been allowed to sign anything.

Mattick was sixty and had been underground since he was nine and had the flat unhurried certainty of a man who has been right about rock for fifty years and wrong about people the whole time and has never once noticed. He went through the workings in order. Two producing at a quarter of what they could take. Two standing in water and worth pumping. One good iron face barely opened. The crystal ground on the eastern shoulder, thin but honest, and he said the word thin twice so that nobody could say afterward that he had sold it high.

"And that's the whole of the hill," he said.

"That's six," Emeric said. "Saban's February note has seven head frames standing."

"Seven standing. Six working, or would be."

"And the seventh."

"The seventh's not worked," Mattick said, and reached for the next sheet.

Emeric waited about two seconds, which was one second longer than the sentence deserved and three fewer than it needed, and then let it go, because there was a five-year contract on the table and a man who had spent fifty years underground had just told him a hole was not worth putting men down, and he had been in this kingdom long enough to know that half the workings in it were abandoned for the excellent reason that they were empty.

Bryda did not say anything.

He noticed that she did not. He noticed it the way he noticed everything, which was completely and then wrongly: she had not filled a silence all day, she had not defended her husband past the one accurate sentence she had allowed him, she had not offered a single word she did not have a use for, and Emeric put the seventh head frame in the same drawer as all of that and labelled the drawer a widow's economy of words, and was pleased with himself for having understood her, and rode home two days later with the misunderstanding folded up small and carried carefully all the way.

He gave her the list before he left, in the yard, which was a mistake of siting he did not work out for another year.

"Corliss ran the burying," he said. "He wrote down every man that had anything on him to write down, and he numbered the rows, so any one of them can be found again without opening the ground. This is the second of the two copies he made. There are things on the last four pages that belong to people up here."

She took it and read the first page and then the second, standing, in the yard, with the wind going.

"Which of them had nothing on them?"

"Thirty-one."

She closed it and put it under her arm and said, "Thank you for the numbers on the rows," and went inside, and did not look at it again while he could see her.

Ilsa was in the passage on his way out with an armful of ore books, fifteen years old, the ledgers stacked to her chin because there was nobody else to carry them, and she stood aside for him with the particular blank courtesy of someone who has been told exactly once what will happen if she says anything at all. He said good afternoon. She said good afternoon. That was the whole of it.

The first barge came down on the eleventh of October and Bram had it in the furnace inside a day and said one word about the grade and then went and said it again to three other people, which for Bram was a parade.

Signed five years at standing, re-read in the third, books open both ways on the ore line. Exclusive. She raised the exclusivity herself before I could, which I had half prepared for and did not enjoy being right about, and then took it anyway on reasoning I have written out below because I want it in her words and not mine: known-bad against best-informed-bet, and she said she had already paid for the information once.

One load a year off Duvrenne, standing, at four times the carriage, on its own line, under the wells and not under ore. Saban is right that a road nobody uses shuts. Ferra has costed it and does not like it and has written that she does not like it. It is the cheapest insurance I will buy this decade and I expect to be told so every March until I die.

I did not spend the two hundred and twelve and I am glad, and I want it on this page that being glad about it took me about four hours and Ward offering me the use of it twice.

She has been running that hill alone for a year with no name on any of it and there was no fire lit in that hall in September. She is a caretaker minding a chair until her daughter is old enough to be handed it, and she is a good one, and she is the only person in three baronies whose word I would take without liking her, because she does arithmetic and arithmetic does not change its mind about you in February. I do not have to read her. I only have to be able to read the sum.

Seven head frames standing, six working. The mine captain says the seventh is not worked. I asked once and did not ask twice, because I had what I came for and because a hole with nothing in it is the commonest thing in this kingdom.
