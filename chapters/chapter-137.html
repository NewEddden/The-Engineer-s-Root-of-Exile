Chapter 137 - Among Gentlemen

They went north again at the end of the week, by road, the way they had come.

It was a slower cart this time and a heavier one. Two long crates lay along its bed with straw packed round them and EXCHANGE STOCK painted on their ends in Ferra's letters, and between them a smaller crate with twenty of Tobe's blacked brass knots in it bedded in sawdust like eggs, and a lock Bram had made for a door he had never seen, and a box of empty bottles out of Colm's glasshouse, each wrapped in a sheet of the standard, each with a small plain shield cut into its shoulder on the goldsmith's wheel and nothing on the shield yet. Wenna sat on the tailboard with her feet hanging and a book on her knee. Dace sat beside the carter and talked to him about Milford, which was where they both came from, and the carter said that he had never liked Milford, and Dace agreed with him, and after that they got on.

The root rode in Emeric's coat, against his ribs, wrapped in the green silk.

He had thought about the silk. It was only a lining, and the chipped stone in the strongbox did not need it to be a chipped stone, and Ferra, asked, had said that a strongbox was not a wardrobe and she did not keep things in it for the sake of what they were wrapped in. So he had taken it. It kept the stone from knocking against the buttons of his coat, which was a reason, and he let that be the reason.

Five days. The relay posts on their ridges. Ward, at each post, stopping long enough to be seen.

The gall store was a different room.

Saban had had it scrubbed down to the stone and limed twice, and the bins were gone and the marks of the bins were gone under the lime, and a new door hung on the passage side, heavy, with a frame round it that was new too and did not match the old wall it was set in. Bram's lock went into the door on the first evening. Emeric fitted it himself with Hobb holding the lamp, and turned the key, and tried the handle, and gave one key to Wenna and one to Dace and kept the third, and Hobb held the lamp.

They set the printers up that night with the shop shut and the house empty. Wendel had gone to his daughter's by the gate at the fourth bell, as he did now; he did not sleep in the house any more. It was the right thing for a master who was not the owner to do, and he did it without being asked and without saying why, and Emeric noticed it and did not say anything either.

The two beds went along the long wall, where the bins had been, on trestles Saban had got from a joiner in the town who had been told they were for a counting bench. Jorrin's rails went on them, and the bridges, and the carriages, and Dace, who had done nothing like it in his life, lay on his back under the first bed with a lamp and a spanner and tightened what Emeric told him to tighten and did not once ask what it was for. Wenna filled the heads. She had watched Ferra fill the heads of the first printer and she did it the same way, slowly, with a funnel of folded sheet, from a jar of the plain ink, and when Emeric took the jar of Kaldo black out of the crate to put it somewhere she said, before he could put it down, "Far end," and he put it at the far end.

The root went in last. He took it out of the silk and turned it over once in his hand, and set it in the socket on the board at the end of the beds with the fine brass leads running away from it to each bridge, and closed his palm over it, and felt it wake. Nothing happened. Nothing would happen. There was not a stone within a mile of it with anything to tell it yet; the brass knots were still in their crate in their sawdust, and the Kaldo black in the town was in Wendel's cellar in stoppered jars, and the two printers stood on their trestles in the new lime with their carriages parked at the corners of their beds and their heads full, waiting for a page that was not coming.

Wenna looked at them for some while. Then she went and sat down at the table under the window and opened her book.

"It'll be a while," Emeric said.

"Mistress Ferra said it would be," she said. "She said to bring a book."

Wendel had done six boilings by then.

They were in the ledger in Hobb's copy of Ferra's hand, one line each, the day and the stone and the master, and the six stones sat on a shelf in the boiling room in six small clay cups with their numbers painted on the cups in white, drained and dull. The ink from each boiling stood in the cellar in its own stoppered jars with a card on each jar tied round the neck. Nobody in the house would have put two boilings in one jar. Wendel had not been told not to. He had been making ink for fifty years, and he knew that no two boilings were ever the same ink however much a man wished they were, and it had not crossed his mind to mix them.

Emeric went down to the cellar with Hobb and the bottles.

He filled them himself, at a bench, with a funnel. Twelve bottles to a house, out of one boiling each. Boiling two for Placit. Boiling three for Hollin. Four for Dray, five for Vennor, six for Strade. And boiling one, the first, the one that had dried on Wendel's stick, for Lisle, because Lisle held half the town and all the woods to the south of it and it would have been a slight not to send him anything, and because Emeric wanted to see what he did with it.

He stoppered each bottle as it was filled and waxed the stopper, and then he wrote the book.

It was a small book, the issue book, ruled in the keep like the other, and it was Ferra's and would go to Ferra, and he wrote it in his own hand because nobody else was going to write it: House Placit, boiling two, twelve bottles. House Hollin, boiling three, twelve bottles, and so on down, six lines. Then he took the bottles up to the shop and gave them to the engraver Saban had found, a thin quiet man who cut glass for the chapel and would cut anything else for money, and by the next evening each bottle had a house's arms on its shoulder in the small plain shield, cut fine, catching the light.

The reams went up in the same boxes. A pen for each house, struck on Jorrin's dies off the master nib and slit by the girl in the finishing room with the lens, laid in a narrow case lined with baize.

Five of the boxes he would carry in himself, one to each dinner, as a guest carries a gift. The sixth he sent out on the fourth day by Saban's boy, with a card in his own hand on Kaldo paper, to the house on the south side of the town that had not asked him to dinner, and in the evening the boy came back with it under his arm.

It had not been opened. The card was still tied to it. A servant at the door, the boy said, a man in brown, had thanked him very kindly, and said that his lordship was grateful to His Highness for the thought and did not need anything, and had given the boy a Copper for his trouble and shut the door.

"Did he say why?"

"No, Your Highness." The boy thought about it. "He was very polite."

Emeric put the box on the shelf in the back room beside the Kaldo black, and Wenna, at the table under the window, looked at it for a while and went back to her book.

The dinners were at the five townhouses in turn, over eleven nights, and there were never fewer than a dozen people at any of them.

Placit's was the first and the largest. The house stood on the north side of the square, next along from the Staple, a tall grey house with too many windows, and the hall was long and warm and lit with more candles than it needed, and there were twenty at table: Lord Aubrey Placit at the head, the eldest of the five and the warden of the Staple, with his wife on his right and her sister beside her and a son who did not speak; and the other four heads with their people round them; and two men from the Staple's floor; and a physician; and a man from Milford nobody introduced who ate a great deal and listened to everything. Ward sat four places down from Emeric, which was as near as a guest's man could be put at a table like that, and ate what he was given, and did not drink, and nobody at the table thought it strange, because they did not think about him at all.

Placit was a large, slow, courteous man with a voice that had been used for a long time to being the last one in a room, and he was good at it. He did not ask Emeric anything for the first hour. He talked about the house, which his grandfather had built, and the square, which his grandfather's grandfather had paved, and the Staple, which had a charter from the Crown three kings back and which he said he did not govern so much as keep, the way a man keeps a clock. He talked about Sethe once, in passing, as a man he had known for thirty years and had been sorry to see come to the end he came to, and he said it with a small sad movement of his head that made it perfectly clear to everyone at the table what he thought of the Crown's judgment and made it impossible for anybody to say he had said anything.

Emeric agreed with him about the clock.

He agreed with him about a great many things. He had come in the door the way he had once come into rooms at the capital, a little late, a little loud, and very glad to be there, and he let his cup be filled more often than he drank from it, and he laughed at the right places and at one or two of the wrong ones. When the talk came round to him, as it was always going to, he let it. He said that he had been young when he came south. That was not news to anybody at the table and they smiled. He said that he had done a great many things in his first years that he would do differently now, that there was a kind of young man who thought a town could be put right by ordering it put right, and that four baronies and some years had taught him that a place ran the way it ran for reasons, and that a sensible man learned the reasons before he tried to change them. He said that he had spent two years reforming things and had made a great many enemies and not much money, and that he would like, now, to learn how the south actually worked. He said it looking down the table at Placit, and Placit, after a moment, inclined his head.

"The south," said Placit, "is very old, Your Highness."

"So I'm told. Nobody told me before I got here."

"No. They wouldn't have. The capital has never cared for how we do things." Placit turned his glass by its foot. "Your father, if I may say so, least of all."

Emeric said that his father had never cared for how anybody did anything, and said it loudly, loud enough to reach the far end of the table and the man from Milford, and people laughed; and in the place in him that kept such things he thought of a small room in the capital and a man bending close to a boy of eight and saying, very quietly, that anything said loud enough to carry was for whoever stood behind the door. He had said it loud. Everyone at the table had heard it. And not one of them, he thought, had the least idea what he had just told them.

At the end of the evening, at the door, Placit took his hand in both of his.

"You must forgive an old man," he said. "We didn't know what to make of you. The judgment, and the post, and the counting house. We thought you'd come to take us apart."

"I came to learn," Emeric said.

"Then we'll teach you." Placit let go of his hand. "The gift was very kind. Too kind. My steward says he has never seen a finer sheet."

"Try the ink."

"I will," said Placit, and smiled, and Emeric could see from the smile that he would not, or not yet, and did not mind. A man who has been given a present by somebody he does not trust puts it on a shelf and looks at it for a while first. Emeric would have done the same.

Hollin's was the third night, and at Hollin's there was a great deal of meat.

Lord Garrat Hollin was a short broad man who talked about money the way other men talked about the weather, as a thing that was always happening and always about to change and always worth remarking on. He talked about the price of rags, which was going down, and the price of galls, which was going up, and the price of a good horse at the Milford fair, and the price of the Staple's new crane, which had been too high, and he talked about them across twelve other people at his table without appearing to notice that any of them were there. He wanted to know what Kaldo's paper cost to make. Emeric told him a figure a third too high, and Hollin was delighted, because it meant that Kaldo was selling it at a loss, and he explained to Emeric kindly at some length why that could not go on.

After the meat, while the table was cleared and the sweet wine came round, Hollin leaned over and put something into Emeric's hand below the edge of the cloth.

It was a purse. It was heavy.

"The usual," Hollin said, not loudly and not quietly, in exactly the voice he had used for the price of the crane. "From the five of us. It's been let go since the old man went, which was a shame, and nobody's fault, and we'd like to put it right. It's only what's always been done. You'll find it's always been done."

Emeric weighed it in his hand below the cloth.

He knew the phrase. He had last seen it in Ferra's hand in the long room, copied off a letter in one of Sethe's boxes, in a column of letters that said the same thing year after year: the usual, as before. He knew what it was. It was the tenth, the tenth part of the Staple's duty that a charter three kings old reserved to the lord of Wayford for the upkeep of the road, and that Sethe had taken into his own purse for eleven years and called by that name, and that had not been paid to anybody since the judgment, and that was not the five's to give, or Sethe's to take, or a gift, or a bribe. It was the barony's, by law, and they were handing it to him under the table as though it were theirs.

He laughed, and put it in his coat.

"I'd heard the south was generous," he said.

"The south is practical," said Hollin, pleased, and turned back to the man on his other side to tell him the price of the sweet wine.

That night, at the inn, Emeric counted it out on the table in front of Ward and Hobb and Saban. Four Gold Rand, three Silver, and some Copper, which was about what a tenth of a quarter's duty through the junction should come to, give or take a clerk's rounding. Hobb wrote the figure on a sheet, and under it, at Emeric's word, in Ferra's hand, received from House Hollin, at table, called the usual; held, and the purse went into a bag with the sheet, and the bag was sealed, and it went south in the morning in the weekly bag with the ordinary rider, to Ferra, who would open her box and put it in.

Dray's was a young man's house and a young man's dinner, and the youngest of the five made it plain before the soup that he had been waiting to give it.

Lord Edric Dray was perhaps ten years older than Emeric looked and a good deal less than that in every other way. He had been the last of the five to come to his house and the last to come to the Staple's table, and it showed; he talked too fast, and laughed first, and asked Emeric three questions about the war before the others had sat down and one about Grayrock's stone that he was plainly not supposed to ask, because Vennor, across the table, looked at his plate. He wanted to know if it was true about the lane and the burning. He wanted to know if it was true that the prince had once put a man through the shoulder with a lance at a gallop. It was not true and Emeric said it was, a little, and Dray was delighted.

He was the only one of the five who opened his gift at the table.

He sent for it after the meat and had the box brought in and opened it in front of everybody, the ream and the case and the twelve bottles in their straw, and took one bottle out and held it up to the candles to look at the arms on its shoulder, and said that it was the handsomest thing anybody had sent him in years.

"We've always had ours from the north," he said. "Lisle's man brings it down with the wool. It's a brown sort of stuff, it goes grey in a year." He turned the bottle. "Is it true it dries as you write?"

"Near enough."

"Then I'll never blot a letter again." He set it down beside his plate as though he meant to use it on the tablecloth. Placit, three places down, said something quiet to his neighbour, and Dray did not hear it, or did, and moved the bottle an inch nearer his plate.

Vennor's dinner was the fourth and the smallest, fourteen at table, and the quietest. Lord Mattias Vennor was a narrow careful man with very clean hands who said perhaps forty words in three hours, most of them to his wife, and when Emeric gave him the box at the door he took it with a small bow and handed it to a steward without looking at it, and Emeric did not see it again. At the table he asked Emeric nothing at all. Late in the evening, when somebody asked him whether he had come in from the manor for the season, he said that he had come in for the dinner and would go out again the day after, that he did his letters at home, that the town was loud and he did not like writing where he could hear the street, and that his steward came in twice a week for anything that needed the Staple. He said it to the man who had asked, and not to Emeric, and did not look at Emeric while he said it, and Emeric thought about it all the way back to the inn.

Strade's was the last.

The house was the smallest of the five, a narrow brick front on the east side of the square with a porch too deep for it, and Dame Oriel Strade had kept it alone since her husband died, and kept the Staple's books in the room over the Staple's arches, and had done both, Saban said, for eleven years without a day's illness or a clerk she had not trained herself. She was a small upright woman in grey who sat at the head of her own table with her hands folded in front of her plate and watched her guests eat the way a clerk watches a column being added by somebody else. There were thirteen at table, and the other four heads among them, and Emeric had the place at her right hand.

She let him talk. He did it well. He had done it now four nights running and it came, by the fifth, without his reaching for it: the laugh at the right place, the cup lifted and not drunk, the small complaint about his father at the right volume, the question put to Placit about the Staple's crane that let Placit explain the crane for a quarter of an hour. She listened to all of it. Now and then she ate.

"You were a very different man at the judgment, Your Highness," she said, late, when the table had broken into four conversations, and without turning her head.

"I was a very young one."

"It was not so very long ago."

"It was a long while," Emeric said.

"I was in the square." She took a small piece of bread and did not eat it. "I watched you. You didn't raise your voice once, that whole day. My husband used to say that a man who never raises his voice is either very sure of himself or has somebody behind him who is. I remember thinking, that morning, that you were the first kind." She put the bread down. "You've raised your voice a good deal this evening."

He answered her badly.

He did it on purpose, and it was not hard. He laughed, a little too loudly, and said that a man could not be expected to keep a straight face his whole life, and that the south had taught him to enjoy himself, and that he hoped she would forgive him for enjoying her wine as much as he had, and he lifted his cup to her. It was a stupid answer, the answer of a young man who had not understood what he was being told and was a little drunk and a little pleased with himself, and he made it as stupid as he could without overdoing it, and he watched her hear it.

Dame Strade looked at the cup. Then she looked at him, for the first time that evening, directly, for about as long as it took to read a short line, and he did not look away and did not let anything show.

"Of course, Your Highness," she said, and turned to Hollin on her other side and asked him something about rags.

At the door, as they were going, her steward brought out the box he had carried in, and held it out to her, unopened, and she took it from him in both hands and thanked Emeric for it very properly, in front of the other four heads and their people crowding the porch, and said that she would think of him whenever she wrote. Then she gave the box back to the steward, and the steward took it in, and the door closed.

She had taken it. Refusing it in front of the other four would have marked her, and she knew it, and she had taken it for that reason and no other, and Emeric knew that too, and she knew that he knew. He stood a moment on the step in the dark.

"Home," said Ward.

They walked back across the square.

It was late and the square was empty. The Staple stood on the west side of it under the stars with its arches dark and its bale crane folded up against the wall like an old heron, and the ram on its weathervane turning a little in a wind that did not reach the ground, and the lamp in its upper window lit, as it always was. Somebody was always up there. Emeric had walked past it four times in two years and then eight more in the last fortnight and he had still never seen that lamp out.

They went past it and up the junction road toward the inn, not fast. Ward walked on his left, half a step behind, where he always walked, and said nothing for most of the way, which was ordinary.

At the inn door he stopped.

"That came back easy," he said.

Emeric looked at him. Ward was looking up the road, the way he looked at everything, as though it might have a knife in it.

"The mask," Ward said. "In the capital, you used to have to find it. I watched you. There'd be a moment at the door. You'd stop, and then you'd go in." He was quiet a moment. "Tonight you didn't stop."

He went in. Emeric stood on the step a little longer, and then followed him.

In his room he lit the lamp himself, and poured water from the jug into the basin, and took his coat off and his shirt off and washed his face, with both hands, for a long time, the way a man does who has come in from a dirty road, and dried it, and looked at the towel as though there might be something on it.

There was not. He hung it on the rail and put the lamp out.
