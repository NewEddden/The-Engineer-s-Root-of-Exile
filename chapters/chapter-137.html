Chapter 137 - The Missing Line

The other press was in the cellar, and nobody outside the six had seen it.

It did not look like a press. It looked like a table. A low flat bed of oak the size of a large page, with a frame over it on hinges, and in the bed, under a thin sheet of glass, set in rows like the squares on a draughts board, a great many small grey stones, each the size of a fingernail. Four hundred of them. Twenty rows of twenty. All cut from the fragments in the bottom of the crystal chest, the ones too small for anything else, that Emeric had kept for four years in a cloth bag because he did not throw away stones.

Under the stones, at the bottom of the bed, a larger one. Flat, grey, the size of his palm. A control stone. Cut from inside, in the room, with a pattern.

You laid a sheet of the sized paper on the glass, and a fine dust of dry Kaldo ink over it, evenly, from a shaker. You closed the frame. And you put your hand on the control stone, and gave it a breath of mana, the easy draw a lamp stone takes, and it told each of the four hundred little stones over it whether to push or not. The ones that pushed pushed the ink. Not much. A hair. The way Emeric had pushed a line of Kaldo ink across a page with his hand in January. Each little stone pushed the ink over it into its own square of the sheet, and held it there, and the ones that did not push left their squares bare, and when you opened the frame and lifted the sheet and blew the loose dust off, what was left on the paper was the pattern. Letters. Lines. A ruled form.

No type. No ink ball. No screw. Nothing to wear out. Nothing on the face of any stone for anybody to copy. To print a different page you went into the room and cut a different pattern into the control stone, and that was all.

A mana printer. Emeric called it that in the cellar, the first time it worked, on the fourth of February, and Bram said that it was the stupidest name he had ever heard for anything and used it from then on.

It could not leave the walls. Emeric had said so before the first stone was set in the bed, at the table, and nobody had argued. A press that anybody with the faculty could work, that wrote with mana, from a pattern nobody could see, was a press that in the wrong hands was a hundred commoners each with a stone with a sign cut in it that would fire. The town had learned what one commoner with one cut sign could do in the third summer. The mana printer would stay in the cellar, and print Kaldo's forms, and the plain press in the mill would print everything that went outside.

Its first run was Ferra's.

The forms she had been ruling by hand for four years: the wage line, the stone ledger, the ore weigh-sheet, the census card, the twin year's index. Fifty of each, in an afternoon, every line ruled true to a hair, every heading in the same hand, a hand that was nobody's. She took the first sheet of the wage form off the glass herself and held it up to the cellar lamp and looked at it, the ruling and the headings in faint grey Kaldo ink, exactly, perfectly, as she would have ruled them herself on a good day with a new pen and no sleep lost.

"It's my form," she said.

"It's your form."

"It's my ruling." She held it closer. "That's how I rule a wage line. With the date column wider than the name. Nobody else does it like that." She looked at him over the sheet. "You copied my hand."

"I cut it from one of yours. In the room." He did not look away. "It's the best ruling in Kaldo. I wanted the forms to have it."

Ferra looked at the sheet a while longer. Then she put it on the pile, carefully, squared, and did not say anything else.

Tess, at the end of the bed in her chair, had picked up the second sheet while the ink on it was still settling, and laid it face up on the bench beside a spent lamp stone, a drained grey one from the charging room's bucket, waiting to be ground for dust. She was looking at the stone.

"It's warm," she said.

Emeric looked round.

"The stone." Tess put her finger on it. "It was cold when I put the sheet down. It's warm now. A little." She took her finger off it and put it back. "Next to the wet ink."

He came and put his own finger on the stone. It was warm. Very faintly. The warmth a stone has after it has been held for a minute in a hand.

He waited. They both waited, by the bench, with their fingers on it, while the ink on the sheet beside it dried. It took about the time it took Bram to pull the next sheet. And as the ink went from grey and wet to grey and dull, the warmth in the stone went with it, and was gone, and the stone was cold.

Tess looked at him.

"What was that?" she said.

"I don't know."

She wrote it on her slate anyway. Emeric watched her do it. It was a line, under the date, small, in her careful hand: wet Kaldo ink warms a spent stone near it. Stops when dry. Then she rubbed her thumb on the stone again, and it was cold, and she put the slate away, and neither of them said anything more about it.



He went up to her room that night.

It was late. The keep was asleep. The light under her door was on, as it always was, and her name was over it in black paint, and the candle on the sill across the passage that nobody had ever admitted to keeping lit was lit. He knocked. She said come in. She did not look up.

She was at the trestle, under the lamp, with the barony's book open in front of her and the pen in her hand. The room was high. It had been the old baron's linen store, and before that, Hale had told him, the upper half of a hall that somebody had floored across the middle a hundred years ago; so the ceiling was a long way up, much further than a room that size wanted, crossed with old dark beams, and the lamplight did not reach it. The shutters were closed. The fire had burned down.

The column was on the trestle beside the book. The alliances. Every house that had written, every daughter and sister and niece, every line ruled and filled and summed, and the last line the Sallow girl's, with its four true words, and under it the ruling running on, empty, to the foot of the sheet.

She had not put it away. She had had a week. It was still there, on the trestle, at her elbow, where she could see it.

He stood at the end of the trestle.

"There's a line missing," he said.

Ferra's pen stopped.

She did not look up. She looked at the book in front of her, at the line she had been writing, and her pen was an inch above the page, and it stayed there.

"In the column," Emeric said. "The alliances. Every house that's written. Every daughter. What each of them is worth to the four baronies." He put one finger on the sheet, at the bottom, at the empty ruling under the Sallow line. "There's a line missing."

"There isn't," said Ferra. Her voice was quite level. "Every house that's written is in it. I checked it twice."

"It's not a house."

She put the pen down.

She put it down very carefully, beside the book, the dark one with the gold band, and laid her hand flat beside it, and he saw her hand was not quite steady, and saw her see that it was not.

"It's the only one I want," Emeric said. "The line that isn't there. It's the only line in that column I've ever wanted. I want it more than all of them." He took his finger off the sheet. "I don't care what it's worth. I don't know how to rule it. I don't think there's a column for it. But I wanted you to know it was missing, and that I'd seen it was missing, before I answer any of the others. Because I'm going to answer all of them no."

Ferra did not move.

She sat at the trestle in the lamplight with her hand flat beside the pen, and looked at the book, not at him, not at the column, for so long that he thought she was not going to say anything at all.

"The stay," she said.

It was the first thing she said. Before anything else. He had known it would be. He had known it since the hall, the night Hale read the sheet.

"Clause nine," said Ferra. "The Chancellor's stay. Written in my hand. Over my name. That the holder can't set aside." She did not look up. "It's the only thing at that table that isn't yours. Everything else on the sheet answers to you. The stay doesn't. That's what it's for. You wrote it. You said: because I'm going to be wrong." Her voice was very quiet. "And if I marry you, it's yours."

"It's not."

"It's not," Ferra agreed. "In the book. In the clause. It's still mine, in ink." She looked at the column beside her. "But every man in the four baronies who ever wants to say you're wrong, and can't, will look at the one person who can stop you, and see your wife." She put her finger on the empty ruling at the foot of the sheet. "The one check on your power, bought. That's what they'll say. And they'll be wrong, and it won't matter that they're wrong, because they'll say it, and the stay is only worth anything as long as everyone believes it's mine." She took her finger away. "I've thought about it. Since the hall. Since before the hall. Since the night on the lane by the cooper's, if I'm honest, which I try to be."

"Ferra."

"I haven't said no," said Ferra. She looked up at him then, for the first time since he had come in. "I want you to hear that. I haven't said no. I'm saying the first thing first. Because if I said the other thing first, I'd never say this, and it has to be said, by me, before anybody else at that table says it for me."

He looked at her. At her face in the lamplight, white, the freckles standing out across her nose, her red hair coming down out of its pins on one side, and her eyes on his, steady, waiting, having said the hardest thing first because she always did.

"Then I'll say the other thing," he said. "I'll say it properly. I've been thinking about how to say it for a week and I've got most of it. It starts with the first summer, when you were sitting at your father's table with the barony's books and you told me you weren't a servant and you didn't come up to the keep, and I knew then, I didn't know what I knew but I knew it, and then the lane by the cooper's, and the river, and the ice cream, and the pen, and every column, and,"

She kissed him.

She got up from the trestle, in the middle of the sentence, and came round the end of it, and put one hand flat on his chest to stop him moving, the way she put her hand flat on a page to keep it still, and kissed him.

It stopped the speech. That was what it was for. He understood that later. At the time he did not understand anything.

She kissed him once, not long, and took her mouth away, and stood there in front of him with her hand still flat on his chest, and looked at him. She looked at him the way she looked at a column that had been wrong for a week and had finally, at the last line, come out right.

"That was going nowhere good," she said.

"No."

"You'd have got to the river and stopped and said something about the stones." She did not take her hand away. "And then you'd have gone on."

"Probably."

"I know you," said Ferra.

And then she did not say anything else, and neither did he, and he put his hands on her waist, carefully, the way he would have held something he was afraid of dropping, and she let him, and he kissed her.



He did not mean to do it.

He felt it come up before he knew what it was. The deep quiet thing in him that Armand said sounded like a stone falling down a well: it did not fall. It rose. It came up through him from somewhere a long way down, and through his hands on her waist into her, and he felt the glyph come up on its own, the way his fire came up on its own in the dark when he was frightened, and light the air between them, a soft pale light, the colour of a lamp stone's, writing itself on the air beside her face in lines.

And they rose.

Not fast. Not like the stable roof. Slowly, steadily, the way the chair rose off the shed floor when the stone took its weight, the way he had risen off his own floor in the first spring, a hand's width, behind the shutters, with the candle on the hearth. Both of them. Her weight and his, taken off together. Her feet left the boards. His left them. And they went up, together, through the high dark room, past the top of the trestle, past the lamp, past the shelves, up into the shadow where the lamplight did not reach, with the glyph's soft light going up with them and showing her face.

She did not stop kissing him. He felt her feel the floor go. He felt her hand on his chest close, once, on his shirt, hard. And then open again, and stay flat, and she did not let go of him, and she did not stop.

The hold did not waver. Five years. The coin on the table in the capital, lifted and held at hand height, every night, until his hand stopped wandering. The hover behind the shutters in the first spring. The river with a child in his arms and a man on his back. Five years of every night, and it held now, steady, both of them, in the dark under the beams of her room, as easily as he would have held a cup.

Her hair touched the timber. He felt it brush the beam above them.

They hung there. Twenty feet above her trestle, in the dark, under the old beams, with the lamp very small below them on the trestle beside the book and the column, and the glyph's light on her face. He could have stayed. He could have held them there all night.

And then she kissed him back properly.

Not to stop a speech. Not once. She put both arms round his neck and kissed him the way he had not known she could, as if she had been deciding to for a very long time and had finally finished the sum, and he lost the thread.

Completely. All of it. The hold, the glyph, the weight. He forgot he was holding anything at all.

They came down.

Faster than he meant. Not a fall; something in him caught at it at the last, from habit, the way a man's hand catches at a rail on a stair he has missed; but fast. Down through the dark and past the shelves and the lamp, and onto the trestle, onto her back on it and him half on top of her, with a crash that knocked the lamp sideways and spilled the barony's book onto the floor, and the inkwell, the big one, the barony's own, went over across the trestle in a black flood and into her hair and down his sleeve and all over the column of alliances, every house, every daughter, every true word.

The lamp rocked and steadied.

Ferra lay on her back on the trestle in a pool of the barony's ink, with her hair coming down into it and his weight half on her and her arms still round his neck, and looked up at him.

And she laughed.

He had never heard her laugh like it. Not the short surprised laugh in her doorway in January, when she had put her wrist to her mouth to stop it. Not the corner of her mouth moving, and stopping. She laughed with her head back on the trestle in the ink and her eyes shut and her whole body shaking under him, helplessly, the way a child laughs, until there were tears running back from the corners of her eyes into her ears and she could not get her breath.

He was laughing too. He had not known he was.

"You," Ferra said, when she could. She opened her eyes. She looked up at him, and past him, at the beams in the dark twenty feet above them, and back at him. "You did that."

"Yes."

"Not a stone."

"No."

She looked at him. He watched her do it: the column, the box, the shaft, the chair she had booked as test apparatus in a ledger in December, the springs on the carriage that went over a ford like a boat. The horse on the downs. The heat in his back. Every line she had ever written about him that had not added up, laid end to end in her head in order, the way she laid a column out, and summed.

She did not ask. She did not say anything at all for a long breath, lying in the ink.

And he saw, looking down at her, that she had half known. That she had had the column on him since the year of the still, and had never been able to fill in the last line of it, and had not asked, because she did not ask for what she had no right to. And that the last line had just come out, and it was not a surprise to her at all. It was a relief.

"Ward knows," she said. Not a question.

"And Rylan. Since the river."

"Not Imani."

"No."

Ferra was quiet.

"Then it goes in my column," she said, "and nowhere else." She reached up and pushed a strand of his hair off his forehead with an inky finger, and left a mark, and looked at the mark, and did not apologise for it. "And in the morning I'll have to explain to Hale why the barony's book is on the floor."

"Tell him I knocked it over."

"I will." Her mouth moved at the corner. "It's true."

He got off her. He got off the trestle and stood up and held out his hand, and she took it, inky, and he pulled her up, and she sat on the edge of the trestle in the wreck of the ink with her hair half down and black at the ends, and looked at the column of alliances lying in the flood beside her, every house in it gone under, and the empty ruling at the foot of the sheet gone under with the rest.

She picked it up by one corner and held it up, dripping.

"I'll make a new one," she said.

"With the line?"

Ferra looked at the sheet, dripping ink onto her knee. Then at him.

"I haven't said yes either," she said. "I said I haven't said no. You'll have to wait for the rest." She dropped the column in the ink. "But I'll make a new one."

She stood up off the trestle. She looked up, once more, at the beams in the dark.

"Again," said Ferra. "Somewhere without ink."
