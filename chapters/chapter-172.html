Chapter 172 - The Long Line

Paskin opened the first cutting of the Wayford line on the twenty-third of February, two miles north of the gate, where the capital road climbed out of the river flats onto the first of the long low ridges that ran all the way to Wayford, and he did it in front of more people than had ever watched him do anything.

He had not wanted them. He had said so at the long table, twice. The Milford line's cuttings had been opened on Kaldo's own ground in front of Kaldo's own people, who had seen earth mages at work on the drains for three years and thought of it as they thought of a good ploughman. This was the capital road. The road every carrier and drover and post rider in the south of the kingdom used to go north. On a weekday morning in February there would be forty carts on it between the gate and the ridge.

There were sixty. Word had gone up and down the road for a week that Kaldo was going to cut the hill, and the carts had come early and drawn off onto the verge on both sides and stopped, and the drovers had let their beasts graze the bank, and a post coach from Wayford had pulled up at the top of the ridge with its passengers all out of it in their travelling cloaks, standing in a row along the edge of the road like people at a hanging.

Linus had the line pegged. He had walked it in the autumn of the fourth year with his theodolite and his chain and two boys to carry the staff, all the way to Wayford and back, calling it the telegraph survey, which it also was; the telegraph's poles followed the road and the road followed the valleys, more or less. The railway would not. A railway wanted gentler grades than a road had any use for and longer, slower curves, and Linus's pegs went off the road at the foot of the ridge and up through a farmer's pasture at a long slant, where the hill was lowest, and Paskin's cutting would take it through the crest in a trench a quarter of a mile long and thirty feet deep at its deepest, so that a loaded train would never see the ridge at all.

Paskin stood at the south end of the line of pegs with his pipe unlit in his mouth. Three of Armand's students stood along it at intervals, two boys and a girl, all earth; the girl was the one who had chosen Paskin at the third Commencement, and she was white to the lips. Odo Pike stood at the north end with his notebook, because the grade was his, and if it came out a hair off Linus's figure he would know it.

"Not at the road," said Paskin, round the pipe, to the girl, loudly enough that Emeric heard it from the verge. "At the hill. The road isn't there."

And then they leaned on it.

Emeric had seen it done on the Milford line and it was still not a thing he could watch without feeling it in his teeth. Four notes, slow and heavy, going down into the ground together; and the ground taking it, at first, the way a man takes a shove he has braced for, and then not. The turf split along the pegs, a long straight seam across the pasture and up the slope. Then the earth under the seam began to move. Not to fly or heave; it went the way sand goes in an hourglass, quietly and all at once, sliding out sideways from the line in two long low banks, and the trench opened down the middle of it like a book.

On the road there was no sound at all.

Then a drover's dog began to bark and could not be made to stop.

It took them the morning to go down six feet over a quarter of a mile. Six feet was what Paskin let them do in a day, because the students went white and then grey and then sat down, and Paskin made them sit down sooner than that; and behind them the levy came with picks and barrows and shovels to dress the sides and carry off what the mages had loosened, and the levy did most of the work, as they had on the Milford line. But it was the four standing still on the hill with their hands empty and the hill opening in front of them that the road saw. That was what the coach passengers would talk about at the posting house tonight, and in the capital in a fortnight.

Ward stood beside him on the verge. He had not said anything all morning.

"That's the most anybody outside this barony's ever seen us do," he said, when the students sat down. "In one place. At once."

"I know."

"I'll say it once," said Ward. "I've said it." He looked up the road at the coach, where a man in a good cloak was writing something in a book against the coach's door. "Somebody's counting."

"Somebody's always counting."

Ward did not answer that. He did not need to.



Three hundred and seventy miles, Odo said, by Linus's pegs. More than the telegraph's poles, because the poles went straight over hills that the rails would have to go round. The Grayrock line had been forty, and had taken a winter, and everybody had said it could not be done in a winter and Odo had done it. The Milford line had been thirty. This was nine times the both of them together, through four lords' land and the king's, over two rivers that would want bridges and through the long ridge country on Lord Lisle's side of the road, which would want cuttings like this one every few miles.

It would not open this year. It would not open next year. Ferra had laid it out at the long table in stages, the first forty miles, to the river crossing, by the autumn, and then on, a stage a season, as the money came in and the rail came out of Grayrock; and she had put at the bottom of her sheet the year she thought it would reach Wayford, and the year was a long way off.

"It'll be the biggest thing the kingdom's ever built," Odo said, the night after the cutting, at the table, over the sheet.

"It'll be the longest," said Ferra. "Which isn't the same." She drew a line under her figure. "Biggest is whatever costs most. And that's still the war."



The telegraph was garbling.

It had done it since the autumn, on wet nights, and worse in February, when it rained most days. A message went into the sounder at the gate tower clean and came out in Wayford with half its letters doubled and a quarter missing, and Wayford's clerk sent back the sign for say again, and the second time it came out garbled differently. Saban's man at the Wayford posting house had sent him a price for wool on the twelfth that had arrived reading, as far as anybody could make out, as a remark about a goat.

"It's the relays," said Tess.

Three hundred and fifty miles was too far for any current Kaldo could make to reach the other end strong enough to work a sounder. So the line went in lengths, and at each posting house along the road there was a relay in a box: a small electromagnet that the weak current from the last length could just barely pull, and when it pulled it closed a pair of contacts, and the contacts let a fresh current from the posting house's own cells into the next length. A tired runner handing on the message to a fresh one, all the way north.

And the contacts were brass. Every time they closed and opened there was a tiny spark between them, too small to see in daylight, and every spark ate a speck of brass. After a winter the points were pitted like a pocked face, and in the wet, with the damp in the boxes, the pits held a film of green, and the points stuck when they should have opened, or did not quite close when they should have closed; and a letter came out as two letters, or none.

Tess had the worst of them on the bench, eight relays her linemen had brought down off the road, and showed him the points under Ivo's glass. Craters. A whole small landscape on each one.

So the forge did a thing that was not a run at all, the way it counted runs, but went in the book anyway, because Ferra put everything in the book: a night in the cellar at the start of March, and out of it a box of contact points, two hundred of them, small grey buttons of the same tungsten as the filaments, harder than anything a spark could eat. And a fixture, one, of steel, a block with a slot and a screw and a set of feeler leaves, that set the gap between any two contacts to the same hair's breadth every time, without the man doing it having to judge.

Tess sent four linemen up the road with the box and the fixture and a copy of the fixture each, which Bram had made from the master in two days. They went from posting house to posting house for a fortnight, in the rain, and opened every relay box on the road, and took out the brass and put in the grey, and set every gap with the fixture.

On the twentieth it rained all day, heavily, the whole length of the road, from Kaldo to Wayford. Saban sent a message north at noon to his man at the posting house, a long one, about tallow, deliberately full of figures.

It came back at four, repeated, as Saban had asked. Every figure.

Saban read the slip standing in the telegraph room, and then read it again, and folded it, and said that this was the first time in a month that he had been able to tell whether his own man was cheating him, and that he would now go and find out.

Emeric stayed in the telegraph room after he had gone, looking at the relay on the shelf over the sounder that Tess had left there for the clerks to see, open, with its two small grey buttons inside. A switch that a current threw. A switch that, thrown, threw another current. One that did not stick. He had known what it was the day Tess put the pitted ones on the bench, and had not said, because there was nobody in the world yet to say it to, and because the next rung after it was so far up the ladder that saying it now would only have sounded like a man boasting of a house he meant to build when he had one brick.

A switch that could be trusted could decide something. Two of them could decide between two things.

He shut the relay box and went down the tower stair.



The third Lantern Night was on the sixth of March, and the balloon went up over the railhead.

Not over the river this year. Sorrel had asked for the ridge, and Ward, who had asked her to ask, had got it. The railhead was at the foot of Paskin's cutting now, two miles out on the capital road, with the first rails going down on their sleepers along the floor of the trench, and the balloon went up beside it on the long tether at dusk with Sorrel in the basket and Linus beside her with his best glass. From four hundred feet over the ridge they could see the whole of the capital road for nine miles north, in the last of the light, to where it went over the next ridge into the next lord's country; and the whole of Kaldo south of them, and the river, and the marsh, and the town's lanterns going up off the wall and the quay and the river stair into the dark.

Emeric went up the ridge with Ward to watch it from the railhead. From there the lanterns were a long slow drift of small gold lights rising out of the town below and sliding away down the valley on the wind, and under them the town itself, which looked different again this year. He could see it in the lanterns' light. Fewer thatched roofs: whole streets in the lower ward gone dark as the brickfield's tiles weathered, where last year they had been raw and pale. The arc lamps on the wall. The works across the river lit. The school's three windows, lit, on the south side of the square, white and small and steady; and they were not candles.

The balloon hung over them in the dark, glowing from inside from Sorrel's burner, like a lantern too big to let go of. Every quarter of an hour Linus flashed a lamp down at the railhead from the basket, a hand over and off it, in the telegraph's letters, for Ward. Road clear. Road clear. One cart, southbound, at the ninth milestone. Road clear.

"She could see an army from up there," said Ward, reading the lamp. He sounded very content. "Two days off."

"It's a balloon on a rope."

"It's a balloon on a rope that can see an army two days off." Ward watched the lamp. "I'm going to want one at Grayrock."

Down in the town, somebody had a fiddle going on the quay, and the sound came up the valley thin and late on the wind. Emeric did not send a lantern up this year. He had not written a name. Ferra was on the wall with the counting room's clerks, who had asked her to, and Imani was at the ward, because three thousand people with fire in their hands meant somebody would burn something, and somebody had: a boy from the dock quarter with his sleeve. Osric came up the ridge at ten to tell them so, unasked, and to say the boy would keep his arm and was asking for another lantern.



Fletcher had wanted something aboard that held her level that was not his wrists. He had said so last spring, a mile over Milford, and Emeric had not forgotten it.

The steady was a wheel.

A heavy wheel of brass, a hand's width across and as thick as two fingers, on an axle that ran in two of the forge's ball bearings; and the wheel and its axle hung in a ring, and the ring hung in a second ring, crosswise, each on its own pair of bearings, so that whatever the case round them did, the wheel could keep pointing wherever it had been pointing. A small battery motor spun it. When it was spinning fast enough to hum, you could tilt the case any way you liked, and the wheel did not tilt with it. The case went round the wheel. The wheel stayed.

"Gyroscope," said Emeric, on the bench, when Tess had spun it up for the first time and tipped the case, and the wheel had sat inside it as still as a stone in a stream while the brass rings swung round it. She had put her finger on the outer ring, very gently, and felt the wheel push back.

"It doesn't want to move," she said.

"No. And that's the whole of it."

Because the wheel stayed level when the ship leaned, the ship's lean showed, against the wheel, as a small turn of one ring against the other. And on the rings, Tess put contacts: four pairs, silver, one for nose down, one for nose up, one for port low, one for starboard low, set so close that a lean too small to feel would close one. A fifth pair on a second wheel, a smaller one, spun on its side, that held her heading, and closed when her nose swung off the line set on its dial. Each pair of contacts, closed, sent current to an electromagnet. Each magnet turned a key.

Brass keys, small ones, the shape Tess had made for the matrix cases when the ship was built, notched at the head; one turn of the magnet, one notch. While the contact stayed closed, the key went on turning, a notch at a time, click, click. When it opened, a spring took the key back to rest.

And each key sat in the neck of a stone.



He cut the trim stones in the forge's chamber, on the eighth and ninth of March, with Tess at the dial.

Ten of them. Eight gravity stones, a pair each for bow and stern and port and starboard, a separate small group under the deck line apart from the main matrix, which they did not touch; and two air stones for her heading, one each side, in the channels. None of them a control stone. Every one an ordinary stone, cut from inside to do one fixed thing, and nothing else, forever.

He sat on the floor of the chamber with the first of them in his hands and the room open, and the stone opened in front of him the way they did: a small locked box, with one shelf. And on the shelf, in the room's own terms, which felt to him like nothing so much as code, he wrote what it would do.

"First," he said aloud, because Tess had asked him to say it aloud the first time and had asked every time since. "If the key goes quiet, it stops."

Tess wrote it.

"If the key jams, if the current dies, if the magnet sticks and the key doesn't move for longer than a breath while the current's on: it stops. It doesn't hold the push it had. It doesn't guess. It goes back to nothing, and stays there, and the main matrix holds her, and Fletcher has his levers." He put that on the shelf, and put it first, at the bottom, under everything, so that it was what the stone would reach for before anything else if anything went wrong. "Then the push."

"Then the push," said Tess.

"It reads its key. The angle of it. Every notch the key's turned, it pushes a hair harder, along its own axis, down. Every notch the key goes back, a hair less." That went on the shelf. "Never more than the limit. The limit's this." He gave her the figure, and she checked it against her column, and nodded. "Past the limit it stops pushing harder and holds where it is. It never goes past."

He had written the quiet clause first on every stone since the oven shed. He did not think about it any more; it was simply the order in which the thing was done. But he noticed, tonight, that Tess had written it in her book first too, before he said it, in the line above the one where he had expected her to start; and that she had drawn a small box round it, which she did not do for anything else.

The eighth stone took him an hour, because he made it wrong. Not the shelf; the axis. He had cut it to push along the line its case would hold it in if the case were set the right way up in the hull, and the cradle for the starboard stern pair had been built by one of Fletcher's men from Tess's drawing turned over. It showed on Tess's bench test, where every stone went before it went into the ship: the key turned, and the stone pushed up instead of down. Tess said "Which end up," without looking up, and went to the column in her book that she had started the night the suit rolled in October, and wrote it in. He went back into the room and found it in five minutes, and cut it again.

The two heading stones were easier. They had only one thing to do and one thing to do when they could not do it.

At three in the morning there were ten stones on Tess's bench, in a row, tried, each with its key in its neck, and nothing wrong with any of them.



They tried the steady in the shed on the twelfth, with the K Discovery on her legs and four men on ropes.

Fletcher had rigged it: a rope from each of her four quarters to a ring in the shed's floor, so that a man on each rope, heaving, could tip her, a little, the way a gust would. Not much. She stood on her three legs and was not going anywhere. But with the trim group woken and the steady spun up and its contacts live, a hand's tilt on her deck would close a contact, and a key would turn, and a stone would push; and they would see it.

Tess at her station with the dials. Fletcher in the bow cockpit with his hands off the levers and in his lap, by order. Emeric on the slip, with his sense out.

"Port," said Fletcher, down the speaking tube to the ropes, and the two men on the port ropes, both of them Fletcher's, leaned back together.

The ship leaned to port. A hair.

Click.

It came out of her through the whole hull, small and sharp, the key in the port trim pair turning one notch. Click. And Emeric felt the two port stones wake under the deck line and push down, gently, a hair, and the hull came back. Click. The key went back. The contact opened. She sat level.

"Again," said Fletcher. "Harder."

They leaned harder. Click, click, click, click, four notches, four hairs of push, the hull leaning and coming back and the men on the ropes feeling her come back against them, and one of them laughed out loud. Then the bow ropes, and the bow pair clicked; then the stern; then all four ropes at once in turns, the men trying to catch her out, heaving in a ragged circle, and the keys clicking round the hull in the same circle a breath behind them, like a clock with something wrong with it, and the ship never leaning further than the width of a finger at the deck edge.

Then Tess cut the current to the port pair's magnet at her station without telling anybody, to see.

The port stones went quiet. At once. Nothing held, nothing guessed; the push went to nothing and stayed there, and the next heave on the port rope leaned her over a whole hand's width, and Fletcher's hands came up out of his lap onto the levers without his thinking about it and caught her.

Tess put the current back.

"Fail quiet," she said, writing. "Good."

"You might tell a man," said Fletcher, from the bow cockpit, into the tube.

"Then it wouldn't have been a test," said Tess.



They flew it on the night of the eighteenth, over the marsh, at two thousand feet, in a south-west wind that came in off the sea in long gusts.

Fletcher took her up himself, on the levers, as he always did. At two thousand feet he set her on her heading by the compass and dialed the heading into the second wheel's card, and then he put his hands in his lap, because Emeric had asked him to, for an hour. Bram sat beside him with the watch.

The first gust came under her starboard quarter a minute later. She began to lean.

Click.

She stopped leaning.

Fletcher's hands came up off his lap an inch and went down again. Emeric saw it from the stair head, through the bow cockpit's door. He did not say anything.

For a quarter of an hour Fletcher sat with his hands in his lap and his whole body leaning a little forward toward the glass and the levers in front of him, as if they were a dog he had told to stay and did not trust. Every gust, the keys clicked, somewhere in the hull, and she did not lean; every gust, his shoulders went tight, and then loose, a breath later, when she had not leaned. He did not speak. Bram read the watch every five minutes, aloud, in his flat forge voice, which was no help.

At the half hour Fletcher said, to the glass: "I don't like it."

"She's level," said Bram.

"I know she's level. She's been level for half an hour. I haven't done anything." He was quiet. "Twenty years I've had a boat under my hands. You feel her in your wrists. Every breath of wind. You know where she is because you're holding her there." He looked at his hands in his lap. "She's level and I don't know where she is."

Emeric came down the stair and stood in the cockpit door.

"Do you want to take her?" he said.

Fletcher thought about it. A gust came, and the keys clicked, and she held.

"No," said Fletcher. "Not yet."

At forty minutes he said nothing. At fifty he unbuttoned his coat and took a flask out of the inside pocket and drank from it and passed it to Bram, which he had never once done at the levers in all the night flights since the first one, because a man at the levers did not have a hand free. At fifty-five he leaned back in his seat for the first time, against the padding, and looked out of the glass at the marsh below, and the coast going away grey to the south, and the line of the river, and the town's lights, and said that he had never in his life seen the marsh from up here and actually looked at it.

At the hour Bram said, "Hour."

Fletcher sat a while longer. Then he reached out and put his hand on the lever in the middle of the board, the new one, with a Bakelite knob, that Tess had labeled in white paint STEADY OFF; and put her back into his own hands, and felt her go into them. The next gust took her a hair to port and he caught it in his wrists without looking, and Emeric felt him settle, the whole of him, the way a man settles into a chair he has owned for twenty years.

"There," said Fletcher. "That's where she is."

He flew her for ten minutes. Then he put the lever back.

"The steady," he said, to nobody, as the clicks began again somewhere in her hull. Trying it out. "I'll call it the steady. It's steadier than I am, and I'm the best there is." He put his hands in his lap. "Don't tell anyone I said that."
