Chapter 172 - The Wall Without Them

The gangs went on a Monday.

Tessaly Rook was at her post on the west side, on the stretch below the ridge where the wall came down off the limestone and ran across the low ground toward the ring canal's first cut, and she saw them go. She did not know what she was seeing at first. She saw men putting down barrows on the flat in the early light, and standing, and looking at each other, and then walking. Not all of them. One in four, perhaps. One in three on the west gangs, where the camp men were thickest. They walked off the flat in twos and threes toward the Western Gate road with their coats over their shoulders, not hurrying, and the gangers watched them go and did not shout, and the men who were left stood by the barrows they had not put down and watched too.

"Where are they going?" she said to Paskin, who was her pair that month, at the next post two hundred paces along.

"The lower field," said Paskin. "Enno's. They signed last week, most of them. The levy muster's today." He looked at the barrows. "Ward told Hobb they'd go. Hobb told the gangers. The gangers didn't tell us."

"Why not?"

Paskin shrugged. "What would we have done about it?"

She did not know. She stood on the footing with her hands on the stone and watched them go, and did the sum without meaning to, because she had been doing sums on this wall since the autumn and could not stop. There were nineteen hundred men on the quarry's tally in the spring. If a third of them had gone, there were thirteen hundred left, or less. And the wall was not a thing that thirteen hundred men could raise in a season. It was not a thing nineteen hundred could. It had been planned for the season as two thousand men and forty mages together, with the mages raising and the men setting, and the Tarrants' chain and Weekes's levels to keep them honest, and it had been planned to the week.

Hobb came along the footing at the second hour with his book, as he did every day, and stopped at her post.

"The prince says the wall doesn't stop," he said. "He says the earth's to be carried."

"By whom?"

Hobb looked at her.

"Oh," said Tessaly.

She had raised earth for thirty years. She had raised a hundred and forty yards of the north wall at Carrow Ford in nine days, the last third of it, with the whole garrison standing on the parapet to watch her, and she had thought then that it was the most she would ever do.

It was not how the wall was built at New Kaldo. She had learned that in the first week, in the autumn, and it had taken her most of the autumn to stop minding. At Carrow Ford a mage raised a wall. She put her hands on the ground and drew the earth up into the shape she wanted, and held it, and bound it, and when she let go it was a wall, and nobody needed to touch it afterward. It cost her everything she had, and it took her a week to come back from it, and it was the whole of the work.

At New Kaldo the mages did the least part. They lifted. That was all. The earth was already cut and dressed and stacked by the gangs from the quarry and the lime pits, heaped along the line in the places the Tarrants' chain said, and the mages put their hands on it and lifted it into line, block and fill and lime-set rubble, to the height of a course, and held it while the masons came along behind and set it with mortar and wedged it and struck the joint, and then let go, and it stayed, because the masons had set it and not because the mage was holding it. The mage did the lifting that would have taken the gangs a day of rope and shear-legs, in the time it took to say a psalm, and then moved on. And the wall behind her stood on the rock and on the lime and on the masons' work, and would go on standing if every mage on the flat dropped dead in the morning. The prince had said that on the first day. He had said it was the only rule he had for the wall, and it was the one she had minded most.

Now the gangs had gone, and the earth still had to be got from the heaps to the line, and there was nobody to carry it but them.

So they carried it.

It was not the same work. She found that out on the first day. Lifting a course into line from a heap at her feet was a thing she did with her hands on the stone and her eyes on the line, and it took the time of a psalm and cost her a little each time. Carrying the heap to her feet from the dump a hundred yards off, where the carts had left it, was another thing. It was not lifting. It was moving. She had to raise the earth and hold it and walk it, a load at a time, across a hundred yards of broken ground, in the air, at the height of a man's knee, without dropping it on the men who were left and without letting it spill. It was the thing the gangs had done with barrows. She did it with her hands out in front of her and the whole load hanging in the air in front of her like a cart with no cart under it, and after the fourth load her arms were shaking.

She had never had to do it before. No mage had. No mage in the realm moved earth a hundred yards when there were men and barrows to do it; it was a waste of a mage.

By the end of the first week they had worked out how. They paired again, a new way: one to carry and one to lift, and changed at noon. They used the men that were left for the barrows on the near heaps and carried the far ones themselves. Paskin worked out a way of carrying three loads stacked, one on another, that cost less than three loads carried separately, and taught it to everybody on the west side, and then to the rest. The earth mages carried and the water mages, who could not carry earth at all, took the lime pits, and drew the water up out of the slaking troughs and back into them faster than any bucket line, and the two fire mages worked the kilns hotter than the kiln men could get them.

And the quarry worked at night.

She saw it begin on the second Thursday after the gangs went, from the door of the lodging after supper. A light at the foot of the ridge, where the quarry face was. Not a fire. A fire was yellow, and moved, and this was white, and hard, and stood still, and it was so bright that the whole face stood up out of the dark behind it as sharp as noon, every drill mark and every wedge hole, with the men on the face throwing long black shadows up the stone. It hissed, faintly, when the wind dropped. Now and then it shivered, and steadied.

She went to look at it the next night, because everybody did.

It hung from a mast, like the wind pump's, with a box at the top and the light inside the box behind glass, and a man at the foot of the mast who climbed a ladder every hour and did something inside the box with a pair of tongs and climbed down again. There was a second mast at the far end of the face with a second light. And from the foot of each mast a line went away into the dark along the edge of the flat, looped from pole to pole on little white knobs of glazed clay like a washing line, down toward the river. She took it for a rope until she stood under it and saw the lamplight run along it. It was copper.

It went half a mile, to the sawmill on the race. The saw worked by day. By night now the saw frame was pinned, and the wheel turned something else, in a new shed built on beside the saw house: a thing the size of a brewer's copper, that hummed.

She walked down the line of poles to it on the third night and stood at the shed door in the dark and listened to it hum, and put her hand flat on the doorpost.

She had spent thirty years with her hands on stone. She could feel a charged lamp stone through a wall. She stood at the shed door and felt for the stone that had to be in there, driving the thing that drove the light, and there was nothing. The wheel turned and the thing hummed and half a mile off the quarry stood white in the dark, and there was not a grain of anything in any of it, anywhere, that she could feel. It was the first light she had ever stood near that had nothing in it at all.

She stood there a long time with her hand on the post. Then she went back up the flat to bed.

The quarry ran two gangs that summer instead of one: the day gang, and a night gang that came on at dusk and cut and dressed and loaded the cars under the light until the man with the tongs put it out at the second hour after midnight. So that in the morning the heaps were already near the line, nearer than the carts could have put them by day with the men who were left, and the mages carried less.

The men who had put up the poles were Bram's, from the works. She heard two of them at the tap one Sunday, filling their bottles. The prince had wanted it run on, one said. To the island. To the works yard and the keep and the long ward, the whole way. And there was no wire for it. Wire was drawn on a bench off the same wheel as the rolls, and the rolls drew rail. Everything the mill made that summer was rail for the hill, and the Chancellor had put that in writing in the spring and the prince had signed under her, and so the copper went as far as the quarry and no further.

"What's the rest of it for?" said the other.

"Ask him."

Neither of them would. Neither did Tessaly.

The wall did not stop.

It rose. That was the thing she would remember about that summer, afterward, when she thought about it; that it rose. It went up in its courses on the full circuit, two miles and three furlongs of it, round the whole flat from the ridge to the marsh and back, with the Tarrants' chain walking along its top every Saturday to check it, and it rose at very nearly the rate it had been planned to rise at with the gangs, because forty-one mages were carrying what six hundred men had carried, and the masons behind them set it as fast as it came.

Nobody had ever done that before either.

The palace went in that summer. She had not known it was to be called that, and nobody on the flat called it anything else. It sat in the middle of the flat inside its own ring of water, the canal that the prince called the water's road, and it was not a palace yet; it was a hole. A great square hole cut down to the rock, bigger than the whole of Carrow Ford's inner ward, with the rock floor of it levelled and the rock walls of it dressed and the first courses of what Weekes called the substructure going in along its floor in vaults: long low chambers of cut stone, arched, running in lines under where the palace would stand. There were forty of them. Nobody on the flat knew what they were for. Tessaly helped raise the springing of three of the arches, in a week when the west side was ahead of the east and she was sent to the middle, and she stood in the bottom of the hole in the cool with the arches going up round her and did not ask.

She had learned not to ask things on the flat. It was not that anybody forbade it. It was that everybody she asked said, pleasantly, that they did not know, and the ones who did know said, pleasantly, that it was the prince's business, and after a while she stopped.

The first quarter was lived in by midsummer.

That was the other thing she would remember. The north-east quarter, the one whose pipes had been laid in the grass the year before under nothing, had houses on it now. Not many. Two streets of them, stone below and timber above, narrow and tall and joined along the street in rows, with slate roofs and small yards behind, and every one of them standing on a drain. She had walked the streets in the spring when they were only walls. By midsummer there were people in them. Families off the island, from the rooms that went at two Silver the month for a corner of a floor, who had put their names in Ilsa's book for a house on the flat at the rent Ferra set, which was less, and been given a key, and a printed paper about the drain and the tap and what was not to go down either, and moved in with their beds on a handcart.

She walked through the first quarter on a Sunday, which was the one day the flat stopped, and watched them. A woman scrubbing a front step that was three months old. Children in the street with a hoop. An old man sitting in a doorway in the sun with his boots off and his feet on the new stone, looking at the wall across the ring canal as it went up, as if he were watching the weather.

At the end of the second street, where the street met the ring canal, there was the water tower.

It was the first quarter's water. It stood on a stone base at the corner by the canal, square, as tall as a church tower, with a tank of tin-lined oak at its top under a slate roof, and pipes going down from the tank into the ground and away under the streets to every house. The aqueduct came to it from the river shelf upstream, a mile off, along the top of the ridge on arches and then down in a covered channel, and filled the tank from the top, because the first quarter lay nearest the inlet, where the channel still ran high enough to do it. The quarters further down would have to lift theirs off the ring. There was a wind pump beside it with a vane on a mast, and a hand pump at its foot, and a hatch in its side at the height of a tall man's head, iron, locked. Beside the hand pump, against the tower's base, there was a small square house of the same stone, with a slate roof and a plank door, and nothing in it that she could see through the gap at the hinge but a swept floor and the end of a glazed clay pipe coming up out of it with a wooden plug in its mouth.

There was a tap at the foot of it, for the street. Brass, on a stone post, with a stone trough under it and a printed paper on the post about not washing clothes in the trough.

Tessaly stopped at it because she was thirsty. She turned the tap, and the water came out cold and clear into her cupped hands, and she drank.

It tasted of stone.

She stood with her hands wet and thought about it. It was not the taste of a limestone well; she had drunk from those all her life in the north and knew what they tasted of. It was something else. Very faint. The kind of taste one noticed only because one had spent thirty years with one's hands on stone and knew all its tastes. A clean faint cold flatness, like the air in a room where somebody had been working with charged crystal all day.

She drank again, to be sure. It was the same.

She looked up at the tower. At the locked iron hatch in its side, at the height of a tall man's head, and the stone cage she could just see through the slats of the tank's housing near the top.

A child came up beside her with a jug, and waited, and she moved aside and let the child fill it, and watched the child go off down the street with it slopping.

She did not ask anybody.

She thought about asking. She thought about it all the way back across the flat to the mages' lodging at the camp's edge, and at supper, and lying awake. She thought about the island, where there were water towers too, she had heard, over the market well and the others, built the winter before she came. She thought about the printed paper on the tap that said nothing about what was in the water. And she thought that if she asked, somebody would say pleasantly that they did not know, and somebody else would say pleasantly that it was the prince's business, and that then she would know that she had asked, and so would they.

She did not ask. She drank from the tap at the foot of the tower every Sunday for the rest of the summer, and it always tasted the same.

Bastian broke his promise the week the gangs went.

He was on the drains, as he had been since the autumn: the water mages' work, drawing the water off the cuttings and out of the culverts before the masons went down into them, and keeping the lime pits wet. It was the work that had taken the most men before the gangs went, because the drains were deep and the ground was wet and the culverts were long, and when the men went the drains took the most of what the water mages had. There were only nine water mages on the flat. Bastian was the strongest of them and the youngest and he had a wife and two sons at home in the capital and he was sending three Gold a month back to them and he did not want to lose his place.

The flasks came round at noon off the handcart. Hobb stood by it with his book and wrote down every mage who took one, and the mark on the flask, and the time. That was the rule. Tessaly had watched the prince say it on the first day, in front of all of them, at the first handcart, with the old master Armand standing beside him: the flask would fill a spent mage in the time it took to drop a lid, and it would fill him at the rate of the chamber's fourth hour, and a mage who took the chamber's rate too often would come up with the tracery, the way knights did, and it would not go away. One at a time. Only when spent. She had seen the old master's face while he said it. Everybody had.

Bastian had taken four in a day in his first weeks, before anybody had worked out what the flasks were doing to the water mages on the drains, and the old master had come down to the cutting and looked at his arms, and found them clean, and Bastian had promised him: one, when I'm spent. He had kept it all winter. He had kept it through the spring.

When the gangs went, he took three a day. Tessaly saw him at the handcart at noon, and again at the third hour, and again at the end of the afternoon when Hobb was packing up, grey in the face, and Hobb looked at his book each time and looked at Bastian and wrote it down. He did it on the Monday and the Tuesday and the Wednesday. On the Thursday she saw him at the cart the third time, swaying a little on his feet, and saw him open the flask with his back turned to Hobb, and straighten as the lid came off, the way they all did, as if somebody had poured warm water down his spine.

He went back down into the culvert.

He came up an hour later at the end of the day and sat down on the edge of the cutting with his legs hanging over, and pushed up his left sleeve to wipe the mud off his wrist, and stopped.

Tessaly was close enough to see it. She had come along the line to the cutting to tell him the west side was finishing, and she stopped too.

On the inside of his forearm, from the wrist toward the elbow, there were lines. Very faint. Thin and pale, just raised off the skin, crossing each other at angles like the veins in a leaf, or like frost, silvery in the low light. Not the thick dark tracery that the knights had up their arms and across their shoulders, that she had seen on the knights on the flat carrying beams with their hoods up. Much fainter than that. Like the first drawing of it. Like somebody had begun it in pencil and stopped.

Bastian looked at it for a long time.

Then he looked up and saw her looking.

"I was told," he said.

She did not say anything.

"I was told. I heard him. You heard him." He looked at his arm again. "One, when I'm spent. I said that to the old man in the autumn. He looked at my arms and I said it." He pulled the sleeve down, carefully, and then pushed it up again and looked. The lines were still there. "Nobody made me. I knew."

"Go to the physician," Tessaly said.

The physician saw him that evening in the long ward on the island, and Tessaly went with him, because he asked her to.

Imani looked at the arm under a lamp for a long time without speaking, with her fingers light on the wrist, and then turned it over and looked at the other side, and then did the same with the right arm, which was clean. She asked him how many flasks, and when, and wrote it down. She asked how he felt, and wrote that down. She asked whether it hurt, and he said no, and she pressed on it with her thumb in three places, and he said no each time, and she wrote that.

Then she looked at him.

"You were told," she said.

"I know. I've said. I was told and I knew." He did not look away from her. "I'm not going to say I wasn't."

Imani looked at him for a moment longer. Tessaly, who did not know her, had heard on the flat that she was the hardest woman in Kaldo, and she waited for the hardness.

It did not come. Imani put his arm down on the table, gently, and wrote something more.

"No flasks," she said. "None. Not one, for a month. Then we'll look again." She wrote that too. "You'll be slow. You'll be spent by noon and you'll stay spent. You'll carry less. Somebody else will have to carry what you don't." She looked up. "That's all. I'll tell Hobb and I'll tell the old man. Nobody's going to say anything else to you about it."

"The prince," Bastian said.

"The prince will say nothing to you about it," Imani said, "because I will tell him not to."

She did. Tessaly never knew what she said. She knew that the prince came onto the flat two days later and walked the drains with the gangers and stopped at the cutting where Bastian was working, slow, at half his rate, and stood on the edge and looked down at him for a moment, and Bastian looked up; and that the prince said, "It's good work," and walked on, and that was all.

Nobody punished him. Nobody said a word to him about it, on the flat or off it, for the rest of the summer.

Tessaly thought about that, afterward. She thought that it was the kindest thing she had seen anybody in Kaldo do. And she thought, without quite meaning to, lying awake in the lodging one night, that the warning had been given in front of forty-one mages at a handcart on the first day, and that Hobb wrote every flask in a book with the name and the hour, and that if anybody ever wanted to prove to a court that a mage in Kaldo had been told what the flask would do to him and had been handed three a day anyway, they would not have to look very hard.

She did not know why she had thought of a court. She put it away.

She found the boy in the fourth week.

He was on the etching floor. She had gone across to the island on the Sunday to see the long ward, where Bastian was having his arm looked at again, and on the way back she went past the etching floor's door in the works yard, which was open because of the heat, and looked in, and saw a boy at the bench nearest the door with a crystal in his hand.

It was a small common stone, a lamp stone, the kind they etched by the hundred. He was holding it in his closed fist, and his eyes were shut, and he was not etching it. He was warming it. She could feel it from the doorway. Not much. A lamp stone takes very little. But he had been holding it warm, she saw, for some time, because the girl at the next bench was watching him with the look of somebody who had been told to wait until he stopped and had been waiting a while.

She came in and stood by the bench, and he opened his eyes and saw her grey robe and went red to the ears and put the stone down.

"How long were you holding that?"

"I don't know, mistress. A while."

"How long's a while?"

He looked at the girl at the next bench, who said, "Since the bell."

The bell had been at the half hour. Tessaly looked at the clock on the etching floor's wall. It was the quarter to.

"Pick it up again," she said.

He picked it up. She put two fingers on the back of his fist, lightly, and felt it: a small steady warmth going into the stone from his hand, not pushed, not forced, just given, the way a pot is warm on the side nearest the fire. She had taught thirty years of boys at Carrow Ford how to hold a stone warm. The best of them could do it for the time it took to say the morning office. The best boy she had ever had had held one for the time it took to say it twice, and he was a master at the capital's cisterns now.

She took her fingers away.

"What's your name?"

"Rafe, mistress."

"Where are you from?"

"The island." He said it as if it were obvious, which, she saw, to him it was. "I was born on it. My mother's at the vats."

"How old are you?"

"Fifteen."

"How long have you been on this floor?"

"Since I was eleven."

She looked at him. A thin fair boy with ink under his nails and a burn on one thumb from the etching acid, and a lamp stone in his fist, warm, and his whole face waiting to be told he had done something wrong.

"Do you want to learn to lift earth?" she said.

He stared at her.

"I don't know what that is, mistress," he said.

"No," said Tessaly. "Nobody here does. That's all right. I'll ask the prince tomorrow if I can have you. Tell your mother."

The prince said yes, and Ferra wrote it in the book. Rafe came onto the flat on the Monday with his coat over his shoulder and stood at her post on the west side all day and watched her lift and carry, and did not speak, and at the end of the day she put a stone in his hand and told him to hold it until she said stop, and did not say stop until the sun was down.

Bastian was at the cutting below the west side that evening, slow, on his half rate. Tessaly walked along the line at dusk to tell him the west side was done, as she had on the Thursday, and found him sitting on the edge of the cutting again with his legs over, getting his breath.

He had his left sleeve pushed up to the elbow. He was looking at the lines on the inside of his forearm. They had not faded. They had not grown either. Imani had looked at them that morning and written that they were the same.

He looked up and saw her. He did not say anything. He pulled the sleeve down over the lines, carefully, to the wrist, and buttoned the cuff.

Then he got up, and turned round, and put his hand flat on the wall.
