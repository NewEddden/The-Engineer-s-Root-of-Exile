Chapter 361 - Five and a Little

Ferra ruled the column before breakfast and then sat looking at the top of it.

Emeric found her at the mess table at a little after six with the door propped open and the book square in front of her, and a line down the page as straight as a plumb bob, and nothing written above it. She had a cup of the cook's coffee at her elbow. It had a skin on it.

"You said you'd rule a new one."

"I've ruled it."

"What's it called?"

"If I knew that," said Ferra, "I'd be having my breakfast." She tipped her head back for him without looking up, and he kissed her, and got a mouthful of sand off her eyebrow. She took hold of his collar when he started to straighten and kept him where he was. "You've got it in your ear. I can see it from here."

"It's in the bed."

"I know it's in the bed. I've been lying on it."

"We could shake the blanket out," he said. "Together. Upstairs. It might take a while."

"After."

"After what?"

She let go of the collar and put the end of her pencil on the empty head of the column. "After that."

Imani came in with her boots in one hand and her hair not yet tied up, looked at the two of them, looked at the pencil, took Ferra's coffee, discovered the skin, and gave it back. "It's in my teeth," she said, to nobody. "Sand. I've been a physician for a long time and I can't think how it got to my teeth."



The cook cleared at nine and left the urn. Ferra had asked for the long table and everybody on the island who could be spared to tell the truth, which came to Fletcher, Noll Finch, Ward, Saban, Imani and Zale, who had been given the end of the bench nearest the door so that he'd have the least far to walk, and had noticed.

Ilenne was at the other end, where she always was, with her hood up and the bucket under the table for her feet. Nobody had asked her. Nobody had ever needed to. She had taken her board down there at breakfast and simply not left, and the cook had brought her a second fish without being told, and Zale had got up again on his stick and gone all the way down the room and sat across from her after all, with the air of a man who had meant to from the start.

Ferra put a jam jar in the middle of the table.

"Plan as of last night," she said. "Her mother's house has asked us to bring her home. To the city, to the door, in the Challenger. We've said yes. We've said the end of the summer. I've written the last of August, because Imani wouldn't tell me which end."

"The end with the chairs put away," said Imani.

"That's sixty-seven days from this morning. I'm going to go round this table and everybody's going to tell me what's wrong with it, and anybody who says it'll be fine puts a copper in the jar."

"What's the copper for?" said Noll.

"The jar."

Fletcher went first, because it was his boat, and he had his chalk out though there was nothing to write on. The Challenger had done everything she had been asked to do, he said. She had also never once been asked anything by anybody out of sight of a tender. The long trials hadn't started. He'd reckoned three months for them in April and he reckoned three months now, and that was three months with nothing going wrong, and he had built enough things to have views on that. Twelve bunks. One galley the size of a coffin. And she'd go a thousand miles in thin water and through a wall and into a sea nobody had a chart of, and when she got there she'd have to be got home again.

"It'll be..." said Noll, and caught Ferra's eye, and stopped. He looked at the jar for some time. Then he got out a copper and put it in, on the grounds that he'd been going to.

Zale called up the room that his ship flew on five veins of six and he could have the sixth seen to when somebody found him the glass for it, and that if anybody wanted the wall looked at again before the boat went near it, he'd be glad to, as soon as this woman would stop looking at his legs.

"No water," said Imani, not turning round. "Not to the ankle. Not till I say. You've got the vessels of a wet paper bag. Put that down, Ferra. He's one of the things that's wrong."

Ferra put it down.

Saban didn't stand up this time. He turned his hat a quarter turn on his knee and said that twelve armed soldiers had come up to the one piece of ground in the world that Kaldo had paid double to hide, and the first anybody had known of it was a seal. That behind the twelve there had been something bigger, which had stayed outside the reef and which nobody at this table could put a name to. That they had one lamp on the end of a pier and a man who happened to be looking. And that he'd been told, and had believed, that nothing came through the wall.

"And somebody put a fork in her," he said. "Which I keep mentioning."

Ward went last. He had his hands round a cup he hadn't drunk from, and he said it to the cup.

"The boat takes twelve."

"You said that last night," said Emeric.

"I'm saying it again. Her mother wants to see us with her own eyes. In her own house, a thousand miles past anything I can do about it, with her soldiers at the door. Twelve's not an embassy. Twelve's what you send when you've made your mind up to lose them." He looked up. "I'd go. I'm not saying I wouldn't go."

Nobody said anything. At the far end of the room Zale was talking low across the table, with his hands, and Ilenne was listening to him with a piece of fish halfway to her mouth.

Emeric looked at Ferra's column of what was wrong, and it was about what he'd have written himself. He could have added to it. He could feel the whole shape of the thing from where he sat, every place it would come apart, one after another, the way you can feel the bad step on a dark stair. A boat that hadn't been tried. Air gone foul three weeks out. A queen who had asked politely in front of her own guards and might have a different manner at home.

"All of that's true," he said. "We shorten the trials. Fletcher takes her out now, tomorrow, and does what he can do in nine weeks. I'll have Tess send the spares over on the Hod. We put in what we can, and we go with twelve, and we go at the end of the summer, because yesterday a man stood in the sea and asked me and I told him we would."

"Copper," said Imani.

"I didn't say it'd be fine."

"You had the voice."

A bench scraped.

Ilenne had got up. She came up the length of the room with that long flat-footed careful step, past Noll, past Fletcher, and she was carrying the bucket. She carried it in both hands in front of her, and it was heavy and full and it slopped on the boards, and nobody at the table quite knew what to do about that, so they watched it come. She stopped at Ferra's elbow. Ferra, without any hurry and without taking her eyes off the bucket, picked up the book and held it against her chest.

Ilenne set the bucket down on the table, in the place where the book had been, and pushed back her hood.

"Not carry," she said.

Zale had come up the room behind her as fast as the stick would bring him, which wasn't very. He got a breath and opened his mouth. "She says..."

She reached back without looking and laid two wet fingers across his lips.

Imani made a very small noise of deep contentment and took a sip of her coffee.

"Boat," said Ilenne. She set each word down and got her weight onto it before she went for the next. "Boat yes. Boat loud. Yes." She let that stand. Then she put her free hand on the table, in the wet, and walked two fingers up it, and stopped them at the edge of the jam jar. "Mother. House. Door. Ilenne walk."

"You can walk," said Emeric. "You walked down the beach. Nobody's..."

"Ilenne say." She took the hand off the table and laid it flat on her chest, and it left a dark print on the green. "Ilenne say. My word. Ilenne say, your word. Same, same day." She moved her head a very little toward the man behind her, whose mouth she still had hold of. "Not Zale say." And then she looked down at the bucket between them, full of the bay, with the light moving on it, and flicked its rim with one fingernail so that it rang. "Not this."

Nobody had thought of the bucket as being a remark.

Emeric looked at it. He thought about her coming along the thick water to the door of her mother's house, in September, in a boat she called loud. A tall thin woman a year and a half out of the sea, with a bucket and a tank and a limit in hours written on Imani's card, and a land man on a stick at her shoulder explaining to her mother's court what she meant, and now and then telling it that he was hungry.

Saban had stopped turning his hat.

"May I?" he said. He said it to her, and it was the voice he used for old men in the market. She looked at him. "When you go through that door," said Saban, slowly, with a hand out flat to show her where the door was. "Who's standing there?"

She considered him for quite a long while. A little slate had started down the backs of her arms.

"Many," she said.

"All glad?"

She breathed out through her nose, the short sound that Zale had taught the island to recognise.

"And one or two of them," said Saban, "might be very pleased to watch you come in on a man's arm, with your skin gone dull, and a foreigner doing your talking. They might bring their friends to see it."

She didn't have all of that. Emeric could see her get the arm, and the skin, and the friends. It was enough. She turned her head and looked at Saban as though she had found him in a cupboard, and she used a word that everyone on the island had been using for a year and a half, so that she had been bound to pick it up.

"Fork," said Ilenne.

"Yes," said Saban. "I thought so." He turned the hat a quarter turn. "Majesty, I withdraw my remarks about twelve faces, for the time being. I'd like to raise a new one. She's right, and you've just agreed to deliver her to those people on a tray."

Emeric put his cup down. "How long?"

It took some doing to get that across, and she let go of Zale to do it, and Zale, released, said nothing at all for once and worked his jaw a little as though it had been stiff. Ilenne held up her hand to Imani with the fingers spread, five, and then brought the other hand up and showed a pinch of air between finger and thumb. She'd been looking at Imani's shade card over her shoulder for eighteen months. Of course she knew what it said.

"Five," she said. "Little."

"That's right," said Imani.

Ilenne laid the five against her own breastbone. "Summer. Six." Imani nodded. Then she touched two fingers to her mouth, and took them away, and held up the pinch by itself, very small, and looked round the table with it so that everyone should have a chance to see how small it was.

"Mouth," said Ilenne. "Little."

Ferra had sat down again with the book on her knee. Her pencil had stopped. "How much does she want? How long, to stand up in front of a hall in two languages and not be corrected?"

"I've been at it a year and a half," said Zale. It was the first thing he had said in some time, and it came out hoarse. "They pointed spears at me yesterday."

Ilenne had turned to the door, which stood open on the yard and the white June glare beyond it. She looked out at it. She put up a hand and moved it across the light from one side to the other and then, deliberately, let it drop, and shut her fingers, and shivered once from the shoulders, with a good deal of art, like an actress at the back of a cart. Then she opened the hand again and raised it.

"Cold," she said. "After cold."

"Spring," said Ferra.

It went quiet enough to hear the urn tick.

Fletcher's chalk had started moving against his thumb. He was doing sums and trying to look as though he wasn't. Emeric knew the look, because he could feel it trying to get onto his own face. Ten months. The long trials done properly, every one, and then done again. A lamp on more than one pier. Zale on his own two legs. Time to find out what had been sitting past the reef. Time, and he could already see the list starting to write itself, to do something about a boat that took twelve.

Ward set down his cup. He hadn't touched it, and he set it down as if it were full.

"We told the man."

Nobody asked which man.

"Last night. Thirty paces from where I took my sword off. He stood up on legs that were killing him and asked, and it took the best part of an hour to get it said, and we told him yes, and he went back in the sea on it." Ward was looking at Emeric, and it was the look he had across the yard at a quarter to five. "He'll have got to that queen by the end of the summer. Maybe sooner. And he'll say to her, they said the end of the summer. And he'll say who said so."

"He'll say I did," said Imani.

She had put her cup down too. Emeric looked at her, and she wasn't looking at anybody.

"That's what you told him to tell him," she said. "'Tell him who said so.' He bowed to me. All the way down. And she'll be full by September, that's the trouble with it. I wasn't guessing. I told a soldier the truth about a patient, and in September it'll still be true, and she'll be fit to go and we won't have brought her." She rubbed one eye with the heel of her hand. "I don't care to have that looked at too closely. I'm only saying it's there."

"It's there," said Ward.

"Yes," said Emeric. "It is." He had been on his way to feeling relieved, and he stopped. "I gave my word on a beach in front of twelve of her mother's soldiers. I don't get to find out the morning after that it's inconvenient. We've done without a lot of things in this town, but I've not gone back on my word to anybody since the day I..."

"Emeric."

In eighteen months she had never said it to him. He hadn't known she could; it had a sound in it that her mouth did something odd with, and it came out with a small hitch in the middle, like a cart going over a stone.

She had come round the bucket and was standing across the table from him, and she had to look down to do it.

"Emeric say yes," said Ilenne. "Good. Good say." She nodded, gravely, so that he should know that it had been noted. Then she put her palm on the table, flat, palm down, the way she'd put it on the water for the captain, and he had thought he knew what that meant. "Ilenne say no."

"I told him..."

"Ilenne." The hand came up and went to her chest, and stayed. She hunted for it. He watched her go through the whole of her small careful store, looking, and he saw her find the thing she wanted and turn it over to be sure. It was a word she'd had from the yard, from Fletcher's men on the Friday, and from Ferra's table. "Mine," she said. "My life. Ilenne spend."

Fletcher's chalk stopped.

"She's got you there," said Imani, after a moment, quite gently.

Ward got up. It took him some time to get his legs out from under the bench. He came down the table and stood in front of her, a broad man in his shirt with his hands at his sides, and he had to look up at her too, and he didn't seem to mind it.

"Begging your pardon," he said, slowly enough. "It's your life. I've no say in that. It's still broken."

She looked at him for a long moment. He'd walked into the sea in his plate last night and held up one of hers, and she had stood at the end of the slab and watched him do it. The slate had got as far as her wrists.

"Yes," said Ilenne. "Broken."

She didn't say anything else about it. She put her fingers to her lips, and cupped them, and leaned over the bucket and made as if to speak down into it. Then she drew one finger round and round on the surface, very lightly, so that the water turned after it.

"Ilenne tell," she said.

"Aye," said Zale. "That's the fish. That's how she called them. Her own voice, into the thick water, and the shoals pass it on." He cleared his throat. "She'd have to be there to do it. Past the last islands. That's my ship, and my ship wants a vein."

"When?" said Ferra.

"Before they start looking out for her, I'd think."

"By the end of the summer," said Ferra, and wrote it, and there was a very slight pause after the word summer.

"It'll have to be good," said Saban.

He'd gone to the reasonable voice again, and Emeric liked it less than the other one. "Consider it from the queen's side. Your daughter's been a year and a half in the cursed land. Your captain comes home half dead and tells you she's alive, and on her feet, and that they've promised her by the end of summer, and that he felt with his own, whatever he feels with, that she's nearly well. You've wept. You've had the door swept. And a few weeks later, a call comes along the sea in your daughter's voice. And it says, I'm well, I'm in good hands, I'm not coming home, don't wait for me." He looked round the table. "What would you think? Because I can tell you what I'd think. I'd think there was a man standing behind her with a knife."

Ferra stopped writing.

Zale started to put some of that into her language, haltingly, and got about as far as the knife. He needn't have gone so far. It had reached her already, somewhere around don't wait. It went down over her in a slow dark wash from the throat, and for the first time since she'd got up from the end of the bench, she took her eyes off the people at the table. She looked at the water in the bucket. Then she said something, in her own language, long and low and quite level, with the stop coming again and again at the back of it, and when it was over she put up her hood.

Nobody spoke.

"What did she say?" said Ferra.

"It's about her mother," said Zale.

"And?"

He looked down at his stick. "It's about her mother."

Ferra waited, and then she wrote something short.

"Spring, then," said Emeric. It wasn't how he'd have chosen to come by it. "After Lantern Night. Fletcher, you can have your three months and whatever you want after them. Saban, I want to know what was out past that reef, and I want more than one lamp. Ilenne..." He stopped, because he wasn't sure what you said. She had her hood up and her back very straight. "Whatever you want for the word work. Whoever you want. It's yours. You say when it's enough."

The hood moved a little.

"I'll say something else," said Ward. He had gone back to his place, and he stood with his hands on the end of the bench. "I'm glad. I want that known. I've sat here for an hour thinking about twelve bunks and I never was so glad of anything in my life as when that lady said cold. And it's not right, and it's still not right, and I'd thank you, Ferra, to write down both."

Ferra wrote down both.

"Twelve," said Emeric. He pulled the jam jar over and looked into it, at Noll's copper. "You said twelve's what you send when you've made up your mind to lose them."

"I did."

"She's asked for the door. Ten months from now we'll come to it, and her mother wants a look at us, and we've got no way of knowing what she's going to think by then." He pushed the jar back. "So let's give her something to look at."

Something happened at one corner of Ward's mouth. It didn't go anywhere, but Emeric had known him a long time.

"How many?" said Ward.

"Don't," said Ferra, not looking up. "Not yet. I've only got the one column."



Zale found her on the slab that evening, because there was only one place to look. It took him eleven minutes from the mess hut. He had started counting them in May.

She had her feet in the sea and her hood back again, and the colour had come out of her arms, most of it. He stopped a little way along from her and leaned on the stick and looked at the water for a while, since it was there.

"You took my job," he said. He said it in Kaldo. There didn't seem any point in saying it the other way.

She glanced at him. "Job."

"Work. What I do. A year and a half, I've been the only man on this coast who could talk to you, and I've been very bad at it, and I was getting to like it." He shifted the stick. "It's a hard thing, to be sacked with a hand on your face. There's men in Kaldo would pay to see it."

She said nothing, but the corner of her mouth had gone in. After a while she looked down at the stick, and then along it to his hand, and then she held up a finger and drew it through the air in front of his lips. Then she pointed at herself.

"Morning," said Ilenne. "Zale say Kaldo. Ilenne say. Zale..." She made a pinch, and tugged at it, as if pulling a loose thread out of a sleeve. "Bad, say no. Bad, bad, say no. Good Kaldo. Not Zale Kaldo. Good."

"You want me to teach you."

"Teach."

"To talk to your mother's court. Me."

She considered it, and he saw her come down on the side of honesty. "Ferra teach," she said. "Zale, hit. When bad."

"Aye," said Zale. "It's a demotion, then." He looked at the water some more. It was going a dull copper, out past the reef. There was nothing on it at all. "I'll want wages."

The light was going. Fletcher's man had started along the pier with his taper. She took her feet out of the sea, and stood, and shook them one at a time like a cat, and when she came along the slab to where he was she stopped, and looked at the eleven minutes that lay between here and the mess hut, and held out her arm to him, crooked, with an expression of great courtesy.

He looked at it.

"Not carry," said Zale.

That laugh again, the big one, with her head back. It went out over the bay, and somewhere over by the flat rocks a seal barked at it. Then she went along beside him, at his pace, all the way, with her hands behind her back.



Ferra did it last thing, at the mess table, with the lamp on the cook's end and the hatch shut and the wind getting up. On the end of the pier, Fletcher's operator had sent what she'd given him, to Tess's pad in the Motion Shop: Going in spring, not summer. Her own say. Stand by for a long list. F.

Imani had the lamp. She had brought it over without being asked and was holding it where it was wanted with one hand, and with the other she was going slowly through Emeric's hair over the end of the bench.

"You've still got sand in here."

"I know."

"There's a lot of it. It's almost a beach. I could let it out by the week."

"After," said Ferra. She had the book open at the column, ruled that morning, as straight as it had been. She dipped the pen and let the drop fall and wrote at the head of it, in ink, in her small upright hand, a single word: Departure.

She looked at it with her head on one side. Under it she wrote, Spring, yr 17. After Lantern Night. Her decision. And under that, Message to the queen, by her own voice, past the last islands. Before the end of summer. Needs: vein, Z's ship.

Then she turned back a page, to the entry from the night before. 24 June. Twelve, out of the lee at dusk, her mother's guard. Asked the whole way. Yes. Gone home the same night. She read it through. The ruler lay ready beside her hand. Emeric watched it, and watched her not pick it up.

Next to Yes, in the margin, she wrote: Not kept. 25 June. Ours.

"It was hers," said Emeric. "She said so."

"She said it was hers to break." Ferra blew on it. "It's still us that said it." She waited for it to dry, and shut the book, and only then, with the book shut and the pen wiped and all of it done, turned round on the bench with her chin up and her hand out for the front of his shirt. "Now," she said. "That blanket."

Imani put down the lamp. On the table beside it, in the jam jar, there were two coppers. Emeric hadn't said a word. He'd put his in on the way out of the meeting, to be safe.
