Chapter 193 - The Blueprint

It took him most of a month, and for most of that month Milford lay on the long-room floor and everybody who came into the room walked round it.

Ferra had stopped moving the table back. There was no point. The table lived against the wall now with the benches stacked on it, and the day's business was done at the side table and in the counting room downstairs, and the long room belonged to a barony a day's ride to the west, pinned to its floorboards in forty-six squares, with a path worn round its edge in the dust where people walked to get to the hearth. Hobb walked round it with his book. Saban walked round it and stopped at the heaps behind the slaughter yards every time, as if he were pricing them. Imani came up once, looked at the bottoms along the leg, black with standing water on the sheets, and said "Ague," and went down again.

He drew on a second floor beside the first: clean sheets, the same size, the same grid, pinned in the same squares along the wall under the windows, with the Tarrants' crosses on them in their places and their heights beside them and nothing else. That was the plan. It began empty and it filled a square at a time, in pencil first and then in ink, and he drew it on his knees in his stockings with the air sheets on one side of him and the plan on the other, looking from one to the other like a man copying a letter.

He drew the ground first, because the ground decided everything.

There were three kinds of it, and the thaw had drawn the lines between them for him in water.

The bottoms were the low land along the leg, the flat floors of every bend, the ground the melt had stood on in sheets. He drew their edges where the water's edges had been, and wrote across them: meadow. Hay, and summer grazing, and never a plough. Ground that flooded every spring would grow grass better than anything in Milford and would drown a crop every third year, so it would grow grass. Where the sheets showed it sour, the dark stains round the fords and the water holes where the herds had trodden it to clay, he drew tile under it, in rows, Colm's drain tile, laid the way the kiln crew had laid it at the fourth division the year it went to bog. And along the leg itself, on both banks, the whole length of it through Milford, he drew a strip of grass a chain wide that nothing was to be put on, and under the strip, in the margin, the rule: no yard, no midden, no slaughterhouse, no tannery, within two hundred paces of the water. Imani had said "Ague." The water went to the sea and never came near the island, and it still went past every steading on its way, and men drank it.

The benches were the long shelves above the cut on both sides, pale on the air sheets, dry, the good deep ground that no river had reached in any man's lifetime and that would be dry by midsummer every year without water. He drew them into fields. Not the old strips; square fields, each one as big as a team and a reaper could work in a day, with a lane between every two so that a cart could get to any field without crossing another. Arable, watered. That was where the bread would come from.

The high flats were everything beyond the benches: the open range, the grey grass going away for miles, threaded with the dips the melt had shown him. He drew them into paddocks. Great squares, fenced, each with water to it, the herd to be moved from one to the next before the grass in it was eaten down to the root, so that behind the herd the grass came back while the herd was somewhere else. He drew troughs in every paddock and pipe to the troughs, and at the far edge of the flats, where no pipe from the benches would ever reach, wells, and over every well a wind pump on its mast.

Then he sat back on his heels and looked at the three grounds, and began the part that mattered, which was what went round.

He drew it as a circle, because it was one.

In the corner of the first sheet, where there was room, he drew a ring the size of a plate, and round the ring, in order, the way a clock is numbered, he wrote the things that went round it.

Wheat, on the benches, in the first year. Turnips in the second, on the same field. Barley in the third, with clover sown in under it, so that when the barley came off the clover was already there. Clover in the fourth, cut for hay and grazed and ploughed back in. Then wheat again. Four fields to a set, one in each year of the four, so that every year there was wheat and turnips and barley and clover all at once, on four fields, and every field had been all four by the end. The clover fed the ground what the wheat took out of it. He knew that as well as he knew anything, and he knew it the way he knew most things in this world, as a fact in a book that would outlive him, and he could not have told Hollis why clover did it and did not try. The turnips cleaned the ground, because a turnip field was hoed all summer and a hoed field had no weeds. And the turnips and the clover were the thing no farm in Randillia had ever had in the winter.

Food for the herd.

That was the ring. The turnips and the clover hay fed the herd through the winter, in roofed yards, instead of the herd being killed in the autumn when the grass went, the way Milford had killed its herd every autumn since there had been a Milford. The straw off the wheat and the barley bedded the yards, deep, and the herd trod its dung into the straw all winter, and in the spring the yards were cleaned out and the dung went onto the benches before the wheat. The bone from the slaughter yards went through a crushing mill and onto the benches with it. The benches grew the wheat and the straw and the next year's turnips. The sheep were folded on the turnip fields behind hurdles, a strip at a time, and ate the turnips where they grew and dunged the ground where they stood, so that the field was dunged without a cart. The pigs ate the whey from the dairy and the scraps from the kitchens. There were hens at every steading. Oxen broke the old sod on the benches the first year, because an ox was slow and would pull all day through turf that had not been cut in fifty years. After that, horses, in collars, for the drill and the mower and the reaper and the carts, because a horse in a collar pulled from its shoulders and a horse in a throat strap pulled from its windpipe, and the difference was the difference between a horse that would pull a plough and one that would pull a cart.

He drew an arrow from each thing on the ring to the next, all the way round, and the last arrow came back to the first. Then he looked at it, and found that he could not see anything on the ring that was not going somewhere, and that nothing had to come in from outside it but the seed, and the seed came from Hollis.

The water he had drawn in his head in the air, over square twelve.

Under each bench, on the leg, where the cut came nearest the bench's foot, an engine house: a boiler and an engine and a pump on the water, the intake on piles in the stream. Three of them, one under each of the three long benches. From the pump, a main up the face of the bench to its high corner, and on the high corner a tower: a timber tank on a stone base, the tower on the western rise at Kaldo three times over, high enough that the main ran downhill from it the whole length of the bench to the farthest field. Mains down the benches in Colm's pipe, socket and spigot and packed collar, the pipe that did not care about the cold. Off the mains, at the head of every field, a sluice, and from the sluice a lined channel along the field's top edge, and from the channel the water let down the furrows between the rows, a field at a time, by a man with a spade and a board.

No sprinkler heads on the fields. He had thought about it for a day and decided against it. The fixed bronze heads Bram had cut a master for at Kaldo threw a fan of water over a circle, and to cover a bench field with them would want pipe under the whole field, miles of it, laid and joined and kept, to do what a furrow did for the price of a spade. Sprinklers on the seed beds and the kitchen gardens, where the plants were small and close and the ground was worth the pipe. Furrows everywhere else.

Drain tile under the bottoms where they were sour. Troughs to the paddocks off the towers where the pipe would reach, and the wind pumps where it would not.

The machines he did not draw on the plan. He drew them on their own sheets and pinned them on the wall over the plan, one sheet each. A steel plough, its share cut from the rail works' plate, which Kaldo could roll now by the ton. A harrow. A seed drill, built off the spreader that had laid the autumn field in bands in the first year, with a hopper and a row of tubes and no stone in it anywhere; the sieve upstream of it, the one that had turned out to be worth more than the spreader. A mower. A reaper, with a horse on it. The threshing drum, off an engine's belt.

Storage. Five steadings on the plan, spread along the benches, not one. Bins, limed inside the way the silo bins at Kaldo were limed; ricks on staddles; root cellars for the turnips. Never one great barn. He wrote that in the margin in so many words, and did not write why, because everybody who would ever read the plan in Kaldo already knew why: a barn on the Kaldo border, full of grain, burned to its footings on one of four bad nights. And beside every steading, a siding off the Milford line, so that what was stored could go east in closed wagons without a cart on a road.

And the rest, round the edges of the plan, where the benches met the town. A mill on the leg, below the last engine house. A dairy, because butter and cheese would go to Kaldo on the rail in a day and milk would not. A salting house and a smoke house, for the beasts that were killed, which would be the ones that were meant to be killed and not the whole herd because the grass had gone. The tallow to the soap works, as the old agreement with Paxton had it. The hides to a tannery, and the tannery below everything, below the mill and the dairy and the last steading, at the bottom of the barony where the leg went out to the reeds, where whatever the tannery put in the water would go straight to the sea. The bone heaps through a crushing mill and back onto Milford's own ground.

And a book. He drew the farm book last, on a sheet of its own, the way Ferra ruled a book, in columns: every field numbered on the plan and every number a page, and on the page the sowing and the seed and the water and the dung and the yield, year after year, and in a book of its own the herd, every beast, with its births and its deaths.

In the south-west corner of the plan, over square forty-four, where the air sheet said not seen across it in Ferra's hand, he wrote in his own: To be walked.

Ferra had watched him draw it for a month without saying a word about any of it, which was how she did things she meant to take apart properly. She took it apart on the last evening, at the side table, with the plan on the wall and the whole table standing round it: Hollis, Bram, Saban, Imani, Ward by the door, Armand in his chair by the hearth with the rug over his knees. She had her book and a pencil. She did not look at the plan while she talked. She had looked at it for a month.

"Fuel," she said.

She could see he had been waiting for it. He had been waiting since the night of the coal sum, with the three engine houses drawn on the leg, each with its boiler.

"Three boilers," she said. "At Milford. And you know what the coal line carries, because I turned the book round and showed you. The pumps in the city have what it gave back from the third stand. There's no more. I'm not giving Milford a ton." She looked at him. "Not one ton. If I give Milford a ton, it comes off a pump in the city, and I've told Imani she'll have her taps."

"You won't have to," said Emeric. "Milford burns straw."

Ferra's pencil stopped.

"The boilers are built for it," he said. "A firebox with a wide throat and a long grate, and a man at the door with a fork, feeding it by the bale. Straw burns fast and hot and it wants feeding all the time, and a boiler that burns it has to be built to burn it and not coal. But it burns." He nodded at the plan. "And Milford is going to have more straw than it knows what to do with. Every field of wheat and every field of barley on those benches is a field of straw. The yards take most of it. What the yards don't take goes under the boilers. Last year's straw pumps this year's water."

Ferra looked at him for a moment. Then she wrote it, and under it, in the column where she put things that were true and were not figures, she wrote: Fuel: its own. Not the line's.

"And under the straw?" she said.

"A wind pump on every tower. And under the wind pump, a hand pump." He did not need to look at the plan. "What must not stop goes on what cannot stop. If the straw's wet and the wind's down, four men and a hand pump keep the tanks up enough for the troughs, and the fields go without water for a day, and that's all. There isn't a field in Milford that dies of one dry day."

"There isn't a stone on it," said Saban.

He was standing at the plan with his hands behind his back, and he said it the way he said a price he had checked, not as a question. But it was a question, and everybody at the table heard it as one.

"No," said Emeric.

"You've a pump house at the home farm with a stone in it that's run for years," said Saban. "You've stones in every tower on the island and the flat. You could put a water stone in every engine house in Milford and never burn a bale. Or you could fly over it on a dry night and water the whole bench yourself, and be home by breakfast." He turned from the plan. "I'm not asking you to. I'm asking why you won't, so that when a man in Milford asks me, I can tell him."

It was the right question. Emeric had asked it himself, on his knees on the floor, on the third day, and answered it, and drawn on.

"Because I'll die," he said.

Nobody said anything.

"Not soon. I hope not soon. But I will. And the day I do, a farm that needs me to water it dies with me, the same week." He looked at the plan. "And a stone farm is nearly as bad. A water stone in an engine house needs a flask to fill it, and the flask needs the band, and the band needs a ship and a crew and a secret, and when the stone cracks it needs Nevin to cut another and me to bind it. Milford is going to be four baronies' bread. I won't put four baronies' bread on a thing that stops when I stop, or when Nevin does, or when the ship can't fly." He put his hand flat on the wall beside the plan. "This farm, a drover can mend. A drover and a smith. Every piece of it is a thing some man in Milford already knows how to fix, or can learn in a season from a man who does. That's why."

Saban nodded slowly, and Emeric saw him put the answer away where he put the things he would sell to other people later, word for word.

Armand, in his chair by the hearth, had said nothing, and did not say anything now. But he had turned his head to look at the plan, and he looked at it for a long time.

"Hands," said Ferra.

"Hollis has said at every harvest since the first metal that there are never enough hands in the barony," she said, "and it was true then and it's truer now. Every man who can lift a sheaf is on the flat, or at the works, or on the rail. I can count them. There aren't any spare." She put her pencil on the plan's corner, on the sheet of machines. "So. The drill does what six men with seed-bags did. The reaper does what twenty with sickles did. The drum does what the flail did all winter. That's the first answer." She moved the pencil. "The second is the rail. Milford is an hour from Kaldo now, and a day from Wayford and a few hours from Grayrock. At harvest the men come to the harvest from all four baronies on the train in the morning and go home on it. Nobody walks for a week to get there and a week to get back."

"And the third?"

"The third is Paxton's." Ferra did not look at him. "Thirty-one men on Milford's rolls who owe. Paxton came to this table in the autumn and told us he'd lose them, and some he has. The ones who stay, the farm hires, at a wage, and the wage is stopped against what they owe before they ever see it, the same as Dace stops it here for the ones who walked. The debt gets paid. The man isn't held." She wrote something. "Paxton gets his money back. More slowly than he wants. But from a man who's working his own ground and eating, instead of from one who's run off to the coast."

Hollis had said nothing all evening. He had stood at the plan with his one good hand on the wall beside it and read it, square by square, the way he read a sheet, and he had not asked anything, because he had been reading it for a month on the floor and had asked what he needed to ask on the days he came up to look.

"It wants a man," Hollis said.

Everybody looked at him.

"A plan wants a man to walk it. Not you." He did not look at Emeric. "A farmer. Who's watered a field and watched it go to bog and knows why. Who'll stand in a Milford bottom in the wet with a Milford drover and not be told it's always been done this way." He took his hand off the wall. "Farris."

Ferra wrote the name before anybody had agreed to it. She knew Farris. So did everybody at the table. Farris, who had looked at three weeks of slate figures from the fourth division's pump house and put the whole of them in one sentence. We are watering the ground on the way to the ground. Farris, who had lent his horse for the first rail's first day because he wanted to see and did not want to be seen wanting to.

"And a man for the water," said Bram.

He had been at the foot of the table all evening with his arms folded, looking at the three engine houses on the leg and the towers and the mains, and not at anything else.

"Pipe and kiln and boilers and a man to build the engine houses and set the intakes. Somebody who'll make the pipe there, out of Milford clay, not cart it from here a wagon at a time." He unfolded his arms. "Colm."

Ferra looked up.

"He's kiln crew," she said. "He's not an engineer."

"He was mine before the works was anybody's," Bram said. "He's made every length of pipe Kaldo's ever laid, and he found the collar, and he's run the works' plate since there was plate. The grit that made the first steel was his grit." He looked at the plan. "And there's a rack of better pipe against the kiln wall that he made the winter the covenant came in and was told he couldn't use. It's still there. He'll want to use it."

Ferra wrote it.

Colm came up the next morning, because Bram sent for him, and stood in the door of the long room in his kiln clothes with the clay on his boots and did not come in until Emeric told him to mind the floor and come in anyway.

Ferra watched him look at it. She had watched a great many people look at the plan in the last month, and they had all looked at it the same way, as a drawing, the way a man looks at a picture of a place he has not been. Colm did not. Colm walked straight to the wall and put his face close to it, very close, and looked at the bottoms along the leg, square by square, and the stains round the fords, and the tile drawn under them, and the edges of the meadow where the water had stood; and then he went down on his heels and looked at the air sheets on the floor, the ones the plan had been drawn from, black and white, with every dip on the high flats drawn on them in water; and then he stood up and looked at the plan again.

He was quiet for a long time.

"The Tarrants did the chain," he said at last. "I saw the crosses from the train. Seven hundred of them." He put a finger on the plan, on the edge of the meadow in square nine, where the line of the standing water ran along the foot of the bench in a curve that nobody with a chain could have followed without walking every yard of it. "Chain didn't give you that."

"No," said Emeric.

Colm looked at him.

Ferra had seen that look on Colm before, once or twice over the years, and on Bram, and on Hollis, and on Fletcher, and she had never known what to write beside it. It was not suspicion. It was the look of a man who has lived next door to a house for years and has just noticed that one of its doors has a second lock on it, and has decided, in the same moment, that it is not his door.

Nobody had ever asked. In all the years she had kept the book, she had never once heard anybody ask.

Colm did not ask now.

"Aye, well," he said.

He took the plan down off the wall himself, sheet by sheet, carefully, and stacked the sheets in their order, and rolled them round each other, square at the ends, the way Fletcher rolled a drawing. Then he held out his hand for the tin tube Ferra had ready on the side table, and put the roll in it, and put the cap on.

"When do we go?" he said.

They went on the first train west in the morning.

Ferra went down to the works yard to see them off, because she had written the plan's cost into the book and she wanted to see the first line of it leave. There was an engine and its tender, and a van for the men, and behind the van three wagons of pipe, Colm's better pipe off the rack against the kiln wall, the whole rack of it, roped down in straw, hard-fired and deep-collared, that had stood where the whole yard could see it for years and never gone into the ground.

Farris was in the van already, on the bench by the door, in his good coat, with his hat on his knees. He had a bag at his feet, and between his boots, standing upright, the tin tube with the plan in it. He had his hand on the top of it. Colm was on the wagon behind with the pipe, sitting on it, because he said he did not trust the ropes and would rather be on top of it if it went.

Hollis had come down too. He stood beside Ferra on the yard's cinders with his good hand in his coat, and watched Farris in the van door, and Farris looked back at him, and neither of them said anything. They had worked the same nine divisions for longer than Ferra had kept the book. Farris lifted his hand off the tube an inch, and put it back.

The engine's watcher said something to the driver, and the driver let the steam in, and the wheels took hold, and the train went out of the works yard toward the Milford line and the west, slowly and then less slowly, with the pipe on the last wagon and Colm on the pipe.

Emeric had not come down. Ferra had asked him the night before whether he meant to, and he had said no. It was their plan now, he had said, and a plan that needed him to see it off was a plan that would need him for the rest of it. She had written that down too.

She stood on the cinders with Hollis until the train was a smudge of smoke going away over the flat, and then went back up to the long room.

The floor was empty for the first time in a month. The air sheets were in the box, under the stones, and the plan was in a tin tube between Farris's boots, and there was nothing on the boards but the worn path round the edge where everybody had walked, and in the middle, where Milford had been, a great square of floor a little cleaner than the rest.

She moved the table back into the middle of the room by herself. It was heavy, and it took her some time, and she did it anyway. Then she sat down at it and opened the book, and turned to a new page, and wrote at the head of it: Milford. The farm. And the date. And under it, the first line, in the hand she used for things that had happened: F. and C. west, with the plan and three wagons of C.'s pipe.
