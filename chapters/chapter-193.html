Chapter 193 - Anything Goes

Rowena mended her yard before she let him on it.

She did it at first light, alone in the middle of the sand, in her plate, while he stood at the stair foot in his. Not the quilted jack this morning; the engraved steel of the quay, bright, leaves and waves on the breast and the shoulders, a half-cloak of blue off the left shoulder pinned back out of the way, and the open helm under her arm. She walked to the north-west corner of the yard and crouched, and put her bare palm flat on the sand.

A glyph came up off the back of her hand. Amber, small, steady, the size of a coin, the color of Paskin's; and it was not wobbling at all. It stayed while she walked. She walked the line of the old drain from the corner to the corner, slowly, bent, with her palm a hand's breadth off the sand and the glyph riding over the back of it; and behind her the sand moved. He felt it go. The long trough a finger deep that had caught her on the first morning came up under her hand and was level, and the clay under it went dense and tight round the old stone of the drain, the way a fist closes.

Then she went to the east wall and stood over the cistern.

That took longer. She knelt there, with both hands down, and the glyph got bigger, and he felt the wet clay under the patch go hard by degrees, from the edges inward, the water in it going down and out through the rock under it and away toward the drop; and when she stood up and stamped on it, it did not give.

She came back across the yard, wiping her hand on her cloak.

"My grandfather's cistern," she said. "I'd forgotten it. Forty years. Thank you." She put her helm on. "Now it's my yard."

Her knights were on the walls again, more of them this morning, and the grey knight among them; and along the north side the whole Kaldo party, Ward at the front with his arms folded, and Imani beside him with her bag at her feet, which she had brought without being asked and set down on the sand without explaining. Ferra had the book. Vantic stood a little apart from everybody with her hands behind her back. Fletcher had come up from the ship. Cade and Tobe had come up with him, and stood at the back by the sword rack, and Tobe had his mouth open.

"Anything," said Rowena. "Plate. Casting. Your sense. Whatever's in that suit and whatever's in you." She drew the long sword; blunted, like yesterday's, the edge rolled. "I'll do the same."

"Anything," said Emeric.

He put on his helm. The world went dark violet through the panes; the yard, the walls, the sun coming up over the inlet, all through the suit's glass, all the same tinted dark, so that nobody looking in at him could see his eyes. The suit's stones sat in their seats round him, waiting, and the band's grey bead sat against the cuff's deciding stone at his left wrist, and he felt the suit the way he had felt it every day for two months, as a second body round his own that would do what he told it without his lifting a lever.

He opened his sense.

Not all of it. Not the flood he had sat in yesterday at the works with his hand on the rock. Ward's way. A burst. Open, read, shut; open, read, shut; like a man blinking in a wind.

She came at him.



He had thought he knew how she fought. He had fought her yesterday. He had not; yesterday she had fought him with her hands and a blade and nothing else, as she had said she would, and this morning she was everything she was, and she came across the sand at him like weather.

Open. She was a weight in his sense, dense and still, coming; her note, the struck bell held in a hand; and in it, already, the shape of what she was going to do, the first movement before the movement, the way Declan had taught him to read it off a man's hips. High, from her right. Shut. He took it high on his right and it was there, and he felt it go into his arms, and for the first time in his life he did not let his arms take it alone.

He drove them.

Not the knight's push into the legs before a jump; that was the thing every knight did, the mana poured down into the body before the work and left there. This was the other thing. The hybrid's. His own mana, going not into his muscles but through them, along them, at the instant of each movement, as if he were a hand inside a glove pulling the glove's fingers; the thing he had found in the summer with the lag still in it and had practiced every night since, alone, in the yard at Kaldo with only Ward there to see. His arms moved faster than arms could move. His blade met hers and threw it back.

She gave a step. Her helm turned, very slightly, as if she had heard something.

Open. He read her: low now, from the left, coming in under. Shut. He met it. Driven. Her blade came off his with a ring like a bell, and he followed it in, and she was not there; she had gone round him, light-footed in plate, and her blade came at his back and he was not there either, and the knights on the north wall made a sound.

She came back at him straight, hard, a knight's charge across six feet of sand, with all her weight behind it.

He planted.

Heavy, through his right heel, all of it, for the space of the instant, and the sand under his boot went down a hand's depth with a sound like a spade; and she came into him and stopped. Her shoulder met his chest plate and stopped there as if she had run into the mole. He felt the jolt of it go down through his leg into the yard. She stood against him for a breath, chest to chest, with their blades locked above their heads; and he felt her feel it.

Not the Plant. A knight could plant. A knight could set his feet and take a charge and not move, if he was big enough and his feet were good. What she felt was that he had not been heavy enough to do it a breath before, and then he was.

She broke off. She went back three steps and stood, and looked at him through the eye-slit of her helm, and he could not see her face.

"Again," she said.



He used the Rail on her next, because it was the thing he had that she would not have seen.

He had built it with Paskin in the spring, in the Milford meadows, and put himself face first into a wet bottom with it on the first try. Earth was not his. It cost him three times what his fire cost him; and a ridge pushed up under his own back foot, a starting block a hand high with a steep face toward him, was very little earth, and very much worth it. He pushed his palm down at his side as he stepped back, and the small amber glyph came up off it, wobbling, too big, the way his always did, and the sand behind his heel rose into a block.

He drove off it.

His legs did the work. Legs, and a knight's push, and the block, and a face to push against instead of loose sand; and light in the air of it, his weight taken off so that he went flat and fast and did not rise. He crossed the yard in a stride and a half. Twelve feet. He was on her before her blade had come round.

She took it on the blade anyway, at the last instant, and gave ground. A lot of ground. Four steps, back, under the weight of it, her heels cutting furrows in her own sand; and the knights on the wall made the sound again, louder.

And he was past her, nearly. That was the Rail. Once it went, it went where it went; it was named for a train, because it could not turn. He came down out of it a stride further than he meant, off balance, and turned, and she was already coming.

Open. She was going to step to her left. Her left foot, there, a pace and a half from his right. He knew where it would come down before it came. Shut.

He loosened the ground under it.

Earth again, the expensive way, a push down through the air from his palm at the sand where her foot was going to be, and the amber glyph came off his hand, and the sand there went to porridge to the depth of a boot; and her foot came down in it.

It caught her. She went down onto one knee in plate in the soft sand, with her blade out wide to her right for balance, and her left side open from the hip to the neck.

He had her.

He saw it the way he saw a sum that had come out. The blade at her throat. The edge rolled, the point blunt, nothing that would hurt her; a touch, the duel's touch, in front of her knights, on her own yard, the second morning. He could put it there in the time it took to think it. One of the strongest knights in Randillia, on one knee in her own sand, with Harrold's third son's blade at her neck.

It would be at his father's table by Lantern Night. Every carrier on the coast would have it by the spring. The drunk prince of Kaldo had beaten the Sovereign of Emberfell on her own yard with magic nobody could name. And Corbin would hear it, and his father would hear it; and every man in the capital who had a use for a weapon would sit up at his table and look south and wonder what else Kaldo had.

He cut at her shoulder instead.

A real cut, and a good one, and slower than the throat by the breadth of a hand; and she got her blade back across in time, from the knee, and took it, and came up off the soft sand under it in one movement, and was standing again.

She looked at him through her eye-slit.

He could not see her face. He did not need to. He felt it, in the next burst, in her note: a stillness, very short. She had seen the throat. She had seen him see it. And she had seen him not take it.



The Turn was Ward's.

It had been Ward's long before Emeric had ever held a sword: the old school's way, which Ward had learned from a master in the capital who had learned it from his, of not meeting a blow square. Give it its road. Let it come, and turn with it, a step and a pivot, so that its weight went past you instead of into you, and you came round on the far side of it with your own blade already moving. Ward had shown it to him on the first night of the relentless sparring, in the dark, in the yard at Kaldo, after he had backed into the mounting block, and it had taken him a year to do it without falling over.

Then he had added to it, last year, with Ward watching: the one thing Ward could not do and Emeric could. At the instant the blow landed, he went light. All his weight, off, for a heartbeat. So the blow did not root him and stop him, the way a blow stops a man standing; it turned him, like a top, like a door on a hinge, faster than he could have turned himself. And at the end of the turn, he went heavy, all of it, into the counter.

She cut at him hard, from high on her right, a cut to end it.

He gave it its road. He went light at the instant it met his blade, and it turned him, a whole half circle on his left heel, and the sand flew; and he came round on the far side of it with the turn's speed in him and went heavy into his counter at the end of it, down, with everything; and his blade came down on hers at the cross with the whole of his weight behind it and more, much more, and nearly took it out of her hands.

She staggered. A step back, and another, with her blade low and her arms ringing, he could see them ringing.

And something in her note went very still.

He felt it in the next burst. Not her surprise at the blow. Something else. She had felt it through the blades: through the steel, into her hands, the way a smith feels through his tongs the moment a bar goes soft in the fire. His weight. Gone, for a heartbeat, at the moment of the impact, so that her blade had met nothing. And then back, at the end of the turn, more of it than any man his size had any right to, all at once, as if something had been added to him and taken away and added again.

And at his heels, at the start of the turn and the end of it, under the suit's black plate, very small, a flicker. In the morning sun, through the dust, nobody on the walls could have seen it. He had been sure of that; it was why he had used it.

She had not seen it. She had felt it. She had mana sense, as good as any he had met; and a glyph was a glyph, whatever its size, and she felt the tiny violet thing come and go at his heels the way a woman in a dark room feels a moth go past her face.

Her blade was low. Her arms were ringing. She was a step and a half from him with her guard down and her weight on her back foot, and he had her again; better than the first time.

He did not go in.

He stood where the Turn had left him with his blade up and let her have the step back, and the next, and her guard. He told himself it was the capital. It was the capital. He stood in the sand in his black suit and watched her get her guard back and knew that was why, and knew also, under it, that he was not going to beat this woman in her own yard in front of her people if he could help it, whatever the capital thought; and did not know which of the two was the true one.

She got her guard back. She did not come at him. She stood and looked at him, and the stillness was still in her note; and then she lifted her blade again, and it went.

She did not say anything about it in the yard. Not then. Not afterward, with her knights there.



After that she was faster.

Not fast. Faster than she had been. She came at him and kept coming, and every stroke started from the end of the last, and he met them, driven, sense open and shut and open; and it went on, and he held it, and he was beginning to think he could hold it all morning when he understood she was not trying to hit him any more.

She was timing him.

He felt it in a burst: her note had changed. It was listening. She was not reading his feet, the way Declan read feet, or his hips; she was reading something else, the thing she had found at the Turn, and she was reading it in him every time he drove a stroke. He did not know what she was reading. He drove the next stroke, and she was there before him.

Not at the stroke. Before it.

The lag. He knew what it was the instant she used it on him; he had known for two years, since Ward had named it in the yard, since the knife in the capital lodging. The passage, Ward had called it: the half breath between sending his mana one way and sending it the other, when he was neither one thing nor the other and there was nothing in his arms. It was smaller than it had been. The delve had been closing it all autumn. It was not gone. Before every driven stroke, for half a heartbeat, he went still, the way a man goes still before he lifts something heavy; and she had found the stillness, and she was reading it, and she was moving in it.

She hit him on the right forearm. Not hard. A touch, with the flat, to tell him.

He kicked up the sand.

Air, the expensive way, a gust off his left palm down at the yard between them, and the sand went up in a sheet off the ground into her face plate and his and the whole yard went yellow and blind. He shut his eyes behind the violet panes and opened his sense all the way, all of it, the flood, and fought her by it. He had done it at Kaldo at night against the knights, and it had worked every time, because knights could not feel anything in the dark and he could feel all of them.

She came through the sand at him without hesitating.

She came straight to where he was, and her blade met his where his was going to be, and he felt her in the flood, dense and close and entirely unbothered, and understood in the same instant that she had her eyes shut too.

"I can feel you too, Emeric," said Rowena, through the sand, quite calmly. "You're the loudest thing in the yard."

She knocked his blade out of his hand.

It went up out of his grip and away to his right in the yellow air, turning; and he put out his empty right hand after it and pulled. Small, and fast, the thing he had learned in Ward's yard on the first nights: a weight drawn toward the hand. The violet glyph came up in his palm the size of a fingernail and went out, and the blade turned over in the air and came back to him, hilt first, and slapped into his gauntlet.

The sand settled.

The knights on the walls had seen it. Some of them. He saw it in their faces through the panes as the yard cleared: the blade going away, and coming back. Some of them had seen the glyph, and some had not, and the ones who had were looking at each other; and they would think, if they thought anything, that the suit had done it. He had put stones in the suit's palms for exactly that, so that anybody who saw a thing fly to his hand would think of the stones and not of him.

She had not been fooled by the suit for a single breath. He felt that too.

He tried the soft ground again. He read her next step and pushed the amber glyph down at it, wobbling, too big; and she put her own palm down as she stepped, without breaking stride, and her glyph came up off her hand small and steady, and the sand under her foot went hard as a step of the dome's stair a breath before his reached it. His went into it and did nothing. She came on.

He went back. He gave ground across the yard, west, toward the drop and the sun coming up over the harbor behind her, and his breath was going now, and the cost of the earth and the air was behind his eyes in a dull ache, three times over; and he thought, the Rail. Once more. It had worked once. If it worked again he could end it without a throat, a blade in her ribs, a touch, honest; a duel she would know he had not had to win the hard way, and that the coast would hear as close.

He put his palm down. The block came up behind his heel. He drove.

And she stepped off the line.

She did not jump it. She did not take it on her blade. She simply was not there; a step to her right, quick and small and early, as he left the block, because she had seen his palm go down and the amber come up and knew, from the first time, exactly where a man on a rail would go and that he could not go anywhere else. He went past her down the line he had chosen, twelve feet across her yard, flat and fast and committed, with nothing at the end of it but air.

He stopped himself in the air.

He had to. He was going off the drop. He threw air ahead of himself off both palms, the expensive way, hard against his own line, and felt the Rail's speed break on it like a wave on a mole; and went light in the same instant, and up, a jump off nothing, the suit's nodes lit, and at the top of the jump, a man's height over the yard's west edge with the harbor fifty feet below him, he stopped. Hung there. The Stop: his weight gone, all of it, so that he hung in the air above the edge of her yard like a man hanging from a rope, and turned, and looked down for her.

The suit would carry it. Anybody watching would see the suit's stones and think the suit. That was why he had built a suit with no levers.

She was already in the air.

She had jumped after him off the yard's edge as he went up. A knight's jump, from a run, her plate and all; he saw her coming up under him, past him, higher than he was, a woman in bright steel against the morning, and knew in the same breath that she was going to come down short. A knight could not stop in the air. Nobody had ever needed to. She would come down past the edge, over the drop, fifty feet onto the harbor wall in plate.

She put her left hand down at the yard below her as she rose.

And a stone came up off the yard into the air. Not sand; a block of the clay under it, the size of a mounting step, dense and hard as brick, torn up off the edge of the yard by the amber glyph on the back of her hand and thrown up into the air under her, into her path, where her foot would be. Her boot came down on it, in the air, a man's height over the edge of her yard.

She stepped off it.

She pushed off the flying block the way a man pushes off a rock in a stream, and changed direction in the air, which no knight could do, toward him, and down; and he was hanging, still, at the top of his Stop, with his weight gone and nothing under his feet, turning, looking the wrong way for her.

Her blade came down on his left shoulder.

On the shoulder. The suit's left pauldron, the plain black shell over the joint that housed the cape's catch; she brought the flat of the long sword down on it out of the air with all her weight and the block's push behind it, and he heard the steel go. A long wet crunch, like a man stepping on a crab. He felt the plate fold in on itself against his shoulder, and the shoulder under it go white with it; and he was no longer hanging. He was falling, onto the edge of her yard, on his back, in the sand, with his weight back and all of it at once and the breath gone out of him.

She came down beside him on her own feet. The clay block came down after her, and broke on the sand, and lay there in pieces.

The rolled point of her blade touched the gorget at his throat.

"There," said Rowena.



Nobody on the walls said anything for a long time.

Then the young knight with the split lip began, by himself, to beat his hand on the wall; and the others took it up, and the grey knight, and the sound went along the north side of the yard and back, steel on stone. Not for him. He lay on his back in the sand with the morning sky going round over him and listened to it and knew that. Some of it was for him.

Rowena took her helm off. She stood over him and looked down at him with the sun behind her head and her face wet and her scar white and her breath going hard, and she did not offer him her hand.

"You had me twice," she said.

He got his own helm off. It took both hands, and one of them did not want to. "Yes."

"On my knee. And after the turn." She looked at him. "And you didn't."

"No."

"Because if you'd put Harrold's queen on her back in her own yard in front of forty of her knights," said Rowena, "it'd have been at his table by the spring, and every man in the capital with a use for a weapon would have been writing to you by the spring." She turned the blade in her hand and set its point in the sand. "Good. You're not stupid. I wondered." She looked down at him. "Your legs are a clerk's."

He looked up at her.

"Not your arms. Your arms are wonderful. I don't know what you do with them and I don't want to know, here. Your sense is as good as any I've crossed." She tapped her own thigh with two fingers, on the plate. "But you fight from the waist up, like a man who's spent his life at a desk, which you have. Everything you have goes through two legs that were built to carry a clerk from his bed to his ledger. You plant, and it goes into the knee. You turn, and it goes into the ankle. You jump, and you can't come down on them fast enough to do anything when you get there." She looked at his knee. "You'll be lame by fifty. Sooner, if you keep that up."

"How long?"

"To fix it?" She thought. "Two years. Every day. Feet and legs. Stairs. Sand. Running uphill with a weight on your back. Standing on one foot while somebody pushes you." She looked across the yard at Ward, on the north wall. "Somebody who won't be careful with you."

Ward looked back at her.

"You've been too careful with him," said Rowena.

"Yes," said Ward.

"Why?"

Ward did not answer for a while. Then he unfolded his arms.

"Because he's his mother's," he said. "And I couldn't keep her."

Rowena looked at him for a long moment, across the yard, in front of all her knights; and then she nodded, once, as if he had paid her a debt in coin she recognized, and turned and walked away up the stair with her helm under her arm.

She had not said anything about the gravity. Not a word. She had stood over him with her blade at his throat and talked about his legs and his knight and the capital, and her note, in his sense, all the while, had been holding something very tight, the way she had held herself still at the top of the quay steps on the first morning with her hand on her hilt; and she had taken it up the stair with her and not put it down.



Imani cut the pauldron off him in the harbor house kitchen.

She did it with Bram's tin shears, which she had packed in her bag in Kaldo for no reason she would give, with Ward holding the suit's shoulder steady and Ferra holding the lamp, though it was broad day; and the crushed pauldron came off in three pieces, folded in on itself like a stepped-on tin. The cape's catch came with it, bent. The shoulder under it was a single bruise from the collarbone to the top of the arm, already going dark.

Imani looked at it. She put her fingers on it, and pressed, and he made a sound, and she said "Good" in a tone that meant the bone was whole; and went on pressing, in other places, while he made other sounds.

"It's not broken," she said. "It'll be black for a fortnight. You'll not lift that arm above your head till Kaldo." She sat back. "Now the legs."

"The legs are fine."

"Take your boots off."

He took his boots off. She took his right foot in her lap, on her apron, and turned it, and bent it, and put her thumbs into the back of his ankle, and then his knee, and then his left; and then she made him stand up on the kitchen flags in his stockings and bend his knees, and watched them, from the floor, with her chin on her fist.

She did not say anything for some time.

"Well?" said Emeric.

"She's right," said Imani.

He looked at her.

"Your left knee's swollen. From yesterday's clay and today's turn. Your right ankle clicks. Hear it?" She bent it. It clicked. "You've been doing something with your legs since the summer that puts more through them than they're built to carry. A great deal more. I've watched you come back from Ward's yard limping on one side and then the other for four months and thought it was the yard." She let go of his foot. "It's not the yard. It's you. Whatever you do when you go fast, it goes through those joints like a cart through a gate that's too narrow for it, and the gate's taking the knocks." She looked up at him from the floor. "Two years. She's right. Feet, knees, hips, back. Before you do that again in a fight."

"I know she's right."

"Then stop looking like that."

"I'm not looking like anything."

"You're looking," said Ferra, from behind the lamp, "like a man who's been told the same thing twice in one morning by two women and would like one of them to be wrong."

He did not answer. Imani laughed, the short dry laugh, and stood up off the flags.

"And you'll not do it," she said. "The fast thing. With your arms and your legs. Not in Ward's yard, not anywhere, until I've seen your knees again at Kaldo and said so." She put the shears back in her bag. "I'm writing it in the chart."

"I'm writing it in the book," said Ferra.

"Two books," said Imani. "He'll not argue with two books." She picked up the three pieces of the pauldron off the table and looked at them, at the black plate creased like paper. "Bram will cry," she said.



The terms were signed at sunset, in the standing room, with all eight chairs filled.

That was Rowena's doing. She had sent for them at noon, every one: the harbor-reeve down from his office by the mole on a stick, eighty, furious at being moved and delighted to be asked; the treasurer; the master of the works with his burned hands; the prior of the harbor chapel; the grey knight, as captain; the master of the vintners, the master of the fishing families, who turned out to be a woman, square and grey, with a net-maker's hands; and the master of the sulphur pits, who smelled of eggs. They sat in their chairs down the long table under the dome, four and four, by office, and looked at each other with the faces of people who had been told for twenty years that they were a table and had never once been one.

Emeric read them the draft.

He read all of it. Ferra's order: the clerk, who would sign for the Sovereign under a seal of his own for a list of things the Sovereign would write down, and answer for them to this table. This table, sat, every month, on the first day, with a book. A school under the dome to make the next clerk. A press, Kaldo's, with a man from Kaldo to set it up and teach two men of Emberfell to run it, and then go home; and the law on the stone printed on a sheet and posted on every hall door and quay and chapel in the duchy. A yard bar and a set of weights in an iron case on the quay wall, from the forge's masters. Drains for the lower terraces, as the Sovereign's healer and Kaldo's healer would agree between them, Imani having been down every well on the lower terraces that afternoon with a lamp and come back with an opinion. All of it in the Sovereign's name. All of it the Sovereign's: the Sovereign's clerk, the Sovereign's school, the Sovereign's press. Nothing of it Kaldo's, or Randillia's, or his.

And for Kaldo: sulphur, by the boatload, from the pits, at a price. One throat of the mountain in the great works, one crack and the furnace over it, leased to Kaldo for its glass and later for whatever else Kaldo chose to melt there. And a sea lane, both ways, Kaldo's coasters to the Sovereign's quay and the Sovereign's boats to Kaldo's, with a factor at each end; Kaldo's factor to be Master Saban.

Saban, at the end of the table, did not move at all when his name was read, and went very slowly red from the collar up.

The table said nothing. Rowena let it say nothing for a while.

Then the grey knight spoke. He spoke to her, not to Emeric, and he spoke in the voice of a man who had been her captain for twenty years and had earned the right.

"Your Grace," he said. "If we take his press and his clerk and his school, and give him sulphur and a throat and a lane, then we're his. His quarrels are ours. Whatever he's doing up there in his barony with his ships and his suits and his black knights, when Randillia comes for him, and it will, it'll come down this inlet after his sulphur." He looked down the table at Emeric, not unkindly. "I'm sorry, Highness. I've seen the suit. I watched you this morning. Somebody's going to want to know what you are, and they'll come here to ask."

"Yes," said Rowena. "They will."

The grey knight waited.

"I have been necessary," said Rowena, "for twenty years." She looked down the long table at the eight of them, in their eight chairs, by office. "Every clerk in this city has carried every paper up that stair to me. Every quarrel on the quay comes to my door. Every boat in the harbor waits on my seal to sail. I have been the only thing in Emberfell that knew how to do anything, since the day I took my brother's chair, and I did it because there was nobody else, and I have let it stay that way because it was easier than teaching anybody." She put her hand flat on the draft. "I would like very much to stop."

Nobody said anything.

"If his quarrels come down the inlet," said Rowena, "they'll find a table that's sat. They'll find a clerk who signs for me, and a law every fisherman can read, and eight people in eight chairs who know what they're for." She looked at the grey knight. "That's worth sulphur. It's worth a quarrel or two. I've had quarrels." She touched the scar, briefly. "I'd rather have them with a table behind me than without one."

The grey knight looked at her for a long moment. Then he bowed his head, not deeply.

"Your Grace," he said.

Vantic was at the foot of the table with her own book. Rowena looked down at her.

"And you, madam. You're the bank."

"I'm the House's factor in Kaldo, Your Grace," said Vantic. "The House holds audit rights in Kaldo's books." She had read the draft twice, in the afternoon, with Ferra, at the harbor house table, very slowly. "This touches nothing the House reads. The lane's new trade, not new debt. The throat's a lease. The press and the school are gifts." She closed her book. "The House has no view."

"Has it not," said Rowena, and looked at her a moment longer than she needed to; and Vantic looked back, perfectly composed, and the moment went.

They signed it at sunset. Rowena first, under her own seal. Then each of the eight, in order, by office, the harbor-reeve's hand shaking on the pen and the fishing families' master writing her name in slow capitals with her tongue between her teeth. Then Emeric, as Holder of Kaldo; and Ferra, as chancellor, under him, with the barony's seal. Through the windows down the side of the room the sun went down into the inlet and lit the whole harbor red, and the mountain's plume above the city went pink, and then grey.

"There," said Rowena. She looked at the eight signatures down the sheet. "Now they've done something."



The suit fitted her at dusk.

They brought the crate up from the ship on a cart, Cade and Tobe and four of her knights, and opened it in the yard by lamplight, with the Kaldo party round it and Rowena's armourer, a small bald man with a leather apron and a face like a walnut, who stood at the edge of the lamplight with his arms folded and the expression of an armourer who has been told that a man from Randillia has made his lady a suit of plate and is prepared to hate it.

Ferra cut the seals. Ward lifted the lid.

It lay in the felt in its pieces, bright steel, every one of them; not blacked, like the Kaldo knights'. Plain. No leaves, no waves, no device on the breast; Ferra had said, in October, that a knight dressed her own suit, and Emeric had said she was right, and that one had not needed writing down. The panes were blue. Not Kaldo's violet; the blue of Rowena's knights' cloaks, which Ivo had matched in his glass from a scrap of ribbon Saban had found in the capital, in a glass so clear that the stones behind it would show their light through it, faint, and nobody would know which. Two hearts. One stick in the left gauntlet's grip and one lift lever at the belt. And under the plate, at the forearms and the thighs and down the spine, the second ring: six more nodes in their barrels, with no rods to the hearts at all, asleep.

The armourer unfolded his arms.

He came into the lamplight and crouched by the crate and picked up the left vambrace, and turned it in his hands, and looked inside it, and ran his thumb along the seam where there was no seam; and put it down, and picked up the breastplate, and looked at that too, for a long time, holding it up to the lamp.

"No joins," he said. Not to anybody. "No hammer marks." He put it down very carefully. "Who made this?"

"Kaldo," said Emeric.

The armourer looked at him. Then he did not ask anything else, and began to lay the pieces out on the sand in their order.

They armed her. The armourer did it, with Ward beside him showing him the straps, and Rowena standing in the middle of the yard in her quilted jack with her arms out, saying nothing. Piece by piece: the greaves, the cuisses, the cuirass with the two hearts in it, the pauldrons with the cape catches in them, the cape itself, furled into a hooded cloak and buckled at the shoulders; the gauntlets. The helm last, winged, with its blue pane.

She stood in it, in the lamplight, and moved her arms, and turned at the waist, and bent one knee.

"It's long," she said. "In the leg. A finger."

"Two," said Emeric. "When I made it. My engineer took one off. She said I'd made you as somebody standing over me." He looked at the greave. "She said I'd been looking up."

Rowena looked at him through the blue pane.

"You'd have built me a giant," she said.

"I did. She corrected me. By a finger." He looked at the armourer. "She'd have taken the other off too, if she'd been sure. She left the straps long. She said whoever armed you would see it."

"I see it," said the armourer. He was kneeling at her left greave with his thumb on the strap. "I'll have it up by morning." He looked at the greave a while longer. "She was right to leave it," he said, grudgingly. "I'd rather take a finger off than put one on."

Emeric turned to Rowena.

He told her plainly. He had decided on the voyage that he would, and in the harbor house he had told Ward and Ferra he would, and Ward had said she would want it plain and Ferra had said that if he made a speech of it she would leave the yard.

"It flies," he said. "The lever lifts you, a notch at a time; the stick sends you where you point it, and when you let go of it, it holds. Ward will show you tomorrow. The second ring wakes only to you. Put your own mana into it and it'll turn you, or throw you, or lift you, faster than any lever. Nobody else in the world can make it do that." He looked at the hearts behind their blue panes. "The hearts run on stones. Any stone cut to the gauge. You'll have a chest of them. When they're spent, any mage can charge them." He paused. "But nobody else can make another suit like it. Nobody can mend a heart if one fails, or a node, or re-cut a stone in it. If anything goes wrong inside it, it has to come back to Kaldo. To me." He looked at her through the pane. "It depends on Kaldo. As long as you fly it, you'll depend on Kaldo. I want you to know that before you take it."

"A leash," said Rowena.

"That's what Ward called it."

"Ward's right." She looked across the lamplight at Ward. "He's usually right. It's very tiresome." She lifted her gauntleted hands in front of her face and looked at them, at the stick in the left grip, and turned them over. "I know what a leash is, Emmy. I married into one. I wore it for fifteen years in Harrold's court and I knew whose hand it was in every day." She closed the left hand round the stick. "This one I can see the end of. That's better than most."

She took it. She did not say so. She stood in it in the lamplight with her hand on the stick, and did not take it off.



He went past the armoury late that night, on his way down to the ship, because he could not sleep; his shoulder would not let him lie on it and his knee would not let him lie on the other side.

The armoury was in the dome's undercroft, off the yard stair, with a door to the stair that stood open for the heat. There was a lamp lit inside. He did not mean to look in. He looked in.

The armourer was at a bench at the back with Rowena's left greave in a vice and a file in his hand, and the long strap off it laid out beside him, working by the lamp with his tongue between his teeth, and did not look up. And in the middle of the floor, on a stand made of two trestles and a pole, the rest of the suit hung, armed on nobody, bright in the lamplight; and two old people were sitting on stools on either side of it with their laps full of white.

A man and a woman, both grey, both small, both in black, with spectacles on their noses and needles in their hands. The armourer had called them Lorcan and Hesta, at the crate, and said they had kept the Sovereign's wardrobe since before she had a scar. They were lacing. That was all. Lace, white, a great deal of it, in long skirts that fell from the cuirass's lower edge over the cuisses, edged at the hem with small white feathers; and they were lacing it on, not sewing it, through the strap holes and the buckle slots and the cape catches that were already there in the plate. Not one new hole. He looked for one and there was not one. Hesta's needle went through a buckle slot on the cuirass and out and through the next with a long thread of white silk, and Lorcan on the other side caught it and drew it through and passed it back.

On the pole above the suit hung a plume, white, long, curled, waiting for the helm. Over the back of Hesta's stool lay a cloak; not Abloh's cape, which was the wing, but a second one, to go over it, white wool embroidered all over in white thread with snowflakes, every one different, trimmed at the hem and the collar with down. And folded on top of it, a scarf, pale blue, the blue of the panes.

"Snowflakes," he said, in the door, before he could stop himself.

Hesta looked up at him over her spectacles.

"She's not seen snow," she said. "Not since Harrold's court." She went back to the thread. "We thought, if she's to go up in the sky in it, she might as well have some."

Lorcan did not look up at all. He caught the thread and drew it through and passed it back.

Emeric stood in the door a while longer and watched them lace. Then he went down the stair to the ship, and sat in the station with the lamp off and his shoulder against the cool of the bulkhead, and did not sleep there either.
