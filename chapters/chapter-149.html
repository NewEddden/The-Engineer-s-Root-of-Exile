Chapter 149 - The Hod

Saban brought it to the table the way he brought anything he meant to be paid for, with the figures already done and a second copy of them for Ferra.

"You're going up to the band every time the ship flies," he said. "Every cell she carries comes down fuller than it went. That's a standing order, and it's a good one. And the ship flies when you need her to fly, which is not often, and when she does fly she's carrying you, and Ward, and the four of them in their suits, and whatever you've got her doing that week, and the cells are what she happens to bring home." He set the sheet down in front of Emeric. "The printers ran dry twice in the summer. The forge eats a rack a day when you're using it. The nodes on the wall want changing every season. Bram wants stone for the new bench and Vesk wants it for the etching floor, and both of them are waiting on Orrin, and Orrin fills four stones a day on a good day and then goes home to lie down."

"I know what we use."

"You know what we use. I'm telling you what it would cost if we were buying it." Saban tapped the sheet. "A small hull. Iron. Fletcher builds it, because Fletcher already knows, and nobody else is going to. Stones enough to lift it and nothing else, no rooms, no galley, no screw, no armoury, no heating in the floors. A hold full of racks. Three men. It goes up at night off the far end of the lake, sits in the band until the racks are full, and comes down before light, and the next night it does it again. It doesn't carry anybody. It doesn't go anywhere. It goes up and it comes down." He sat back. "It's a lighter. It's a coal lighter that goes up instead of along. And I'll run it, because it's a business, and I know how to run a business, and you don't have the time."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Bram, at the far end, had started to smile at coal lighter and stopped himself.

Ferra had already turned Saban's second sheet over and was reading the back.

"You've costed the crew," she said.

"Three men. Wage and a half, because of the hours and because of what they'll know."

"You've costed the hull and the stones and the racks." She turned the sheet back. "You haven't put a price on what it brings down."

"No," said Saban. "That's yours."

Fletcher built her in five weeks on the lower water, on the same stocks where he had built the ship, with the same six men, who were told she was a lighter for the ore trade on the lake and had no reason to think otherwise, because she looked like one. She was forty feet and blunt at both ends and deep in the middle, with a flat top deck and a single hatch in it, and no rail worth the name, and she was the ugliest thing Emeric had ever paid for. Fletcher brought her up the river under tow behind the steamboat, and stood on the quay below the outside foundry when she was warped in, and looked at her with the specific distaste of a man who has been asked to build something beneath him and has built it properly anyway.

"She'll float," he said. "She'll do nothing else. Don't ask her to."

"She's not meant to."

"Good." Fletcher looked at her a while longer. "What's she called?"

Emeric had not thought about it. Saban had.

"The Hod," Saban said.

Fletcher considered it, and the hull, and nodded once, because it was the right name and he would not have said so.

Emeric and Bram fitted her over eleven nights in the shed behind the outside foundry, with the doors shut and Tobe at them. It was not the ship. There were no stations, no deciding stone that could hold her level in a crosswind, nothing that could make her go anywhere but up and down. Six gravity stones in two rows under the deck beams, fed from a bank in the keel, and a reserve set of six above her middle, set and unset, the way he had learned to do it over a lake at two miles. The racks went into the hold in four tiers, bolted through, with the cells standing in them neck up and the necks open, so that whatever air she flew in, the cells drank. Bram made the rack frames in iron and then, when Emeric stopped him, remade them in oak, because the iron kept the cells from the air round them and there was no point carrying a hold full of stone up to the richest air in the world in a cage that would not let it breathe.

"You could have said that before I made them," Bram said.

"I didn't think of it before you made them."

Bram looked at the iron frames on the shed floor, and then at him. "You didn't think of it," he said, "and you're going to think of something else about iron in a week, and I'm going to want these." He had them carried to the back of the shed and stood against the wall.

It took Emeric most of a week to see why he wanted them. By then it was too late to tell him he was right, because he had known it, which was the way with Bram.

Saban found the pilot on the lower water.

His name was Ned Tallis and he had been a lighterman on the estuary for twenty years, taking coal and stone and timber off the coasters in the roads and bringing it up to the town quays on the tide, in the dark, in all weathers, in a flat heavy boat with no more sail than would move her. He was a short square man of about forty-five, bald, with a lighterman's forearms and a lighterman's habit of not saying anything that did not need to be said, and he had come up the river on the steamboat with Saban because Saban had asked him to, and had been told nothing on the way except that there was work, and that it paid, and that it was night work.

Saban told him in the shed with the doors shut, with Emeric there, and Bram, and the Hod on her blocks under the lamps. He told him all of it. The band. What the Hod was for. What she did. Where she went. What would happen to him if he told anybody, which Saban said once and plainly and did not decorate.

Ned Tallis listened to all of it. Then he walked round the Hod on her blocks, once, slowly, and looked at the underside of her where the stones were, without touching them, and came back.

"Up," he said.

"Up."

"How far up?"

"Two miles," said Emeric. "About."

Ned Tallis thought about it. "And she comes down the same way."

"The same way."

"Not sideways."

"She can't go sideways. She goes up and she comes down. There's a screw on a pole at the stern for holding her head to the wind, and that's all."

"That's a lighter, then," said Ned Tallis, as if it settled something. "I've been taking lighters up and down on a tide for twenty years. Up's no different to me. It's just further." He looked at Saban. "What's the wage?"

Saban told him.

"And a half," Ned Tallis said, "because of what I'll know."

"That's already in it."

"Then that's fine," said Ned Tallis, and went to look at the hatch.

He took her up on the fourth night after, off the far end of the lake, with Emeric beside him on the deck and the two hands Saban had found for the racks, Garrick and a boy named Wes, below in the hold with lanterns. She went up the way the ship had gone up the first time, with the deck coming away from under a man's feet and the lake going small, and Ned Tallis stood at the little lever with his hand on it and his feet apart and did not say one word the whole way, and when the cold came on he put his other hand inside his coat.

At the bottom of the band the cells in the hold began to glow, faintly, tier after tier, and Garrick called up through the hatch in a voice that had gone very high.

Ned Tallis looked down through the hatch at the light coming up out of his hold, and then out at the dark on every side, and then at Emeric.

"Well," he said. "That's a cargo."

Ferra put a price on it in the third week of the Hod's running.

She did it at her own table in the counting room, with Saban's figures on one side and her own on the other and the barony's books open between them, and she did not ask anybody's opinion, because it was a number and numbers were hers. Emeric learned of it the way he learned of most of what Ferra did, which was by reading it afterward.

She had worked it from the only price for charge that had ever existed in Kaldo, which was Orrin. What the barony paid Orrin by the day, and Vesk by the day for the hours he spent at the bench and not at the etching floor, and how many stones of the common cut either of them could fill in a day before he had to stop, and what a mage from the capital would have cost by the day if one could have been got to come, which one could not. She had set that against what the Hod cost to run a night: three men at wage and a half, the wear on the stones, Fletcher's hull written down over ten years, Saban's own hours at a figure he had not been told she had put on them. And what the Hod brought down in a night, in full cells, counted and entered by Saban's rack hand at the quay.

It came out, after all of it, at a little under a Copper for a stone of the common cut, full. Orrin's came out at three.

She wrote it in the book. Charge, the common stone full, from the Hod, to the works: one Copper. From the bench: three Copper. The works would buy their charge from the Hod's account at that price from the first of the month, and the Hod's account would pay the Hod's crew and Fletcher's note, and whatever was left would go to the fund.

And under it, in the margin, smaller, where she put the things that were true and were not figures:

This is the first price ever set on charge as a thing sold by the measure and not as the hire of a man. I do not know what else it will be the first of.

Emeric read it that evening, and read the margin twice, and did not say anything to her about it, because there was nothing he could have said that she had not already written.

The root came out of Ferra's strongbox in the last week of autumn, when the net had been down for two months and there was nothing left in the stone that anybody needed, and a great deal that nobody should ever need again.

It was one of Amara's two. It sat in his palm on the forge-room bench exactly as it had come out of his mother's comb lining in the treasury box, dull grey, carved over every face in its fine repeating lattice, worthless by every account the kingdom had ever written of it. The other one, the chipped one, the only unbound control stone left in the world, stayed in the strongbox. He was not going to risk that one. He was not entirely sure he was going to risk this one.

Armand sat on the stool at the end of the bench with his stick across his knees. Bram stood at the forge's controls. Ferra had come down, and stood by the door with her book, because she had written the fifth condition and she wanted to see the last of what it covered.

"If it doesn't work," Emeric said, "we've lost a root that we'd have had to keep in a box for the rest of our lives anyway. And we've lost the only way I know of to find out whether a control stone can be made into anything but what it was cut as." He looked at the stone. "The bands want it. If the six are going to wear their stones on their wrists, the stones have to be a shape you can wear. And I'm not putting one of your stones in the forge until I've seen what the forge does to one that doesn't matter."

"It matters," Ferra said from the door.

"It matters less."

She wrote something.

He went in first. He did it on the bench with his hand flat over the stone and his own stone in his other hand, and went down into the delve and found the root's room, which was not a room so much as a long low vault full of what the net had been, fifteen knots' worth of listening folded away in the dark: the page heads, the batch voices, the chain of relays, the whole shape of the thing that had read Wayford's ink for a summer. He did not look at any of it. He had read it all once and that was once more than he wanted. He took it, the whole of the vault, the way he would have taken a room's furniture into a cart, and carried it through his own door into his own room, and set it down there in a corner, and shut a door on it that only he could open. It took most of an hour, and at the end of it the root was empty. Not empty the way a spent stone was empty. Empty the way a house is empty when the last cart has gone and the doors are still on their hinges. The binding was still there. The stone still answered him. There was just nothing in it.

He came up, and handed it to Bram, and Bram put it in the forge's cup.

The forge took it. It emptied it first, of the little charge a control stone carries in its own body, the way it emptied everything, and then it lifted it and held it and warmed it, and Emeric fed the shape in, the one he had been carrying in his head for a month: a bead, round, a little smaller than a knuckle, the size of the beads he had made for the envoys' chains. He felt the stone go soft in the forge's hold, and give, and take the shape.

And then he felt the lattice.

He had not known what would happen to it. That was the whole question. The lattice was carved. Every account said it was carved. Every account said some craftsman whose name nobody had kept had sat with a graver over each of these stones, generations ago, and cut that fine repeating pattern into every face by hand, for reasons nobody had kept either. And carved things did not survive being melted. He had expected, if he had expected anything, a smooth grey bead with no pattern on it at all, and a stone that no longer answered.

It was not carved.

He felt it go, as the stone softened: the lattice on every face sinking away into the body of the stone, like frost off a window when the stove is lit. And he felt it come back. As the bead took its shape and began to cool in the hold, the pattern came up out of the stone's body onto the new faces, all of them, every one, the fine repeating lines climbing out of the grey from inside and running across the curve of the bead without a break, the way they had run across the old flat faces. As though it had never been cut into the stone at all. As though it had always been the stone, all the way through, and the faces were only where you saw it.

Bram, at the controls, said something under his breath that Emeric did not catch.

The forge set the bead down in the cup. Emeric picked it up. It was warm, and round, and dull grey, and carved over every face in its fine repeating lattice.

He went down into it. The binding held. It answered him exactly as it had answered before, from a smaller place.

"It works," he said.

Armand said, "Now do a pebble."

Emeric looked at him.

"You have one result," Armand said. "One. From a thing nobody understands, behaving in a way nobody predicted. Before you tell me what that means you will show me what the forge does to a stone that has never been anything but a stone. Or I shall spend the next month wondering whether your forge draws patterns on things."

It was the right question. Emeric had not thought to ask it, and would have in a day, and was annoyed that he had not asked it first.

Bram found a pebble in the yard, a plain grey one out of the gravel, and washed it in the trough, and put it in the cup. The forge emptied it, which took no time at all, since there was nothing in it, and lifted it, and warmed it, and Emeric gave it the same shape he had given the root. It went soft and took the bead's shape and cooled and was set down.

It was a smooth grey bead with nothing on it.

Armand leaned forward on his stick and looked at it for a long time, and then at the other, and then at the first again.

"Then it was never carved," he said. "It grows. Whatever that pattern is, it is the stone, and the stone keeps it the way a crystal keeps its own angles. Every account I was ever taught is wrong. Some craftsman did not sit with a graver." He sat back. "Nobody made these. Or nobody made them the way we were told."

Ferra wrote that down.

Emeric put the plain bead in his pocket to throw back in the yard, and put the root on the bench, and went back into the delve, and carried the vault back out of his own room and through its door into the bead, and shut it there, and came up.

"Now you can do mine," he said.

"Not today," said Ferra.

"Not today," he agreed.

It was Bram's iron that made the flask.

Emeric had been thinking about the reserve since the night over the lake. About eight stones spending themselves in the empty sky, set and falling, and a count of nothing to spare. And about the other thing he had thought that night and not said: that a dead zone in the band itself, if there was ever such a thing, would not last, because the medium up there was too thick to hold a hole open, and would close over it the way deep water closes over a bubble. But not at once. Not in the time it took a small iron hull to fall out of it.

What he wanted was a piece of the band he could carry down and let out where he needed it. And the thing that kept the medium out of a place was iron. He had learned that on Ward's plate and paid for it in weight on every suit since. It stood to reason that the thing that kept the medium out would also keep it in.

Bram had the frames. He had known he would want them, and when Emeric came to him with a drawing he took one of the iron rack frames off the wall and cut it up, and forged a flask out of it, a squat thing about the size of a quart jug, with a thick iron lid that screwed down on a lip lined with Rowena's pitch. He made four. Emeric sent them up on the Hod with their lids off, stood in the hold among the racks, and told Ned Tallis to screw them shut himself, at the top of the band, before he started down.

Ned Tallis brought them back in the morning in a sack, and set the sack down on the forge-room bench, and said, "They're heavier."

They were not. Emeric weighed them. But he knew what Ned Tallis meant, because when he put his hand on one of them he could feel, faintly, through the iron, the way he could feel a banked fire through a stove's door, something packed very tight inside it. Not a stone's hum. Not a charge. The medium itself, dense as it lay in the band, shut in a quart of iron and carried two miles down to where the air was thin with it.

He made a dead zone in the forge yard to try it in. That was a thing he knew how to do and did not like doing: a spent chip, overloaded until it gave, and the hole it left in the medium where it gave, a patch of nothing the width of a cart that would take the better part of a day to fill back in. He set a lamp stone on a post in the middle of it, charged, and the lamp stone went dark as soon as it was in, the way the one on the cord had gone dark over the lake.

He carried one flask in, and stood by the post, and unscrewed the lid.

He felt it go. It went the way breath goes out of a man on a cold morning, all at once, in a rush he could feel on his face without it being air, out of the neck and into the nothing around it. And the lamp stone lit.

It lit and stayed lit. The patch of nothing round the post was not nothing. It was thin and getting thinner as the flask's charge spread and went into the hole on every side, but it was there, and the stone on the post drew on it and glowed. He counted. At sixty the light was beginning to waver. At eighty it was guttering. At ninety-two it went out, and the patch was nothing again, and the flask in his hand was an iron jug with nothing in it.

"Ninety," he said. He came out of the dead patch. "About ninety. Of a lamp. A gravity stone draws harder than a lamp, but a gravity stone doesn't need to stay lit for ninety, it needs to stay lit long enough to get a hull to the edge of the patch." He looked at the flask. "Four of those in the Hod, lids shut, at the helm. If she hits a hole in the band, or the racks go dry at the top, Ned opens one and she has a minute and a half to get herself out of it."

"And the reserve," said Bram.

"And the reserve. The reserve's first. The flask is after."

Armand had come down to watch, and had watched it all from the edge of the yard on his stool, and said nothing. He had been in the ward most of the morning, and he looked it: grey under the eyes, his hands folded on the head of his stick and held there, hard, so that they were still.

Emeric took the second flask out of the sack to try it again, at a different distance, and walked back past the stool to the edge of the dead patch, and his thumb slipped on the lid, and it came loose.

It was a foot from Armand.

He felt the rush go out of it, and he felt where it went. It did not go into the dead patch. It went into the nearest thing that was emptier than itself, and the nearest thing that was emptier than itself was an old man who had spent six hours that morning holding a door narrow with his eyes shut, and had been spending the same six hours every day for two months.

Armand made a sound. It was not loud. He sat up very straight on his stool, all at once, as if somebody had struck him flat across the back with a board, and his stick went over on the flags, and his hands came off the head of it and stood out in front of him, open, and his mouth opened, and for a moment he did not breathe.

Emeric got the lid back on. It took no time at all. It was far too long.

Armand breathed.

He sat on the stool with his hands out in front of him and looked at them. They were not shaking. Then, slowly, he put them down on his knees.

"That," he said, in a voice that was not quite his, "was very fast."

"Are you hurt?"

"I do not know." He turned one hand over and looked at the palm, and at the inside of the wrist, where he had put his own fingers on ten thousand other men's. "I was nearly empty. I had not known how nearly. And then I was full." He was quiet a moment. "In the time it takes to drop a lid."

Bram had come across the yard. He picked up the stick and held it out, and Armand did not take it.

"I have felt that once before," Armand said. "Not in my own body. In the gallery. Twice, from the gallery, in other men's bodies, watching. The fourth hour." He looked up at Emeric. "That was the rate. That is what they do in the chamber, for fourteen hours, in waves. That was a mouthful of it, and I have had a great deal of practice at being filled, and I am not a boy of nineteen."

Emeric stood with the flask in his hand and did not say anything.

"It will refill a spent caster," Armand said. "Yes. You will want to write that down. You will want to use it, one day, when you have a man on a wall who has run himself dry and needs to be full again in the time it takes to open a lid." He took the stick from Bram at last, and set its foot on the flags, and put both hands on its head. "And when you use it, you will be doing to him, for one breath, exactly what they did to Ward and Rylan and Declan and Osric for fourteen hours. The same total. Faster. You told me yourself, years ago, that it is a parameter." He stood up, carefully. "Mind the lid."

He asked at the table two nights later.

He asked the way he had asked the first time, before the ship's first ascent, in front of the six, which was not a thing he did. He asked to go up in the Hod, one night, as a passenger, into the band. He gave his reasons. He had read the band now for a year on instruments at the bottom of it, from the bench and the racks and Vesk's account and Emeric's, and he had never been in it. He was the only man on the table whose whole trade was the medium, and he had never stood in the richest of it. And he had been filled once now, by accident, from a flask, and he wanted to know what it was to be in the thing the flask had been filled from.

He did not give the other reason. Emeric saw him not give it, and saw Imani see it too.

"No," Ferra said.

She said it before anybody else could say anything. She did not raise her voice.

"You're past seventy," she said. "The air up there stopped Emeric's breath and put a headache in Ward that Ward admitted to, which I have never heard him do about anything. It's cold enough to stop a hand. The Hod has no cabin and no heated floor and no rail worth the name, and no stations, and no deciding stone, and a lighterman at the lever who has been doing it for three weeks. And you hold this barony when he's out of it." She had her book open, and did not look at it. "Imani said all of that to you before the ship went up, and you didn't go. I'm saying it again. No."

"I heard Imani," Armand said. "I did not go." He turned his head, slowly, and looked down the table. "Imani?"

Imani did not say anything.

She sat with her hands on her register and looked at him, and Emeric saw her look at his hands on the table, folded, still, where anybody could see them, and then at his face, and she did not say anything at all. She had said no the first time. She did not say it now. Emeric did not know why, and he thought, from the way she held her mouth, that she did know, and did not like knowing.

"Then I will go," Armand said.

"It's not a veto," Ferra said. "My veto is on him." She did not look at Emeric. "You're not bound by it. I can't stop you. I'm telling you no, and I'm writing down that I told you, and that you went."

"Write it," said Armand, not unkindly.

"It's said, then," Emeric said. "What Ferra said. And the rest of it." Everybody looked at him. He made himself say it plainly, the thing he had been carrying since the night over the lake and had not put into words at the table, because it was his ship and his band and his idea and saying it aloud made it true. "The Hod goes up every night. She's small and she's simple and she's light and there's less in her to fail than in the ship. But if she comes into a dead patch in the band, a real one, one we haven't seen and I don't know is there, or if her racks run dry at the top because something's wrong with the feed and nobody catches it, then the stations go, and she's on her reserve, and the reserve is six stones and a count. And if the reserve doesn't hold, the flask gives her ninety. And if that doesn't hold, she falls. Two miles. With Ned Tallis and Garrick and Wes in her." He made himself go on. "And one night, with you."

Nobody said anything.

"It hasn't happened," Saban said at last. "Three weeks. Twenty-one nights."

"It hasn't happened yet," said Emeric. "I'm saying it at this table so that when it happens, nobody at this table can say they didn't know."

Ferra wrote it down. All of it. He watched her do it.

The cold came on at about a mile, the way they had all said it would. Armand had been told, and had put on everything Imani had made him put on, and it went through all of it as though it were not there. Ned Tallis gave him the lee of the hatch coaming and a blanket over the knees and did not otherwise acknowledge that he was aboard, which Armand found he was grateful for.

He had his instruments. He had meant to use them. He had the glass in his lap in its case, and his book, and a pencil, because ink would freeze, and he had meant to take a reading at every quarter of the way up, and write it down in his own hand, so that there would be a record of what a mage from the tower read in the band that was not Emeric's and not Vesk's.

He took the first two. His fingers would not do the third.

And then the band came up round them.

He had tried, afterward, to write down what it was, and had not been able to, and had crossed out every attempt, which was not a thing he did. The nearest he came was the thing he had said at the bench to a young man with an argument four years before he could prove it, that mages who crossed the northern passes came to the top of the road with more in them than they started with. He had crossed the northern passes himself, once, at thirty, and had come to the top with more in him than he started with, and had put it down, as the tower told him to, to the thin air going to his head.

It had not been the thin air.

It was all round him. It came up through the deck and in through the blanket and in through the wool and the skin under it, not fast, not like the flask, not like a board across the back, but everywhere at once, gently, the way warm water comes up round a man who lowers himself into a bath. He felt it go into his hands first, because his hands were the emptiest part of him and had been for years. He felt it go into the long fine muscles of the fingers that had been shaking in the mornings since the spring before last, and that he had held folded on the head of his stick in front of other people so that nobody would see, and he felt them stop.

He took his hands out from under the blanket and held them up in front of his face in the dark.

Still.

Not held still. Not gripped still. Still, the way a young man's hands are still, without being told. He looked at them for a long time. He had forgotten. He had forgotten that it was not a thing a man did, holding his hands still. It was only a thing that happened, when a man was young, without his ever noticing.

The Hod hung at the bottom of the band with her hold alight, tier on tier of cells glowing up through the hatch, and Garrick and the boy Wes moving among them below with their lanterns turned down because they did not need them, and Ned Tallis at the lever with his feet apart. And Armand sat in the lee of the coaming with his hands in front of him and his breath coming short in the thin air and the cold at his face, and was, for the first time in more years than he wanted to count, not tired.

He did not know how long he sat there. The schedule said two hours, filling, and then down. At two hours Ned Tallis looked at him. Armand did not look back. At three hours Ned Tallis looked at him again, and then at Garrick, who had come up through the hatch to say the racks were full, and Garrick looked at Armand, and went back down the hatch without saying anything. Nobody told him to come down. He was the grey robe. He was the Secretary of the Arcane, and the oldest man aboard by forty years, and the man who held Kaldo when the prince was out of it, and not one of the three men aboard the Hod was going to be the one who told him it was time.

He knew that. He sat there anyway.

At four hours, a little past, with the eastern edge of the world beginning to show grey under the band, he said, "Down, if you please, Master Tallis," and Ned Tallis said, "Aye," and took her down.

He came into the ward at the second bell as he always did, and Imani looked up from her table and put her pen down.

He had come up the stair without stopping.

He was not young. He was an old man with white hair and a stick. But he stood in the door of the end room straight, and his colour was a colour she had not seen on him in two years, and when he crossed the room to the first bed and sat down on the stool he did not stop to get his breath first. He put his stick against the wall and held out his hand for the first stone, and it did not shake.

"How do you feel?" she said.

"Thirty," said Armand. He said it with a kind of wonder, as a man reports the weather in a country he had not expected to see again. "I feel as I did at thirty. I had forgotten entirely what that was." He closed his fingers round the stone in Hew's hand. "It will not last. I know that. It will go out of me the way it goes out of a stone left in the lake air. But I feel thirty."

Imani watched his hand over Hew's for a long moment.

"Would it do for them?" she said. She said it very quietly, so that the beds would not hear. "The band. If they went up. The way you did."

Armand did not answer at once. She saw him think it through honestly, all the way, and she saw him not want the answer he got to.

"No," he said. "It came into me gently, because I had something to meet it with. I have been filled all my life. There is a floor in me." He looked at Hew, who was asleep, or pretending. "There is no floor in them. What came into me like bathwater would go into them the way the flask went into me. I felt the edge of it, at the top, where it is thickest. For them, that edge would be the chamber." He shut his eyes and settled his hand. "No. Not the band. The cup."

Imani wrote that down.

It was going by the next evening. He could feel it go. It went out of him slowly, not all at once, the way the warmth goes out of a stone when you take it off the stove: he was forty by supper, and forty-five by the time he lay down, and he slept better than he had slept in years, and did not know how old he was when he woke.

Emeric came up to the keep's east room in the morning to ask him about the flask, and found him standing at the window.

He was not looking at the lake. He was looking at his hands. He had them up in front of him, at the height of his chest, in the grey light off the water, palms down, fingers spread, the way a man holds his hands out to be looked at by somebody else. And they were still. They were quite still. Emeric stood in the door and did not say anything, and Armand, who knew he was there, did not turn round.

"I should like to go back," Armand said, to the window.

Emeric did not answer.

They stood like that, the two of them, the old man at the window with his hands out and the young one in the door, for what felt like a long time. The light came up a little off the lake. A gull went across it.

And then, while Emeric watched, it started. Very small, at first. A fine fast shiver in the smallest finger of the right hand, and then the ring finger beside it, and then the whole hand, the way a leaf starts on a still day before any of the others. Then the left.

Armand watched them for a while longer. Then he folded them, one over the other, and held them there against his chest, hard, until they were still, and turned round.

"The flask," he said, in his ordinary voice. "You wanted to ask me about the flask."
