Chapter 149 - The Converter

It looked like a pear that had been asked to do something unreasonable.

That was Tess's word for it, and once she had said it nobody in the works could see it as anything else. It stood on the foundry floor between the blast furnace and the casting pit, taller than Bram, an iron vessel the shape of a fat pear with its narrow end at the top and open, slung between two great iron pins on two stone piers so that the whole of it could be tipped forward on them like a jug to pour. Inside, it was lined a hand thick with Grayrock's white sandstone, ground and wetted and rammed into place in layers with a wooden maul. In its bottom, through the lining, were a dozen small holes, and under the bottom was a box, and into the box ran a pipe as thick as a man's leg that came across the floor from the engine house, where a new cylinder had been put on the works engine for no purpose except to blow.

A Bessemer converter. He had told them the name and why it was named, which was for a man none of them would ever meet who had done this first, and Bram had said that it was a stupid name for a pear.

The idea was simple, and he told it once, at the bench, with chalk. Cast iron from the furnace was iron with too much carbon in it, which was why it was hard and why it broke instead of bending. Steel was iron with a little carbon. Wrought iron was iron with almost none. For a thousand years, on Earth, getting the carbon out had meant hours of a man stirring a puddle of iron in a furnace with a bar, or days of baking it in charcoal, a little at a time, the way Bram had made every ounce of steel Kaldo owned. This did it with air. Pour the molten iron in the pear, and blow air up through it from the bottom, through the holes, and the oxygen in the air burned the carbon out of the iron the way it burned the carbon out of a log. And the burning made heat. So the iron did not cool while you blew it; it got hotter. No fuel. A ton of iron turned to steel in a quarter of an hour by nothing but wind.

Bram had listened to all of it with his arms folded. At the end he had said that if that was true, then every smith in the world had been wasting his life for a thousand years, and Emeric had said yes, and Bram had said that in that case he would believe it when he saw it.



The first blow was on the second of June, in the afternoon, with the whole works standing back against the walls.

They tipped the pear on its side and poured the iron in from the furnace ladle, white and spitting, and tipped it up again, and Tess opened the valve on the blowing pipe.

For a breath nothing happened. Then sparks came out of the mouth of the pear. A few, and then a great many, a fountain of them going up out of the open top in a long orange spray and falling back onto the floor in a hissing rain, so that the men nearest went back further. Then the sparks became a flame. A long one, yellow, roaring up out of the mouth, and then white, white and very bright and very loud, a sound like a waterfall going the wrong way, and the whole foundry was lit by it as though somebody had opened a door into the sun. It went on. It went on longer than anybody there had ever stood in front of anything so loud. The heat came off it across the floor in a wall. The men at the walls had their arms over their faces. Bram stood where he had stood, with his arms folded, squinting into it, and did not move.

And then the flame dropped.

It did not go out. It shortened, all at once, and went thin and dim and blue at the edges, as though something had run out. Tess shut the valve. They tipped the pear over on its pins, slowly, with the long bar, and out of its mouth into the waiting ladle came a stream of steel, running like milk, so bright nobody could look straight at it.

It went into the ingot mould. It cooled. Next morning Bram took the ingot to the hammer, red hot, and put it under, and the first blow cracked it across like a biscuit.

He looked at it for some time. Then he took it off the anvil and put it in the scrap bin, and came back, and put his hands flat on the bench.

"That's not steel," he said. "That's a mistake."

Emeric knew why. He had known before the pour; he had known, if he was honest, since he drew the pear, because it was the first thing that had gone wrong for the man the pear was named after, a long time ago in another world. The air that burned the carbon out did not all leave again. Some of it stayed in the iron, and iron full of air broke red hot. And they had blown it until the carbon was all gone, so what was left was not even steel, only soft iron with air in it.

He did not say so. He said what he had said at the bench, that the air burns the carbon out, and Bram looked at him as if he would very much like to put him in the scrap bin with the ingot, and went off to think.

The second blow was on the sixth. Bram ran it. Bram had decided, in four days of thinking, that the first had not been blown enough, and that if the trouble was something left in the iron the answer was to burn more of it out; so he let the flame drop, and kept blowing, past the drop, a minute, two, three, with the pear roaring thin and blue and a smell coming out of it like a struck flint. The steel that came out of the second blow did not go to the hammer. It crumbled in the mould as it cooled. Bram broke a piece off it with his fingers.

"Burned," said Tess.

Bram did not say anything at all. He put the second ingot in the scrap bin with the first.

That was the rule. A thing that failed failed again before it worked. Emeric had written it down on the first page of the forge book and the first page of the works book and he believed it, but he had not known until he watched Bram's back, walking away from the scrap bin for the second time in a week, how much he would want to break it.

He kept his hands in his pockets.



Bram found it on the ninth, and he found it by remembering his grandfather.

He told it at the bench in the morning, to Tess, not to Emeric, which Emeric thought was very probably on purpose. His grandfather had made steel the old way, in a crucible, a pot of it at a time, and his grandfather had kept a bag by the furnace of a black crumbling ore that came out of the lead workings on the hill, the stuff the lead miners threw on the spoil heap because it was no good to them. Into every crucible his grandfather had thrown a pinch of it. Bram had asked him why, once, as a boy, and the old man had said it made the steel sweet, and boxed his ear for asking. Bram had never known what it did. He had thrown it in too, for thirty years, because his grandfather had.

"It takes something out," said Bram. "Or it puts something back. I don't know which." He put a lump of it on the bench, black and dull and crumbling, with a purple sheen on the broken face. "Whatever the air's doing in there. This undoes it."

Emeric looked at the lump on the bench and kept his face exactly as it was.

Bram smelted a small pig of it with some of the furnace iron in the old cupola that afternoon, and it came out of the mould a strange metal, white and brittle, that broke with a bright flat shining face like the face of a looking glass. Bram held up a broken piece of it to the light from the doors.

"Mirror iron," he said.

"That's its name," said Emeric. "On Earth. Spiegeleisen. It means mirror iron."

Bram looked at him with great dislike for knowing that, and then went back to the pear.

The third blow was the next morning. Bram stood at the valve himself. He watched the flame through a square of Ivo's dark glass, the one from the powerhouse, and when it dropped he shut the valve on the drop, exactly on it, not a breath after. Then he had Tess tip the ladle of melted mirror iron into the mouth of the pear, a small ladle, a tenth of the charge or less, and there was a great white flare and a hiss and a gout of brown smoke that went up into the roof. Then they poured.

The ingot went under the hammer at noon. It did not crack. Bram drew it out into a bar, and turned the bar, and drew it again, and it came out under the hammer as smooth and sweet as anything he had ever drawn from his own crucible. He let it cool. Then he put the end of the cold bar in the big vice and leaned on the other end with all his weight, and it bent, slowly, round, until it was bent nearly double, and did not crack.

He took it out of the vice and ran his thumb along the outside of the bend, slowly, twice.

"A ton," he said. "In a quarter hour."

"Less than a ton. It's a small pear."

"A ton," said Bram, who had spent thirty years making steel a pot at a time, and walked out of the foundry into the yard, and stood in the yard for a while with the bent bar in his hand, looking at the river.



Fennick Crake's first job was the second drive at the gravity shaft, and he was right about it.

The first drive, the one the mine had cut in the spring with the earth mages and the picks, ran east off the shaft bottom along the top of the body for forty yards. The second was to go under it, through hard grey stone with no grain to it, the kind that took a pick off its handle. Hask, who had run the shaft since the winter, wanted it done the old way: big charges, deep holes, one great blast at the end of each shift, and the spoil mucked out in the morning.

Fennick said that was how you made a mess.

He said it cheerfully, at the shaft head, in his burned coat, with his maimed hand in his pocket and a fuse in his other hand that he kept turning over. Big charges broke rock everywhere, he said. Up into the roof, down into the floor, sideways into the body, which was the one thing in that hill they did not want broken. What you wanted was a lot of small charges in a lot of shallow holes, in a pattern, and fuses cut to different lengths so that they went off one after another, a breath apart, each one breaking a little rock into the space the last one had made. Like taking a loaf apart with your fingers instead of sitting on it.

Hask said that was more holes than any crew in the south could drill. Fennick said that was a crew problem and not a rock problem. They argued about it for most of a morning. Then Hask let him try it on one face, to shut him up.

It took twice as many holes and half as much powder. The face came down in a neat heap at the foot of the drive, broken small enough to shovel, with the roof above it smooth and sound and the body of the gravity stone in the wall of the drive not so much as cracked. Hask stood at the foot of the heap with his lamp and looked at it for some time, and then went and found Fennick and told him he was a lunatic, and asked him to show the crew how he cut the fuses.

Ward counted the powder back into the store that evening. He counted it twice, and it was right to the ounce, and Fennick stood by watching him count with an expression of enormous martyrdom.



The hospital opened on the sixteenth.

It stood on the hospital ground above the ward, the ground she had walked every week since the autumn, high enough that nothing in Kaldo drained toward it. Two long stone wards, one for men and one for women, with tall windows down both sides that opened, and a third, smaller, for children, because Imani said children got well faster among children. A room for cutting, with a north light and a stone floor that sloped to a drain and walls of lime plaster that could be scrubbed. A room for births. A laundry with its own copper. And under all of it, its own water, from its own cistern on the slope above, through its own sand filter, in its own pipe, that had never been near anybody else's, and a tap in every ward.

She had drawn every room of it. Emeric had drawn nothing. He had given her the pages she asked him for and the money Ferra let him have, and stayed out of it, and she had stood at the foot of the slope every week of the winter with her drawings and argued with the masons until the masons were afraid of her.

The town came up to see it. Doran blessed it, plainly, at the door, in a dozen words. The first patient was a carter's boy with a broken arm who was carried into the children's ward on a board, howling, and stopped howling when he saw the windows.

And at the end of the day, when the town had gone, Imani came to Emeric in the cutting room with her instrument roll in her hands and laid it open on the scrubbed table.

"One thing," she said.

He looked at the roll. Her knives, her forceps, the needle holders Bram had made her, her father's razor in its strip of leather. All of them good. All of them, every one, freckled with brown at the joints and along the edges, where the boiling had found them.

"I boil them," said Imani. "Every time. Every one. You told me to and I do, and I'm right to, and they rust. I scour them and they rust again. Every time I boil a knife it's a little worse than it was." She put her finger on a spot of brown at the hinge of a forceps. "I want instruments I can boil a thousand times that come out the same as they went in. Can you do that?"

He could.

He did it in the forge that week, one night, one ingot, the size of a brick. It needed one thing Kaldo had and did not know it had. The dyehouse's yellow, the terrible yellow that every bad batch of paper had been dyed with since January, was made from a heavy black ore that Saban bought from a man on the coast for the dyers by the sack; and what made the yellow yellow was a metal in it that nobody in the south had ever seen by itself. The forge saw it. He told it what was in the ore, and what to take out of it, and what to put the metal into, and how much: an eighth part, by weight, in Bram's best steel, mixed through it to the last grain. It took most of the night and most of the feed bank, and he came up the narrow stair grey to the lips. In the morning there was an ingot on Bram's bench that looked like any other steel, and Bram, who had been told what it was for and nothing else, forged Imani's instruments from it over the next ten days: a set of knives, two forceps, a needle holder, a probe, all of them plain and heavy and exactly to her drawings.

She boiled them in front of him, in the hospital's copper, on the last day of June. She boiled them for an hour. Then she lifted them out with the tongs, one at a time, and laid them on a clean cloth on the table, and looked at them.

They came out bright. Every one. Bright as they had gone in, without a freckle.

She picked up the needle holder and turned it in the light from the north window, and looked at the hinge, and then at him.

"What's it called?"

"Stainless steel."

"Stainless," said Imani, trying it. She put it down on the cloth beside the others, square, the way she put things down that she meant to keep. "How many can you make?"

"An ingot a season. Maybe. It eats the forge."

"Then I'll make them last." She rolled the cloth up round them. "I make everything last."



He took a bout off Osric on the twentieth, in Ward's yard, at the dawn drill, for the first time in five years.

It was not much of a bout. It did not look like much from the wall. Osric came in the way Osric always came in, big and fast and straight, the way a knight came in when he had been made a knight and knew what a knight could do; and Emeric did not try to stop him. He went sideways, a half step, the step Ward had been beating into him on that sand since the first morning on the road south, and let Osric's blade go past where he had been, and turned inside it, and put the edge of his practice blade flat against the side of Osric's neck, under the ear, and held it there.

The yard stopped.

Osric stood very still with the flat of the blade against his neck and his own blade out past Emeric's shoulder, where Emeric had been. Then he lowered it.

"Again," said Osric.

They went again. Osric put him on the sand that time, and the next, and the one after that. But the first one stood, and everybody on the wall had seen it.

Afterward, at the pump, with nobody near, Emeric pressed the clasp of his band and looked at the little lit picture in the grey bead. The bar of light stood where it had stood at dawn, to the hair. He had not drawn on it. He had not drawn on anything. He had not let himself, the whole bout; he had felt the temptation come up in him when Osric came in, the old trick, the low warm push into the legs and the eyes that he had been teaching his body all spring to do without thinking, and he had not let it, because he had wanted to know.

Now he knew. It had been his feet and his eyes and five years of Ward. Nothing else.

Ward had seen him look at the band. Ward did not say anything about it. At the gate, going in, Ward said that his left foot was still a clerk's foot and he could do the half step a thousand more times before breakfast tomorrow, and Emeric said he would, and did.



All that month the K Discovery went out at night.

Low. Never higher than the tops of the trees on the marsh edge, and only over water, down at the bottom of the reach, at the hours when the town was asleep and the marsh was empty but for birds. A man's height. Then two. Then the height of the shed's roof, with Tess's dials reading every stone and Tess's book filling and Fletcher at the bow helm watching the black water go down and away under the glass. A little higher each week. She went forward on her main stone and back on her drums, slid sideways on her channels, turned on the spot, stopped with her fins. She did everything she would ever do except climb. That would come. He did not mean to hurry it, and Ward and Tess and Fletcher had all, separately and without consulting one another, told him that if he hurried it they would get off.



Ferra came on the steamboat on the last evening of June.

It was not a trip for anything. That was the point. Somebody Came went down to the coast house every week now with the barony's paper and the soap and the spirit, and came back with salt and copper and the post, and every week it went at dawn and came back at dusk; and on the last Thursday of June, when the boat was due back up the river at sunset, Ferra had said at the table, without looking up, that she had never once in her life been on the river at sunset with nothing to count, and that she would like to know what it was like. And she had looked up, at him, briefly, and back down.

So they went down at noon in the boat with the salt casks, and came back up at evening, the two of them, at the rail on the after deck, with Fletcher at the wheel behind them in the little wheelhouse and the engine thumping steadily under their feet.

The sun went down over the marsh on their left. The river went from brown to copper to the colour of the K Discovery's dome, and then to grey, and the marsh went dark on either side and the birds went over in long lines going home, and the air came off the water and got cold, the way it did on the river in June, all at once, the minute the sun was gone.

Ferra had not brought a shawl. She had been on the river at noon, with sums. Nobody had told her what it did at sunset.

She did not say she was cold. She stood at the rail beside him with her arms folded and her shoulders up and watched the river. He could feel the cold on her; he could feel it the way he felt everything now, without trying, the small hard warm note of her at his side gone tight and drawn in on itself. He did not say anything. He unbuttoned his coat.

She looked at the coat. Then at him.

Then she turned, and stepped in front of him, between him and the rail, with her back against his chest, and took the two edges of his open coat in her hands and pulled them round in front of her and held them there, closed, over her folded arms, so that she was inside his coat with him, and the rail in front of both of them, and the river.

He put his arms round the outside of the coat, over her hands.

He ran hot. He had always run hot; since the downs, since the road to court in the cold with her behind him on the horse, he had known how to run hotter, a little, low, without a glyph, without anybody seeing. He did not need to now. She was warm in a breath. He felt her shoulders come down. He felt her lean back.

Nobody talked.

The boat went up the river in the dusk and then the dark, with the engine thumping and the wake going out behind them in two long grey lines and the first lamps of Kaldo coming up ahead on the wall, small and white, one, then eight, then all of them. The stars came out over the marsh. A heron got up out of the reeds on the left bank and went off low over the water, very slowly, like a man in no hurry to get home. An hour, near enough, by the stars. They did not say anything the whole time. He did not want to. He did not think she did. Her hair was under his chin and smelled of the soap works and very faintly, underneath, of ink.

Behind them, in the wheelhouse, Fletcher steered.

He steered with great concentration. He steered as though the river were full of rocks it had never had, and as though the channel he had known for forty years might move at any moment if he took his eyes off it. He did not look at the after deck once. When the boat came up under the east bridge and into the lamplight from the wall, he rang the bell for the slip two full minutes before he needed to, very loudly, three times.

Ferra did not move until the third ring. Then she let go of the edges of his coat, and stepped out from inside it, and stood at the rail on her own, and put her hair back with both hands where it had come loose. She did not look at him. She looked at the slip coming up out of the dark.

"That's what it's like," she said.

And went down the gangway first, with her chin up, past Fletcher's wheelhouse, and did not look at Fletcher either, who was looking at the slip as though he had never seen a slip before in his life.
