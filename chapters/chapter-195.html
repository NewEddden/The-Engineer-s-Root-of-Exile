Chapter 195 - Paxton's Yard

Farris had never been further west than Kaldo's west farms, and he had not wanted to go.

He had said so, once, to Hollis, on the night Hollis named him at the table, walking back along the lake road in the dark with the lamps of the island on the water beside them. He was past fifty. He had farmed the same ground since he was a boy, and the same ground his father had farmed, and when the prince came and cut it into nine divisions and made him carry dung to fields that had never had dung carried to them, he had done it and grumbled and been proved wrong in a year, and had said so, and had never been proved wrong since about anything that grew. He knew every field on the lake farms by the way it smelled in March. He did not know one field in Milford.

"Then you'll learn them," Hollis had said. "The way you learned ours. By walking them in the wet."

That was all Hollis had said, and it was enough, and here he was.

The train came into the Milford siding in the middle of the morning, and stopped with a long hiss, and Farris sat on the van's bench with the tin tube between his boots and did not get up until Colm banged on the van's side from the wagon behind to tell him they had arrived, in case he had not noticed.

The siding was a long platform of packed cinder and a shed and a water tower on legs for the engines, and a wooden stock pen at the end of it that nobody had put anything in yet, set down on the flat a mile outside the market town with nothing round it but grass. The grass went away on every side as far as Farris could see. He had never seen so much grass in one place. It was grey with last year's growth still, and green under it, and the wind went over it in long slow waves the way it went over the lake, and there were cattle out on it in the distance, dark specks, a great many of them.

There was a man on the platform. He was broad, and he had a cheerful face, and he was waiting for them with his hands in his coat pockets and his hat pushed back, and he came down the platform to the van door before Farris had got his bag out.

"Dunmore," he said, and put out his hand. "I'm the steward's factor. You'll be the prince's farmer."

"Farris."

"Farris." Dunmore shook his hand as though he were weighing it. "I sold your prince his first cows, you know, in his first year. At a yard just the other side of the town there. He paid my price without haggling, which nobody does, and I heard after that somebody in Kaldo had checked it against three years of our own sale books first." He grinned. "I'd have liked to hire whoever that was." He looked past Farris at the wagons, at Colm sitting on the pipe, and at the engine, which was blowing off steam at the water tower. "Will it take beasts?"

"What?"

"The line. The engine." Dunmore nodded at it. "Will it take beasts? Live. In wagons. To Kaldo." He said it as though he had been waiting a long time to ask somebody. "Because if it will, I've a hundred head I'd like in Kaldo's market by Saturday, and it takes my drovers five days on the road, and they lose weight every mile of it, and so do the drovers."

Farris looked at the engine for a while. He had never thought about it. He thought about it now, the way he would have thought about whether a field would take barley.

"Wants a wagon with sides," he said. "And a man in it with them."

"That's what I thought." Dunmore looked very pleased. "That's exactly what I thought. I've been telling the steward so since the harvest." He took Farris's bag out of his hand before Farris could stop him. "Come on. He's waiting for you. He's been waiting since the letter came, and he's been walking about the house for a week reading it."

Paxton's house was on the edge of the market town, a long low stone house with a yard in front of it and a row of cattle sheds down one side, and it smelled of cattle, which Farris found he did not mind. Paxton met them at the door. He was a quiet grey man, older than Farris by a few years, with a cattleman's hands and a cattleman's way of standing, and he looked at Farris the way Farris would have looked at a bullock he was thinking of buying, and said good morning, and took them in.

He had cleared his table. It was a long table, scrubbed, in a room with a fire and a window on the yard, and there was nothing on it but an inkstand and a lamp, unlit.

"Show me," Paxton said.

Farris took the cap off the tube and drew the plan out and unrolled it on the table, and Colm, who had come in behind him with clay still on his boots from the wagon, put the inkstand on one corner and the lamp on another, and his own two hands on the other two.

Paxton stood over it for a long time without touching it.

Farris watched him. He had thought about this all the way out on the train, sitting on the van's bench with the tube between his boots. He had thought about what a man would look for first, in a plan for his own barony drawn by somebody else's prince, and he had decided it would be the thing he was most afraid of losing. And a cattleman would be afraid of losing his cattle.

He was right. Paxton's eyes went over the whole of it once, quickly, the way a man's eyes go over a letter to find his own name in it. And then they went to the high flats, and stopped there.

The high flats were paddocks now, on the plan. Great squares, fenced, with troughs in every one and wind pumps at the far wells, and in the margin, in the prince's small hand, the herd written against them. So many head to the paddock. So many paddocks. And the rule, beside it, underlined: Moved on before the grass is eaten to the root.

Farris saw Paxton read the figure. He saw him read it again. Then Paxton looked along the plan to the benches, to the yards drawn at the five steadings, roofed, with the turnip clamps and the clover ricks drawn beside them, and read the figure in the margin there. So many head. Kept. Through the winter. Fed.

He had expected to find his herd cut back. Farris could see that he had. He had spent a week walking about his house with the letter, waiting to be told that his grass was going under the plough and his cattle were going to the slaughter yards to make room for somebody else's corn, because that was what a man from a grain barony would want to do with a cattle barony, and Paxton had lived next to Kaldo for twenty years and knew what Kaldo was.

The herd was not cut. There were more of them on the sheet.

And they were not killed in the autumn. That was the other figure, and Farris watched Paxton find it: the autumn kill, which Milford had made every year since there had been a Milford, every beast that could not be carried through the winter on hay killed before the grass went, and salted, and sold at whatever the autumn market would give for it, which was never much, because every barony in the south was killing at the same time. On the plan the autumn kill was a line in the margin with the figure beside it, and the figure was small. Only the beasts that were meant to be killed. The rest kept. Fed on turnips and clover in the yards, and bedded in straw, and out on the grass again in the spring, heavier than they had gone in.

Paxton went quiet.

He had been quiet already. This was a different quiet. Farris did not know him well enough to read it, and he knew he did not, and he did not try. He only saw that Paxton's hand, which had been at his side, came up and rested on the edge of the table beside the plan, and stayed there, flat, the way a man's hand rests on the rail of a pen with a beast in it that he has not yet decided about.

Nobody said anything for a while. The fire settled in the grate.

"Sit down," Paxton said at last. "Both of you. Dunmore, the ale."

He had his grievance ready. Farris had known he would. Everybody in Kaldo knew about Paxton's grievance, because Paxton had carried it to the long-room table himself in a box and gone home with it unanswered, or answered in a way he did not like, which was the same thing to him.

"Thirty-one men," Paxton said, "on my rolls, who owe. You'll know about them."

"I know about them."

"Then you'll know I can't hold them. Your prince's notice took the law away. A man who owes me four years' rent can put his children in a handcart tomorrow and walk to Kaldo, and some have, and I can't stop him." Paxton turned his ale cup on the table. "And now you come to me with a plan that wants hands. A great many hands. For drains and fences and pipe and ploughing and yards and I don't know what. And the hands it wants are the same hands that owe me, and can walk."

Farris had thought about this on the train too. He had not had to think very hard. The answer was in the plan, in the prince's hand, in the margin beside the list of hands, and Ferra had read it out at the long-room table the night before they left and he had listened.

"The farm pays a wage," he said. "Every man who works on it. Drains, fences, pipe, whatever. A wage, the same as the works in Kaldo pays." He put one finger on the margin. "And by the barony's order, if the man owes, the wage goes against what he owes first. Before he sees it. A Copper a week, or two, or what's fair, stopped and paid to you."

Paxton looked at the finger, and at the margin.

"So the debt gets paid," Farris said. "Instead of walking off down the road to the coast. The man stays because there's work and a wage, not because he's held. And you get your money back off him while he's digging your drains." He took his finger off. "Slower than you'd like. But you get it."

Paxton was quiet again. Then he asked the only question he asked all afternoon.

"Who keeps the book?"

"Ferra."

Farris saw it land. He did not know why it landed, not all of it. He knew that Paxton had sat across the long-room table from Ferra at least twice, once over a contract and once over his box of rolls, and that whatever had happened at those tables, Paxton had come away from them knowing what Ferra's book was. Paxton's hand came off the edge of the table.

"Then it'll be paid," he said. "To the Copper."

That was the end of the debts.

The bone was harder, because the bone was not a grievance. It was a point.

Farris knew about the bone. Hollis had told him on the lake road, walking home, with a kind of grim enjoyment. Milford had heaps of it behind its slaughter yards, years of it, that nobody had any use for but the dogs, and two autumns ago Paxton had written to Kaldo offering it crushed, by the ton, carted to the Kaldo road, at seven Copper the hundredweight, which was near three times what it was worth anywhere in the south. And Kaldo had bought it, at his price, as much as it could pay for, because the lake farms needed it and the beds had taken the dung. Every Copper over the bone's worth was a line Paxton was writing against the notice. Everybody at the long-room table had known it. Kaldo had paid it anyway.

"The bone," Farris said.

Paxton waited.

"It stays here." Farris put his finger on the plan again, on the slaughter yards behind the market town, and the heaps drawn behind them, and the line from the heaps to a crushing mill on the leg, and from the mill out to the benches. "On Milford ground. Crushed here and spread here. On your wheat. Kaldo won't be buying it." He took his finger off. "You'll want it all yourself."

Paxton looked at the line from the heaps to the benches for a long time.

Farris watched him understand it. It took a while, because there were two things in it and they pulled against each other. He had lost his point. The price he had set to make it would never be paid again, because there was nobody to pay it to; Kaldo had stopped buying, and nobody else in the south wanted Milford's bone at any price. And in the same sentence, the bone that had been a heap behind his slaughter yards for twenty years, that he had tried to sell to a neighbour at three times its worth to make a point about a law, was going onto his own fields. His own wheat. On a farm on his own barony that would feed four baronies and that he would steward.

He had lost his point and gained a farm, in the same sentence.

He did not say anything about either. He picked up his ale cup and drank from it and put it down, and looked out of the window at his yard, where Dunmore was standing by the cattle sheds with his hands in his pockets, looking at the engine's smoke going up on the flat a mile off.

"You'll want a man who knows the flats," Paxton said, "for the paddocks. There's one I'd send you to. He won't like you."

The man was a drover called Aske, and he was seventy, and he had walked the Milford flats behind cattle for sixty of those years, and he did not like Farris.

He did not like the plan either. Farris unrolled it for him on the tailboard of a cart at Aske's own gate the next morning, with Aske's dogs lying in the grass watching them, and Aske looked at the benches drawn into fields and the bottoms drawn into meadow and the flats drawn into paddocks, with fences, and he spat into the grass and said that a plough was a knife in the grass. He said it the way a man says a thing his father said.

"Sixty years I've walked this," Aske said. "And my father before me. The grass was here before the plough and it'll be here after. You cut it and you've killed it. You fence it and you've killed the drove."

Farris did not argue with him. He had argued with men like Aske for twenty years on the lake farms, and he had been a man like Aske himself the winter the prince came and told him to carry dung, and he knew that nothing anybody said had ever changed his mind. What had changed his mind was a field.

"Walk down with me," he said.

"Where?"

"The bottoms. By the first ford."

Aske looked at him for a while. Then he whistled up the older of his dogs and came.

It had rained in the night, and the bottoms by the first ford were wet, and they walked down into them from the bench's edge with the water coming over their boots. Farris did not say anything on the way. He walked to where the plan had the dark stain drawn, round the ford, where the drove road came down to the water, and stopped there, and stood in it.

It was as the plan had drawn it. The ground round the ford was trodden hard, grey, a clay like a potter's, from sixty years of cattle standing at the water in the winter, and the rain stood on it in pools that did not go down because there was nowhere for them to go. Nothing grew on it but a thin sour moss. Along the drove road going away up the bench the grass was thin, and bare in a wide pale track where the herds had walked it three and four abreast, and on both sides of the track, where they had not walked, the grass was thick.

Farris put his heel into the trodden ground and it did not give. He put it into the grass beside the track, and it went in to the ankle, soft, black.

He did not say anything. He stood there and let Aske look at it.

Aske looked at it for a long time. His dog went and drank at the ford and came back. The rain began again, lightly.

"That's the herd," Aske said at last. "That's where they stand. It's always been like that."

"Aye."

"It's the winter does it. When they're down here for the water."

"Aye," Farris said. "And if they had water in a trough on the flats, they wouldn't be down here in the winter standing in it. And if they were moved on before they'd eaten the flats to the root, they'd not have to come down here for the grass either." He looked at the thin track going up the bench. "It's not the plough that kills grass. It's standing on it."

Aske did not answer. He looked at the ford, and at the trodden ground, and at the thick grass either side of the drove road, for a long time.

"One paddock," he said.

Farris waited.

"One. On the high flats, where I choose. Your fence, your trough, your moving on." Aske spat again, but not with much conviction. "And when it's killed my grass, you'll pull your fence out yourself, and I'll watch you."

"Done," said Farris.

Aske did not shake on it. He whistled to his dog and walked back up the bench in the rain, and Farris watched him go, and knew that he had not been convinced, and knew that it did not matter. He had not been convinced either, the first year. He had carried the dung anyway, to show the prince he was wrong, and the field had shown him instead.

Colm had the clay tested by the third day.

He had gone off on his own the first morning, while Farris was at Paxton's table, with a spade and a bucket, and dug in six places along the leg's banks below the benches, and carried the buckets back to the siding shed and made test tiles out of each by hand on the shed's floor and fired them in a hole in the ground with a fire of straw. Four were good. One was very good: a fat grey clay from a cut bank a mile below the siding that fired hard and rang when he tapped it, and did not warp.

He built the kiln there. Not a big one. A beehive of brick, off the drawing Bram had given him for the kiln at Kaldo in the first year, which Colm had built three times since and could build in his sleep, with the siding's engine bringing the brick out from Kaldo in the next week's wagons. He had local men for it inside a day. Paxton sent him six, and Dunmore found him four more, and Farris found him three of the thirty-one, men with debts and no work, who came to the siding on the third morning with their hats in their hands and asked whether it was true that the farm paid a wage.

Colm put them to work. And on the first afternoon, before a single tile had been made, he did the thing Farris had seen Bram do at Kaldo in the first year, the thing he had heard every man at the works talk about since.

He turned a gauge ring.

He did it on the siding shed's bench, with a scrap of iron plate off the wagons and a file and the master he had brought from Kaldo in his bag: a ring of hardened iron, the bore of a drain tile, cut true. He filed the new ring to the master while the thirteen of them stood round and watched, slowly, a stroke at a time, trying it on the master every few strokes, until it sat on the master's face and there was no light between them anywhere. Then he held it up.

"Every tile you make," he said, "goes through this. If it doesn't go through, it's not a tile, it's a pot, and it goes back in the pit. If it goes through loose, same. Nobody's eye is good enough. Mine isn't. The ring's is." He put it on a nail over the bench, where every man would pass it going to the pit and coming back. "That's the rule. There's no other rule."

One of the three men with debts, a young one, looked at the ring on its nail for a long time.

"Who made the first one?" he said.

"The master smith at Kaldo," Colm said. "Off a rule cut in iron and bolted to the castle yard, that nobody's ever allowed to change. And this one off his." He picked up a spade. "Now dig."

Farris walked the forty-fourth square in the second week.

He had seen it on the plan: the one sheet in the south-west corner with nothing on it but its four crosses and their heights, and To be walked across it in the prince's hand. He walked it. It took him a day. It was reed, most of it, low wet ground where the leg spread out and slowed before it went on to the sea, full of birds, with a heron that got up in front of him three times and went away complaining, and in the middle of it, on a low island of firmer ground, the ruin of a steading nobody had lived in for fifty years. He drew it on a sheet in pencil as well as he could, which was not well, with the crosses at its corners, and wrote the reed's edge where he had walked it, and sent it back to Kaldo on the Saturday wagon. It came back the next week pinned into its place on a copy of the plan, in ink, with a line under it in Ferra's hand: Walked by F.

It was in the third week that he noticed the thing he had been not noticing since he got off the train.

The prince had not come.

He had not come to the siding to see them off the train. He had not come to Paxton's table. He had not come to the ford, or to Colm's kiln, or to the square with the heron in it. He had not written, except once, a short letter to Farris in his own hand, that said the plan was theirs now and he would read the farm book when Ferra had it, and nothing else.

At first Farris had thought it was because the prince was busy, which he was; everybody knew what the prince was, in Kaldo. Then he had thought it was because the prince did not care much about farms, which was not true, and he knew it was not true, because he had carried dung for the man.

He worked it out on the tailboard of the cart at Aske's gate, on the morning they set the posts for the one paddock, with the plan unrolled under his hands for the fortieth time and a stone on each corner.

Everything on it was a thing he could do. Or Colm could, or a Milford man could learn. There was not a line on the plan that needed the prince to come and stand at it and say how. Not the engine houses, which Bram's men would build and Colm would run and any man could stoke with a fork. Not the fences, not the drains, not the ring of turnips and clover and dung going round in the corner of the first sheet. Not the book. The prince had drawn every line of it himself, on his knees on a floor, for a month. And he had drawn it so that he would never have to come.

Farris sat on the tailboard with his hands on the plan and thought about that for a while. He did not know what to think about it. He found, after a while, that he did not mind it. It was the way you would draw a thing if you meant it to last longer than you did.

They broke the first sod on the first bench on the last day of the month.

It was a field at the bench's upper end, below where the first tower would stand, a field of old grass that had not been cut since Paxton's father gave up the plough. There were two oxen on the plough, because a horse would have fought the old turf and an ox would not, and a Milford man at the oxen's heads, one of Paxton's six, and at the handles, because nobody else would take them the first time, Farris.

The plough was new. It had come out on the second week's wagons from Kaldo, with its share cut from the rail works' plate and ground bright, and its mouldboard curved in one long sweep of steel that Jorrin had bent on a former off the prince's drawing. Farris had looked at it for a long time in the siding shed. He had ploughed with wood and with an iron-shod wooden share all his life. He had never put his hands on a plough that was steel from the point to the heel.

There were more people at the field's edge than he had expected. Colm, with clay on his arms from the kiln. Dunmore, with his hat pushed back. A dozen of the kiln men and the drain men, leaning on their spades. Paxton, on his horse, at the far corner of the field, a little way off from everybody, the way he had sat his horse by the Tarrants' cross in the thaw.

And Aske. Farris had not asked him. He was there anyway, at the field's gate, with his dog, leaning on the gate with his arms folded on the top bar, and his face said that he had come to watch a man make a fool of himself.

Farris put the share in at the headland, and set his hands on the handles, and nodded to the Milford man at the oxen's heads.

The oxen leaned into the yoke.

The share went in. He felt it go in, through the handles, into his arms: the point catching the old turf and biting and going down, and the steel going under the roots, where no plough had been in fifty years, and the whole of the plough taking the weight of the ground and going forward with it. And the mouldboard lifted the cut, and turned it.

It came over in one piece. That was what he would remember. Not in clods, the way old turf came up off a wooden share, torn and broken. In one long ribbon, black, the width of the share, lifting off the ground and turning over in the air along the whole curve of the steel and lying down again beside the furrow upside down, with the grey grass under it and the black earth on top, shining wet in the sun. And behind it another, as the oxen walked, and another, the ribbon going on and on without a break down the length of the field, turning over, black and shining, like a thing being unrolled.

The smell of it came up off the furrow. Fifty years of grass and roots and rain. Farris breathed it in and kept walking.

At the far headland he turned the oxen and looked back up the field.

One furrow, straight, the length of the field. One long black ribbon lying beside it in the grey grass, unbroken from the gate to where he stood.

At the gate, Aske had not moved. He was leaning on the top bar with his arms folded, looking down the furrow. His face had not changed. But he was looking at it, all of it, from the gate to the far headland, and he went on looking at it for a long time after Farris had turned the oxen and started back.
