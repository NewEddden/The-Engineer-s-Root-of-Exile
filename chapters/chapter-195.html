Chapter 195 - Go Home, Emmy

The Duvrenne beach was black for a mile.

Not the sand. The sand was pale, as pale as the Isle's; but laid over it all along the shore under the low dunes, in hollows and pools and long dark tongues running down toward the tide, was the burning water, coming up out of the ground by itself the way Rowena had said it did, and lying there in the morning sun with a rainbow on it and a stink coming off it that reached the ship at anchor half a mile out. Gulls stood round the pools and did not go in them. Two miles along the coast to the east, on a rise behind the beach, a walled town sat in the sun with its smoke going straight up: Duvrenne town, with its harbor on the far side of it, which Fletcher had said they would not go near.

There were people on the beach. A dozen, with buckets on poles and long wooden scoops, skimming the pools; and a line of casks at the top of the beach by a hut, and a mule standing in the shafts of a sledge, and a man sitting in the hut's doorway on a stool with a book on his knee, who did not get up when the ship's boat came in through the surf with Cade at the oars, and Saban in the stern in his good coat, and Emeric beside him in a plain one with the hood up.

The man in the doorway was the beach-reeve. He took the duke's tenth on everything that went off the beach, Saban said, and he had a price written on a board nailed to the hut, in Duvrenne's letters and Randillia's both, so that nobody could say afterward they had not seen it. Saban read the board. Then he sat down on an upturned cask beside the reeve's stool and talked to him for a quarter of an hour about the weather and the price of fish and the reeve's back, which was bad, and Emeric stood at the edge of the hut's shade with his hood up and listened and did not say anything at all; and at the end of the quarter of an hour Saban bought five casks of the morning's skimming at the price on the board, and paid the tenth on top of it, in Randillian silver, counted out on the reeve's book.

"Paper," said Emeric.

Saban looked at him. The reeve looked up.

"A receipt," said Emeric. "In your own hand. The date. The beach's name. Five casks, the price, the tenth, and your mark." He looked at the reeve. "Two copies. One for us and one for your book."

The reeve considered him from under the brim of his hat. He took in the plain coat and the hood, and at the boots, which were not a fisherman's boots, and at Saban, who smiled at him pleasantly and did not explain.

Then he wrote it. Twice. In a slow square hand, with his tongue in the corner of his mouth, on two leaves torn from the back of his book, and put his mark on both, and gave one to Saban and kept the other, and went back to watching the beach.

The skimmers rolled the five casks onto the sledge, and the mule took them down across the sand to the water's edge with a man at its head, sliding on the sledge's oak runners over the soft sand where a wheel would have sunk to the hub, and they went into the boat one by one, heavy and slopping and smelling of eggs, with the sea coming over the gunwale. Cade rowed them out through the surf. Nobody on the beach looked at the ship lying half a mile out with her stones dark and her deck closed, except as a boat; a rich one, strange, a long bronze boat with a dome.

Rowena was at the rail when they came up the side.

She had been there the whole time, Fletcher said, in a plain coat and a hood of her own, with the long glass from the bow cockpit; and she had kept to the deck, as she had said she would, and not set foot even on the boat's thwart, though Cade had offered. She watched the casks come up on the tackle and go down the hatch into the stores. Then she looked at Saban, who was folding the reeve's leaf into his pocketbook, and at Emeric.

"You asked for paper," she said.

"Yes."

"Two copies. With the date, and the beach's name, and his mark." She had heard it, through the glass or across the water or on Saban's face. "On a beach where every man in that town buys the stuff by the bucket and nobody's ever asked for a receipt in his life."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because if anybody ever asks where Kaldo got its oil," said Emeric, "I want to be able to put a piece of paper on the table with a Duvrenne reeve's mark on it and say: there. Bought, on a beach, at the posted price, with the duke's tenth paid. Five casks." He looked at the hatch. "Lawfully."

Rowena was quiet.

"That," she said, "is how a smuggler talks."

She went down into the saloon. He stood at the rail with the reeve's receipt in Saban's pocketbook beside him and the black beach a mile long in the sun, and did not answer her, because there was no answer that was not either a lie or the island.



They lay off Duvrenne until dark, and lifted, and went back across Randillia under the Band in five hours, and lay the rest of the night in the lee of the goat island off the inlet mouth; and at first light on the seventh day they ran up the inlet on her foils for the last time.

Ferra was on the quay.

She was at the foot of the harbor house steps in her coat with the book, where he had left her, as if she had not moved; Imani beside her, and Vantic a step behind; and the grey knight, and a dozen of the knights, and a crowd, because the bell in the harbor tower had been ringing again. Fletcher brought her in on the motor. Emeric came up the side onto the quay and Ferra looked at him, once, quickly, all over, the way she looked down a column for the figure that was wrong; and found nothing, and looked away.

"Did it go well?" she said.

"Yes."

"Good." She did not ask what had gone well. She turned to Rowena, who had come up the side behind him, and gave her the briefest bow Emeric had ever seen anybody give a Sovereign. "Your Grace. Your clerk signed."

Rowena stopped on the quay.

"The day you left," said Ferra. "The afternoon. A coaster from the south with grain wanted her dues released so she could sail on the evening tide, and the harbor-reeve's man brought the paper up the stair to the standing room, as he always does; and you weren't there." She opened the book. "So your clerk read it, and read the list you left him, and found harbor dues on it, and signed. For the Sovereign, in her absence. Under his own seal." She turned the book so that Rowena could see. "I watched him do it. I was at the end of the table writing letters. He was so frightened his hand shook, and he signed anyway, and the harbor-reeve's man took the paper down the stair, and the coaster sailed on the evening tide." She closed the book. "Nothing broke."

Rowena stood on her quay and looked at Ferra.

"He's nineteen," she said. "He's the harbor-reeve's grandson. I chose him because he was the only clerk in the dome who'd ever argued with me." She was quiet. "He signed."

"He signed," said Ferra. "And then he went and was sick in the passage, and came back, and signed two more." She put the book under her arm. "I wrote it down. I thought you'd want to know it happened. Not that it would." She looked at Emeric, briefly. "That's the line."



Rowena saw them off at noon, on the quay, at the foot of the ship's gangway.

Not at the dome, and not in the standing room, and not in her solar; on the quay, in the sun, in front of her knights and the crowd and the grey knight, with the K Discovery lying at the stones behind him and Fletcher in the bow cockpit with the motor warm and Cade at the lines. It was a Sovereign's farewell to a foreign prince, and she gave it as one. She thanked the Holder of Kaldo for his visit and his gift and the terms, in the voice of the high table, cold and correct, and the crowd listened, and the herald stood by with his staff. The Kaldo party went up the gangway one by one behind him, Saban and Vantic and Maren and Orrin and Vesk, Imani and Ferra, until there was only Ward, at his shoulder, where Ward always was.

Then she stepped forward, close, and spoke low, so that the crowd could not hear it and the knights could not and the grey knight, three paces off, could not; only Emeric, and Ward beside him.

"In the yard," said Rowena. "At the turn."

He did not say anything.

"Your weight went." She did not look at Ward. She did not need to; she knew he was there, and she was saying it in front of him on purpose. "At the moment my blade met yours. Gone. As if I'd struck at a leaf. And then back, at the end of the turn, more of it than you've any right to, all at once, and something at your heels, small, that I felt go past like a moth in a dark room." Her face was perfectly still. "Your suit didn't do that. The suit was doing something else. That was you."

The gulls went over the quay.

"Amara couldn't do that," said Rowena. "I knew her better than anybody alive, and she couldn't do that. Nobody I've crossed blades with in forty years could." She looked at him. "I don't know what it is. I don't want to be told, any more than I wanted to be told who the wine was for." She paused. "Hide it. From everybody. Your father, your brothers, the Church, every mage in Randillia with a sense in his head, every knight who's ever put his blade on yours. Everybody but me." She glanced, at last, at Ward. "And him. He knows. I can see he knows. Good."

"Yes," said Ward.

"Then it's three of us." Rowena stepped back. She was the Sovereign again, at once, on her own quay in front of her own people; and only the scar at the side of her face that pulled when she spoke said she had said anything at all. "I keep things," she said. "Ask anybody on this coast."

It was more than three. He did not say so.

She looked at him a moment longer.

"Go home, Emmy," said Rowena.



They went down the inlet on her foils in the early afternoon, in the sun, with the spray going off her in two white sheets and the terraces going by on either side green and brown and bare-vined; and an hour below the city, coming round a long bend under the western cliffs, they met a convoy coming up.

Fletcher saw them first and took her off the foils, so as not to swamp anybody. Five ships. Not galleys; merchantmen, tall, under sail, beating up the inlet against the land breeze in a long line one behind the other, with their sails grey and patched and their hulls black with a long sea, and flags at their mastheads that Emeric did not know. A foreign cut to their bows. No soldiers on their decks, no rams, no guns; men at the braces, and a boy in the rigging of the second waving at the strange bronze boat as she went past them on her hull with her stones dark, and a man at the rail of the leading ship with a long glass to his eye, watching her go.

Ward looked at them through the iris port with his eyes narrowed.

"Traders," said Fletcher. "From over the bay. Or further." He looked at the flags. "Further, I'd say. That's a long sea on their hulls."

"Not a war convoy," said Emeric.

"No."

He watched them go past, and up the inlet behind, five ships in a line under the cliffs with their patched sails; and found that he did not mind them at all, which surprised him a little. He had spent seven years minding every ship he did not know.

It was Zale. He would not know it until his brother told him. His brother was on the leading ship's deck with the long glass to his eye, coming home to his mother from somewhere past the edge of the map, and they passed each other in the inlet by the length of two ships, and the bend took the convoy before Emeric had finished looking.

They lifted at dusk, out past the inlet mouth, out of sight of anything but the sea.



Fletcher saw the front from three miles up.

It lay along the whole of the east, across their road home, from the sea to as far north as anybody could see: a wall. Cloud, solid, going up from a dark base on the sea to a top so high that the last of the sun was still on it, red, long after it had gone from everything else; and inside it, in the dark under the red, lightning. Not a flash. A flickering, continuous, from one end of the wall to the other, like the lamps of a city seen through a window in a fair, going on and off and on without a pause.

Fletcher watched it with his hands still on the stick, and did not speak for a while.

"No," he said.

Emeric had known he would say it.

"Not over," said Fletcher. "Look at the top of it. That's higher than your pencil tick. That's higher than anything." He pointed at the lightning without taking his hand off the stick. "Not through. Ambrose's strap or no strap. There's ice in that, at this height, and ice is weight on the matrix, and lightning in the ice." He looked at the wall. "And not under, in the dark, on the sea, with a swell under that I can't see and a wind coming out of the bottom of it that'd lay a ship like this over on her side."

"Then we wait."

"Then we wait," said Fletcher. "It's going east. They always go east. It'll be over Kaldo by midnight and past it by dawn." He settled in his seat. "And we'll go home behind it, on the wind it leaves, and that'll be a thing to see."

So they waited. Two miles up over the empty sea, dark, south of the front, in still clear air with the stars out overhead and the wall flickering in the east; the matrix holding her on a notch, the steady clicking its trim keys, which cost almost nothing, because hovering cost almost nothing, and every stone in her was still full from the Band. Fletcher took the first watch and Ward the second. Maren made soup. The ship hung in the sky with twelve souls in her, all night, waiting for the weather to blow past, like a coaster in the lee of a headland.

He told them about the island after supper.

Both of them. He had decided on the voyage north that he would, and he had decided on the beach at Duvrenne that he would do it tonight, before Kaldo, before anybody else, in the saloon with the door shut; because term two was term two, and because he had told Ward on the deck at Emberfell and had seen Ferra see the door shut and say nothing.

He told them all of it. The lake. The dome under the sand and the gully at its edge and the oil in the gully, light, nobody's; the tree, and the white sap, and what it was. The cliffs and the beach of grey flakes, and the flake in his pouch, which he put on the long table between them and did not explain, and Imani picked up and turned over and put down, and did not ask. The yellow lamp on Kesrath's tower. And what he had decided, in the gully, with his fingers wet: that it was the Crown's land, and his father's; that taking it would be theft; that if Kesrath ever saw it, there would be ships in the strait; and that he would come back and take it anyway, later, in secret, when Kaldo had no other way.

Imani listened with her chin on her hand.

Ferra listened with the book closed under her hand and her face perfectly composed, and did not move at all, and did not take her eyes off his face; and when he had finished, she did not say anything for so long that Imani looked at her.

"You went ashore," said Ferra, "on the Crown's land."

"Yes."

"On the island. The one two crowns have been killing each other over since before either of us was born. In the middle of it. In a suit. In the dark." She did not raise her voice. She never raised it. "With Kesrath's tower in sight."

"Yes."

"And you planned it." Her hand on the book did not move. "At breakfast. On the fifth day. You had the face on, and you went down to the ship with Ward afterward and shut the door for an hour. I saw you shut it." She was quiet. "You'd decided the night before, hadn't you. To go and look. When Fletcher put his chart on the harbor house table to show Rowena the run, and you saw the island lying on the way."

He did not answer, which was an answer.

"And I stayed in Emberfell," said Ferra. "So that I'd have clean hands. So that whatever you were doing, the chancellor of Kaldo had been at the Sovereign's table writing letters for two days in front of thirty people, and could say so. I sat at the end of her table for two days and wrote eleven letters about wool and harbor dues so that I'd have clean hands." She looked at the book. "And every one of those letters is true. And every one of them is a lie now, because it's an alibi, and I didn't know I was writing one."

"Ferra."

"You didn't overrule me," said Ferra. "That's what I mind. If you'd told me at breakfast and I'd said no and you'd gone anyway, I'd have written it in the book and we'd have had it out at the long table and it'd be over by now. Said so. Overruled." She opened the book, at last, and looked at a page without reading it. "You didn't ask me. You decided it with Ward behind a shut door and put me on a quay with a book to make it clean." She closed it again. "That's worse. You know it's worse."

"Yes."

"Don't say yes like that."

"Like what?"

"Like it costs you something." She stood up. "It's supposed to cost you something. That was the fourth term. You don't get to make it easy on yourself by agreeing with me." She put the book under her arm. "I'm going to bed."

She went. The saloon door shut behind her, not hard. Ferra did not shut doors hard. She shut them exactly, so that they would not need shutting again.

Imani sat at the long table and looked at the door, and then at him.

"She's right," said Imani.

"I know."

"You should have told us. Both of us. At breakfast." She picked up the grey flake off the table again and turned it in her fingers. "I'd not have minded the island. I'd have told you to take a cork jacket and come back. She'd have minded. And then she'd have helped you do it properly." She put the flake down. "Now she can't help. She can only mind."

"Yes."

"Stop saying yes," said Imani, and got up, and went after Ferra.



The heater in the women's cabin failed off at the fourth hour of the night.

It did exactly what it had been made to do. Bram's thermostat, a little strip of brass and iron riveted together, which bent with heat and lifted the fire stone off its seat when the cabin was warm enough and let it back when the cabin was cold, had bent once too often in the long night and cracked across its rivet; and a cracked strip did not let the stone back down. It failed off. Never on; that was the rule for every stone in the ship that made heat, so that nothing could ever cook a sleeping man in his bunk. Cade found it with a lamp, crouched at the iron box at the foot of the berths, and held it up between his finger and thumb.

"It's done what it's meant to," he said. "Failed safe." He looked at it. "There's no spare strip. Bram never made a spare. I'll make one in the morning off a hinge, if I can find brass." He looked at the two women, standing in their nightclothes in the passage in blankets with their breath showing. "It'll be cold by the hour. Two miles up. It's cold already."

It was. Emeric could see it on the cabin's pane, the frost coming in at the edges in ferns.

"The dome," said Imani.

Ferra looked at her.

"The cabin aft. His, on Fletcher's drawings, and nobody's on yours. It's the warmest room in the ship, it's got the thickest felt, it's next to the main stone, and the bed's wide." Imani pulled her blanket tighter. "And he's a stove. I watched the frost not come on the port beside him on the climb." She looked at Emeric. "You can sleep in the saloon."

"I'll sleep in the saloon," said Emeric.

"No you won't," said Imani. "The saloon floor's cold too and you're the only thing in this ship that's warm. I didn't mean it." She turned to Ferra. "The three of us. That bed. His cloak over the blankets. Or you can lie in that cabin and shiver till dawn and be cross with him from four feet of ice instead of from beside him." She waited. "I know which I'd rather."

"No," said Ferra.

"Don't be stupid."

"I'm not being stupid. I'm being cold." Ferra's teeth were very nearly going. "It's a different thing."

"You'll be cold and stupid," said Imani, "and in the morning you'll have a chill, and I'll have to treat it, and I'll be cross with you, and then there'll be three of us cross and only one of us warm." She took Ferra's arm in its blanket, firmly, the way she would have taken a patient's who did not want to go to the bed by the window. "Come on. You can be angry with him lying down."

Ferra looked at Emeric. Her face, in the lamplight, was white with cold and perfectly composed, and her eyes were not composed at all.

"I'm still angry," she said.

"I know."

"I'm going to be angry in your bed."

"I know that too."

"Good," said Ferra, and went aft.



The dome cabin was warm.

It had been his on Fletcher's drawings, and his for exactly one night of the trials; then Ferra had written the manifest and put him in the first cabin with Ward, because, she said, the holder of four baronies slept where his captain could hear him breathe, and the dome had been the chart room ever since. It was the only warm room in the ship, he thought afterward; the dome's felt was twice as thick as anywhere else, because it was nearest the main stone, and the main stone was full and humming faintly in its housing behind the bulkhead even with the ship on a hover. The bed was the one Fletcher had built into the dome's curve, wide, low, with a let-down desk above its foot that nobody had ever let down; and he put his cloak over the blankets, the heavy one, for the cold, and got in in his shirt in the middle of it, and they got in on either side.

Imani on his left. Ferra on his right, with her back to him, on her side, as near the edge as the bed would let her be, with her blanket pulled up round her ears and a hand's width of cold between her shoulder blades and his arm.

Nobody said anything.

Imani lay on her back for a while beside him with her hands folded on the cloak, looking at the curve of the dome a yard above their faces, where the electric bulb in its cage had been turned out and only the faint grey of the frosted port came in; and then she turned on her side toward him and put her cold feet against his shin, without apology, and he jumped, and she said "Stove," in a satisfied voice; and was asleep. All at once. Between one breath and the next, the way she slept in the ward on a chair in a corner between two bad nights, as if sleep were a door she knew where to find in the dark.

Ferra did not sleep.

He could feel her not sleeping, a hand's width away. She lay with her back to him, very straight, with her blanket up round her ears, and her breathing very even and very controlled, the breathing of somebody who is lying awake on purpose and will not be seen to. The cold came in at the edges of the cloak. He could feel it on her side; she was the one at the edge.

After a long time she moved back. Not to him. A little. Half the hand's width, as if she had shifted in her sleep, though she was not asleep.

After a longer time she moved again, and her back was against his arm.

He did not move. He lay with his arm where it was and felt her back against it through her nightdress and her blanket, cold at first, and then less cold, and then warm. She did not say anything. She did not turn round. But her breathing changed, after a while, and slowed; and then, in the dark, with a long sigh that she would not have let him hear if she had been awake to stop it, she turned over, the whole of her, toward him, and put her face against his chest in the open neck of his shirt, and her cold hand flat on his ribs, and was still.

He felt her breath on his skin. He felt her counting.

Not aloud. He could feel her lips move against his chest, very slightly, over and over; she had her ear on his heart, and she was counting it, the way Imani counted his wrist. He did not know whether she knew she was doing it. He felt her get to some number, and lose it, and start again, and lose it again, slower; and then not start again.

She was asleep.



He did not sleep.

He had not expected to. He lay in the middle of the dome's bed under his own cloak two miles up over the sea in the dark with the frost growing on the port and the main stone humming behind the felt, and the two of them asleep on him, and did not sleep at all, the whole of the rest of the night.

They were not on either side of him any more. That had lasted about a quarter of an hour. They were on him. Imani had come in from the left as soon as she was properly asleep, the way water comes into a hollow, and lay half across his chest now with her head in the hollow of his shoulder and her arm flung over him to the other side, where it had found Ferra and stayed there, on Ferra's hip, out of the way of nothing; and one of her knees was over his, heavy and warm, and her hair was everywhere, across his mouth, across his throat, across the cloak, a great dark soft weight of it that smelled of the ward and something warmer under it, and every time he breathed in he breathed some of it. Ferra was on his right, against his side, all of her, from his shoulder to his ankle, with her face in his shirt and her hand on his ribs and her feet, which had been ice, tucked between his calves where it was warmest, and her red hair coming down out of its night plait across his arm in the grey light from the port.

The heat was enormous. All three of them under the cloak in the felt-lined dome; his heat, which never stopped, and theirs, and the blankets holding all of it in, until the air under the cloak was like the air in Rowena's bath house and he could feel sweat starting at the back of his neck where Imani's hair lay on it. Neither of them minded. Neither of them was awake to mind. They had come to the heat the way cats come to a hearth, and settled in it, and stayed.

He lay very still.

He thought about everything. That was the trouble. He lay on his back in the dark with two women knotted round him and thought about all of it at once, the way a mill runs when the sluice is open and nobody is at the gate: the island, and the gully, and his fingers wet with oil; the tower's lamp; Ferra's face at the long table saying you didn't ask me; Rowena on the quay, Hide it, everybody but me; the flake in his pouch, and a beach of grey, and Sela's fingers on her collar in the snow; the reeve's receipt in Saban's pocketbook; Imani's hand on Ferra's hip, which Ferra would never in a hundred years have let anybody put there awake, and which she had covered, in her sleep, with her own; the berth in the women's cabin, and the felt that was not a wall; the plan. The plan, and Ferra's column, and its four wrong guesses, and the shape of the thing he had not told either of them that he had been building in his head since the goat island, piece by piece, the way he built a machine before he drew it.

And under all of it, the simplest thing in the world.

They were here. Both of them. Warm and asleep and wound round him like the vines on Emberfell's walls, with their breath on his skin. He could feel the whole length of Ferra against his side, every breath of her, the soft weight of her where she pressed into him; he could feel Imani's knee over his and the curve of her hip under his hand where it had come to rest on her without his deciding, and the slow heavy rise and fall of her across his chest. He could turn his head an inch and his mouth would be in Ferra's hair. He could move his hand an inch and Imani would wake, and look at him in the dark, and know, and not mind.

He could wake them both. Now. In the dark, two miles up, in the warmest room in the ship, with the felt twice as thick as anywhere else. It would take one word. Less than a word.

He did not.

He lay there with that knowledge in the dark like a coal in his hand, and held it, and did not drop it and did not close his fingers on it; and the only thing between him and the word was himself. Not the felt. Not Ward. Not Ferra's anger, which she had taken to sleep with her and would wake up with, he was fairly sure, and would hand back to him in the morning precisely, so that it would not need handing again. Himself. A promise he had made on a berth to two women that he had not quite said out loud, and a plan in his head that was better than this, and the fact that she was angry and had come to his bed anyway, and that a man did not take what was offered in her sleep by a woman who had told him three hours ago that he did not get to make it easy on himself.

So he did not. He lay, and held it, and did not.

It was the hardest night's work he had done since the forge.

Imani sighed in her sleep and moved her knee higher. He shut his eyes. He opened them again. Ferra murmured something into his shirt that might have been a figure. Somewhere aft, through the felt, the main stone hummed. Somewhere above, very faint, Ward's boots crossed the top cockpit's floor and stopped, and crossed back. The frost on the port grew its ferns. He counted the rivets in the dome's curve above his face by the grey light, there were forty-one, and counted them again, and got forty-three, and gave up. He thought about the press for Emberfell, and how many sorts of type it would need, and lost count of that too. He thought about the thermostat strip, and how a strip that failed off was a good strip, and that he would tell Bram so, and that Bram would want to make a better one, and that he would want to help, and how a better one would be bimetal of a different pair, perhaps, and then Imani's hair moved across his mouth and he lost that as well.

Toward dawn he was so restless he could have got up and walked round the ship twice, and could not move an inch without waking one of them; and lay there, rigid, wide awake, burning, with his eyes on the grey port, waiting for it to go less grey.

It did, eventually.



Imani woke first, as she had slept first.

She woke all at once, the way she had gone to sleep, and lifted her head off his shoulder and looked at him from a hand's breadth away in the grey light; with her hair everywhere and a crease down her cheek from his shirt and her eyes clear and rested and entirely awake. She studied him.

"You haven't slept," she said.

"No."

"At all."

"No."

She looked at his eyes, and his mouth, and the side of his neck, and took his wrist off her own hip, where it had been all night, and put two fingers on the inside of it below the band, and counted, and frowned.

"You look like a man who's been up three nights in the ward," she said. "Your pulse is a hare's." She let go of his wrist. "I'm prescribing sleep. Today. All of it. As soon as we're down." She sat up under the cloak and stretched, enormously, with her arms over her head, and yawned, and pushed her hair off her face with both hands. "I slept beautifully," she said, with a satisfaction that was very nearly cruel. "I've not slept like that in a year."

Ferra woke.

She woke slowly. She lay with her face in his shirt for a while, not moving, in the way of somebody who knows exactly where she is and is deciding whether to admit it; and then she lifted her head, and looked at him, and at the cloak, and at Imani sitting up beside him stretching, and at her own hand flat on his ribs, which she took away.

She sat up. She put her hair back, what was left of the plait. Her face was pink from sleep and the heat, and rested, and very composed.

"What hour is it?" she said.

He told her.

She looked at the port, at the grey going gold at its edges, and the frost on it melting from the top in the heat of the room. Then she reached over him, without asking, to the chart shelf where his book lay, and took his pencil, and wrote on the inside of her own wrist, where she sometimes wrote a figure when there was no paper: the hour. And under it, after a moment: 9.

"Nine hours," said Ferra. She looked at it. "I slept nine hours." She said it as if it were one more thing to hold against him. Then she looked at his face, properly, for the first time, in the light. "You look terrible."

"Thank you."

"You do. You look like a man who's been dug up." She gave him back the pencil. "Did you sleep at all?"

"He didn't," said Imani. "I've prescribed it."

Ferra looked at him a breath longer. Something went across her face, quickly, and was gone, and she did not let him see what it was.

"I'm still angry," she said.

"I know."

"I'll be angry till Kaldo. Possibly longer." She got out of the bed, carefully, over Imani, and stood on the cold floor in her nightdress with her blanket round her shoulders. "I'll tell you when I'm not." She went to the door; and at the door, with her hand on the latch, she stopped. "The year turned," she said, without looking round. "In the night. While we were up here. Nobody noticed." She looked at her wrist. "I've written that down too."

She went out. Imani lay back down on the pillow, on her side, and looked at him, and laughed, very quietly, through her nose.

"Sleep," she said.

He got up instead and went up the sealed stair to the top cockpit, because he could not lie there one minute longer, and found Ward at the stick with the dawn coming up gold in the glass in front of him over the sea, and the front gone. Gone east, as Fletcher had said, in the night; nothing left of it in the whole of the sky but a long low bank of grey on the eastern horizon going away, and the air behind it washed and clean and enormous and empty, and a wind in it out of the west that Emeric could feel in the ship's hull, pushing.

Ward looked at him over his shoulder.

"Sleep well, Highness?" said Ward.

Emeric sat down in the second seat and did not answer.

Ward looked back at the sky. He did not say anything else at all, very loudly, all the way home.



She went home behind the storm on its wind, and it was the fastest anybody had ever gone anywhere.

Fletcher took her up into the Band's foot and opened every neck and the main stone with it, and the westerly that the front had left behind it took hold of her from astern and threw her. He timed it off Linus's marks, coming the other way: the Chalk, the long white cliff, under the bow, and the light on Skerrow Head; Emeric with Ottoline's chronometer open on his knee in its rings, and his eyes so heavy he had to look at the dial twice.

"Five minutes and two seconds," he said.

Fletcher looked at him.

"Thirty-one miles." Emeric did the sum, slowly, and did it again, and wrote it on Linus's card under the westbound run, in pencil, in a hand that wandered. "Three hundred and sixty-nine miles in the hour. Over the ground."

Fletcher looked at the sea going by far below, which went by the way it always went by from high up, slowly, as if nothing were happening at all.

"Nothing's ever gone that fast," he said.

"No. Nothing has."

Fletcher thought about it. "I'd like that written down somewhere a bit more permanent than a card," he said.

"Ferra will write it down."

"Ferra's not speaking to you."

"She'll write it down anyway," said Emeric, and found he was smiling, for no reason, with his eyes shut.

Vantic wrote her letter on the way. She sat at the long table in the saloon with her travelling desk open and her own book beside it, and wrote her month's report to the House in her small upright hand, as she had written it every month since she came to Kaldo; and when Emeric came down the stair past the table for water she did not cover it, and he did not look, and saw anyway, because it was upside down in front of him and he could not help it, the way a man cannot help reading a sign. The voyage to Emberfell. The Holder's offer of grain, declined; the Sovereign's harbor dues, high. Terms agreed for sulphur and a lease and a lane, which touch nothing the House reads. Return by sea.

Nothing else. Not the Band. Not Duvrenne's beach. Not the island. Not a word about two days that had gone north across the kingdom in the dark, from one end of the page to the other.

She sanded it and folded it.

"The House has no view," said Vantic, to the letter, and sealed it.



They came down on the sea off the river mouth in the middle of the morning, and ran home up the river on her foils in the sun, through the channels and past the marsh and the dock quarter, with the snow still lying on the river wall from midwinter and the town's chimneys smoking; and Fletcher took her off her foils at the shed and brought her the last hundred yards in on the motor, at a walking pace, and set her down on her blocks on the slip so gently that nobody aboard felt it.

The river doors were open. Bram was on the slip.

Tess was not. Emeric had expected her on the slip in her chair with her book, as she had been when they left, and she was not there; and he came down the ladder and stood on the slip in the cold, which was Kaldo's cold, sharp and clean after Emberfell's eggs and vines, and looked up the slope toward the works.

Osric was at the works door.

Not on the slip, where the garrison always was when the ship came in. Not at the quay. Up the slope, at the works' side door, the one Tess used, because it had the ramp; standing beside it with his back to the wall and his hands behind him, in his coat, in the snow, looking at nothing in particular, in the manner of a man who happens to be standing by a door and has been standing there for some time.

Emeric stood and watched him.

"How long," he said to Ward, who had come down the ladder behind him, "has he been doing that?"

Ward looked up the slope at Osric by the door.

"Since the fire drills," said Ward.

Emeric said nothing. Not to Ward, and not, when Osric saw them on the slip and came down the slope a few minutes later with the face of a man who had just remembered he was a garrison knight, to Osric. He said good morning, and Osric said good morning, Highness, welcome home, and asked how Emberfell had been, and did not look up the slope once while he asked it.



Bram had the bung out of the first cask before the second was off the tackle.

He knelt on the slip in his apron in the snow with the cask on its side in front of him and the bung in his hand, and looked into the hole, and smelled it, and made a face; and then he put two fingers in, and brought them out black and shining and thin, and rubbed them together, and smelled them again. He held them up to the cold sun.

Ferra came down the ladder behind the cask with her book under her arm. Bram looked up at her from where he knelt, the whole of her, boots to hair, the way he went over a casting for cracks.

"Father," said Ferra.

"Mm," said Bram, and looked back at his fingers; and she went up the slope toward the works side door, where Tess would be.

"What is it?" he said, to Emeric.

"Oil," said Emeric. "From the ground. Duvrenne's. They skim it off their beaches."

Bram rubbed his fingers. "It's thin."

"It's thin now. Boil it, and it comes apart. The lightest part goes off first, and you can burn it in a lamp with no smoke, or in an engine. Then something heavier, for lamps. Then grease. Then what's left, which is pitch." He looked at the cask. "Every axle in Kaldo. Every bearing. The lathe. The converter's blowers. The ship's legs." He was so tired he could hardly see the cask. "Everything that turns."

Bram looked at his fingers, and at the cask, and at the six more coming down the tackle behind it, four from a beach in Duvrenne with a reeve's mark and two with Rowena's seal in their bungs; and then at Emeric, with the long look of a smith who has been handed a new metal and told what it will do and does not believe a word of it yet and wants very badly to find out.

"Then I'll want more than seven," said Bram.

"I know."

"A lot more."

"I know," said Emeric. "I've been thinking about that."

Bram wiped his fingers on his apron and asked about the brooch. Emeric told him that the queen had looked at it across her solar before she looked at anything else in the room, and asked where he had got it; and Bram nodded, once, as if the brooch had done well and he had expected nothing less of it. Then he looked at the bright Emberfell shoulder on the black suit coming down the tackle in its crate, with its card still tied to the catch, and his face did a great many things.

"What happened to my shoulder?" said Bram.



He went up to Armand's room before he slept, because he had said he would.

The old man was in his chair by the fire with the blanket over his knees, as if he had not moved since midwinter eve, and looked up when Emeric came in, and saw his face, and did not ask him to sit down; he sat down anyway, on the stool by the hearth, where he had sat on the last night.

"Well?" said Armand.

"It brightened all the way up," said Emeric. "Slowly. Like he said. And then at four miles it brightened faster. All at once. As if we'd come up through the floor of something." He looked at the fire. "There's a floor. At four miles. And above it, for a mile, the mana's so thick every open stone in the ship filled in the time it takes to drink a cup of tea, and stayed full, and pushed harder for it. And above that, nothing. A lid." He was quiet. "And it felt good. For the first half hour it was the best air I ever breathed. Maren sang. Saban sang with her. And then it made us bleed. All of us, after most of an hour. Headaches and sick stomachs and nosebleeds. And nobody's lips went blue." He looked at Armand. "It wasn't the thin air. It was never the thin air."

Armand sat very still in his chair.

"The bottom of something," he said at last.

"The bottom of something."

The old man turned his face to the fire. He moved his hands on the blanket. His mouth worked once, and stopped.

"Fifty years," said Armand. "I carried his books down the tower stair the week before the letter came, and he told me, and I didn't ask." He shut his eyes. "Thank you. Now go to bed. You look like death. Imani will have my hide if you fall asleep on my hearth."

He went to bed. He slept for fourteen hours. He did not dream about anything at all.
