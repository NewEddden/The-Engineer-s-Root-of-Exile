Chapter 103 - The Hold

The boat was out in the deep water off the east wall, where the river ran fastest and nobody fished, and she showed no light at all.

Emeric did not see her. He felt the skiff come up against something big and wooden that did not move with the water, and heard a rope thrown and caught, and then hands had him under the arms and he was going up a side that scraped his face, and over a rail, and down onto a deck that smelled of tar and wet rope and something sharper under both. Lamp oil. A great deal of it. He knew the smell from the dyehouse, where they burned it in the drying lamps before the stones came.

A hatch opened somewhere in front of him with a groan of old hinges. They put him through it feet first, with no ladder, and let go, and he fell perhaps the height of a man in the dark and came down on his side on boards in water, with his hands bound behind him and no way to break it, and the breath went out of him.

Somebody in the dark near him made a small sound. High, and quickly stopped, the way a child stops a sound it has been told not to make.

Then Rylan came down through the hatch on top of him, the same way, heavier, and rolled off him into the water, and lay still for a breath, and then was moving, on his side, toward the sound.

"Finn," he said. It was barely a word.

"Da?"

Above them, the quick voice laughed, and said that there, now he had seen him, hadn't he, a bargain was a bargain, and the hatch came down.



It was entirely dark.

Not the dark of the river stair, which had had the lamps on the wall above it. Not the dark of a room at night, which had the line of a door under it, or a window's grey. This was the dark of being shut inside a thing, under a deck, in the river, at the new moon, and there was no difference at all between his eyes shut and his eyes open. He tried both.

He lay on his side in an inch of cold bilge with his bound hands behind him and listened.

The river, everywhere, against the hull, close, talking. The anchor rope somewhere forward, creaking as the current leaned on it. Finn breathing, fast and shallow, a little way off. Rylan breathing, near, slow, too slow, the way a man breathes who is making himself. And other breathing: two more, at least, in the hold with them. One somewhere toward the stern, sitting, he thought, by the sound. One nearer, toward where Finn's breathing was, on something higher than the floor.

They had a lantern. He could smell the hot tin of it. Shuttered, shut tight, so that no line of its light showed. They could open it whenever they liked. He could not.

Above, on the deck, feet. Two pairs. And voices, through the boards, the quick one most.

The quick one talked the way some men talk in the dark, to hear that they are still there. Emeric lay in the bilge and listened to all of it, and some of it he would remember for the rest of his life word for word, and most of it he would only remember the shape of.

The oil was in the barrels by the mast, and over the deck and down the hatch combing, and there was more in the forepeak. At the turn of the night, when the town's lamps were the only lights and the barn fire was out and the men were walking home tired, they would set her alight, and get into the skiff, and go. She would burn to the waterline and the river would take what was left. In the morning the town would find the knight's lantern in the water at the river stair, and the water gate unbarred from the inside, and the knight gone, and the prince gone, and the knight's boy gone, and the town would do the sum itself. The knight had taken the prince for money and something had gone wrong on the water. Everybody in Kaldo would spit at the knight's name. His widow would have to leave.

"Marrow says it reads better than a ransom," the quick voice said. "Nobody pays a ransom for a story. Marrow says people believe a story for nothing."

"Quiet," said the other voice.

One word. Flat. Not loud.

The quick voice went quiet.

Emeric lay very still in the water.

He knew that voice. He did not know from where. It had said one word, and the word had gone into him somewhere below thinking and stuck there like a burr, and he could not get at it. A voice that did not say much. A voice he had heard say very little, a long time ago, and had not listened to, because it had said so little.

He could not see. He could not move his hands. He could not have pointed at anything in this hold if he had been able to see it.

Not without you in the room, Armand had said. And then, on the second night, with the stone in his fist and the hall below them full of the knights' notes: two breaths, better.

Armand was not in the room. Nobody was going to find his hand for him if he could not find it himself.

He found the cord on his wrists instead. He pushed against it, hard, until the wet rope bit into the old burn scars on the backs of his wrists and the pain of it came up sharp and bright and very simple, the hardest thing he could feel. He held on to that. Then he let himself out into the dark.



He went thin. Thinner than he had gone in Armand's room. He did not have a fire to warm his face or a chair under him or a voice telling him which way was back. He let it go out of him the way breath goes on a cold morning, and kept letting it, and the cold of the bilge went away somewhere behind him, and the hold came back to him out of the dark.

All at once. The way the walls of a room come back to a man who claps his hands.

It was long and low and narrow, and the ribs of the boat curved up on either side of him close enough to touch, and the deck was a hand above Rylan's head when he sat up. There was a ladder, fixed, at the after end under the hatch. At its foot, on an upturned bucket, a man, sitting, with something across his knees that came back to Emeric as a cold dead length with a hard curve at one end, and nothing in it at all, which was how wood and steel came back. A crossbow. Wound. The man was bored, and cold, and a little afraid of the dark himself.

And forward. On a crate, near the curve of the bow, not three paces from Emeric's feet, a second man. Bigger. Sitting with his elbows on his knees and a knife, a long one, the cold dead length of it lying along the inside of his forearm. And at his feet, within a lunge of the knife, a small bright quick flutter, very frightened, very tired, that was nine years old and had been in this hold for days.

Finn. Alive. Tied, hands and feet, lying on sacks against the curve of the hull.

And Rylan, between Emeric and the boy, on his side in the water, his note drawn so tight now that it sang.

He went up.

Through the deck. It was harder; the planks were thick and tarred and the oil in them was a smear of nothing. On the deck above, two. One by the mast, moving, restless, never still, the quick voice, and his note was the same as his voice, loud and bright and careless and sure of itself. And one aft, by the hatch. Still. Standing. The way a man stands who has stood watches all his life and knows how to stand them.

Emeric reached for him.

And knew him.

He knew him before he knew that he did. It came up in him the way a smell comes back, a smell from a room you were in once, years ago, on a day you have not thought about since, and before you can say what it is your whole body has gone back to that room. A road. A fire, at night, at the edge of the trees, with a ring of tired men round it. A cup of bitter tea coming round. Two men at the fire not lifting their cups. One of them broad and easy. The other one lean and quiet, who had said perhaps ten words since the gates, and had sat on the far side of the fire from Emeric every night on the road south, close enough to touch, and had gone into the trees in the end, boots first, without a sound.

He had not known, then, that he could feel anything. He had felt it anyway. His body had kept it, the whole of it, the note of the man, and kept it for three years in some place below thought, and brought it back now in the dark the way a dog brings back a scent, all at once and certain.

Garrin.

He would know that note anywhere now. In a crowd. In the dark. Across a river.

The hold went wide and grey round him, and the deck, and the river under the hull, all of it, thin, and he did not know which part of it was him.

The cord. The cord on his wrists, biting. He went to it. He went back into the pain of it as if it were a door, and through the door into his hands, and up his arms, and was lying on his side in the bilge in the dark with his heart going and his head split open behind the eyes and water in his mouth.

It had taken longer than two breaths. He did not know how much longer.



He moved his mouth close to Rylan's ear. Rylan's head turned to meet it. Neither of them made a sound in the water.

"Two in here," Emeric breathed. "One at the ladder, on a bucket, with a bow. One on a crate by Finn with a knife. Two on deck." He stopped. "One of them's Garrin."

Rylan did not move.

"I know," he breathed. "He came to my door."

Emeric let that go. There was no time for it.

"I can cast without pointing," he said. "Only at what I can see. I can see now." He felt Rylan not understand, and not ask. "Turn your back to me. Hold still. It'll burn."

Rylan turned in the water, slowly, without a splash, until his bound wrists were a hand's width from Emeric's face.

Emeric could not see them. He could feel them, thin, from the inside of the dark: the hard dead coil of the cord, and inside it the warm of Rylan's wrists, and the tightness. He looked at the cord with the other thing, since there was nothing for his eyes. He put all of himself onto the one place where the cord crossed itself on the inside of Rylan's left wrist, and nowhere else, as he had put a flame on the end of an iron bar across the width of Bram's bay.

A thumb of flame. No more. In a hold that stank of lamp oil.

It came. A bead of yellow, the size of a pea, standing on the cord, and in its tiny light, for the first time since the stair, he saw: the cord, the inside of Rylan's wrist, the grey skin of it already blistering at the edge of the bead, Rylan's fingers spread and perfectly still. Rylan did not make a sound. The cord went black. It parted. Emeric let the flame go before the light of it could reach the men.

It had been there and gone in the time of a breath. The man on the bucket at the ladder shifted, and sniffed, and said something to the man on the crate about the stink of the oil, and the man on the crate told him to shut up.

Rylan's hands were free. He left them where they were, behind him, crossed, as if they were still tied.

"When I go," Emeric breathed, "take the ladder. Then come to me."

He felt Rylan nod.



He had seen men die of the sickness in the first autumn, in Imani's ward, in the old plague house before it was a ward, when there had been nothing to do for them but hold their heads. The last of it took some of them hard. They went stiff, all at once, and then they shook, and their heels drummed on the boards, and their breath went in and would not come out, and the sound it made in their throats was a sound he had not been able to forget in three years of trying.

He made it now.

He threw himself back in the bilge as hard as he could and let his whole body go rigid, and then shook, and drummed his heels on the planks so they boomed through the hold like a fist on a door, and dragged his breath in through his teeth with the sound the dying had made, and let it stop, and dragged it in again.

The man on the crate swore.

The man on the bucket said what is it, what's he doing, and the man on the crate said he's having a fit, isn't he, the prince is having a fit, and the quick voice on the deck above said what's that, what's going on down there, and the flat voice said nothing.

"He's dying," said the man at the ladder. He sounded frightened. "He can't die down here. He's meant to be in the boat when it goes. He said."

The man on the crate came off it.

Emeric felt him come, a heavy warmth getting bigger in the dark, and heard the shutter of the lantern scrape, and a slit of yellow light opened in the hold, and swung, and found him. He let it find him, rigid, shaking, his heels drumming, his eyes rolled back. The man bent over him with the lantern held out in one hand and the knife in the other, peering down, his face a hand above Emeric's knees, to see whether the prince was dying, because the prince was meant to be in the boat when it went.

Everybody leans over, Rylan had said, in Ward's yard, in April. A man who's down is a man everyone stops watching. The one who's beaten him leans over. Nobody can help it.

Emeric brought his legs up.

They had drilled it every morning since April, on the sand behind the guardhouse, in the frost, in the rain: the legs up and round, one over the neck and under the arm and the other locked over the ankle, and the hips up, and the whole of the body behind it, until the man on top of you was held in the vise of your own legs by his own neck and could not get his head out. He got it. He felt the lock close. The lantern fell and rolled and its light went sideways along the bilge and showed him, for a breath, the man's face, close, astonished, going dark.

The man was bigger than he was. Heavier. The man got his feet under him and began to stand, to lift Emeric off the floor with him by his own neck, the way a big man will, and Emeric did the other thing.

He made himself heavy.

Not light. He had made himself light a thousand times behind the shutters, hung a hand above his own floor. This was the other way, down, the same thing reversed, his own weight and then more than his own weight, a sack of iron, an anvil, pulling straight down through his hips and his locked legs into the man's neck. It cost him nothing. On himself it never cost him anything. The man went back down to his knees as though somebody had dropped a beam on him, and then further down, and the sound he was making stopped.

At the ladder there was a short hard sound, like a green branch breaking, and a body going over against the rungs, and a crossbow going off into the hull with a thud that shook the boat.

Emeric held on.

He held on until the man in his legs had been still for a count of fifty, and then for fifty more, because Ward had told him once that a man who lets go too soon gets up with a knife in him. Then he let go, and let his weight come back to his own, and the man slid off him into the bilge and lay with his face in the water, and did not move it out.

Emeric lay on his back in the dark with the lantern guttering on its side beside him and his legs shaking.

He had killed men. He had killed them with fire on the bridge, and at the gate, in the war, from across a river and over a wall, with the whole of the town's defence behind him and the dark and the distance between. He had never killed a man with his own body. He had never felt one stop.

Rylan was beside him. Rylan's hands were on him, in the dark, finding his bound wrists, his face. Then forward, past him, to the boy on the sacks. Emeric heard the cords on Finn being cut with something, the dead man's knife, and heard Finn make the small high sound again, and Rylan say something into his hair that was not words.



On the deck above them, the quick voice said: "What's happening? What's going on down there? Kel? Abbot?"

Nobody in the hold answered him.

Emeric felt them both, through the deck, without trying, the way he would feel them now for the rest of his life. The quick one by the mast, loud and bright and suddenly afraid. And by the hatch, the other. Garrin. Still. He had not moved at all. He was standing exactly where he had stood, by the hatch, and listening, the way a man listens who has heard the thing he expected and is deciding.

He decided.

He moved. Three steps, quick and light, across the deck toward the mast. The quick man's note flared, bright and high, surprised, and there was a sound on the deck above them, small, close to the boards, a sound like a man setting down a heavy sack in a hurry. And the quick man's note went out.

It did not fade. It went out, all at once, the way a wick goes out between a finger and a thumb.

So that nobody would be left to say the name.

Then the hatch slammed. Something heavy dropped across it, a bar, an oar, and was kicked into place.

Then the smell of the oil changed. It went hot and thick and sweet, all through the hold at once.

And through every seam in the deck above them, one after another, from the mast toward the hatch, a thin line of orange light came on.
