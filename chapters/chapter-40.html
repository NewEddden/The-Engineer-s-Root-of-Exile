Chapter 40 - Enough

The granary was finished on the twenty-ninth of June, four days inside the schedule, and Vantic wrote the four days down and said nothing about them at all, which Emeric had learned by then was how the man cheered.

They cut from the twenty-eighth of July and were in by the fourteenth of August, off more than twice the ground the first year had given them, and there was no ceremony about that either, because the second time you do a thing it is work.

What Emeric remembered afterward, out of eighteen days, was three carts abreast on the mill road at six in the morning in a mist, and Halloway holding his second wagon back behind the small ones in the heat exactly as he had every year of his life and every year of his father's, and Tam Hesk calling the loads in a voice that had broken since last August and was now a bad instrument played very well.

And the fifth division running an hour and a half faster than the schedule said it would, because two corners had come out of the ring in March, and Pell's nephew standing at the straightened run watching the water go past his own boots with an expression that Emeric had the sense to look away from.

Ferra brought it to the long room on the twentieth of August with Vantic in the room, because money was going to be decided in it.

She did it in order and she did the order on purpose.

"Seed first," she said. "Every acre of the nine divisions, graded, into the bins, before one grain moves anywhere else. Master Dunnel's clause, and it does not come out for a debt, a covenant, a baron, or a bad week, and I am saying that with the factor sitting there."

"My house does not want your seed," Vantic said. "I have put that in writing twice."

"Then this is the third time and I shall enjoy it more than the first two." She ruled a line. "Winter reserve. Two thousand three hundred and forty people to the next cut, on the ration we ran last winter, with four weeks over it, which is the margin I want and which is two weeks more than you had last year and eleven weeks fewer than the old baron used to keep, and he kept eleven because he could not count."

"Take the four weeks," Emeric said.

"I was not asking."

"Take the six."

Ferra looked up.

"Explain it now rather than at the end," she said, "because if I put six in at the top I have to take it off something at the bottom and I would like to know what I am doing while I do it."

"Because last November I found out what four days of slack in a food store is worth in the middle of an argument about who eats first, and I would like to buy myself a fortnight of not having to be clever." He shrugged. "Take it off my four parts if it has to come off something."

"It comes off the surplus, which is the same thing by a longer road." She wrote it. "Six weeks. Noted, and I am recording that you asked for it and I did not."

"Milford," she said next, and it went quiet.

The barony's four parts of the harvest had been sold in June of the year before, fixed price, a third of it paid in advance, and had then not existed, because they had burned. In November, Emeric had gone over the western border with Dunnel and a page and had asked Paxton for the only thing he could think of to ask for, which was to be allowed to owe it in grain instead of coin, over two harvests instead of one.

Paxton had read the page, and asked one question about the wording of the second clause, and signed it, and had then talked about cattle for two hours.

He had not asked for a penny of interest, or for security, or for a word of thanks, and he had not mentioned the four hundred and ninety men on the hill or the letter about his wife's chest, and Emeric had ridden home understanding perfectly well that the not asking was the largest single item on the whole page.

"Last year's four parts and this year's four parts," Ferra said. "Both at the price set in June of the year before last, which is a good deal under what grain is worth this month, and which I costed on Tuesday and then did not tell you the number for two days because I wanted you to sleep." She said it evenly. "It is a great deal of grain for a small amount of coin. It is also the only reason there was no writ against this barony in April, and I would take the same deal again on Thursday."

"Write the number," Emeric said. "I'll look at it on Friday."

Then the debt.

The first service payment fell due in October and the money for it came off the harvest, and Ferra set it aside as a figure with a line under it and did not discuss it, because it was not a decision.

And then what was left.

She put the last sheet down and let them read it themselves, which was how she delivered anything she thought would be misread if she said it out loud first.

It was a surplus. It was a real one, in a real building, with a lid on it.

It was also, when Emeric had gone down the page twice, roughly what a careful man would call enough and not one grain past it.

"Say it," he said.

"It is enough," Ferra said. "It is the first page I have written in this barony in twelve years that comes out on the right side of nothing, and I have been in this trade long enough to tell you that enough is the most dangerous number there is."

"Go on."

"A shortfall makes men count. Everything this place has, it got because something was missing." She closed the book. "Nobody argues in front of a full bin. They will not argue with me about the ration in November, and they will not argue with you about the second wheel, and by February nobody will remember what the margin was for, and in eleven months we will do the whole of this again with everybody a little more relaxed and a little less exact, and that is how it goes wrong. It does not go wrong in a bad year. In a bad year everybody is awake."

Ward had been at the window with his back to the room for most of it.

"That's not the danger," he said.

"Then say the one you mean," Emeric said.

"Two hundred men came up that hill in August because there was a full building at the top of it." Ward did not turn round. "You've just built a better one."

He went up on the rise on the evening of the twenty-second, on purpose, and made himself stay there.

He had done it by accident the year before, standing in the same place with the whole floodplain gold in front of him and the ring running silver between the divisions, and had let himself have about a quarter of an hour of it before the next thing arrived. He had thought about that quarter of an hour a number of times since, generally at about two in the morning, and had come to the conclusion that a man who can only be happy by accident is going to be happy roughly as often as he is lucky.

So he went up with the specific intention of enjoying it, and gave himself half an hour, and discovered at about eleven minutes that a thing you have scheduled is much harder to feel than a thing that ambushes you, and that this was extremely funny, and sat on the wall laughing at himself in an empty field for a while, and then had a very good twenty minutes indeed.

The light went off the water division by division, west to east, the way it had.

Two divisions had different men on them than last year. One had a hedge with people under it that would be there in a hundred years because he had written it in as a term and not a favour. One had forty acres in the middle of it that were in the field now, on a joining charge paid in days, and the man who owned them had two sons.

He walked back down through the town and Nessa was in her doorway with the light behind her, and Wren, who was six now and had grown about a foot, was sitting on the step doing something violent to a different wooden horse.

"You're the one that made everyone boil the water," Wren said.

"I am."

"You already told me that."

"You already asked me."

Wren considered this, found it to be a fair point, and was clearly annoyed about it.

"Finn sat at a gate all summer," she said, in the tone of a person laying a serious charge. "All summer. And he gets paid and I don't."

"Wren," said Nessa.

"She's right, though," Emeric said. "That is the situation."

Wren narrowed her eyes at him, suspecting sarcasm, found none, and went back to the horse, having decided the matter was now on record.

The letters came in the first week of September, and there were two of them, and then Ward brought the third thing, which was not a letter.

The first was from the house in the capital that dealt in contracts of that kind, the one which had answered him in four lines a year before to observe that a barony recently assaulted by three of its neighbours was not a barony against which one bought a year of future iron. It was rather longer this time. It offered to contract for a season of the foundry's output at a rate Ferra called serious, and it asked, politely, about the availability of grain.

"Nothing has changed about us in their eyes," Ferra said, reading it. "Not one thing. We did not get better. We failed to fail, which they can now see from a distance, and a man in a room in the capital has drawn a line through last September's note and written a new one."

The second came through Saban, from a factor in Duvrenne who had never heard of Kaldo eleven months ago and had wanted his ore paid for before it moved. He was now prepared to extend credit on the season, unasked, on terms Saban described as insulting in the ordinary way rather than in any special way, and had enquired whether Kaldo's landing on the western arm would take a second barge.

Ward came in on the ninth with the third.

"Man at the inn," he said. "Two nights, the fourth and the fifth. Paid in Wayford coin, which isn't a thing by itself, half the coin in that inn is Wayford coin. Bought nothing, sold nothing, met nobody, walked out to the mill road twice."

"And?"

"Asked the miller's boy how many bins were in the new building." Ward's face did not change. "Then asked him what was on the lids."

Emeric sat with that for a moment.

"What did the boy tell him?"

"Everything he knew, which is that there's seven and they've stone lids and his uncle helped carry two of them and the prince cut something into the frames." Ward shrugged very slightly. "He's twelve. He was proud of it. I'd not have him told off for it and I'd rather you didn't either."

"Name?"

"No name. Gone on the sixth, up the Wayford road, on his own horse." Ward went to the door. "That's the whole of it and there's nothing in it and I'd not have brought it to you in June. I'm bringing it in September because in September there's a full building on that rise."

He got the list out that night and left the space empty at the top, and wrote underneath it:

Seed first, reserve second, Milford third at a price set before the fire, the October payment fourth, and what was left after all of that is the surplus, and it is the first surplus this barony has had in twelve years and it is exactly enough. Ferra says enough is the dangerous number because nobody counts in front of a full bin. Ward says the dangerous thing is the bin. They are both right and they are not talking about the same danger and I have spent two days deciding which of them to spend the winter on, which is the wrong question, because it is the sort of question a man asks when he wants permission to only do one of them.

Six weeks of margin instead of four, taken off the surplus, asked for by me and recorded as asked for by me. If it is still sitting there next August I will have been wrong and it will have cost the field money, and I have written that here so that I cannot improve it later.

I lost the drum vote in March using my own arithmetic and I am still slightly sore about it in a way I do not intend to admit to Maren, and the hoeing got done, and the small households kept their sons for five weeks, and I have watched the fifth division all summer trying to work out whether it looks like a field that had its hoeing done. It does. I cannot prove it and it does.

Bern Ostry, Wick, Tulley. The plank is on the wall at four camps and in Ferra's book as a general order under Imani's name, dated the third of May and not the third of June. She wrote nine of those clauses five years ago for a steward who lost the paper. I have been telling myself since June that the thing I gave her was authority. What I actually gave her was somebody who reads.

In October there was one house in the world with an interest in what is inside my granary and it took me eleven years of harvests to buy it. Tonight there are four, and I can name three, and the fourth asked a twelve-year-old what was cut into the lids.
