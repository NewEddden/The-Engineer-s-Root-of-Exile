Chapter 40 - Box

It was Ferra who put the two columns side by side, and she did it because of four words.

"The numbers are correct," she said, at her table, on the second evening of July, reading it back to him as he had told it to her. "That's what he said. In front of all of them."

"Yes."

"Not that he took it. Not that he was sorry, or wasn't. That the numbers were correct." She had the garrison's book open in front of her, the copy she had made of it last summer, and her own book beside it. "That's an answer I'd give. When somebody's asked me the wrong question and I don't mean to help them find the right one."

He had heard it in the yard as a confession. He sat down across from her and heard it again, in her flat voice, and did not like how it sounded.

She had both columns already. She had had them since the summer, in different books, under different headings, because that was where they belonged: Corliss's draws on the garrison's share, small, regular, nine years of them, and in her own sort of the barony's payments by payee, the coffin-maker's block she had shown him last summer, a dozen boxes a year under no family's name. Box, and a sum.

She had never laid one against the other, because nobody had asked her to, and she did not guess past her data.

She laid them now. She ran her pen down the draws and her finger down the boxes, date against date, year after year, and he watched the two columns come together like the two halves of a torn page.

A draw. Within three days, a box. A draw. A box. In the first winter of the plague the boxes came every week, and so did the draws, and the draws got no larger, because it was the same small sum every time, and in the worst month there were five. Every draw had a box after it. Not one box in nine years had come without a draw before it.

And the sum of each draw was the price of a box, to the copper, and a little over.

"The little over," Ferra said, and put her pen on it. "That's a spade's worth of somebody's night. Or bread for whoever dug." She set the pen down, square to the edge of the page, which she did when she had finished. "He wasn't taking it. He was spending it. On them."

Emeric looked at the two columns, joined.

He had held every one of those dates in his own hands, a year ago, over two nights, and checked them four ways. Every fact he had held was true. The garrison's book had told him what had moved and when, and in whose hand. It had never once told him why, and he had stopped asking at the point where the book stopped answering, and gone to Corliss in the yard and asked him to agree with it.

Corliss had agreed with it. And then he had stood there, both times, waiting for the second question.

"I asked him whether he took it," Emeric said. "I never asked what for."

"No," said Ferra, and left it there, and he would have liked it better if she had not.

He went to Cooper's Row that same night, because he did not trust himself to wait until morning and find a reason not to.

The coffin-maker was a lean old man who worked by a single lamp in a shop that smelled of sawdust and pitch, with three plain deal boxes stacked against the back wall and a fourth on the trestles half-finished. He knew who was at his door and did not seem surprised to see him there at that hour. A good many people came to that door at night.

Yes, he said. The knight. For years. Always at night, always in coin, always the plainest box, deal, no handles, and a receipt every time, made out to the garrison: the box, and the sum, and nothing else. The coffin-maker had thought it a strange thing for a man to want from a coffin-maker. He never said who it was for and the coffin-maker never asked, because it was not the coffin-maker's business who was in a box once it had left the shop. The knight carried them away himself. Sometimes with one other man, sometimes with two. In the plague winter he had come so often that the coffin-maker had started keeping one made up against the wall for him, ready, so that he would not have to wait.

"He'd stand there," the old man said, and nodded at the place inside the door where Emeric was standing, "while I counted it. Never once short."

"Does he still come?"

"Still. Not so often, now the plague's gone off." The coffin-maker looked at him. "It's been his own coin since last summer. He told me so, the first time, as if I'd asked."

Emeric stood in the doorway and worked out which week of last summer it would have been, and did not need Ferra to tell him.



Doran came up the hill the next morning with his hat on.

He had come up it once before, in a year, in July, about the rows, and on that occasion he had taken his hat off at the gate. This time he walked through the gate yard and past the petition line and into the hall with it on his head, and stopped in front of Emeric's table, and did not sit down, and did not take it off.

Somebody had told him. Emeric did not ask who.

"I told you," Doran said. "Last summer. That there were dead in this town with no entry and no rite, because the entry came with the fee and they had nobody to pay it. That somebody bought a box. That somebody dug at night." He had not raised his voice, and his hands were folded in front of him, and the knuckles were white. "I told you I had never asked who. I was ashamed of that, and you saw it."

"You did."

"Nine years he carried them. In the plague winter, every week. He bought the boxes out of a garrison's share because there was no other purse in this barony that would have opened for it, and he dug them in with his own hands, and two or three others whose names you will not get from me, at night. And on the mornings after, I walked past the turned earth on my way to the church and did not stop, because no fee had been paid, and I let that be my reason. I never asked him. He never..." Doran stopped, and took a breath. "He could have gone to Wexler for the money. He knew what Wexler would say. He has had you to go to for a year."

He let that sit.

"You have been busy," he said.

He turned round and walked back out through the hall, still with his hat on, and the petition line parted for him without being asked.



Imani did not come up the hill. He went down to her, that evening, and stood at the side door, and did not ask to come in.

She came out into the laundry yard with her sleeves rolled and a pen stuck through the knot of her hair, forgotten there, and looked at him, and he saw that she had known before he opened his mouth, and that she had known a good deal longer than that.

"You knew," he said.

"I washed them." She did not lower her voice, and there were two women at the kettles who could hear every word, and she did not care. "Most of them. The ones he carried. I told you that much at Doran's table, and you wrote it down, and you never asked me who bought the box. I'd have told you if you'd asked me. Maybe."

"Maybe."

"It was his to give. Not mine." She folded her arms. "I kept his name the way I kept the rest of them. In here." She touched her own temple, once. "Five years. Nobody else was keeping them."

"I named him a thief in front of the whole garrison."

"You did. I heard about it before you'd left the yard. Everybody in this town did." She looked at him the way she looked at a wound that somebody had dressed badly and then been proud of. "Do you know what you did in that yard? You didn't ask him. You asked him in front of the whole garrison and half the castle to stand there and tell them he'd been burying the people nobody would pay for, at night, with a spade, like a sexton. You think a man like that was going to say so? In front of them? He'd sooner be a thief. You gave him the choice between those two and he took the one he could live with."

"I gave him the floor."

"You gave him a floor in front of everybody." She took a step toward him. "When I'm wrong, I'm wrong in my own ward, and I put it right there, in front of the same people."

He started to answer.

"No. Listen. When you're wrong, you're wrong in that yard, with your eyes and your father's crown on it, and the whole town's wrong with you. And when you change your mind, they don't."

He opened his mouth to answer her, and found that the answer he had ready was the one he had given Doran in his head all morning, about four confirmations and the books, and that it was not an answer to anything she had said.

So he did not give it.

"You're right," he said instead.

"I know I'm right." She was still angry. She did not stop being angry because he had agreed with her, and he thought more of her for that, not less. "What are you going to do?"

"Say so. In the same yard. To the same men."

"They won't believe you."

"Some of them won't."

"Most of them won't." She unfolded her arms. "Do it anyway."



He found Corliss on the Wednesday, before the yard, in the armory, where he was scouring a mail shirt in a barrel of sand as Bram had said it should be done.

"What was it for?" Emeric said. "The second question. I never asked it."

Corliss went on turning the barrel. The sand hissed inside it.

"You have the books now, Your Highness," he said.

He did not say anything else, and he did not stop turning the barrel, and it became plain that he was not going to do either, and Emeric went out.

He did it on the Thursday, in the garrison yard, to the thirty-two and to the walls above, which were fuller than they had been for the sort.

He kept it short and kept it specific. He said what he had said there the month before, and that it had been wrong. He said what the draws had paid for, every one of them: boxes from the coffin-maker on Cooper's Row, for the dead of this barony who had nobody to pay for them, for nine years, and in the plague's first winter every week. He said that he had held the dates in his hands for a year and never asked what they were for, and that the fault was his and nobody else's. He did not say anything about the four confirmations, because they had been true and they had been beside the point, and saying so would only have sounded like a man reaching for his excuse.

Then he said that the repayment was cancelled, and that what had been taken from Corliss's pay since June would be paid back to him from the barony that week.

Corliss stood in his place in the line and heard it. When it was done he stepped out and inclined his head, exactly as far as he had the month before and not a hair further, and said, "Thank you, Your Highness," clearly, so that the walls could hear it, and stepped back.

It was correct. It was complete. There was nothing in it at all.

The walls emptied slowly. Emeric heard some of what went down the stairs with them, and it was about what Imani had said it would be. The garrison had heard Tuesday's words in June very clearly. It heard Thursday's as a prince tidying up after himself, and by the end of the week the story at the north well was that the knight had been caught with his hand in the box and the prince had found a way to make it go quiet.

Rylan said, that night, that he had worked it out the day before Ferra did.

He said it at the guardhouse table, without looking up, the first thing he had said to Emeric about Corliss since June. He had been beside Emeric the day they found Corliss in the guardhouse yard last summer, and had seen his hands when he reached for his belt.

"Why didn't you say?"

"You didn't ask me either." Rylan went on eating. "You saw them too."

Emeric had. Once last summer, and once in June, in front of the whole garrison. Rylan did not tease him about anything for the rest of that week, or most of the week after.



The burials went into Ferra's book the following Monday.

It was one line, and Emeric wrote the order for it himself, and took it to Hale, and Hale entered it in the daybook without any remark at all, which from Hale was its own remark. From that day, the burial of anybody in the barony who died with nobody to pay for it was the barony's cost. The box, the ground, the digging by daylight, the fee that put the name in the book and the rite that went with the fee. Nobody would have to apply for it, or be assessed, or prove that they were poor enough. Doran would send the account at the end of each month, and the barony would pay it.

Ferra ruled a new column for it. She headed it with one word, the same word that had sat for nine years in the coffin-maker's line under no family's name, because she said it was the right word and she saw no reason to change it.

Box.

On the Tuesday he went down to the exchange in the evening and stood at the back of the room, where he usually stood, while Doran took the letters.

There were twelve men on the benches now, up from seven. The wall-crew man was among them, the one Ferra had shown his number. He had a slate on his knee and a stub of chalk, and he had been copying the same few letters off the sheet on the wall since the start of the hour, over and over, with his tongue between his teeth.

When Doran came down the row to him, he held the slate up.

Doran looked at it. Then he looked at the man, and nodded, and said, quietly, "Read it."

The man looked at his own slate as if it might have changed while he was not watching it. Then he read it, out loud, one letter at a time and then all together, slowly, in a voice that carried further than he meant it to in the quiet room.

It was his name.

Doran said, "Again," and he read it again, faster, and two of the men on the next bench, who had not managed theirs yet, bent their heads back over their own slates as though they had been reminded of something.

Doran stayed beside him after the lesson had moved on. He had spent nine years walking past turned earth with no name over it. He looked down at the slate, at the name in chalk, and then he went on to the next bench without saying anything, and Emeric, at the back of the room, looked up at the high windows and kept looking at them.
