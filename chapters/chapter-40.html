Chapter 40 - What We Do With Them

Doran gave him the church without a single condition.

Emeric had come up the steps with an argument prepared. He had three of them, in order, escalating, and the third one involved the crypt and was not pleasant, and he had been entirely willing to use it.

Doran heard the first sentence, said the nave and the side chapel and the room behind, and asked whether Emeric wanted the benches out or left in.

That was the whole negotiation.

He went away from it mildly off balance and noticed, walking back down, that he had spent two entire lifetimes on the assumption that a man in vestments always wants something. It had never once failed him. It had not failed him in the capital, where it had been true of every churchman he had met, and it had not failed him in the other place either.

Apparently good clerics existed. He turned it over for a while, arrived nowhere, and filed it with the other things that did not fit.

Imani ran the ward and was better at it than he was.

He said so on the second morning, in front of her four assistants, in the plainest words available, because it was true and because it was the cheapest useful thing he could do in that hour and she was going to need those four women to stop glancing at him every time she gave an order.

It worked immediately and completely and he was slightly ashamed of how well it worked.

Petram carried. He had carried for eleven days in the spring and he carried now, stretchers and water and buckets nobody wanted to look into, still under sentence, with no guard on him any more because there was nobody spare to be one.

Rylan worked the same room for four days. Neither of them made anything of it. They passed each other in a doorway perhaps thirty times, and one of them was the man who had put poison in a cup and the other was the man who had gone looking for his cousin, and the whole matter sat there unaddressed in a room full of dying strangers, which is where that kind of thing generally sits.

The room behind the church had a door, and Ward stood outside it for eleven hours.

Critical cases only, one at a time, carried in and carried out. Nobody else inside. Imani, the man on the table, Emeric, and the door shut.

He put a hand on the first one and found the distress the way he had found the bird's, which was not difficult, because it was the loudest thing in there by a very wide margin. He took the edge off it. The man stopped fighting, and then stopped making the noise, and then lay still enough to be worked on.

That was the entire extent of the magic, and he was rigorous with himself about it. He was not healing anybody. He was doing what a physician does with poppy, without the poppy, which was worth a very great deal in a barony that had none and was not worth calling a miracle.

The other thing he did in that room was harder to account for.

It happened on the fourth man, and it happened because he had spent twenty minutes trying to tell Imani where a vessel ran and had failed, and had watched her nod in the particular way that people nod when they are going to go on doing what they were doing.

He had described it three ways. He had drawn it on the back of a ledger sheet with a stub of charcoal. He had used the word behind four times and the word under twice and both of those words were wrong, because the thing had no in front of it and no on top of it, and language built by people who had only ever seen the outside of a body was not going to carry it.

So he put his other hand on her wrist and took her in.

What Imani experienced, she told him a long time afterward, was that the room did not go anywhere. That was the part she could never get anyone to understand and eventually stopped trying to. The room stayed. The table stayed. The man on the table stayed, and the noise of the nave through the wall stayed, and nothing dimmed or faded or dissolved in any of the ways a person might expect from a story.

What happened was that there was suddenly more of it, in a direction she had not previously had, and the inside of the man on the table was in that direction.

She did not scream, which Emeric had been braced for. She went completely rigid for about two seconds and then she leaned in.

He showed her where the vessel ran, with the vessel there, and his hand on it, and nothing between the two of them and the fact of it.

He showed her where the bone had gone, and why the fragment had to come out before anything got closed over the top of it, and she said aye out loud in a room where there was no air and no need for any.

He showed her the closure she had made forty minutes earlier on the previous man, and then a clean one beside it, and said nothing at all in the way of criticism because none whatsoever was required and she was already reaching for it.

He showed her why the thread gets boiled, which she had been doing for a year on his say-so and had never once been given a reason for, and which he could now simply point at.

She learned to suture in about forty minutes. It would have taken eleven weeks in the other direction and at the end of the eleven weeks she would have been doing it his way rather than understanding it.

They came out and the man on the table was still bleeding and there was another one behind the door.

Imani washed her hands and said nothing for a while and then said, "That was not a thing you can do."

"No."

"Right," she said. "Next one."

She was entirely aware that something had happened to her and she was intelligent enough to know it had not been ordinary and she had nine men on the floor outside waiting for that table.

So she filed it, the way a working woman files anything in the middle of a bad week, and she came back to it a long time later and asked him a question about it that he did not enjoy.

Ward stood outside that door for eleven hours and did not ask one thing about any of it, then or ever.

Rylan brought food at the end of the eleventh hour. He came in without knocking, put a bowl and a cup on the sill inside the door, and stood there while Emeric washed his hands in a basin that was already the wrong colour.

"Sit down and eat that."

"There are two more."

"There are two more in an hour," Rylan said. "Sit down."

Emeric sat down and ate it.

Neither of them said anything about Corliss. Not that night, and not at any point in the following fourteen years.

That was where the coldness ended.

The prisoners were the first thing anybody argued with him about.

There were more wounded men than Kaldo had beds, most of them Grayrock, a good number in a state where the question was academic and a good number where it was not, and Emeric gave the order that they went into the same rooms as everybody else and got treated in the order they were going to die in rather than the order of whose they were.

The argument came from four directions inside a day. From the garrison, which he had expected. From two of the venture men, which he had not. And, most uncomfortably, from a woman whose son had come off the flank with a burned hand and who stood in the nave and asked him, reasonably, and at volume, why the man who had burned it was getting linen.

He did not win that argument. He ended it, which is a different thing, and he was aware of the difference and wrote it down that way.

And he did not end it alone, which is what actually settled it.

Imani had been treating them for a day and a half before anybody thought to raise it with her, because it had not occurred to her that there was a question in it. When somebody finally did, she answered in one sentence, without putting down what she was holding and without looking up, and went back to work.

Nobody argued with her. Two and a half years and nine hundred dead had bought her that, and she had never spent any of it before.

Renner's body came off the field intact and did not spoil.

Emeric collected it himself on the second evening, which surprised a number of people and did not surprise Ward. It was face down in the sixth division about three hundred yards from the tower. The horse was two fields away, unhurt, and was eventually given to a carter.

He had it carried up and laid in the crypt and did the work on it himself, and the work was neither difficult nor pleasant and took twenty minutes, and it held.

He was explicit with himself about why, in writing, that night.

He had written the burial law eighteen months earlier for a garrison that was leaving its own dead in a ravine, and he had written it in absolute terms precisely because he had known even then that absolute terms are the only ones that survive a bad week. Every man gets a burial. Every man. There was no clause in it about whose man, and he had not left it out by accident.

Desecrating a body is barbarism. It is barbarism when it is done to a friend and it is barbarism when it is done to the man who came here to kill you, and the only difference between the two cases is how much you want to do it.

He was not going to preside over the second one in order to feel something about the first.

The body was going back to Grayrock. He decided that within the week and then did nothing about it for a considerable time, for reasons that had nothing to do with the body.

Paxton left silently and nobody stopped him.

The order had been given six weeks earlier to four men in a room and had never been written down anywhere. Milford's colours hung back, so Milford's colours were not engaged, not pursued, not counted and not mentioned. A hundred men walked north-west off the river road in good order in the middle of the afternoon and not one crossbow on the flank was turned to follow them.

Sethe did not know that, and was not going to.

Sethe surrendered and there was nothing to it.

He was not brave about it and he was not a coward about it. He had come with forty men on information he had been given, the information had been wrong, and he had watched the arrangement fail in ninety seconds. He put his sword on the ground, gave his name, and asked who he was speaking to.

He intended to be alive tomorrow. Everything about the man, for the whole time he was in Kaldo, was organised around that and nothing else.

Emeric went to see him on the fourth day and stayed about half an hour.

Sethe had the room in the north tower with a window and a fire in it. He was forty-odd, dry, careful with his hands, and had the particular courtesy of a man who has worked out that being pleasant is cheaper than being difficult. He stood up when Emeric came in, which he did not have to do, and he did it without any suggestion that he was doing Emeric a favour.

And then he spent half an hour trying to find out what had happened to him.

He was good at it. Emeric had expected bluster, or resentment, or the sort of aristocratic performance that had filled two hundred receiving lines in another life, and got none of it. What he got was a man buying information, competently, with the only currency he had left, which was conversation.

He asked about the armour first, and was direct about asking. Where the steel came from. Whether it was capital work. Whether there was more of it. He had seen four men in black plate go through a line and he wanted to know what they were and how many there were and whether they could be bought.

He asked about the crossbows second, and asked better questions about them, and was visibly pleased with himself for having noticed that every bolt on the field had come off the same pattern.

He asked, with great care, about the thing that had happened to his mages, and Emeric let that question sit in the room until it went cold.

And in half an hour he did not ask, once, in any form, how the ground had been arranged.

He had walked two hundred men into a ninety-foot lane that had been dug six weeks earlier by farmers with a rope, and he had lost a battle in it, and the only lesson he had taken off that field was that the southern barony had better equipment than expected.

Emeric noticed it at about the twenty-minute mark and then spent the remaining ten deliberately confirming it, and it was the single most useful thing that happened to him that month.

Sethe, for his part, came away having formed a view, and the view was that Emeric of Randillia was an exiled boy who had been sold some good steel by somebody and had got extremely lucky with a piece of wet ground, and that neither of those things would hold twice.

He was civil the entire time. So was Emeric. Neither of them said one true thing to the other in half an hour and both of them enjoyed the exercise more than either would have admitted.

The bill was the cruellest thing Emeric did in three years and he never raised his voice once.

He did not name a ransom. He did not send a figure. He did not send anything that a herald anywhere in the kingdom would have recognised as a communication between two noble houses.

He had Ferra prepare an account.

It was itemised. It was in her handwriting, in the ordinary format she used for a delivery of lime, with the columns ruled and the working shown and a total at the bottom.

Standing crop destroyed, nine hundred and fifty bushels, at the current market rate, which she cited and sourced.

Wages of every man employed on earthworks, by name, by day, at the posted rate.

Treatment and keep of the wounded, and the wages those men would not be earning for the period they could not work, calculated per man.

Cartage of eleven days of stores, given in cart-days.

And the value of the trade Wayford had cancelled without notice, against standing orders, in the four weeks before the march.

The covering line was one sentence and said that Baron Sethe would be released upon settlement of the account.

He was not being ransomed. That was the whole of the point and it was not lost on him for a moment.

A ransom is an honour of a kind. It says a man is worth taking, that his person has a value, that two houses are engaged in a transaction that has rules and precedent and is conducted between equals. Men have been ransomed in that kingdom for four hundred years and there are forms of words for it and a herald who knows them.

A bill says a man is an expense. It says he came onto somebody's land, broke things, and is expected to pay for what he broke, in the same format as a merchant who short-delivered a load of tin.

And every line of it was defensible. That was the part that could not be argued with anywhere, by anybody, and Emeric had made certain of it before it went out. There was no punitive figure in it. There was no multiple. There was nothing on that page a magistrate would have struck, and Keller would have gone through it line by line and passed all of it, and Emeric had actually imagined him doing so while Ferra was ruling the columns.

He was courteous throughout, which made it considerably worse.

Sethe was fed what the keep ate. He was housed properly. Nobody insulted him, nobody gloated, nobody came up the tower stairs to look at him. He was visited twice, was treated with perfect politeness on both occasions, and was told nothing.

He was processed.

Wayford paid inside five weeks. Wayford paid, and Wayford's steward saw the itemisation, because Ferra had not sent a figure, she had sent a page, and a page gets read out in a hall by a clerk who has to say every line of it aloud in front of whoever happens to be standing there.

The line about the trade cancelled without notice was the sixth item of eleven.

Sethe asked him one question on the way out, in the yard, mounted.

"What is it you actually want?"

"I wanted a meeting," Emeric said. "I wrote four times. The last one I paid a merchant to put into your steward's hands and stand there while the man took it."

Sethe did not say anything.

There was a fraction of a second in which something went across his face, and Emeric saw it, and read it as the ordinary discomfort of a man being reminded of a courtesy he had not returned, which is a thing that happens between lords constantly and means nothing at all.

It was not that. It was a man in a yard, on a horse, having just paid for a broken crop by the bushel, discovering that four letters he had read and set aside without much thought were now the most expensive documents he had ever handled.

And working out, in that same fraction of a second and for the first time, precisely how much damage a piece of paper can do if you let it get where it is going.

"I shall remember the hospitality," Sethe said, in a tone that had exactly one meaning.

He gathered the reins and rode out through the Western Gate and over the Left Bridge with two men, and took the river road west, and did not look back at the field.
