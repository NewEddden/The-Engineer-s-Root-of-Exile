Chapter 363 - The Press

Ferra had been costing the list for three weeks and was beginning to take it personally.

It had come to her on the twenty-sixth of June as one page torn out of the back of his notebook, folded twice, with sixteen lines on it and nothing beside any of them but a word or two. Answer Towers. Rider Frames. Missiles, with a line under it. The stone press, restarted. A fusion research reactor, in letters so small she'd had to hold it to the lamp. He had given it to her on the island the morning after the bucket, and said, "Can we afford it?" in the voice of a man asking whether there was any more coffee, and she had looked at him until he went away.

By the end of July it was forty pages. She had them in a box file on the long room table with the shutters open and the noise of the upper ward coming in, a tram bell, somebody's dog, a man selling peaches who was being optimistic about his peaches. Every line on his page had become a sheet of its own, and every sheet had what it needed, and who could make it, and how long, and what it would cost in coin, in mana, in men, in glass, in weeks of the Forge; and then a line at the bottom in her own hand, which was the line that mattered and which most of the sheets didn't have yet.

He came in at eleven with his sleeves rolled and sand still in the seams of his boots from the boat, and stood behind her chair and read over her shoulder, the way she'd told him a hundred times not to.

"You've crossed out the train."

"You put two question marks on it."

"I put two question marks on it because I'm going to take it seriously one day."

"Then take it seriously one day." She moved the sheet out of his reach. "Not in the next nine months, with half the Works on a boat."

He leaned down and put his chin on top of her head, which he knew she hated, and read on. She let him. He smelled of the boat and of the island and very faintly of Ilenne's tank, which everybody on South Sentinel did by now, a mineral sea smell like the inside of a shell.

"The press," he said.

"Yes."

"You've got a bottom line on the press."

"I've re-run it." She found the sheet. It was the sheet she had been carrying round the inside of her head for a week, at meals and in the bath and at three in the morning, and she put it flat on the table and smoothed it with both hands as if it might get up. "Same as every year. A plain fire stone out of the press still costs you three out of the ground. Water, the same. Light, the same. The mines are open and the seams aren't thinning and the Church still sells at the old price because it doesn't want anybody asking why. So for anything you could buy, the answer's no. The press loses. It's a fuel plant in a country full of oil." She put her finger on the next line down. "But you don't want anything you could buy."

"No."

"Bram's whirlpool mine wants a water stone that holds half as much again as any stone of its weight that's ever come out of a seam. So does Armand's tower, for the sense stone in its cap. Nobody digs those up. Nobody sells them. There's nothing to set the cost against, because there's no price." She tapped the line. "So I've written that it's not a cost. It's a part. And the part is made one at a time, for one job, by name, and the press doesn't run a single stone that isn't on that list, and the day somebody suggests running a cart of them for sale, I'm going to use the veto on that person personally."

"Fair."

"I still don't like it."

"I know."

She signed the bottom of the sheet anyway, F., and put it on the pile with the others. Then she sat back against him, because he was there, and let her head fall back on his stomach, and looked up at him upside down.

"Thirty-one of them," she said. "Out of forty. That's how many have a line at the bottom. The rest want a person."

"I've got a person."

"You've got Bram, and Bram's going to be on a boat."

"I know," said Emeric. He was looking at the window. "I've got a person."



Tess was late to the meeting, which she meant to be.

She had come up from the Motion Shop in the chair, because it was the last day of July and it had been a long one, and she'd left the chair at the bottom of the keep stair under Osric's eye and come up the rest on the stick. Seventy-one steps. She counted them every time. She had got them down from eleven minutes to four since the spring, and the fourth minute was the bad one, and she didn't want anybody holding a door for her through it. So she was late. By the time she came along the gallery the long room door was open and they were all in there, and she could hear Cade talking about slips.

She stopped in the doorway to get her breath back without anyone seeing her get it back.

They had the box file open and Ferra's sheets in piles down the table. Bram was in the chair nearest the window, with his forearms on the table and his apron on, because he had come from the foundry and would be going back to it. Cade was standing, with his cap in his hands, which was how she knew he'd already been told something he didn't like. Ambrose had his meter case on his knees, from habit; he took it everywhere, as if somebody might at any moment ask him to measure something. Armand sat by the cold fireplace in his cardigan, looking as though he'd prefer it lit. Ferra had the head of the table. The king had the end of it, with his back to the wall and the page out of his notebook in front of him, the original, folded twice, soft as cloth.

"Two," Cade was saying. "I've got one slip that'll take a seventy-five-metre hull, and hull two's on it till September having her frames looked at."

"Then lay a second," said Emeric.

"A second slip or a second hull?"

"Both. Five and six. A pair, off the same patterns, so they fly as a pair." He didn't look up. "You won't finish them before we sail. I don't want you to. I want them on the slips when we go, and I want you still building after."

Cade turned his cap round once in his hands. "And the little one. Zale's sister."

"Before we sail. Off his ship's patterns. Flies with the convoy, sits on the sea." Emeric did look up then. "And Zale's sixth vein comes before all of it, because it's carrying a letter that has to be at the wall water before the end of the summer, and I've got a promise riding on that letter already that I've broken once."

"The glass is coming down from Orrin's on Monday," said Bram, to the table. "I'll fit it myself."

"Thank you."

Bram grunted. Then he looked up, past everybody, at the door, and saw her there, and didn't say anything, and looked back down. That was all. It was the way he'd looked at her across the dynamo house floor for years when she'd come in quiet and he wanted her to know he'd heard.

She came in. Nobody got up. Ferra pushed the empty chair at the table's corner out with her foot, an inch, and Tess sat down in it and hooked the stick over its back and didn't say thank you, and Ferra didn't look up from the page, and that was exactly right.

"Where are we," said Tess.

They were at the list. Ferra read it. She read it fast, line by line, and after each line she said who, and where she couldn't say who she said nothing, and the nothing sat in the room and got heavier.

The Answer Towers: the orb on the tower, the stasis field round it, a sense stone in the cap that knew a spell from a stone thrown and answered the spell with its own opposite before it landed. Armand for the counters. Bram for the masonry. A grown stone in the cap, from the press. Nobody for after.

The whirlpool mines: a steel drum on a mooring chain in the harbour mouth, a tempered water stone inside it that turned the sea round it on a signal from shore and could pull a hull off its line or hold it, with fins against the spin and a ballast tank and a depth valve so that it kept its station even dead. Bram. A grown stone, from the press. Nobody for after.

The Rider Frames, which Ward wanted. The gyro stabilizer, for the carriers, for when the flywheels came out of the Motion Shop. The Sand Room. The relays. The missiles and the net that went with them, which the king had underlined because he didn't like the word, and Tess still didn't know whether he meant the word or the thing.

"Struck," said Ferra, and read those too. "The jet. Dragons decide."

"That's what it says on the list," said Emeric.

"I know it's what it says. I'm reading it." Ferra went on. "The float field's next step, not this year. The floating train, struck. The denim goes to Abloh, who wants it, and the mill, and isn't anybody's business at this table. Lithium cells and the Jacquard loom go to the Motion Shop to be started and not finished." She stopped at the next one and held the page a little nearer. "A fusion research..."

"Struck," said Emeric.

"What is it?" said Tess.

"Not this year."

"That's not what it is, that's when it is."

"Not this decade," said Emeric, and something in the corner of his mouth said he'd been waiting years for her to ask, and wasn't going to tell her now. "Next line, Ferra."

"The stone press. Restarted." Ferra laid her signed sheet on top of the pile, the one with a part, not a cost on it, and read out the rule, all of it, the one-stone-at-a-time and the by-name and the veto, flatly, so that everybody would have heard it. "For the towers' caps and the mines' hearts. Two to begin with. Nothing else."

"Bram's press," said Armand.

"Bram's press," said Ferra. "On the island. Which Bram's kept oiled for three years and which Bram's going to be a thousand miles from in April." She put the sheet down. "And there it is again. Who."

Nobody spoke.

Tess looked down the table at what was on it. It wasn't a list any more. It was the whole of the next year of her life, and the one after, in Ferra's neat piles, with a space at the bottom of every sheet where a name had to go. She had known it was coming since the twenty-fifth of June, since the pad had chattered on the Motion Shop bench in the middle of the afternoon and she'd read it off the tape: Stand by for a long list. F. She'd stood by. She'd spent a month standing by, and the whole of that month she had known, the way you know which bearing's going to go, whose name it was going to be.

She found she wasn't afraid of it. She noticed that, and put it away to look at later.

"Me," said Tess.

Ferra's pen was over the first sheet. It didn't move yet.

"The press. The towers' stones and the mines'. The relays at this end, with Ambrose; the line room's his anyway, he can put up with me. The gyro, because the flywheels are mine. The Answer Towers after the first two, round the walls and out to the holdings. The cells and the loom." She heard her own voice going on, even, the way it went when she was teaching the logic class and a boy in the second row had got lost and she wasn't going to let him see her slow down for him. "Dagny's old Wayford team, if Dagny goes. Cade for the hulls; I'm not building boats. And the frames I'll look at, but they're Ward's knights who'll be in them, so somebody who stays home and hits things for a living has to tell me what's wrong with them."

"Osric," said Ferra.

"Osric," said Tess, and didn't let her face do anything.

"That's a great deal," said Armand mildly, from the fireplace. "For one woman on a stick."

"I'll be off the stick by spring."

"That's not what I said."

"I know what you said." She looked at him, and he looked back, and he had the grace to look a little ashamed of himself, which on Armand's face, these days, looked like a young man being told off. "It's the same list it would be if I were walking. It'll take the same time. The stick doesn't do the sums."

Ferra wrote. Tess watched her do it, the first sheet and then the second and then on down the piles, the same five letters at the bottom of sheet after sheet, in Ferra's small upright hand, Tess., with a stop after it, and some of them with a line under. It took a long time. Nobody filled it.

Bram had not said a word since the glass. She looked at him when Ferra was about halfway down.

He was already looking at her.

He had his big forearms on the table and his hands loose, and the apron was burnt in the place where it always was, and his face didn't do anything at all. It never did. She had learned to read it at twelve, in the year he took her in, because there had been nothing else to read and she'd needed to know whether she was in trouble. She read it now. He looked at her for about as long as it took Ferra to write her name three times, and then he looked down at his own hands on the table, and turned one of them over, palm up, and back.

That was all. She didn't know what it meant. She thought she probably did.

"Then that's who," said Emeric, at the end of the table, quietly, and didn't make anything of it either. He had the notebook page in his hand. He folded it once more, smaller, and put it in his shirt pocket. "Ferra. What's left without a line?"

"Nine," said Ferra. "And the train."



She went over to the island on the Thursday Hod with Dagny and two of the Wayford men and a crate of quartzite blanks, and the Dust House was exactly as she'd last seen it, which was Bram's doing. The press stood in the middle of the floor on its iron bed with a tarpaulin over it and the screw greased and the gauge glass clean. Someone had swept under it. Someone had swept under it every week for three years, by the look of the broom.

"He's been coming over," said Dagny.

"I know he has."

"He's never once said."

"No," said Tess. "He wouldn't."

They pulled the tarpaulin off. The two Wayford men took the press's head up on the screw a turn at a time, which was the bit she couldn't do any more and wouldn't have done before either, because it was a two-man screw. Dagny laid the bed: a block of the quartzite, the host, fine-grained and grey, that Emeric had settled on years ago after trying everything else in the yard; a scoop of water-stone dust packed into the cup bored in its face, for the seed, to tell the new stone what it was going to be. Then the liquid mana, out of the cold flask in its iron sleeve, measured by Tess's own hand into the press's well. That part she wasn't giving anybody.

The first run at the standard dose would match a stone out of the ground. That was three years ago. This one wanted half as much again, and half as much again cost twice the dose, and past twice a wild stone's hold the host cracked inside as it set and leaked for ever after; she had a cracked one in a tin at home to remind her. So she measured, and checked it, and measured again, and Dagny watched her do it without saying anything, which was Dagny's way of saying a great deal.

"Close it."

The head came down. The screw groaned. The gauge needle climbed and sat. Under the iron, under a great weight of steel and the cold, something began, very slowly, that would take five weeks to finish, and she felt it start with her sense the way you feel a kettle begin to tick before it sings: the mana going into the stone and not coming out.

"Five weeks," said Dagny.

"About."

"And one at a time."

"One at a time, by name, for one job." Tess sat down on the bench by the wall, at last, and put the stick across her knees. "Ferra's veto, if not."

Dagny laughed. Then she went and got the log, and Tess wrote the date in it, and the dose, and the weight of the host and the dust, and under that, Water. For the north mine. Bram's.

She'd brought the other thing over in the same crate, wrapped in sacking. When the log was written she unwrapped it on the bench and looked it over: a wall-sensor stone on a bracket, a relay behind it, and a pen arm on a pivot that rested against a drum of paper turned by clockwork, one turn a day. It was the ugliest thing she had built since the ambulance. Steady mana drew a steady line on the drum. Mana that was being pushed somewhere it didn't want to go made the pen shiver, and the shiver came out on the paper as a fine fringe of chatter that a person could look at a week later, and hold up to the light, and measure.

"What is it?" said Dagny.

"A seismograph."

"A what?"

"His word. For earthquakes, he says. Where he got it from." She shrugged. "A mana seismograph. The crews'll call it the drum by Tuesday." She wound it, and set the pen down on the paper, and watched it draw. It drew a line, quite straight, with a very faint shiver along it from the press across the room. She watched that for a while. "It'll read the press while it's growing. I want to know what it looks like when a stone's about to crack, before it cracks."

The second one, which was its twin and which she had built in the same week, was not going on any bench where Dagny could see it. It went to Kaldo on the Saturday in the same sacking, and the king carried it down himself, into the cellar under the castle and through the second door, and stood it on the shelf in the Forge's stores among the sealed jars and the racked stones that nobody in the city knew were there; and it sat and ticked, and drew its line, and the line was steady.



He started on the fourth of August.

Ferra knew it was the fourth because she was the one who'd signed for the carbon paper.

A ream of it came up from the printing works that Monday morning with a requisition in his hand, for the long room, and nothing else, and she'd signed it the way she signed everything, and thought nothing of it, because the long room went through paper the way a horse went through hay. She thought nothing of the parcel that went the other way either, the same afternoon, to Master Aubin at the Polytechnic: the school's own set of texts for the first engineering year, the ones the copy room hand-copied for every new class, every one of them marked in the margins in his pencil. Aubin's clerk sent back a line to say the copy room would have them done early for once, by the spring, and was there anything in particular. Only to the marks, he had written back. Exactly to the marks.

None of it was strange. Every piece of it was the kind of thing that crossed her desk forty times a week.

What was strange was the mornings.

On the Tuesday he came down to breakfast looking as though he'd been dragged backwards through the dust mill. He sat down at the end of the kitchen table and looked at the bread for a long time before doing anything about it. Imani, who had slept nine hours and been up for one, buttering, looked at him across the table, and then at Ferra, and then back at him, with an expression of deep and unmistakable satisfaction.

"Poor thing," said Imani.

"I'm all right."

"You're not all right. You look like a man who lay awake all night." She bit her toast. "With his eyes open. Thinking about things."

"I slept beautifully," said Ferra, who had. She had gone to sleep with her face in the back of Imani's neck and one hand on his chest and her knee in somewhere it probably shouldn't have been, and she had woken up in the same place, warm right through, with the sun on the wall. "I don't know what you're complaining about."

"I'm not complaining."

"He's not complaining," said Imani. "He's being noble. You can tell by the eyes."

He opened his mouth. Then he shut it, and took the bread, and ate it, and Ferra thought she saw something go across his face that she couldn't have named, and then it was gone. It looked, oddly, like relief.

On the Wednesday it was the same, and on the Thursday. On the Thursday Imani poured his coffee for him without being asked and put two sugars in it, which was a joke, and he drank it, which was a worse one. They made the most of it. It had been one of the good jokes for a long time, the one about him lying awake beside the two of them, too hot and too full of thoughts and too stubborn, and in the morning looking as if he'd been in a fight, and the two of them as fresh as paint. They'd earned it. They didn't see why they should give it up.

It wasn't until the Thursday night, the fourth night, that Ferra woke at the wrong hour and found the bed with only Imani in it.



The lamp was on in the long room. She could see the line of it under the door from the end of the gallery.

It was ten past two by the clock on the landing. She stood there in her nightgown with her feet cold on the boards and thought about it. He'd been up late every night that week, then; not lying awake at all. Up, and dressed, and in the long room with the lamp, and back into bed some time before dawn, slipping in between the two of them so carefully that neither had woken. She'd been teasing a man for a week about a thing he hadn't done, and he'd let her.

She opened the door.

He was at the far end of the table with his back to the wall and the lamp turned low. There was a ledger open in front of him, a long one, cloth-backed, the kind she bought, and he was writing in it fast and small, a full page, the kind of steady close hand he used when he wasn't thinking about his hand at all. There was a sheet of carbon under the page and a second sheet under the carbon. Beside him, closed, was a second ledger the same, new, its spine not yet broken, and a cup with nothing in it.

He looked up. He didn't shut the book. He just stopped writing, with the pen still on the paper, and looked at her.

She came down the room and stopped at the corner of the table. She didn't try to read it. She could have; she read upside down better than most people read the right way up. She kept her eyes on his face.

"Every night since Monday," she said.

"Four."

"You let us think you were lying awake."

"You were enjoying it so much."

She looked at the new one, waiting, and at the one under his hand, already a finger's width fatter at the open edge than when she'd bought it. "What's it called?" she said.

He considered her. He had ink on the side of his second finger and a crease down his cheek, she couldn't think from what, and he looked about as tired as she'd ever seen him look outside a war. And there was something in his face besides, under the tiredness, that she had seen on it a few times over the years, at the start of things, on the nights he couldn't wait for morning to find out if he was right.

"Long division," said Emeric.

She waited.

He didn't add anything. He just went on looking at her with that thing in his face, and she understood that it was for somebody, and that it wasn't for her, and that she was being trusted not to ask.

It annoyed her very much. She would have liked to know. She was the treasury; she was supposed to know where everything in the castle was going, including his nights, and here were two ledgers she hadn't costed, and by the look of the empty one, a good many more to come. But she had sat beside him a long time and she could see the size of what was on the table and the size of the time he had, and she did that sum before she could stop herself, as she did every sum, and didn't like the answer either.

"How long?" she said.

"March."

"Every night?"

"Most."

"You can't."

"I can do about two hours," he said. "I've done the arithmetic."

"I've done it too." She came round the corner of the table and put her hand into his hair at the back, which needed cutting again, and pulled his head against her stomach, through the nightgown, and held it there. He let her. She felt him breathe out, all the way, for what might have been the first time since Monday. "You're king of the whole of Randillia. You've got a boat to build and a convoy to find and a woman to take home and Tess's whole life to hand over before April, and you think you can do two hours of long division every night on top of it, by lamplight, and still be any use to anybody in the morning."

"Yes."

"Come to bed."

"Work first."

She stood very still for a moment, with her fingers in his hair, because she knew exactly whose line that was, and how many times she'd used it on him across this same table with a pen in her hand while he tried to pull her away.

"Oh, that's low," said Ferra.

"I learned it from somebody."

"You learned it from me, and it's mine, and you're not allowed it." She tightened her hand in his hair, not quite gently, and tipped his head back so that he had to look up at her. "Half an hour. Then I'm coming back down here and carrying you up the stairs myself, and I'm Chancellor, and nobody in this castle will say a word about it."

"Half an hour," he agreed.

She kissed him, upside down, and tasted the cold coffee, and let go of his hair.

At the door she stopped, and looked back once. He'd already bent to the page. The lamp was on his face and the pen was moving, fast and small and steady, and under the page the carbon was taking every word of it down onto the second sheet without him having to think about it, and she found that she was standing in the doorway in the cold at a quarter past two in the morning doing the sum again. Two hours a night. Eleven pages, perhaps, if he was quick. From now until March.

She went back to bed and lay down in the warm, behind Imani, and didn't sleep. She lay with her eyes open looking at the line of the window and counting.

At a quarter to three the long room door opened and shut, and he came in on his stocking feet, and stood at the end of the bed for a moment, and then got in, very carefully, on the far side of Imani, who said something into the pillow that might have been cold feet and might have been his name, and pulled him in by the shirt without waking, the way she pulled the blanket.

In the morning he looked dreadful. Imani poured his coffee and put two sugars in it and said, with great tenderness, "Poor thing. Again?"

Ferra, across the table, met his eyes over the bread.

"Again," she said. "He's very noble."
