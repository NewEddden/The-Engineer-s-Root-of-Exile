Chapter 331 - The Pitch Lake

They buried Ade Fenn and Robin Sennet on the morning of the sixteenth of December, on the seaward face of the big dune above the Claim's beach, where the sand was firm enough to hold a side and a man at the head of the grave could see the water both of them had gone into.

It had taken three days to be allowed. Every man and woman in the detachment had written a line on the back of Ottoline's roll card before they went aboard, which had been Fenn's idea: what to do with you, if it came to that. Most had written home. A good many had written Kaldo, which was the same thing said shorter. Fenn's card said where I go in, in capitals that went uphill. Sennet's was blank on the back, because he had been the youngest diver on the Isle and had not believed a word of it; so Rylan had gone on the set on the fourteenth and asked Ferra, by way of the K Amara's clerk and the line room, to find Sennet's mother. The answer came back up the same road on the fifteenth, in Ferra's own voice, read off a slip. With Sergeant Fenn, then, if he'll have him.

"He'll have him," Rylan had said, to the horn, and then had to go outside for a while.

Declan dug it.

He had a spade out of the drum's kit crate before anybody else was up, and when Rylan came over the dune's crown at first light with the other spade on his shoulder, Declan looked at it, and at him, and said, "No," and went on digging. So Rylan sat down on the sand with the spade across his knees and watched.

Sand was bad to dig. It came back in as fast as you threw it out, a long slow hiss down the side, until you had a dish no deeper than your knee. Declan worked it the way he worked a level on a well site, without hurry and without stopping; and when the sides began to run he went back over the crown and came back with four planks off the crate the jack's spares had come in, and shored them. They still had KALDO WORKS stencilled on them, and THIS WAY UP, which in a grave was a joke nobody made.

The battery in the field above the village fired twice while he dug: a deep flat cough from behind the village roofs, the ball's long hum, the thud of it into the gully's far side a mile inland, where there was nobody now because Rylan had moved them all into the pipe trench at midnight. Declan did not look up for either.

Rylan knew why he was digging. Declan had had the high mage in Ivo's glass from noon on the thirteenth, with the round in the magazine, and Rylan had asked for leave at a quarter to four. He knew to the minute when Fenn had gone off the bottom of the jetty ladder after Sennet, because he had written it in the detachment's book himself, in a hand that was not a good hand. So did Declan know it. Neither of them had said one word about it in three days, and neither of them was going to, and Declan was digging.

The new skin on Rylan's right forearm, where the ferry had burned the sleeve off him in August, pulled in the cold when he sat still. He sat still anyway.

By eight it was deep enough, and the divers brought the two of them over the crown. Eleven, in their sea coats, Fenn on a stretcher between four and Sennet between four more, both sewn into the grey canvas off the drum's spares with a sailmaker's needle and palm, neat, the way Fenn had taught them to patch a bag. They lowered Fenn first on two ropes, then Sennet beside him, and drew the ropes out and stood back. Along the dune's top the rest of the detachment who were not on the trench stood in the wind with their caps in their hands, three hundred perhaps. Two of Kesrath's boats lay off the village with their oars up, watching, and nobody looked at them.

Wilf Abery stood at the foot of the grave in a scarf that was not his. His own was at the bottom of the sea off the jetty's end. This one was Cady Lorne's, bright blue, knitted by somebody who had run out of blue at the end and finished in yellow, and on any other morning the whole dune would have had something to say about it. Cady stood beside him and nobody said anything at all.

There was no priest. Fenn had once told a brother of the Light that if God had wanted men to swim He would have given them a better sea. So Rylan stood up off the sand, and found that everybody was looking at him, which he had known they would be.

"He'd want it short," he said. "He talked the whole of October. Stood in that bay to his chest in his drawers and talked, and every one of you swam because he wouldn't shut up till you did." He looked down at the canvas. "He'd want it said he was right about the cards. So he was right about the cards. And Robin Sennet came up at the wrong end of a jetty, and Fenn went for him. That's all. I'll tell the rest at home. Properly."

The divers went first, a handful each, then the long slow line along the dune's top, bending and straightening; and then Declan filled it, alone, as he had dug it. He drove a post of grey driftwood at its head that nobody had seen him bring up from the beach, and knelt by it with his knife and cut the names. His letters were square and careful and a little crooked, like a man who wrote stiffly and had decided to do it anyway.

ADE FENN. ROBIN SENNET. And under them, smaller, KALDO.



The set lived in the still house, on a shelf over the condenser, in the warm.

It was the only roof Kaldo had on the Isle, a stone hut at the gully's head round the still, with the gas burner under it lit off the well's own breath four years ago and never let go out; and since the thirteenth a prison for one. The high mage of Kesrath lay along the back wall on his hurdle, a big grey man with a fine thin nose, both legs splinted to the poles and his back strapped flat with three belts, his hands tied to the frame and a regular on a stool beside him. He did not sleep much. He watched the set.

Breakfast was biscuit, and tea warmed in a tin on a fire stone, which was not fire. Rylan had told the Grayrock captain so on the first night, when the captain asked whether the order covered tea: a fire stone burned nothing. The captain had said it was fire to him and drunk the tea anyway.

The set clicked twice at a quarter to nine, which was the K Amara, and it was Ward.

"The last two towers," said Ward. "Both. Walked out at dawn. Chests cold, hands up, down their own stairs. Nobody fired." A pause. "There's nothing standing in that city that's theirs."

Behind Rylan somebody put a tin down on the bench very carefully.

"And him," said Rylan.

"On his back. Imani says days." Another pause; Rylan knew Ward's pauses, where he took out the parts he did not mean to say. "Hand's reset. The suit's gone. He did a thing in the square yesterday I'll tell you about at home."

"Augustine?"

"Ran."

Thirteen years ago on the south road Rylan would have asked six more questions and got answers to none of them.

"Will he be on?"

"Not today."

"Right," said Rylan, and gave the Isle in the order Ward liked it. The forty sail off the Claim's west end had come in to the village on the night of the thirteenth, while the gully was still dead, and put ashore everything they had carried for a month: levy, perhaps a thousand; and knights, which was new. Knights in plate, the old kind, made the old way; Declan had counted fifty-eight from the bluff. On the fifteenth the levy had dug a battery in the open field between the village and the dune, a bank of sand and brushwood faggots with eight bombards behind it, gravity stones in their breeches, throwing at the gully since noon.

"They're throwing at an empty gully. They'll work that out. The jack's in the gully, and if they get the range of the jack they've got the wells without walking." He let a breath go. "Three came in at four this morning. With the bombs. And the broth. I want the battery. In the field. Bank and guns. Not the village, not the boats. I'm asking leave."

The horn hissed. Ward could not say yes; it was the fleet's rule, and he had made it the fleet's himself with his hand flat on the table's rim.

"I'll take it down to him," said Ward.

The Grayrock captain was leaning in the doorway with his arms folded, the grey square man who had looked at the fog at Kesrath's end of the light sand table in November and said that if they came out of it nobody would know where from. He had been right, and had been decent about it.

"The bank's faggots," he said. "Dry as a sermon. One fire round into it and you'd not need the escort."

"No."

"I know it's the order. I'm asking what it's for."

"The pitch lake burns," said Rylan. "And the wells burn. There's gas coming up that well that's kept a burner lit four years without anybody feeding it, and a lake of tar a quarter mile off across the rock. Light anything on this island and you'll be putting it out for a month. Or you won't, and there's no island." He held up his tin. "That's what it's for. That and the tea."

The captain nodded, and went out.

On his hurdle the mage said something to the regular on the stool in his own tongue, low and hoarse, the same handful of words he had been saying since the thirteenth. Nobody in the still house understood a word of it, and everybody knew what it was. What was it. What did you do.

The answer to Rylan came up at a little after noon in the K Amara clerk's careful voice, read off a slip. "His Majesty says yes. The battery in the field. He says you name it."

Rylan sat with that a breath, for what it meant that it was the king's yes, and not the voice of the man flat on his back four hundred miles south who would have given it.

"Named," he said. "First light."



He named it again at first light on the seventeenth from the dune's crown, with the handset at his mouth and the sky going the colour of wet slate behind the village, so that every hull and every sergeant's helm on the Isle heard it and the clerk in the K Amara wrote it down.

"Target is Kesrath's earthwork battery in the open field above the village. The bank and the guns." Through Declan's glass, lent for the morning, he could see the crews behind the bank with their breath going up white, and a man with a bucket. "Not the village. Not the boats. Not the road. Three high, four low."

He did not see three come. She came over at the Band's foot with her stones held quiet and no light on her, and the first he knew of her was the field. The bank went up out of itself in four places at once, a great brown heave of sand and brushwood and something that was a gun carriage turning over slowly at the top of it; and then the sound came up the dune, four cracks run together like the world's biggest door slammed in the next room, and the sand on the dune's face jumped and slid.

Dynamite. He had seen it bring down a hillside at the quarry above the works, with Bram swearing at the fuses. He had not seen it fall on men.

The crews ran toward the village roofs, some dragging others. Behind the battery, on the field's far edge, the knights sitting their horses there in plate did not run. They turned north-east, inland, along the dune's back, in good order.

Three bombards were still on their carriages, and their crews were laying them.

"Four," said Rylan. "Two left of the bank. One right. They're laying on the drum."

She came in low, which was why he had put her second: down the strait out of the north-west at a hundred feet with the dawn behind her, black, her keel just clear of the dune's crown, the noise of her stones like wind in a chimney, the whole shape of her coming straight at them out of the grey. One of the crews threw his rammer down and ran. The other two did not. Her bombs went off the racks under her in a string, and she was past before they landed, and the three guns went up together. A wheel went rolling over the field end over end for a hundred yards and lay down in the stubble.

"Pulling out," said four's master. "East, on the burn, over the wells. Clear?"

Fletcher had stood the whole detachment under the K Discovery's can on the slip to make sure they knew what that meant: the scream, the steel going cherry red at the lip. Afterburner, Emeric called it. No flame; fire stones burned nothing. Hot air, a great deal of it, over whatever she went over. East over the wells meant over the jack, and the still house with its burner, and north of her line by a quarter of a mile, the lake.

He looked at her line, and the lake, and the wind, which was off the sea behind her and would take her plume away east and down. He looked again, because he had been told to.

"Clear," he said. "Stay south of the drum. Go."

She went. The scream came across the field so loud that the men along the dune's crown put their hands over their ears and the gulls on the beach went up in a white sheet; and four went away east low and getting faster, the air behind her shaking like the air over a road in August and the lip of her can coming up dull red. Rylan watched her plume go over the drum's lid and on across the rock, a long clear ripple that lit nothing, that bent the thorn bushes at the gully's lip flat and let them up again; and then she was a red spark going up over the island's spine, and the gulls came down.

"She went," said Rylan, into the set.

In the field the bank was a row of brown holes with dust coming off them. Rylan counted what was lying there, because Ward would ask, and wrote it in the book against the hour: eleven he could see. There would be more under the bank.

Declan held his hand out for the glass, and looked a while, and said, "Lake."

The knights had gone along the dune's back and onto the lake road, and were going in one after another at the gate of the pitch works on the lake's west shore, the only stone roofs between the field and the boundary stones, and sending their horses on east, empty, toward their own end.

"Ah," said Rylan.



The pitch works had been the village's for longer than the Claim had been a charter: three long sheds of island stone with tarred roofs, end on to the lake, a yard between them walled to a man's chest, six brick kettles along the yard's lake side for boiling crust down to the tar the village caulked its boats with, a crane of grey timber at the shore for the blocks the cutters broke off the edge. And stacked along the inside of the yard wall under canvas, four high, the village's winter tar in barrels. Rylan had counted them from the dune in November with nothing else to do. Three hundred and some.

It was a quarter of a mile from the well head across the rock.

He drew the rules on the still house bench in chalk where the mage could watch, and said them twice to every sergeant and once into the set for the knights' helms. Air only: no powder within fifty yards of the wall, because a powder flash in a pitch shed would be the last order he gave. No lit edges; every blade went in cold. No lightning in the sheds or the yard, where the barrels were. Lightning on the open sand only, and only into a man whose chain was cut.

The chains were the thing. Every knight in plate had worn one since there had been plate: a short length of steel off the back of the belt, links as thick as a finger, dragging in the dust behind the heel, so that a stroke of lightning that came into the plate went down the chain into the ground and not through the man. A ground chain. Kaldo's suits had a copper braid in the leg for it, and nobody thought about it. A lightning round into a knight with his chain on was a wasted chip, and the chips were issued by the tin and counted.

So it was Declan. He went up the slag heap behind the works at ten, on his belly, with a Grayrock regular beside him holding the lightning rounds; and at a quarter to eleven the first Kesrath knight came out of the yard gate to look at the heap, a big man in old black plate with a white plume, because somebody in Kesrath had told him he was an officer. His chain lay in the sand behind him in a little curve, like a dog's tail.

"Chain," said Declan, very quietly, and fired.

The air crystal's flat smack, like somebody beating a rug. The chain jumped and lay down in two pieces. The knight did not feel it; he turned his head toward the heap, because he had heard something.

"On," said Declan.

The Grayrock man fired. Rylan, in the lee of the yard wall forty yards along, saw a small blue snap on the breastplate like a spark off a cat in winter, and the knight went stiff from his heels to his plume and fell over backward the way a plank falls, without bending anywhere. The plume lay in the sand beside his head, very white.

Nobody else came out of the gate. So they went in.

It took the rest of the seventeenth and all of the eighteenth, and Rylan did not remember most of it in order afterward, which Osric had told him was usual. He remembered the first shed: dark, thirty yards long, its floor beaten tar gone soft and slick in the middle from sixty years of the kettles' heat, so that a man in plate went down on it as if on ice. Kaldo's knights went in with their suits lit under the plate and their blades cold, and were stronger, a little, and quicker, a little; and the Kesrath knights were better, a good deal, because a dark room with a bad floor was what they had trained in all their lives and the island had trained Kaldo's for open sky. The regulars came in behind with the self-loaders on air, the brass ringing down on the tar. At ten yards an air round would not go through good plate, but it would knock a man down, and a man down on that floor stayed down long enough.

He remembered a knight with a red beard coming over a pile of crust at him with an axe, and taking the axe on his right forearm plate, and the new skin under it telling him all about it; and hitting the man in the face with his helm, which Ward had told him in the yard never to do, and it working, and the man sitting down in the tar holding his nose, and Rylan saying, "Sorry," and meaning it, and sitting on him.

A boy from the lower town called Ewan Corrie took a crossbow bolt through the thigh in the second shed's door and swore so inventively for so long that the Kesrath knight who had shot him stopped to listen. Nobody of Kaldo's died in the works. Rylan found that out at dusk on the eighteenth, going through the sheds by touch with a lamp he would not light, and had to sit down on a kettle's rim for a while.

Of Kesrath's he counted nine dead, in the sheds and on the sand where Declan had been; and nineteen who put their hands up in the third shed at four on the eighteenth, when it was the last shed they had and the regulars held both its doors. Their officer took his helm off, set it down on the tar, and said something Rylan took to mean that they had been told there was nobody on this island but a few hundred levy and some divers. Rylan said that was about right, and that he was sorry about the knights.

The rest, forty or so, went out of the east gate on foot along the lake's south shore toward the boundary stone with their wounded between them. Rylan let them go. That was the order too.



And behind them on the shore, where the lake came down to the sand in a long soft lip of black between the works and the stone, there was a mage.

Rylan could feel nothing of him; he was a knight, made on the floor like every knight, and the only thing he could feel coming was weather. But he saw him: a tall young man in a long dark coat, standing on the sand between the lake and his retreating knights with his hands up, and over his hands, in red, a ring and a core and strokes going through it. Two hundred yards. Too far for an air round to do anything to a man in a skin, and Declan on the far side of the works with no line through the sheds.

"No," said Rylan, out loud, to nobody, the way a man says it to a dog already in the river.

The mage did not throw it at Kaldo. He threw it at the lake: at the soft lip between his own people and the works, where the crust was thin and the fresh black came up through it in fat slow bubbles that burst and breathed. The ring went out of his hands in a long low sheet of red, and the lip took it.

Smoke first, black, rolling up thick as wool. Then the bubbles began to catch. A bubble as big as a man's head swelled up through the black and burst, and its breath took fire in the air with a soft cough, a ball of yellow flame the size of a cartwheel that went up and was gone; and the next, and the next, along the shore, cough, cough, cough, faster than a man could count, until the lip of the lake for two hundred yards was a low wall of smoky yellow fire with the black pouring up out of it into the dusk.

The Kesrath knights going away along the shore had stopped and turned round to look at it. So had the mage, as if he had expected something smaller.

It was a little after five, and the wind came round at dusk as it did on the Isle and blew from the west, steady, along the lake.

Rylan stood in the works' east gate and worked it out, because there was nobody else to and he could feel the regulars behind him waiting.

The wind would take it east along both shores, wherever the edge was soft, toward the stones and past them; and past the south stone on the flat ground at the lake's east end was Kesrath's camp, its tents and horse lines, and under canvas by the shore, because it was the only flat ground, their oil, carried down in barrels from their well under the tower for the landing's lamps and cooking. That was theirs.

And this end was his. The ground fell from the lake to the south-west, toward the rock and the gully; it was why the old seep had come up where it did. Pitch that burned was pitch that was hot, and hot pitch ran. It would come off the lip onto the rock under its own smoke, the way tar came over a kettle's rim when somebody let the firebox get away; and a quarter of a mile down the rock were the well head and the derrick and the three full drums of the tank farm, and the still house with a burner in it that had not been out in four years. And behind him in the yard, fifty yards from the burning lip, three hundred barrels of tar.

He had told the Grayrock captain on the sixteenth that they would be putting it out for a month, or there would be no island. He had meant it as a thing to say.

"Right," he said, and turned round.

The barrels first, because they were nearest: every knight who could still stand, two to a barrel, lift and carry, low on their stones, out of the yard's west gate and across the sand two hundred yards to the dune's foot, and down, and back. A knight could carry a barrel of tar on his stones as easily as a man carries a bucket. Two could carry it without dropping it, which mattered more. Rylan stood in the gate and counted them past by the hundred, because if he did not count them nobody would.

Then the trench. Ward had put a short-handled entrenching spade with a folding blade on every pack in Kaldo, and been laughed at for it in the yard by men who were not laughing tonight. A line along the rock below the lip from the works' corner, as long and deep as they could make it, the spoil thrown up on the lake side into a bank, so that whatever came off the lip came into the bank and stopped.

"How deep?" said the sergeant of the first company, a woman from the brickfields with a voice like a gate.

"Dig till I find out."

He had two loaded earth stones in the kit, the only two on the Isle, filled in Kaldo by a Circle mage who did the weather glass in ward two. He gave one to the Grayrock captain and walked out with the other to where the slope steepened toward the gully, with the fire's heat on his face at forty yards like an open oven door, and put the stone to the rock and woke it. The ground came up in a long brown hump, chest high and grinding, thirty yards of it. It was a good bank. The lip was two hundred.

He put the dead stone in his pocket and went back for a spade.



It was old Mossop who said about the sand.

Rylan never found out where he had come from. At about seven he was simply in the yard among the knights carrying barrels: an old man from the village, small and bent, in a leather apron burned through in a dozen places, with a long-handled scoop over his shoulder like a shepherd's crook. He had boiled the village's tar in those kettles fifty-one years, and had come up the sled track when he saw the glow, because it was his lake.

The first company had found the works' cistern, for cooling the pans, with a hand pump on it, and had a chain of buckets going from it toward the lip. Mossop went across the yard faster than Rylan would have said he could walk and took the first bucket out of a regular's hands and set it down.

"Not water. Never water on tar." He looked at Rylan as at somebody who ought to have known. "It goes under, and boils, and throws the tar up off itself all over you. Burning." He held up his left hand, all one shiny pink scar from the wrist. "My father put water on a kettle once. Once."

"Then what?"

"Sand. Thick. Keeps the air off. You let it sit and it crusts, and you don't walk on it, and you don't poke it." He looked out at the lip, and his old face did something Rylan could not read. "Sixty years. Never seen her lit."

So the water went on cloths, tied wet over the face below the eyes, because the smoke came down the wind in long greasy rolls and a man without a wet cloth in it was coughing in a minute and on his knees in two. And the buckets went on sand.

The line was there by eight, and Rylan did not order it: two hundred yards of people a pace apart from the dune's foot to the bank, the buckets going up it full and back down the other side empty. Tar buckets, canvas buckets, cook pots, a regular's helm. At the lip end the last man threw.

Wilf Abery and Cady Lorne were near the lip end, because they were knights and young and nobody had told them not to be: Abery big and steady with his feet set, taking each bucket in both hands and swinging it on to Cady the way a man pitches hay, Cady turning and throwing and turning back. Abery had her blue scarf wet over his mouth and nose. Cady had nothing over hers, and was coughing, and would not stop.

"Lorne," said Rylan, going past. "Your face."

"He's got my scarf." She threw. "Sir."

Rylan took the wet cloth off his own face and tied it over hers while she stood still for it, coughing, and went on down the line without one until somebody at the cistern gave him a piece of somebody's shirt.

The sand did what Mossop said. Where the throwers could reach, the fire went down under it with a hiss and a long sour reek and left a grey skin on the lip like the skin on cold porridge, with smoke coming up through it in threads; and the bubbles that came up under the skin burst dark and did not catch. It worked within a bucket's throw of the bank, and along the shore for as far as the line had people, which was about sixty yards. The lip was burning for two hundred, and going further, a yard at a time east along both shores, with the smoke over it lit orange from below and rolling away on the wind toward the stones.

At about nine the pitch came down off the lip where the bank ended, at the works' corner, where nobody had a spade yet: a black tongue a yard wide, slow as cold honey, with a skin of fire on it that came and went, feeling its way down the rock toward the gully. Rylan saw where it was going, and saw that the regulars nearest him had seen it too and were looking at him. He went at it with his spade. So did the brickfields sergeant and six more and then a good many; they cut a trench across in front of it and threw the spoil uphill, and the black came into the spoil and pushed, and the spoil held. Then it went round the end. They cut another below. It came into that one and stopped, and lay there with its fire going out in patches under their sand, smelling like the end of the world, and they stood over it with their spades and did not trust it.

The heat was what nobody had been ready for. A pitch fire did not roar and fall in and finish like a house; it lay there and burned low and threw its heat out sideways steady as a forge, so that at ten yards a man's face went tight and his eyes ran into the cloth, and at five the hair on his arms went. Plate was worse than nothing. Rylan's forearm plate got hot enough that he threw it on the sand, and after that there was only a sleeve between the new skin and the heat, and the new skin told him about it all night, at length, like a man in a tap who has had one too many and found somebody who will listen.

What he mostly felt about it, he noticed, was that Nessa would want to see it, and say she had told him; and she had not told him anything about this at all.



The first of Kesrath's came out of the dark at about ten.

Rylan was on the bank at the works' south-east corner, where the lip turned away along the south shore toward the stone; and out of the smoke beyond the burning, along that shore, came six or seven lanterns, and under them men with spades.

The regulars at the bank's end saw them in the same breath, and the rifles came up off their slings all along the line with a noise of steel Rylan felt in his teeth.

"Hold," he said.

"Sir, they're..."

"They've got spades," said Rylan.

A dozen of Kesrath's levy in their brown coats with their faces wrapped, and a sergeant with a lantern on a pole. They had come round the burning along the sand and stopped a hundred yards off, on their side of it, and were looking at the Kaldo bank and the Kaldo rifles. Then the sergeant looked at the fire between them, creeping east toward his camp a yard at a time; and turned his back on Kaldo's rifles, and put his lantern pole in the sand, and began to dig. His men looked at each other, and began to dig too.

The rifles went down along the bank's end one at a time, like men sitting down in church.

By midnight there were two hundred of them, in companies, with spades and buckets and long scoops like Mossop's, because their end of the island had dug tar out of that lake as long as the village had; and they began to cut along the south shore east of the fire's front what Kaldo was cutting on the west. Neither side went near the other. Between the two lines lay a hundred yards of burning lip that nobody could reach from either end; and in the middle of it on the sand, a little back from the edge, the boundary stone, a squat white pillar with the Claim's mark cut on its west face and Kesrath's on its east, standing in the firelight with the smoke going over it, exactly halfway.

And they had brought three earth mages. Rylan watched them begin, a little after midnight, on the far side of the gap: three men in long coats with their hands out, three glyphs coming up broad and brown in the smoky dark, and the sand in front of them rising, not in a hump as his earth stone had raised it but in a wave, a foot high and ten yards long, rolling forward off the shore onto the burning lip and lying down on it, and the fire going out under it in a long hiss of steam. A hundred buckets in one breath. Then the three stood with their heads down, breathing, for a long time, and did it again.

At about one Declan went across the gap. Rylan saw him on the far side of it, on Kesrath's sand, with three of Kaldo's knights behind him carrying the drum's spare sand sacks, filled at the dune and carried in their arms low over the burning on their stones, where nobody on foot could go. Declan had worked it out without telling anybody, as Declan worked things out. Now the knights were going back and forth over the gap a man's height above the fire with their arms full, and dropping the sacks where it was worst, and going back for more; and a Kesrath sergeant on his bank stood with his lantern and watched them go over his head with his mouth open.

At about two a Kesrath levy man went through the crust. He had stepped out onto the grey skin the mages' sand had made to throw further, too soon, and the skin held him a step and then went, and he was in to the knee in black that was still hot under it, screaming. The two nearest had him under the arms and could not get him out; the black held the leg the way a bog holds a boot.

Declan was there before Rylan had taken a step. He came down off the air beside them with his boots a hand's breadth off the crust, put his arms round the man from behind, and lifted on his stones, straight up, slowly, the way a man draws a post out of wet ground; and the leg came out of the black with a sound Rylan would hear for a while, and the boot did not. Declan carried him back to Kesrath's bank and put him down.

His hands were burned. Rylan could see it in the firelight across the gap, the palms and the insides of the fingers. The Kesrath sergeant said something to him. Declan nodded once, the way he nodded to a horse, and went back up for another sack.



Commander Varo Corvane came to the stone at about three.

Between midnight and three the gap had shrunk from a hundred yards to thirty to none, until there was one bank along the south shore from the works' corner to well past the stone, and the two bucket lines ran into each other at the stone and became one line without anybody deciding it should; so that men of Kaldo's and men of Kesrath's took buckets from each other at the boundary stone and passed them on, and nobody could have said whose bucket was whose.

Abery was there, taking buckets from a Kesrath boy who could not have been seventeen, in a brown coat too big for him, and swinging them on to Cady; and they did it like that for an hour, the three of them, without one word in common. At some point the boy started counting under his breath in his own tongue, and Abery started counting with him, wrong, through the blue scarf, and the boy laughed, and Cady told them both to shut up and throw.

Corvane came along the bank from the east with a lantern and a spade: a thick short man of fifty or so with a grey beard the fire had taken on one side, so that he looked halfway through a shave, and a commander's coat that had been good that morning and was now the colour of everybody's. He stopped at the stone and looked at Rylan, and at the black-faced line passing buckets past it.

"You're the captain," he said. He spoke Randillian carefully, like a man walking a plank, with the strait in his vowels. "The voice on the dune. The first night."

"Rylan."

"Corvane. I have this." He moved the lantern a little, east, at the camp and the shore and all of it. "The field. The knights who are left. These." He looked at the fire. "Him."

"Your mage."

"He is in my tent, tied to the pole. My king will have him, after."

"Don't hang him on my account."

"Not on your account." Corvane looked at the lake. It was still burning out past the bank, a low yellow line under the smoke where the black lay too far out for anybody's sand; but shorter, and lower, and nowhere coming toward anybody. "His grandfather cut tar on this shore. Mine also. He knows that lake. He knew."

Rylan had nothing to say to that. After a while, because the man at the end of the line was coughing, Corvane set his lantern on the bank and took the next bucket and threw it, and Rylan took the one after. They did that for some time.

"Your knights," said Corvane eventually, throwing. "In my works. Nineteen."

"The village's works. On the Claim." Rylan threw. "They're in our still house. Fed. Two of them are hurt and they've had the broth."

"The broth."

"For wounds. Stops them going bad."

Corvane threw. "And the high mage."

"Alive. Legs and back. Fed." Rylan took the next bucket. "He asks what it was."

"What was it?"

Rylan threw the bucket.

Corvane looked at him sideways over the burned half of his beard, for about as long as it took the next bucket to come up the line. Then he took it and threw it, and said, "No. I would not tell me also."



The light came up grey behind the lake at a little after seven on the nineteenth, and showed them what they had done.

The lake's whole west and south lip, from the works to well east of the stone, was a long grey crust of sand and pitch fused together, a yard wide in places and twenty in others, lumpy and cracked, smoking from its cracks in thin blue threads. Past it the black lay flat and dull with no fire on it anywhere; a bubble came up now and then and burst, and nothing caught. On the north shore the fire had run east on its own and burned out against a spit of bare rock that nobody had known about, and that Mossop said had always been there and they could have asked him.

The black tongue lay crusted where it had stopped in the second trench, with a regular asleep on the spoil beside it. It had come a hundred and forty yards; the well head was four hundred and some below it. Rylan paced it later, because Ward would ask.

Nobody had gone back to anybody's lines. Along the bank people had simply sat down where they stood when the last of the fire went out, Kaldo's and Kesrath's in among each other on the sand with their spades and their wet rags round their necks; and walking along the bank's top Rylan found that he could not tell one from another. Everybody was black, faces and hands and hair, the whites of their eyes and the pink of their mouths when they coughed. He could have told a Kesrath coat from a Kaldo one by the cut, if he looked, and he found he had stopped looking.

Abery was asleep sitting up against the bank with his chin on his chest and the scarf, black now too, round his neck, and the Kesrath boy asleep on his shoulder. Cady sat beside them awake, with no eyebrows at all, looking at the lake. She saw Rylan see the eyebrows, and he watched her decide to wait to say anything about them until there was somebody to say it to who would laugh.

His coat was open. The second button had gone at last, sometime in the night, into the sand or the pitch or somebody's bucket, and the third with it for company; and he stood on the bank in the grey light with his coat hanging open like a carter's and found that his eyes had done something under the black on his face that he had not told them to do. He let them. Nobody could see.

They agreed it at the stone at about eight, the two of them, standing in the soot, with nobody near enough to hear but Corvane's officer and Declan, who stood a little behind Rylan with his hands wrapped in strips of somebody's shirt.

It did not take long. Corvane's end behind Corvane's stones, Rylan's behind Rylan's. Nobody in arms across the line. Kesrath's dead from the works and the field carried back to them that day under a white cloth, by Kaldo's people to the stone and by theirs from it. The nineteen knights and the high mage to stay in the still house, fed and written in the book, until the crowns said otherwise.

"And the line," said Corvane. "Across the lake."

There had never been one. That was the oldest joke on the Isle: the stones ran along the dune ridge to the lake's two shores, one on the north and this one on the south, and between them lay a quarter of a mile of pitch nobody could set a stone in, and two crowns had argued for sixty years about where in it the Claim stopped.

"Through the middle," said Rylan. "That one to this one. Straight."

"You cannot stake a line on tar."

"You can on that." Rylan looked at the crust. "When it's cold."

Corvane looked at it too, the long grey lumpy skin that two hundred of his men and three hundred of Rylan's had thrown there one bucket at a time.

"I cannot sign anything," he said. "I have no seal for it."

"Neither have I. That's two of us." Rylan put his hand out. It was black to the wrist, and so was Corvane's when he took it. "Nobody signs. We just stand here."

Corvane held it a little longer than a man does who is only shaking it. Then he picked up his lantern, which had been out for an hour, and went back along the bank toward his camp, stepping over his own men where they slept.

The Grayrock captain had come up behind them and was looking past the stone, east: Kesrath's camp with half its tents black, the levy asleep all over it; and past it the island going away in the grey to the tower on its headland, with nothing between.

"There's nothing between us and that tower," said the captain. "Not this morning. We could be there by Sunday."

"No."

"Because?"

"Because those were the orders," said Rylan, "and both crowns have a right to the island." He leaned on his spade. "And I'm tired."

The captain thought about it in his square way, and nodded, and went to find something to eat.



Escort four came up the strait that afternoon with the rest of what the K Amara could send, and in it two more crates of Hester's broth, so that there were jars enough at last for everybody who needed one, which by then was most of the Claim. On the first lid somebody had written in chalk in very large capitals, and the chalk had survived four hundred miles: TWO JARS A MAN. THEIRS TOO. I.

The medics did the burns on canvas on the drum shed's floor: forty-one of Kaldo's from the night, faces and hands and forearms and one man's whole back where his plate had been hot; and the levy man Declan had lifted out, whom Corvane sent across the stone on a board at noon because his own surgeon wanted the leg off. The detachment's senior medic, a slow tall Milford man who had worked two years under Imani and talked about her as other men talk about weather, cleaned it with the broth from knee to ankle and did not take it off. He said they would see. Declan let them do his hands last, when there was nobody else, and looked at the wall while they did it.

In the still house a girl from the lower town stopped by the high mage's hurdle at about four and gave him a jar, because he was a wounded man and the lid said theirs too, and held it for him while he drank. When he had drunk it he looked up at her and said it again, hoarse, the handful of words. She did not understand him, and would not have told him if she had. She wiped his mouth with the corner of her apron and went on to the next.



The crust was cold enough to walk on by the morning of the twentieth, if you walked on planks.

Declan set his level on its legs at the north shore stone at eight, his hands in fresh strips, and sighted across the quarter mile of black to the south stone, where a regular held the staff; and Rylan went out onto the lake on the planks with a bundle of iron pickets from the well's spares on his shoulder and drove one every fifty yards where Declan's arm told him, with a strip of white rag from the medics' stores tied to each.

Out on the black it was very quiet. The planks creaked. The pitch was cold and hard as road, and here and there a bubble came up through it, as big as a man's head, and burst with a soft wet sound, and nothing happened. Rylan stood in the middle of the lake with a picket in his hand, in the place two crowns had argued over for sixty years, and it was a lake of tar with a man on it holding a stick.

The last fifty yards went through the crust the night had made, through sand and pitch fused grey that had been burning lake at five on the eighteenth and was ground now. The picket went into it harder than into the black, and rang under the hammer.

On Kesrath's side of the stone Corvane's officer had set up a level of his own and was sighting the same line from the other end. When both had finished he and Declan looked at each other across the quarter mile. The officer held up one hand flat and tipped it a very little, the width of a finger. Declan looked through his glass again for a long time, and held up his own hand, and tipped it the same.

Neither of them moved a picket. Nobody signed anything; there was nothing to sign it on. There was a line of iron pickets with white rags on them going straight across the pitch lake of the Isle of Shards from one white stone to another, with the sea wind moving the rags, and a man of each crown at each end who had looked along it and agreed.



The set clicked twice at nine that evening, which was the K Amara.

Rylan was in the still house with a tin of tea on its fire stone. The nineteen knights were asleep in rows along the north wall. The high mage was awake on his hurdle. Declan sat on the floor against the condenser with his wrapped hands in his lap and his eyes shut, where he had been since the staking, and the Grayrock captain was in the door with his arms folded, because that was where the Grayrock captain stood.

Rylan gave his four lines, and then a fifth and a sixth, because there were more than four tonight. The lake lit by Kesrath's mage at dusk on the eighteenth. Put out by both sides by dawn. The ceasefire at the stone. The line staked through the middle, pickets and rags, stone to stone; nothing signed. Nobody of Kaldo's killed since the thirteenth; forty-one burned, in the book. The coat he did not mention.

The horn hissed. The clerk's voice, away from his mouthpiece, said, "Hold, please," to somebody who was not Rylan; there was a sound of something being moved, and Ward very low saying something short that ended in "flat"; and then nothing for a breath.

"Rylan."

It was Emeric, flat and tired, from somewhere lower than a man's mouth usually is when he talks into a set, as if the mouthpiece had been brought down to him and not him up to it. Rylan had heard that voice on and off every night for thirteen years, and had not heard it like this.

"Here," said Rylan.

"I heard about the lake."

"Their mage lit it. We put it out." Rylan looked at the floor. "So did they."

A long breath across four hundred miles of sky, with the K Amara's deck humming faintly behind it, and somebody, Imani he thought, saying something that was plainly about how long this was going to take.

"Hold the line," said Emeric. "Don't cross it."

Rylan looked across the still house at Declan with his eyes shut, and the nineteen asleep along the wall, and the high mage watching the set; and out past the Grayrock captain's shoulder to where, a quarter of a mile off across the rock in the dark, the pitch lake lay flat and cold under the stars with a row of white rags going across it.

"Wasn't going to," said Rylan.
