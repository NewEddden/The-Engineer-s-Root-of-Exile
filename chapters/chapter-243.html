Chapter 243 - The King's Leave

The king stood up.

He did it slowly, with both hands on the arms of the chair, the way he had come down the three steps at the elevation, like a man thinking about each part of a thing before he did it. The prince Corbin, a pace in front of him at the edge of the dais, turned his head at the sound of the chair and, after a moment, stepped back to his place behind it. It was done gracefully. Everything Corbin did was done gracefully. He stepped back the way a man steps out of a doorway to let an older man through, and the hall saw him do it, and thought well of him for it.

Harrold did not look at him.

He stood at the front of the dais with his hands at his sides and looked down the length of the hall, over the Crown's table and the floor and the benches, to the south door, and when he spoke it was in the voice Keller had heard him use at every sitting he could remember, for rolls and summonses and the reading of instruments. It was a loud, dry voice with nothing in it. It went to the back of the hall and up into the gallery and, Keller had no doubt at all, out under every door in the building.

"The lord of Kaldo," the king said, "holds of the Crown."

Nobody answered. It was not a thing that could be answered.

"He holds his barony of the Crown, and the three baronies the Crown gave him, and the duchy the council made of them by two parts in three, in this hall, with the lords spiritual giving their voices first." He did not look at the east bench. "His company is entered in my chancery. My rider counts it at the quarter days. His powder is made under my licence, and my officer goes into his powder house at any hour he pleases, and has. His rolls come to my chancery each quarter with each man's name and each man's parish on them, because I required it, and they have never once been late." A pause, the length of a breath. "His priest's chapel stands under a licence of the close. His justice at Wayford the Crown reviewed, and found within its rights, and said so in one line, in my hand. That line was read in this hall by the Crown's own man of law, and I heard him read it."

Keller kept his hands flat on the table.

"Everything done at Kaldo," the king said, "was done under my leave. What the Crown has looked at and what it has not troubled itself to look at. All of it. There is nothing in that barony that I have not had every quarter's chance to stop, and I have not stopped it." He let that stand. "I did not stop it because I did not choose to. That is what leave means."

It went along the benches, not as a sound this time but as a stillness. Keller had sat in that hall through a great many things said from the dais, and he had heard the benches go quiet for anger, and for fear, and once for grief. This was none of those. It was the quiet of a room full of men who had been told a thing they would have to think about in the carriage home, and had not yet begun.

Then the king turned his head and looked at the Crown's table.

"The Crown's table," he said.

Keller stood.

"A thing done by the Crown's leave," the king said. "Whose act is it?"

There were perhaps four answers a careful man could have given to that, and three of them had qualifications, and Keller had spent his life giving careful answers with the qualifications in. He looked up at the king on the dais and understood that he was not being asked for any of the four. He was being asked for the one that went in the roll.

"The Crown's, your grace," he said.

"And can the lords spiritual summon the Crown to answer before them?"

"No, your grace." It was the oldest thing in the rolls; it did not need looking up. "They cannot. They never have. The Crown is not summoned. It is advised, or it is not."

"Thank you."

Keller sat down. His left hand went to the edge of the nearest paper on the table, which was the Visitor's report, and found it square, and stayed there.

He saw the whole of it then, in the space it took to sit, the way a man sees the whole of a room when a shutter is opened.

The lords spiritual had found a practice at Kaldo to be heresy. The practice was the lord of Kaldo's, so the lord of Kaldo was to come and answer. But the king had just taken the practice off his son's back and put it on his own. Everything at Kaldo was done under the Crown's leave; a thing done under the Crown's leave was the Crown's act; the Crown's table had said so, into the roll, in four words. And the Crown could not be brought before the lords spiritual. So there was nobody left to summon. The son had nothing to answer for that was his. The father could not be made to answer at all.

Which left one question, and only one, and the king had built the whole of his short speech so that it could only arrive there.

If the practice at Kaldo was heresy, and it was the Crown's act, then the question was not whether the lord of Kaldo was a heretic. It was whether the king had kept his oath.

And keep the faith. Keller had stood on the west stair after the elevation and told the new duke that it was the one clause in the coronation oath nobody had ever been able to tell him how a man would keep, or how he would break. He had said it idly, as a thing he noticed. He had also told him, three steps higher up the same stair, that the council could advise a king by a plain majority, and could only act against his person by two parts in three.

The oldest rule in the rolls. The highest bar there was.

The king had picked it up and put it between his son and the east bench. He had taken the matter out of a court where one word from the lords spiritual was enough and put it into the only court in the realm that could not move against him without two voices in every three. And he had done it by telling the truth, all of it, in the loud voice, so that nobody could say afterwards that the Crown had been ignorant or deceived. He had not denied a line of the paper on Keller's table. He had signed under all of it.

It was the best piece of law Keller had seen done in that hall in his life. It was done by a man who was not a lawyer and had never pretended to be one. And it was a man standing on a floor of thin ice and stamping on it, so that everybody watching would be sure where he stood.

Augustine had not moved.

He sat at the head of the east bench with his hands folded, as he had sat through all of it, and looked at the king with the same pleasant attention he gave to everything, as a man watches a good move at a board he has no money on. When the king had finished with the Crown's table and the hall had begun, very faintly, to breathe again, the cardinal rose.

"Your grace is right, of course," he said. "The Crown is not summoned." He said it without any weight on it, a small correction accepted. "The lords spiritual have found what they have found, and that stands. But if your grace takes the practice at Kaldo on himself, then the lords spiritual cannot ask the lord of Kaldo to answer for it." He inclined his head, very slightly, to the dais. "They can only ask the council whether the king has kept his oath."

"Then ask it," said the king.

"The Church does." Augustine turned a little, to the chancellor on his stool. "And asks the council to name the sitting at which it will answer."

So it was said. The chancellor, who had to be helped to his feet, and who read everything the same, read from the old form that a question touching the king's own person went to the whole council sitting with the lords spiritual, and was answered by two parts in three, and not otherwise, and that the council should name its day. The steward consulted the roll. The day was named: the first sitting after the new year, three weeks off, when the roads north would be passable for the lords who had not come.

Keller wrote it down himself, at the foot of his own sheet, in his own hand. He did not know why. There was a clerk to do it.

"One thing more," the king said.

The hall had begun to shift. It stopped.

"There will be men who say the king will ride south before the new year and not come back." He said it flatly, as if it were a line in a report about the weather on the north road. "There will be men who say the lord of Kaldo will ride north. There will be men who say both, in the same breath, in the same wine-house." He looked along the benches, and some of them looked away. "So that nobody need say either. The Crown places the king's person in the keeping of the lords spiritual, in this house, until the council has answered. The king will not leave this house. Nobody will be sent for. Nobody will ride south."

Keller heard it, and it took him a moment longer than it should have to understand it, because it was not a thing that had ever been done and there was no form for it in any roll he had read.

The king had put himself in custody. Of his own motion, on his own floor, before anyone had asked for it and before anyone could. He had done it so that there would be no writ south, no rider, no brother at the gate at Kaldo with the settlement in his hand and a kind word in his mouth. The prince Corbin's offer had been generous and public and unanswerable, and the king had not answered it. He had simply made it unnecessary. Nobody would ride south, because there was nobody in the south who had been summoned, and there was a king in his own house under guard to make sure of it.

The price of it was that the king was under guard.

Keller looked at the dais. He looked, because it was the only thing in the hall he could not stop himself looking at, at the prince Corbin standing a pace behind the empty chair. Corbin's face showed exactly what a son's face should show when his father had just done a hard and honourable thing in front of the whole kingdom: concern, and pride, and a little rueful admiration, all together, very well done. He caught Keller looking, and gave him the smallest shake of the head, as one man says to another, well, what can you do with him.

Keller looked away.

Augustine came down from the east bench to accept it.

That was the form, or he made it the form, since there was no form. He came down the two steps at the head of the bench and walked out across the floor in the red, not quickly, an old man with very clean hands, and stopped below the dais where the king could see him, and bowed. It was a full bow, a proper one, the bow of a subject to his sovereign, and he held it for exactly the right length of time, and straightened.

"The lords spiritual receive your grace's person," he said, "in your grace's own house, with honour, until the council answers." His voice was as level as it had been all morning, and as pleasant. "Your grace will want for nothing. Your grace's household will attend you as always. The Church asks only to be at the door."

"The Church may be at the door," the king said.

"Your grace is very gracious."

The king looked down at him from the dais.

Keller had seen that face at every sitting for longer than he could remember, and he had seen it do nothing, which was what it did best. It did nothing now. The king looked down at the cardinal the way he looked at a clerk who had brought him a roll, with no more in it and no less, and then he nodded once, and turned, and went back to his chair, and sat in it, and put his hands on its arms.

Augustine bowed again, to the chair, and went back to his bench.

That was all. Keller watched the whole of it, because he watched everything, and what he saw was courtesy: a cardinal who had pressed his case with perfect manners and had it turned against him and accepted the turn with perfect manners, and had received a king's surrender of his own person as if it were a gift he was sorry to have to take. There was nothing else in it anywhere. There was not a flicker. Keller, who had spent his life looking for the flicker in men's faces when a thing went against them, looked for it in the cardinal's, and it was not there. He noted that, as he noted everything. He did not know what it meant. He thought it probably meant that Augustine was a better churchman than he had given him credit for.

The cardinal's clerk came to the Crown's table at the end of the sitting and packed the paper back into its plain box, sheet by sheet, row by row, in the same order, and bowed, and carried it away. The rolls in his own clerks' hands went with it. The printed list. The small letter.

He went back to his room over the chancery and did not light the lamp for a long time.

There was a letter to write, and he did not know how to send it.

The ordinary bag went south once a week, and the next was four days off, and every letter in it passed through the chancery's own post room, where there were clerks who sold what they read for the price of a supper; Keller had had two of them dismissed in his time and knew there were others. The Crown's rider went at the quarter, which was weeks away. The Ducatis House had couriers, and the lending houses, and Keller trusted none of them with a letter from the Crown's table to the lord of Kaldo on the night the king had given himself into the keeping of the Church. He sat with his hands on the table in the dark and considered it the way he considered everything, one door at a time, and found them shut.

At some time after the ninth bell there was a knock.

It was Kassim. Keller knew him the way he knew everyone in the king's household, by sight and by office and not otherwise: a lean, grey, quiet man of about the king's age who had been in the king's service since before Keller had come to the chancery, and who stood in doorways, and read the king his letters. He had never once in all those years come up the chancery stair. He did not come in now. He stood in the door with a shuttered lantern in his hand.

"Master," he said. "You'll be writing to the south."

Keller looked at him.

"His grace thought you would," Kassim said. "His grace thought you would not know how to send it." He set the lantern on the floor inside the door, as though he meant to wait, and folded his hands. "There's a man who can take it. Not by the post. He's taken letters for his grace since before the prince in the south was born, and nobody has ever read one. Write it short, and seal it, and give it to me, and it will be at Kaldo inside the week."

"Whose man is he?"

"His grace's." Kassim considered. "And one other's. You needn't know whose."

Keller did not ask anything else. He had spent his life not asking questions whose answers he would have to write down, and here was a man at his door offering him a road he had not known existed, on the king's word, on the night the king had gone into custody to keep that road from being needed. He lit the lamp. He took a sheet. He squared it.

He wrote it short.

My lord. The cardinal laid before the council this morning the burials, the rolls, the tower's register, the Circle's postings, Master Armand's letter, the soldiers' prayer, your priest's chapel licence and the school list. The lords spiritual found the practice at Kaldo to be heresy and asked that you come north to answer. His grace took the whole of it on the Crown, as done under his leave, and so on himself. The question is now whether the king has kept his oath to keep the faith. The council answers it at the first sitting after the new year, by two parts in three. Until then his grace has placed himself in the keeping of the lords spiritual, in the palace, of his own motion, so that nobody should be sent for. Nobody has been. I have the honour to be, et cetera. Keller.

He read it twice. He took out of his own motion and put it back. He sealed it, and gave it to Kassim, who put it inside his coat without looking at the direction, and picked up his lantern.

At the door Kassim stopped.

"He told me to say one thing to you," he said. "If you asked nothing. You've asked nothing, so I'll say it." He did not smile; it did not seem to be a face that did. "His grace said the Crown's table answered well this morning, and he would not have asked it anything if he had not known the answer."

He went down the stair with the lantern. Keller heard the street door, and then nothing.

He sat for a while with the lamp and the empty table. Then he got up and went to the window, which looked over the square toward the close, where the snow lay grey on the cathedral leads in the dark and there was a light in one window of the cardinal's house, high up, on the north side, where the books were. He watched it for some time. He did not know what he was waiting for it to do.

It did not go out while he watched. After a while he stopped watching it, and went to bed, and lay awake, and thought about thin ice, and about a king standing on it with his whole weight, and about the fact that ice bears a man exactly as long as nobody else steps onto it with him.
