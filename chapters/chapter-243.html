Chapter 243 - Zale's Lead

Hedda was at the filter beds when he found her, on the fourteenth, in a sheepskin coat three sizes too big and a pair of carter's boots, with a long rake in her hands and the steam going up off the warm sand round her into the frost.

She was one of Armand's fifteen. She had come south with the rest of them in the summer of the drains, a carter's daughter from Wayford with a water mage's glyph and no money and no patience at all, and stood on the bank of a flooded trench in June with her hand out and taken the water up over the bank in a brown rope while he watched; and when the drains were done she had not gone back to Wayford, nor back to the tower. She had gone to the treatment works, where the river came in brown and went out clear, and asked the foreman what he did with a water mage, and the foreman, who had never had one, had said he would find out. He had found out that she could tell a bed was choking a week before his gauges could, by putting her hand on the sand. She had worked the beds ever since.

She saw him coming along the walk between the beds, with Ward behind him, and leaned on her rake and watched him come the whole way without moving, which nobody in Kaldo did any more when the duke walked up, and which he liked her for.

"If it's the beds," she said, "they're fine. Three's slow. I've told the foreman."

"It's not the beds."

"Then what?"

"I want you to teach me to lift water."

Hedda looked at him for some time with her chin on the end of her rake.

"You," she said.

"Me."

"You're fire. Everybody knows you're fire. You lit Maren's plate in the snow with your hand." She looked past him at Ward, as if Ward might explain, and Ward looked back at her with no expression at all. "And the other thing. The heavy one."

"I'm both. I'm not water. That's why I'm asking somebody who is."

"It'll cost you three times what it costs me."

"I know."

"And you'll be bad at it."

"Very."

Hedda considered that, and the sky, and the beds steaming in the frost, and her rake.

"You asked me in June," she said. "At the trench. What a stone would need." She took her chin off the rake. "Nobody ever asks me anything. I've been here a year and a half and the foreman asks me if it's going to rain." She propped the rake against the bed's wall. "Not here. If you fall in a bed you'll muck my sand and I'll have to rake it out. The castle well. It's got a wall round it."



The well in the inner ward was old, older than the keep, with a wall of dressed stone to a man's waist and a windlass over it with a rope and a bucket, and a stone trough beside it for the horses. Hedda leaned over the wall and looked down, and spat, and listened, and nodded.

"Forty feet to the water," she said. "Good. That's far enough you'll feel it and near enough you'll not kill yourself." She turned round. "Put your hand over it. Flat. Like you're warming it."

He put his hand out over the well's mouth, palm down, and felt down into the shaft with his sense: the stone going down, wet and cold, and far down the dull close weight of the water, a disc of it lying in the dark like a coin at the bottom of a cup.

"You know where it is," said Hedda, watching his face. "Good. Now. Don't lift it."

"I thought that was the lesson."

"Everybody thinks that. The tower thinks that. Water doesn't want to come up. It wants to go down. It's always going down, everywhere, all the time; that's what water is, it's a thing going down. You try to lift it, you're fighting the whole weight of it all the way up, and it'll fight you back, and you'll lose." She held her own hand out beside his over the well, not touching. "So you don't lift it. You give it a road. Make the road and it'll go along it; it doesn't care which way, so long as it's a road."

"Like a siphon."

"I don't know what that is."

"A pipe. Full of water. One end in the water, the other end lower, over the side. The water goes up and over because it's pulled down the far side."

Hedda thought about it.

"That's it," she said. "That's what I do at the trench. I don't push it over the bank. I hold the outside of it. The skin. Like a sleeve. You hold the sleeve and the arm comes through it by itself." She looked at him sideways. "You'll not manage the sleeve. Nobody does, the first time. Try."

He tried.

The glyph came up off his palm blue and thin and wavering, a water mark he had seen a hundred times on other people's hands and never once drawn on his own, and he felt it go down the shaft and touch the water, and the water felt it, cold and close and enormously heavy, and did not move. He reached for the skin of it, the outside, the sleeve. There was no outside. There was a disc of water at the bottom of a hole, and it lay there.

"You're lifting," said Hedda.

"I'm not."

"You're lifting. I can see your shoulders. Stop it."

He stopped it. He let go of how heavy it was, which he had not known he was holding, and reached again for the edge, the place where the water met the stone round it and the air above it, a ring, the thin hard skin a coin of water has where it touches anything that isn't water; and it was there, very faint, like the edge of a sheet of glass in a dark room. He held it.

"There," said Hedda. "Now pull the sleeve up."

The water came up the shaft.

It came slowly, a rope of it as thick as his wrist, wobbling, up through the dark toward the light, and he could feel every foot of it, and every foot of it was heavier than the last. It came up over the lip of the well's wall in a long trembling arc, bright and brown in the winter light, and he swung his hand toward the trough, and the arc swung after it, and for the length of a breath it ran clear from the dark of the well to the trough, a bridge of water in the air between them, as he had seen her do it at the trench.

Then the sleeve went.

He did not feel it go. He felt his own face go grey and cold, all at once, and the sweat come out on his lip, and something in his chest go tight as it went at the end of a long night in the delve; and then the whole rope of water, a bucket's worth at least, gave up being a rope and came down where it was, which was across the front of him, from collar to boots.

It was very cold.

He stood in the inner ward with his hand still out and the water running off the end of his nose and out of his sleeves and into his boots, and Ward, at the gate, did not move a muscle of his face; and Hedda looked at him, and at the trough, which had got about a cupful, and at the puddle spreading round his boots on the frozen flags.

"That's a bucket," she said. "My little brother does that. He's nine." She turned the windlass once with one hand, idly, so that the real bucket went down the shaft on its rope and hit the water with a far-off slap. "A bucket's got a handle."

"Again," said Emeric.

"You're grey."

"Again."

She looked at him a while longer. Then she shrugged, the way the beds' foreman said she shrugged at everything, and put her hand out over the well again beside his.

"Hold the sleeve," she said. "Not the arm. Every time you think about how heavy it is, you've let go of the sleeve and taken hold of the arm. Don't think about how heavy it is. It's not your business how heavy it is."

The second rope got as far as the lip of the wall and fell back down the shaft with a long hollow splash that came up out of the dark a breath later. The third came over and got halfway to the trough and fell on the flags. The fourth reached the trough, a thin rope, wavering, and ran into it for the length of a slow count of five, and he let it go at the end of the five himself, on purpose, before it could let go of him; and sat down on the well's wall with his head between his knees, wet to the skin, his hands shaking.

Hedda looked into the trough.

"That's a jug," she said, after a while. "Two, maybe." She turned round. "You'll be sick tonight."

"Probably."

"Same time next week," said Hedda, and went back to her beds.



He was not sick. He was hungry, ravenously, as he always was after a day of costly work, and cold to the bone till he had eaten, and had a headache like a band round his skull that the third bowl of Maren's soup took away; and at nine that night, with his hair still damp from the bath, he went down the hill to the ward.

Imani was in the dispensary, alone, with the lamp turned low and the day's last cards being put away, and the silver cup of the stethoscope lying on her bench where she had left it like a coin somebody had dropped.

She had listened to everybody. She had said she would, at the feast, and she had: every patient in the ward, every cut finger and every old man with a cough and every woman who came in with a child with a fever, and every nurse, and every member of the garrison she could catch in the yard. She had a page in her register for each of them now with a line on it that said what she had heard, in her hard upright hand. And she did not know what any of it meant. She had said so to him three days ago, on the stair, quite flatly, as if reporting that the ward was out of lint: she could hear it, and she did not know what she was hearing.

So he had said he would come down after the last patient and tell her.

She looked up when he came in, at his damp hair, and the grey still under his eyes, and the way he held himself, and her mouth went thin.

"Hedda," she said.

"You've heard."

"Half the castle's heard. The kitchen girl saw you sitting on the well with your head between your knees and ran and told Maren you'd been drowned." She put her pen down. "Hedda came to me at six, to say if you were sick in the night it was water cost and you'd to drink something warm and salt. She thought I'd want to know." Imani's mouth did something at one corner. "I said I'd heard the duke was bad at it. She said he was very bad at it, and she'd see him next week."

"She's a good teacher."

"She's a terrible teacher. That's why you'll learn." Imani picked up the stethoscope and held it out to him. "Teach."



He taught it the way he had taught Bram the dynamo, and Tess the lamp, and Imani herself the first thing she had ever let him teach her, on a scrubbed table in the old ward: the thing first, then what it was for, then her hands on it.

"Two sounds," he said, and put the plugs in her ears and the cup flat on his own chest, over his shirt, on the left, and held her wrist so she would hold it there. "The first is lower. The second's shorter, sharper. Hear them?"

Imani nodded. Her eyes had gone unfocused, looking at nothing, as they did when she listened.

"They're doors," he said. "The heart's four rooms, two on top, two below. Between them, and out of them into the great vessels, there are doors that only open one way, flaps of skin, so the blood can't go back where it came from. When the big rooms squeeze, the doors behind them slam shut. That's the first sound. When they let go, the doors in front of them slam shut, so nothing falls back in. That's the second." He let go of her wrist. "So every beat, you're hearing four doors shut. Two and two."

"Doors," said Imani.

"And if a door doesn't shut properly, you hear it. A door that leaks, the blood rushes back through the gap, and you hear a whoosh where there should be quiet, between the two sounds or after them. A door that's gone stiff and won't open all the way, the blood has to push through a crack, and that whooshes too, but at a different place." He thought. "A murmur. That's the word. A murmur's a door that's wrong."

"Where?"

"That's the next thing." He took the cup off his chest, and she looked at him with the plugs still in her ears and her face asking why he had stopped. "There are four places to listen. Each one sits over the place where one door's sound comes through best." He put his fingertips on his own chest, through his shirt, one place at a time. "Up here, right of the breastbone, at the second rib. Up here, the same, left. Down here, at the bottom of the breastbone, on the left. And here." His fingers went low on the left side of his chest, out toward his side, under the nipple. "This one's the one that matters most. Where the tip of the heart taps the wall. You can feel it with your hand on a thin man."

Imani put the cup at each place on his shirt in turn, and listened at each, longer at the last.

"They're different," she said, surprised.

"They're different. And press it hard, the skin on the cup, for the high sounds, the sharp ones; lay it on light for the low ones, the rumbles. Hard and you hear the high doors. Light and you hear the low." He watched her try it, firm and then barely touching, and watched her eyes change. "There's a cup made only for the low ones, open, without the skin. I'll make you one."

"Not tonight."

"Not tonight."

She made him turn round on the stool, and put the cup on his back through his shirt, and he told her the lungs: that clean breath was soft and dry, like wind a long way off in a wood; that a crackle, fine, like hair rubbed between your fingers by your ear, meant water in the lung's small bags, from a weak heart or a sickness of the chest; that a whistle meant a tube narrowed, as in the breathless sickness, where a man fought to get his air out. She made a sound under her breath at the crackle, a small surprised sound, and he knew she was thinking of every old man in her ward who had drowned slowly in his own bed while she listened at his chest with her ear and heard nothing she could name.

"And the belly," he said. "Most people don't listen to the belly. Listen to the belly."

"Why?"

"Because it talks. A gut that's working gurgles. All the time, every few breaths, little noises, like a drain. You'll hear it in anybody who's eaten in the last day." He sat back on the stool. "And if you put the cup on a belly that hurts, and listen a full minute, and hear nothing at all, it's stopped. A quiet belly is a belly in trouble. Something's blocked it, or something inside it is so sore the gut has stopped moving round it, the way you'd stop walking on a broken leg." He looked at her. "Then you don't wait."

Imani took the plugs out of her ears and sat with the stethoscope in her lap.

"I've had three of those this winter," she said. "Bellies that hurt on the right, low, and fever, and you put your hand on and they scream. Two of them got better. One didn't." She looked at the silver cup. "I've never once listened to one."

"Listen to the next one."

"I will." She turned the cup over in her fingers. "Now Armand."

He had known she would come to it. He had come to it on the stair, coming down the hill, and had decided on the stair.

"In the Hod," he said. "In September. When I had my hand on him and his heart went in step. I told you what I saw. I didn't tell you the word."

"There's a word."

"Where I was there's a word for everything." He looked at the door, which was shut, and the shutter, which was shut. "Atrial fibrillation. The top rooms of the heart, the small ones, are called the atria. Fibrillation is that shiver. When the top rooms stop beating and shiver instead, they can't set the time for the big ones, so the big ones go when they can, out of step. Every man in your ward with the stumble you talked about, the ones on foxglove who die in their chairs, most of them have that."

Imani said the word under her breath, twice, as she would have said a drug she meant to remember the dose of.

"And you can count it," he said. "Without me. Without mana. With that." He nodded at the stethoscope in her lap. "When the heart goes out of step, some of the beats are so weak, so early, that the big room's hardly filled before it squeezes. The beat's there; you'll hear it in the cup. But there's so little blood in it, it never gets as far as the wrist. So you put the cup on his chest, here, at the tip, and your other hand's fingers on his wrist, both at once, and count both for a minute. And the wrist comes up short. Ninety at the chest and seventy at the wrist." He held up his hands. "The difference is how bad it is. The pulse deficit. The bigger it is, the more of his beats are wasted."

She was writing. She had pulled a card off the stack without looking and was writing it as he said it.

"And the foxglove," he said. "You've been dosing him by guess."

"By leaf," said Imani. "A pinch. Since the Hod."

"By weight. On the balance. Dried leaf, ground. Start lower than you think and go up slowly over days, because it builds in the body. It slows the big rooms down, so they've time to fill between beats, and the beats that reach the wrist come up." He touched the card. "And the body tells you when it's too much. The pulse goes under sixty, you stop. He's sick to his stomach, you stop. He tells you the lamp's got a ring round it, or the world's gone yellow, or green, you stop, and you don't start again for days."

"Yellow," said Imani.

"Yellow-green. Like looking through old glass."

She wrote it. Then she got up and went to her box of cards on the shelf, and took out a card he knew by its corner, much handled, with ARMAND on it in her hand; and sat down again and tore it across, once, cleanly, and put the halves in the stove, and began a new one.

When she had finished it she put the pen down and looked at him, and he saw that she was angry, and that it was not at him.

"Months," she said. "By the pinch."

"You kept him alive by the pinch."

"I kept him alive by luck." She put the card in the box under A, and closed the lid, and sat with her hand on it. "Next time you've a word, you say it. In here. I don't care where it comes from. You say it in here with the door shut, and I'll decide what to do with it."

"Yes."

"Good." She picked up the stethoscope again. "Now. Shirt."



She did his back first, for the lungs, and she was thorough about it.

He sat on the stool with his shirt over his knees and his back to her and the stove ticking, and she stood behind him and put the cup between his shoulder blades, and then lower, and then low on each side over the ribs, and at each place she put her other hand flat on his skin beside the cup and said, "Breathe in. Breathe out," in the voice she used in the ward, until about the fourth time, when she said it differently. He breathed in. He breathed out. Her hand stayed on his back after the cup had moved on, flat between his shoulder blades, warm, and did not need to be there for any reason that had to do with lungs.

"Clean," she said. "Wind in a wood. Turn round."

He turned round on the stool. She stood between his knees with the plugs in her ears and put the cup on his chest, on the skin this time, at the first place, right of the breastbone; then the second; then the third; and then low on the left, at the tip, where it mattered most, and held it there, and listened.

She listened for a full minute. He watched her face while she did it. She had a small crease between her brows when she counted, and her lips moved very slightly, and her eyes were on the wall behind him and not on him at all.

Then she took the cup away.

"Not sixty-two," she said.

"No?"

"Eighty-eight." She took the plugs out and let them hang round her neck. "Sitting down. At rest. On a stool, in a cold room, in his own ward, with a nurse." Her mouth did the thing at the corner. "It was fast on the north tower's leads too. You'd think a duke could keep a better count."

"You'd think a nurse would stand further off."

"I'm not a nurse," said Imani. "I'm the ward."

She held the stethoscope out to him.

"Now you," she said. "I've never had anybody listen to me. Not once. I've listened to half of Kaldo, and nobody's ever listened to me." She sat down on the edge of the examining couch, where the walking sick sat to be looked at, and began to undo the front of her dress.

He did it as he would have done it for anybody. He made himself. The first place, right of the breastbone, at the second rib, the cup on her skin above the edge of her shift: her heart came up into his ears close and quick and loud, two doors and two doors. The second place, the same on the left. The third, low on the breastbone, where her shift was undone to the waist now and he could feel her breathing under the heel of his hand.

The fourth place was low on the left, at the tip, where the heart tapped the wall.

On a man it was under the nipple, on the flat of the chest. On a woman it was there too, and so it was under the breast; and to listen there properly a physician lifted the breast with the back of one hand, or asked the woman to, or slid the cup beneath it, carefully, where it lay against the ribs. He had done it in his head ten thousand times out of the books the trial had put there, on women who were patients and nothing else, and never once with his hands.

He stopped with the cup in his fingers.

"The last one's here," he said, and touched the place on his own chest, low on the left, to show her. "Under."

"I know where it is," said Imani. "You showed me on you." She looked at him, steadily. "Go on, then. Do it properly."

"Lift," he said. "Or I will."

She did not lift. She took his left hand, the one without the cup, and put it where it needed to go, under her, and let go of it.

He lifted her breast with the back of his hand, gently, as a physician does, as the books said, and slid the silver cup into the warm place beneath it against her ribs, and held it there with two fingers; and her heart came up into his ears so loud and so fast and so out of all order that he forgot for a breath what he was supposed to be doing with it.

It was not steady. It was not anything like steady. It went fast and then faster and then caught on itself, once, as a heart does when its owner holds her breath, and went on again faster than before; and she could not hide any of it from him, not one beat, because he was holding the cup, and she knew he was, and he watched her know it. She looked at the wall. Her face was perfectly calm. Her heart under his hand was not calm at all.

He counted. It took him three tries.

"A hundred and four," he said.

Imani did not answer. She had found his wrist, as she always found it, with two fingers inside it below the band, as if it were hers to take; and he felt her count, felt her lips move, felt her get to some number and lose it and start again and lose it again, somewhere past thirty, the way she had lost it in the dark of a ship's cabin over the goat island two winters ago.

"I've lost it," she said.

"I know."

"I never lose it."

"You always lose it."

"Only yours," said Imani, and took the plugs out of his ears, both at once, with her free hand, and pulled him down by them.

The stethoscope went somewhere. The cup was under her still, and then it was not; he heard it hit the couch and slide off and hang by its tube, swinging, and did not care. Her mouth was hot, and the dispensary was cold, and his shirt was on the stool and her dress was round her waist, and she pulled him in between her knees by the back of his neck with a hand that had held down a man having his leg off, and he went, and put both hands on her where the cup had been, and she made a sound against his mouth that he had never heard her make in the ward, and that the ward had certainly never heard. Her heart was under his palm now with nothing between, and he did not need a cup to count it, and it was not a hundred and four any more.

Then she put her hand flat on his chest, over his own heart, and pushed. Not hard. Exactly as hard as she meant to.

"Not in my ward," said Imani.

He stood back, breathing.

She sat on the edge of the couch with her dress round her waist and her hair coming down out of its tie and her face as calm as it ever was, and only her breath giving her away; and then she began to do up the front of her dress again, button by button, with steady fingers.

"Work first," she said. "Then fun. You taught Ferra that, and Ferra taught it to me, and I'll not have it said in this town that the healer was had on her own examining couch with the stove going out." She did the last button. "Somebody might come in with a cut finger."

"At ten at night."

"People cut their fingers at all hours." She stood, and picked the stethoscope up off the floor where it hung, and looked at the silver cup, and wiped it on her sleeve, and put it round her neck. "Get your shirt on. You'll catch cold, and then I'll have to listen to you, and you'll crackle."

They went up the hill together in the frost, under the lamps on their posts, with her hand in his and the stethoscope still round her neck, its silver cup swinging on her breast as she walked, as if she might need it on the way; and she did not take it off at the keep's gate, or on the stair, and he did not ask her to.



She was still wearing it at breakfast.

The long room had half of the six at the table by eight, and Zale at the end with a cup of Maren's black drink, reading Ferra's word list for pleasure, as he had every morning since the book was done; and Ferra came in late from the counting room with her book under her arm, and came round behind Emeric's chair on her way to her own, and bent and kissed him good morning on the mouth, briefly, the way she did now every morning in front of whoever was there, as if it were a thing she had decided to have entered in the record.

And Imani, across the table, without a word, without looking up from her porridge, leaned over and put the silver cup flat on his chest through his shirt while Ferra was still bent over him; and held it there, and listened; and took it away.

"Ninety-six," said Imani.

Ferra straightened up slowly. She looked at Imani. She looked at the stethoscope round Imani's neck.

"And yours?" said Ferra.

Imani took a spoonful of porridge.

"Eighty," she said, to the porridge.

Ferra sat down in her own chair, and opened her book, and wrote something in it, and closed it again before either of them could see; and would not show it to them that day, or the day after, though Imani asked twice, and he asked once, and Zale, who had understood perhaps a third of what had happened and enjoyed all of it, offered to buy it from her for a shard flake.



Zale was to go down on the eighteenth, a Monday, on the Emberfell coaster, to his own ship, which had wintered in Emberfell's inlet under his mate and would sail as soon as he came aboard; and on the Sunday night the long room gave him a supper.

It was not a feast. Ferra had said there would not be another feast that winter if she had anything to do with the household book, and she had everything to do with it. It was Maren's soup and Maren's bread and a pair of Milford's ducks, and the black drink after, which Maren now roasted in the evenings so that the smoke went up the back stair at night when nobody minded, and boiled for exactly as long as the sheet said and not a breath longer, under protest. Zale at Emeric's right, as at midwinter. The six. Ward at the door.

It was over the duck that Zale mentioned the rock oil, and he mentioned it the way a man mentions the weather in a place he has just left.

"I put into Kesrath in the autumn," he said. "On the way up from the south. Their southern coast, the little ports, for water. I'd not been in for three years; they don't love a Randillian ship." He pulled a duck leg apart with his fingers. "There's a new thing in the chandlers. In jars, like your Duvrenne burning water, but lighter. Thinner. You can put it straight in a lamp. It smells like a tar pit, and it burns brighter than whale oil, and they sell it out of the back of the shop to men they know, and not to men they don't." He ate. "I'm a man they know, a bit. A chandler I've bought from for ten years sold me a jar and told me where it comes from, in the end, after a lot of wine."

Nobody at the table asked where. Saban, at the far end, reached for the bread.

"The Isle," said Zale. "They've sunk a well. At their end, inside the old fort walls at the far end, under the tower, where nobody but Kesrath's soldiers go. They found the stuff coming up in a gully near the walls, he says, two or three years ago, and dug down after it, and now they've a well, and a man to work it, and they bring it off by night in barrels marked as water for the garrison." He wiped his fingers. "And they sell it in the southern ports, quietly, a jar here and a jar there, and say nothing at court in case anybody in Randillia hears they're taking anything out of that island at all. Because if they're taking oil out of their end, why shouldn't Randillia take it out of the middle?" He shrugged. "Your father won't like it, if he ever hears. I thought somebody here might want to tell him. Or not."

"It's the king's business," said Ward, from the door.

"It's always the king's business," said Zale cheerfully. "That's why nobody tells him anything."

Emeric ate his duck. Across the table Ferra was writing nothing in her book and looking at nothing in particular, and Saban was buttering his bread with a care that would have done credit to a jeweller, and Bram had stopped eating entirely and was looking at Zale with the face of a man who has been told where a seam of something runs and is trying very hard not to ask how deep.

Zale did not notice, or noticed and let it go. He went on to Kesrath's harbour dues, which were a scandal.



The rest of it came out with the black drink, when the ducks had gone and the candles were half down, because Ferra asked about the west.

She asked about it the way she asked anybody about anything: what it cost, and how far, and who had it. And Zale, who had been asked about the west in every port from Emberfell to the spice coast and had never once been asked by anybody who wanted the answer in a column, told her; and it went on for an hour.

The bird islands, first. Islands so white with the dung of the sea birds that nested on them that you could see them white from ten miles off, and smell them a day before you could see them; and the islanders went out to them in boats and dug the stuff, and carried it home in baskets for their gardens, and grew yams in it the size of a child. Emeric thought: guano. He said nothing. Ferra wrote BIRDS and a question mark.

The cane islands, where the tall grass Zale had brought in the moss grew in fields to the horizon, taller than a man on a horse, and the islanders cut it and chewed it and boiled the juice down in iron pans into a brown sticky sugar, and let what was left ferment in the sun into a drink that would take a man's legs off. Emeric thought: molasses. Rum. Ferra wrote CANE, and under it, after a glance at Maren standing in the door with the coffee pot, SPIRITS.

The hillsides of the tree that would not rot. Whole hills of it, tall, straight, with leaves like a man's two hands together; the islanders built their boats of it and their houses, and both lasted fifty years, and the worm that ate every ship in the western sea to lace in three years would not touch it. Emeric thought: teak, and the deck of every hull Fletcher would ever lay, and did not let his face move.

The trees the drink came from, the thick dark sweet one Zale had brought at midwinter: pods growing straight out of the trunk itself, not on the branches, as if somebody had stuck them on, yellow and red and the size of a man's two fists.

And then, far out, past the last of the cane islands, a water stone.

"In a channel between two islands," said Zale, "where the current runs like a river even when the wind's dead. Deep. The divers there go down on a rope with a stone in their arms, and they say it's on the bottom, and it's as big as a cart. A water stone as big as a cart. Glowing, a bit, if the light's right." He turned his cup round on the table. "Nobody can lift it. Nobody's tried very hard; the current would take a ship off its anchor. But the fish round it are twice the size of anywhere else. A man took me out to look at it in his boat once and I looked down and saw a fish under the boat the length of the boat." He drank. "I didn't go swimming."

The table was quiet. Armand, in his chair by the fire with the rug on his knees, had opened his eyes.

"As big as a cart," said Armand.

"So they say. I've not seen it. I've seen the fish."

Emeric did not look at Armand, and felt Armand not look at him. A water stone grew by its water; it was sized by how much water lay round it and how hard that water moved. Every stone in every river in Randillia was the size of a walnut or a hen's egg. A stone the size of a cart meant a current out there like nothing in Randillia, or water out there that was something more than water, or both; and if one stone out there was that size, he did not know what else was.

"There's a tree too," said Zale, reaching for the bread, as if remembering it. "Out past the last of the cane islands. Hard as iron and greasy, like it's been sweating. Little blue flowers." He tore a piece off. "The islanders make clubs from it. And mallets."

Emeric set his cup down.

"Blue," he said.

"Short petals. Pale. Why?"

"Does the wood float?"

Zale stopped chewing.

"How do you know it doesn't?" he said.

Emeric did not answer. He did not say the name he had for it, either, because the name was in a dead language from a world nobody at that end of the table had heard of, and Zale would ask what tongue it was, and he had no answer for that he could give to his brother. He thought about it instead. About a ship's shaft turning in the sea through a hole in her stern, and the bearing round that shaft, under the water, where no grease would stay and no oil could be got at: a bearing of a wood so hard and so full of its own oil that it ran in the sea for thirty years and never needed anything; and about a diesel he had not yet drawn, and every ship after it; and about how many trees that would take.

"How far out?" he said.

"Far enough that I'm not taking you." Zale reached for the bread again. "You've got the face Mother gets before she buys a boat."

Across the table, Ferra had already started writing.



"You'll go past them?" said Ferra. "The last islands?"

It was later. The candles were stubs. Armand had been taken up to bed by Imani, protesting, and Bram had gone down the hill, and Saban sat with his fourth cup of the black drink, at peace. Zale had the walnut case on the table in front of him with its lid open, the liquid compass in its rings and the sea clock in its brass drum, ticking.

"I'll go to them," said Zale. "I've never gone to them. Nobody I know has. The islanders at the cane islands won't take you; they'll sell you water and cane and their daughters' shell necklaces, and they'll point west and say there are more islands out there, three days, four, and then they'll say there's nothing past those, and not to go." He touched the glass over the clock. "With this I can find my way to them and back and know where I've been, to the mile, every day, by the sun. Nobody's ever been able to do that out there. A man goes west for four days by his guess and turns round and comes back by his guess and he's a hundred miles out by the end of it and the reef has him." He shut the lid on the case. "So I'll go and see. To the last of them. Not past. And come back, and write it down, and the next man won't need to guess."

"Why not past?" said Ferra.

Zale looked at her. Then he laughed, his easy sea laugh, and shook his head.

"Because the islanders say not to," he said. "And I've found that when men who've lived somewhere for a thousand years tell you not to do a thing, it's usually worth asking why before you do it, and it's usually something you'd rather not find out by doing it." He finished his cup. "They say the water gets thick past the last islands. Whatever that means. Sailors' talk." He stood. "And I've a coaster at six."

He went to bed. Ferra sat looking at the door he had gone out of.

"Thick," she said.

"Sailors' talk," said Emeric.

Ferra did not say anything to that. She wrote one word in her book, very small, at the bottom of the page under WEST, and drew a box round it.



There was no wind in the morning at all.

The river lay under a white sky as flat as a plate, steaming a little in the cold, and the lighters at the quay rode on it with their brown sails hanging off their yards like washing; and the Emberfell coaster sat at the end of the quay with her plank down and her funnel cold. Her master, a careful Emberfell man with his coal, meant to take her down on the ebb under her sail and raise steam at the river's mouth for the sea, as he did every winter run, and he stood on her deck with his hands in his pockets looking at the sky and saying a great deal, in the Emberfell way, about men who expected a careful master to burn coal on a river.

Zale came down the quay steps at six with his sea bag and the walnut case under his arm, and the coat buttoned on the wrong side, and stopped at the foot of the plank.

"Corbin's waiting for the drunk," he said. "Still. I said that last time." He looked at Emeric. "He's stopped waiting, I think. Somebody's told him there are boats down here with legs on them. Running up and down the coast at the speed of a horse." He grinned. "He'll not believe it. I'm not sure I do. I've only heard it from a Duvrenne pilot with a bottle." He shifted the case under his arm. "If you've got boats with legs, brother, you might show me one, one day."

"One day."

"One day," said Zale, and went up the plank.

The coaster cast off at a quarter past six. Her men poled her off the quay and the ebb took her, slowly, and she drifted out into the river and turned her bow down it, with her big brown mainsail up and hanging slack from its gaff, and went downstream on the current no faster than a man could walk beside her on the bank. The master said something to the sky that carried to the quay.

Zale went aft. He stood at her stern rail with his back to Kaldo, looking down the river at the white sky over the far bend, and after a while he began to whistle.

It was low and not loud, a sailor's tune, the kind that went with hauling on a rope, a few notes up and a long one down, over and over. On the quay somebody laughed. Every sailor on every river whistled for a wind on a calm morning, and every sailor knew it did not work, and did it anyway, the way a man touched wood.

Emeric had his sense open, as he always had it open now. He had it open on the river without thinking about it, on the coaster drifting away down it a hundred yards off, then two hundred, a dim shape of wood and rope and the bright small notes of her crew; and on Zale at her stern, a deep slow steady note, the deepest on that river, the one he had felt across a table the winter Zale first came, and wondered at.

He felt something gather at the coaster's stern.

He could not have said what. It was too far off, and it was not his, and it was gone almost as soon as he felt it: a drawing-together of something at Zale's place on the rail, like the breath a man takes before he lifts, and then a going-out, forward, over the deck, into the canvas.

A quarter of a mile down the river, the coaster's mainsail filled.

It filled all at once, with a sound that came back up the water like a slap on a table, and swung out to starboard on its boom, round and taut; and the coaster heeled a little, and put her shoulder into the river, and began to walk away downstream faster than the ebb was going. On the quay, the lighters' brown sails hung off their yards like washing. The steam came straight up off the river beside them. Nothing on that river moved but the coaster, and she went away down it with a white curl at her bow, and her master was standing on her deck looking up at his sail with his mouth open.

At her stern rail, Zale had stopped whistling. He did not turn round.

The quay was laughing now, all of it. The lightermen shouted after her that she'd a wind in her pocket and could she spare them a breath; and an old man by the bollards said the sea always looked after a whistler, he'd seen it before, it was luck, and spat in the river for more of it.

Emeric stood at the top of the quay steps and watched the sail go round the bend.

Ward was beside him. Ward did not have mana sense, and had seen a sail fill on a calm morning, which happened, now and then, when a little breath of wind came down off the hills in one place and not another.

"Lucky," said Ward.

"Lucky," said Emeric.

He did not say anything else. He had learned that from Zale, he found, standing there: how a man stood with a thing in his chest he had not decided whether to tell, and let the person next to him call it luck, and went on looking at the river.



He wrote the list that night, in the back of his notebook, at his own table, with the shutter closed.

He had not meant to write it. He had sat down to draw the head tank's valve, and at the end of an hour he was still looking at the first line of it, and turned to the back of the book instead, where nothing was ever drawn, and began to write down what the islands had; and then, because it would not stay out of the page, what each thing was for.

The coffee in Maren's kitchen first, and then the works' night shift and the tabulator room, and then, if Milford could ever be made to grow it, Saban's. The corn for meal and bread, and the pigs and the hens, and starch for the laundry and the paper, and a line under it, hard: never in the ration unless it is soaked in lime first, and a note to himself to tell Maren why, which would be the truth, because the truth was a sickness of the skin and the gut and the mind that came on whole villages that lived on corn and had not been told. The beans for Farris's rotation, and oil, and a sauce Maren would want to hold a year in a barrel. The bark for Imani, and something bitter with fizz in it for the table that he had drunk with gin on his other world on a terrace in summer, and would not mention. The cloves for teeth before they were for cooking. The nut oil for Abloh's soap, the hard white bar that lathered in the hardest water in the four baronies. The gum for a cable under the sea to Emberfell, one day, and for the holes in Kaldo's back teeth, sooner.

And the other half of the page, the things Zale had not brought and could not have. The tree that would not rot, for decks. The birds' islands, for Milford's fields. The cane, for the still house. The pods on the trunk, so that the thick sweet drink did not depend on his brother remembering. The greasy wood with the blue flowers, for the shaft of every ship he would ever build.

The water stone. He wrote it down at the bottom, and then sat looking at it, because it was not a use. It was a question. And he did not know what to write after it.

Ferra came in near midnight with her own book, and read the list over his shoulder without asking, as she read everything; and then sat down across from him and copied it, the half of it she could put a price against, into a fresh page of her own, in two columns: what it would cost to have, and what it would cost not to.

The second column was longer. She drew a line under both, and looked at him.

"It's all out there," she said.

"All of it."

"And nothing here to fetch it in." She closed the book. "Not yet."



Saban's man in Duvrenne wrote back on the twenty-ninth.

He had a man in Duvrenne as he had a man in most places: a factor's clerk in Duvrenne town who had once been very short of money, and was not any more, and who wrote to a cousin in Emberfell every month about the price of pitch, and whose letters went on to Kaldo by the sea lane in Saban's bag. This one had gone to the southern ports of Kesrath across the strait on Saban's money as soon as the coaster's word had come in, and come back, and written.

It was true. All of it. The jars in the back of the chandlers'. The barrels marked as water for the garrison, coming off the island by night. The gully by the old fort, and the well, and the man to work it, and a second well begun. The Duvrenne houses had heard of it in the autumn and were furious, because the burning water off their beaches was their monopoly and Kesrath's oil was lighter and cleaner and cheaper; and the houses' princess had written, he said, to Kesrath's court, in the politest possible terms, asking whether Kesrath's king knew what his garrison on the Isle was doing; and Kesrath's king had written back, in more polite terms still, that his garrison drew water.

Saban read it out at the long table, the six round it, the door shut, Ward at the door.

When he had finished, nobody spoke for some time.

"Well," said Bram at last. "That's that, then."

"That's not that," said Imani. "That's the beginning of it." She looked down the table at Emeric. "You want to go."

"I want to go."

"To the Isle. To the middle of it, by the lake, a mile inside your father's end, and put a well in the ground in the dark, and bring the oil off by ship."

"Yes."

"It's theft," said Imani. "You said so yourself, two years ago, in a saloon with the door shut. I was there. You said it was theft from your father, and it would start a war if Kesrath saw it, and you'd do it anyway one day when there was no other way." She folded her hands. "Is there no other way?"

"There's Duvrenne's beaches," said Saban. "By the cask, at the reeve's posted price, with the duke's tenth paid and a princess at the other end of the duke's tenth who'd sell us a cask a month for the pleasure of knowing exactly how much we needed it." He folded the letter. "And who now, it seems, has a quarrel with Kesrath of her own over the same thing. She'd not thank us for joining it."

"Then there's no other way," said Imani.

"There's not having it," said Ward.

The table looked at him. He stood at the door with his hands behind him and looked at nobody.

"The land is the king's," said Ward. "The Claim is the king's. Whatever comes out of the ground in it is the king's. Taking it is taking from the king." He said it flatly, without heat. "It is the king's son taking from the king. That is still taking."

"Then tell him," said Emeric.

Ward's eyes came round to him.

"Tell him," said Emeric, "if you think he should know. You've written to him before." He held Ward's eyes. "Tell him there's oil in the middle of his island, and that Kesrath's taking it out of the far end already under his tower, and that the moment the court hears, the court will want to send a regiment to the Isle to put a well in, and Kesrath will send two to stop them, and men will die on the sand for it as they always have. Tell him that, and see what he says."

Ward did not answer. The fire settled. Bram, who could not abide a silence at a table, picked up his cup and put it down again without drinking.

"He would say leave it in the ground," he said at last. "And then the court would hear anyway."

"Yes."

"And then the regiment."

"Yes."

Ward looked at the shut door. Then he went back to looking at nobody.

"I will not write it," he said. "Not yet." And then, flatly: "I will be on the ship."

Armand spoke from his chair by the fire, which he did not often do at the long table now; he saved it.

"The balance on that island," he said, "has held for sixty years on one thing. That nobody takes anything out of the middle. It was never kindness. It was that whoever took first gave the other crown its war." He turned his old head toward the table. "Kesrath has taken first. Out of its own end, under its own tower, which it will say is not the middle and is not the Claim; but it has taken. The balance broke in the autumn and nobody's told either king." He looked at the fire again. "When a thing's already broken, the question isn't whether to break it. It's who picks up the pieces, and whether they cut themselves."

"So you'd go," said Bram.

"I'd not go anywhere. I'm eighty-five." Armand closed his eyes. "I'd send someone who doesn't leave marks."

They went round. Bram, for, because Kaldo needed it and every cask from Duvrenne cost what a cask from the Isle would cost in a month, and because a man who had seen what Tess got out of seven casks in a still in November did not need persuading. Saban, for, because Kesrath, selling quietly, could never complain loudly; a crown that swore its garrison drew water could not stand up in any court and say that somebody else was drawing oil. Armand, for, as he had said. Ward, not against; on the ship. Imani, last of the five, for, with her lips pressed together, because she had sat with the alternative in her own head for a week and the alternative was Kaldo going cap in hand to Alina's houses every spring, and she would not have that, for him or for anybody.

"Ferra," said Emeric.

Ferra had not spoken at all. She had her book open in front of her and her pencil in her hand, and she had been writing the whole time; and she finished what she was writing, and put the pencil down, parallel.

"Yes," she said. "On one condition."

"Go on."

"You tell me before you go." She did not raise her voice. She never raised it. "Not after. Not when you come back with sand on your boots and say you went to look. Before. The night before, the hour before, I don't care; but you stand in front of me and tell me where you're going and when you'll be back, and I write it down." She looked at him across the table. "I was told after, once. I've not forgotten it. I'll not be told after again."

"Before," said Emeric.

"Every time."

"Every time."

Ferra wrote it. He watched her write it, in her square capitals, at the top of a fresh page, as she wrote any standing order of the table: a line on its own, with the date, and her initial, so that it could be found again in ten years by anybody who needed to know when it had been agreed and by whom.

Then she looked up at the six round the table, and at him.

"When?" she said.
