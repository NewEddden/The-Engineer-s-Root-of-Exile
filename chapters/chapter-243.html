Chapter 243 - Planted

Armand did not go home.

He went as far as the palace gate with the boy and the lamp, the last of them to leave the table room, and at the gate he stopped. The sleet had given over. The sky had cleared behind it and gone hard and black, and the bridge ran away into the dark toward the keep with its lamps strung along it, and the boy stood waiting with the lamp held up and his breath smoking.

"Go on across," Armand said. "Tell Paskin I have readings to take. I'll sleep in the palace."

The boy looked at him. Boys at the tower had learned not to argue with the grey robe before they learned their letters. He went. Armand watched the lamp go out along the bridge, small and steady, until it was a spark among the others, and then he turned and went back in, along the inner corridor that ran round the drum, to the gallery where the gauges were.

He had said three words at the table. It was mine.

There had been no copy of his letter on the table. He had looked for its place in the row and found the place empty, because there was no copy anywhere in Kaldo. He had written it alone, on a steady morning years ago, to a man who had been a poor pupil of his for one winter forty years before, and sent it alone, and told the table afterwards. He had given no reason in it, because a reason would have been the only thing anybody read. The same total, over three nights. The chamber kept consecrated. He had wanted the boys in the chamber to live through the fourth hour. The only copy in the world was on the cardinal's desk, and it had been read in open council with the other honest pages, and together they had put the king into the Church's keeping.

So he had said it was his, and he had offered to go north and be tried in the king's place, and Emeric had said no. Twice. He had given no reason, which was how Armand knew he had several. The arithmetic did not need saying. Twelve days to the capital in a good season, more in this one. Cold inns. A heart that Imani had told him, plainly, was asking him every day for less. He would have died in a posting house in the second week, and the council would have read the death as a confession, and the boy had seen all of that in the time it took to say no.

Armand had not argued. He had sat down.

That left him the one thing on the table that could not be sent anywhere.

The gallery was a curved walk of stone between the corridor wall and the glass, lit by a single lamp stone at the gauge. The screens were up for the night, hung on their rails outside the panels so that no light from the drum could reach the court. Inside them the dome stood dark and warm, and in the middle bed the tree stood in its own faint light.

It was a forearm high. It had been a seed in the spring. It was a thin grey stem, with the bark just beginning to crack along ridges that would one day, if the drawings were right, be edges; and it had ten leaves, open flat, the way they opened at the line Ferra had painted on the gauge from the number he had brought down out of the band. With the lamps out, the veins held a faint blue-white, like a sliver of charged lamp stone seen through a hand.

He read the gauge because he had come to read the gauge. Then he sat down on the bench against the corridor wall, and that was when it began.

It was not new. He had carried it for three years on stairs and in cold air: a weight behind the breastbone, as if someone had laid a flat hand there and was leaning on it by degrees, then an ache down the inside of the left arm, then, if he was fool enough to keep walking, the jaw. The rule was to stop and let it pass. It passed in the time it took to count a hundred slowly.

He counted a hundred slowly. The hand went on leaning.

He counted another, and at eighty the weight shifted and settled deeper, and the arm went from an ache to something like cold water running under the skin, and he understood with great clarity that this was not the kind that passed.

He took his pulse at the wrist and lost it twice before he could count it. He did not need the count. He had been a measuring man for sixty years and he knew what his own body was telling him. It was telling him tonight.

Well.

He looked at the tree, because it was the thing in front of him, and because for most of a year it had been the thing he looked at when he wanted to think.

One tree. One, in the whole of the world that anyone knew of, grown from the only spare seed Kaldo had, and every seed it gave back would be wanted three times over before it was picked. The wall wanted them. The ships wanted them. Emeric wanted them for things he had not yet told anyone about. And a second tree could only ever come from a seed, which meant spending a stone that could otherwise be spent on something useful, and a dome to grow it in, and a year of flasks. Ferra had done the sum aloud at the table in the summer and nobody had liked it.

Every bound stone in Kaldo was in use, and every one of them would stay in use for as long as the person it was bound to was alive.

He looked down at his wrist.

He wore the band turned inward, as he always had, so that the stone lay on the soft side of the wrist where the skin was thin. Grey. Many soft faces. The lattice on every face, fine as the scratches on his scale plate. He had been told it was carved when he was a boy, and he had told other boys the same, and then last spring he had stood where he was sitting now and watched one of its kind split along its seams in wet earth and stand up out of the ground on a stem, opening like a pair of cupped hands.

After tonight, the stone on his wrist would be bound to nobody. After tonight, it would be a seed.

He thought about being buried with it, and saw the problem at once. A stone in a coffin was a stone in a box, and boxes were opened. Graves were opened. He had lived long enough to know what a grave was worth to a man with a spade and a buyer, and a Hollow Crystal was still a curiosity to every dealer in the kingdom. And it would not wake in a box. It had needed soaking before, under the glass. It had needed wet, and warmth, and rot, and time.

Then he thought, because he had grown up on the edge of a farm and had never entirely stopped being that boy, of the hawthorns.

Every hawthorn on every field edge in the kingdom stood where it stood because a bird had eaten a haw and flown on and dropped the stone where it sat, softened by the bird's gut and planted in the bird's own muck. Nobody had ever put a hawthorn in a hedge. The birds did it. Half the trees on any common were planted that way. It was not tower learning. It was the kind of thing a shepherd knew and never thought to write down.

A seed that is eaten is a seed that is planted.

He laughed, once, which hurt a great deal.

It was not a good theory. It had no control and no second subject and no chance of either. It rested on hawthorns. He had failed pupils for less. But he would not be there to be failed, and if it came to nothing, what it cost was one old man who was already spent and one stone that would have gone into a drawer the day after his funeral to wait for somebody else's wrist. If it came to anything, the table had its second tree. He had given worse reasons for better things. He had also given better reasons for worse ones.

He gave the band the small push it wanted, the trickle any farmhand could have given it, and the clasp let go. He laid it across his knee and looked at it properly for the first time in a long while.

Bram said the bands were one piece now. Bram said it the way he said a thing about metal: in the years on the wrist the stone had grown into its iron, the way the lattice in the forge sank into a stone and grew back over every new face, so that where the grey met the seat there was no line any more, only a place where one became the other. You would not get the stone out without breaking one or the other. Bram had said so at the table, as a reason for leaving them alone.

Armand had nothing to leave alone for. He opened his penknife with his teeth.

He cut at the join. The first time the blade skated off the iron and left a bright score across it. The second time his hand jumped and the point went into the pad of his thumb. He sat for a moment and bled onto his own knee and looked at it with the detachment he would have given a spilled reading. Then he braced his wrist against the brass rail below the gauge, which had been set level and did not move for anyone, and let the rail be steady for him, and worked the point in along the join where the grey went into the iron, a hair at a time, and levered.

On the third try something tore, not loudly. A sliver of iron came away with the lattice still bitten into it, and the stone came out into his palm with a raw bright edge on one face, faintly warm from his wrist.

Write it first. He had made them do it on the programme, three readings on three days before anybody went near the etching floor, so that the baseline would exist before the thing it was compared to. There was a gauge book on the shelf below the rail, Ferra's, ruled in her columns for the readings at three heights. He took it down and turned to the back, past the last of the columns, to a clean page, and found the pencil on its string, and could not at first make the point meet the paper.

He did not head the page. He never headed a thing that was not yet anything.

I am dying. It is the heart. It has not passed in the time it passes, and it will not.

I have swallowed the stone from my band. Do not look for it. It is in me.

A seed that is eaten is a seed that is planted. Every hawthorn in every hedge is proof. It is the only proof I have.

Do not take me to the churchyard. Doran will be hurt by this and I am sorry for it. Put me in the ground beside the tree, outside the glass, as close to it as the footings let you. When you can afford the glass, build a house of windows over me.

If nothing grows, you have lost an old man and a stone that had no other wrist to go to. If something grows, it is the table's.

Tell the tower my heart failed. It is true.

He read it through once. The lines wandered down the page like a first-year's. He did not correct them. Correction was for errors, and these were only his hands.

There was no water in the gallery. He put the stone on his tongue and swallowed it dry, and it went down hard and slow, and he felt it the whole way, a small definite point travelling behind the weight in his chest and past it.

He laid the pencil in the gutter of the book, and left the book open on the bench beside him, and leaned back against the corridor wall with the glass in front of him and the tree's light coming faint through the screens' edges.

The weight did not lift. But low down, under it, where the stone had gone, there was warmth. It spread slowly outward from a point, and he knew it before he could name it, because he had felt it three miles up, on the Hod's deck in the second hour, when the cold stopped mattering and his hands forgot that they had ever shaken.

That was a pity. He would have liked to write it down.

The gallery went dim at the edges, then dimmer, not the way a lamp goes out but the way a room recedes when you have been sitting too long in it. His hands on his knees were a long way off. He could not feel the bench. He thought, quite clearly: So this is how it goes. Not so bad.

And then, because the one thing a delver never has to be is nowhere, he opened his door.

He had not meant to. It was habit, like a man reaching for a rail in the dark. His room was where it always was, the tower study he had furnished it as the first time: the assessment stone in its cup, the ink stain, the burn on the table's edge, the one loose brass corner. And the shelves. Every shelf he had ever put up in there, loaded with everything he had ever looked at with his own eyes, sixty years of it, down to the smudges. Pupils' faces. Scale plates. A boy of nineteen in a chamber gallery in the fourth hour. A hull held level over a river by an old man's open hands.

If he was going to go, he would rather go from here.

He walked along the shelves. He had never walked to the end of them before; there had never been any need. They went on further than they should have. At the far end, where there had only ever been wall, there was a stair going down, and he was a curious man and always had been, and nothing was hurting anymore.

He went down.

Emeric slept badly and woke before the light, which was not unusual, and went down to the gallery to read the gauge before anyone else was up, which was not unusual either.

The first thing he saw was Armand, sitting on the gallery bench with his back against the corridor wall and his chin on his chest, his band lying across his knee and his hands open on his thighs, palms up.

He stopped in the doorway.

He knew before he crossed the floor. He knew it the way you know a room has been empty for days before you have looked in every corner of it. The old man's face was the colour of the gallery stone. There was dried blood on his thumb, and a dark spot on the knee of his trousers where it had dripped. His hands, when Emeric knelt and took one of them, were cold right through, the cold of a thing that has been sitting in an unheated stone passage all night and has not been making any heat of its own.

Emeric held the hand anyway.

He did not say anything. There did not seem to be anything to say to a man who had taught him to put work into a stone and let the stone carry the weight, in a yard in front of twenty people laughing, every morning for eleven days. Who had knelt in the wet in a good coat to look at a clay collar. Who had lifted a hull three feet off a river and walked it twenty yards, and then lain grey on the bank and said: you could have helped. Who had said, over this gauge, that it was a poor way to build anything, and had then gone three miles up in the cold so that it would be built a little less poorly.

Who had said at the table last night, it was mine, and been told no, and not argued.

Emeric had thought at the time that he was sparing him something.

After a while he saw the book open on the bench, and read it, because Armand had left it open to be read. He read it twice. The second time his eyes stopped at the hawthorns and did not move for some time.

Ward was in the corridor. Emeric did not know how long he had been there. He did not turn round.

"Fetch Imani," he said. "Not a runner. You."

Ward went.

Imani came fast, and did not run, because a physician who arrived out of breath was one more patient. She knelt on the gallery stone where Emeric made room for her and did not look at Emeric at all. She took the cold wrist and felt it, and her face did not change. She moved her fingers to the side of the neck, under the jaw, and held them there.

Emeric watched her not count. He watched her start again.

"Emeric," she said, in a voice he had never heard her use. "Put your hand flat on his chest. Under the coat."

He did. Through the shirt the old man's breastbone was warm.

Not the gallery's warmth; there was none. His hands were ice and his face was stone and under Emeric's palm, slow and heavy and perfectly even, like a big engine idling in a shed, something was beating.

"He's not dead," Imani said.

"His hands..."

"His hands have been out on his knees in a stone passage in winter all night. His hands are the last thing he cares about." She had her head down against his chest now, her ear flat to the shirt, one hand up for silence. When she lifted it her face was doing something Emeric could not read. "Sixty in the minute. Even. Not one missed. Deep breath, slow, full, all the way down, the way a sleeping boy breathes. I have been listening to that heart for three years and it has never once sounded like that." She pulled back one eyelid with her thumb. The pupil drew down small against the gallery lamp. "He's in there. He's asleep. He's better than he was at the table last night."

"He swallowed it," Emeric said. "The stone from his band. It's in the book."

She read it on her knees, fast, the way she read a patient's own account of his fever, looking for the one line that mattered. He watched her get to the churchyard, and to Doran, and stop for half a breath there, and go on.

"He wrote this to be found dead," she said.

"Yes."

"He's not." She said it again, flatly, as if it were a correction to the record. Then she put two fingers back on the neck, to be sure, because she was Imani.

Emeric sat back on his heels. For a moment he did nothing but breathe. Then, because the grief had nowhere to go and he did not know what else to do with his hands, he opened his sense.

He had not done it yet. It had not occurred to him to use it on a dead man.

The medium in the gallery was wrong.

He knew this passage the way he knew his own rooms: the faint steady thickness of it, the leak from the dome that no seal quite stopped, the way it lay heavier by the glass and thinned toward the corridor. Now it was thin everywhere. Thin the way the air in the pattern store went thin when Bram ran the Mana Forge and it ate a rack in a day. It was not lying still. It was moving, all of it, slow and steady and in one direction, the way water moves toward a drain.

Into Armand.

Not into his middle, where the stone was; Emeric had half expected that, and there was something there, a knot, a small hard place that was not sitting still the way a stone in a drawer sits still. But the draw was not coming from there. It was coming from all of him, the way heat goes out of a whole body and not only its hands.

And past that, much further in, where Emeric had no right to look and did not look, he felt a door standing open.

He knew that feeling. Any of the six would have. It was the thing you felt at close range when another delver opened into his room: the pressure of it, like a change in the weather. Every delver's door felt a little different. This one was Armand's, precise and dry and slightly impatient. He had felt it a hundred times across the table.

He had never felt it this far down.

"He's delving," Emeric said.

Imani looked up.

"He went into his room. When it happened. I think he went in to..." He stopped, and found he did not want to say it, and said it anyway. "To be somewhere of his own when he went. And he's still in there. He's gone further in than I've ever felt anybody go. Further than I go." He put his hand flat on the gallery stone as if the floor might tell him something. "The room isn't free. It costs by the head and by the work. He's paying for it. That's where it's going. Everything in this gallery is going into him, and it isn't enough. It's thinning already."

"What happens when it runs out?"

"I don't know." He did not soften it. "When a mage runs dry, he stops. When a man in a room runs dry, I don't know what he stops doing."

Ward had come back and was standing at the corridor end with the lamp. "The Hod," he said. Not an argument. He only put it on the table.

"Three miles up, at night, in midwinter, in that cold," Imani said. "It would take an hour to get her off the ground and he'd be on the deck the whole climb. His heart is doing well at this moment and I will not bet that it stays doing well through that."

They were all three quiet.

Emeric looked past her, through the glass.

The screens were still up. Between their edges he could see a strip of the dome's inside, the flags and the beds and the tree in the middle one standing in its faint blue-white light, leaves open flat. Open flat at Armand's line. The number Armand had gone three miles up to read, one night in the summer, at the rail of a ship with a shelf built to his height, and brought down at dawn so that Ferra could paint it on a gauge.

Band density.

"It's here," Emeric said.

Imani followed his eyes and understood at once, and he watched her not like it.

"You go in there for an hour at a time," she said. "You come out and your hands are warm for a day. Nobody has ever been left in it. Not a night. You're talking about weeks."

"It's held at his line. The same as the band." He heard himself making the argument quickly and made himself slow down. "The Hod's crews stand in that air for eight hours at a time, three nights a week, and nobody's marked. He slept a night in it himself. It's thinner at the floor than under the apex, so lying down he'd have the least of it. And nobody is pushing anything into him. That's the difference. The chamber pushes. He's pulling. He'll take what he needs at whatever rate he needs it, and he can't take faster than his room spends."

"You don't know that."

"No," Emeric said. "I don't."

She looked down at the old man, at the ice-cold hands and the warm chest, and put her fingers on his neck once more. Emeric waited.

"I go in with him," she said. "Tonight, and every night until I say otherwise. I'm a band-wearer and I've been up in the Hod; if it marks anybody, it will mark me too, and then at least we'll know. I check his arms and his neck on the hour. If a single line comes up on him, he comes out, whatever it costs him."

"Yes."

"And you don't go into his room."

"I wasn't going to."

"I know," Imani said. "I'm saying it so it's said."

Bram made the litter. He came up from the works with Ward, and looked once at the old man on the bench, and at Emeric's face, and did not ask anything. He went away, and came back inside the hour with two ash poles and a length of sailcloth, and a width in his head that was the width of the pass-box's inner door, less two fingers.

Ferra came across in the grey with her coat buttoned wrong and read the gauge book standing up, all of it, the columns and then the page at the back. When she had finished she tore the page out along the stitching, very neatly, and folded it twice, and put it in the inside pocket of her coat, where she kept the key to the box.

"That's his," Emeric said.

"It's a Circle master asking in his own hand to be buried in unconsecrated ground beside a tree the Church has never heard of," Ferra said, "in the week the Church is reading our paper aloud in council. It's his. It goes in the box." She looked at the cartridge rack by the port. "The pass-box costs us, every time the doors go round. How many times today?"

"I don't know."

"Then I'll count them."

They took him through on the litter, tipped on its side and then level again, stooping under the inner door, Ward at the head and Emeric at the feet and the old man strapped in with his hands folded under the straps. The air on the far side was close and warm and smelled of earth, and had the density of the band in it, the density of the Hod's deck in the second hour, and every hair on Emeric's arms stood up as it always did. He felt Ward's step hitch at the door and then go on.

Imani chose the place: on the flags on the north side of the middle bed, close to the tree, where the bed's stone edge would keep his head and nobody's weight came near the roots. They put down a pallet and a blanket and laid him on it, on his back, with his head toward the bed. His coat. His shoes. His hands folded on his chest, because Imani folded them.

Nobody put any earth over him. That was the only part of the note they did not do.

The doors went round. Bram threaded a cartridge home into the iron collar till its mark met the stop, and the spike took the seal, and behind the glass the dome gave its small sigh as the charge went back into the air. Emeric, outside now with his hand flat on a panel, felt the room fill, and the leaves, which had begun to fold with the doors open, part again.

And felt it start to thin.

Not quickly. Not all at once. But steadily, the way it had in the gallery, moving slow and patient in one direction, down out of the apex and across the flags toward the old man on his pallet, until he could feel the place round Armand as a kind of shallow hollow in the air, the way a stone makes a dip when you set it on a sheet.

Ferra threaded the second cartridge before the hour was out. She wrote the time in the gauge book, in her own column.

Imani sat on a stool at the old man's head with her bag at her feet and her own band on her left wrist, and at the top of the first hour she pushed back his sleeves to the shoulder and turned his head gently and looked at the side of his neck in the tree's light. Emeric watched her through the glass. She looked for a long time. Then she looked up and found his face through the panel and shook her head once.

Nothing. The skin was old and thin and spotted and clean.

He told the tower himself, at noon. He went across the bridge and stood at the foot of the night stair in the keep, where Paskin had sat since morning with the students crowded above him on the steps, and told them that the old master's heart had failed in the night, and that he was sleeping, and that he was at the palace in Mistress Imani's care.

It was all true. It was all of it that the tower could be told.

Paskin took the afternoon class. Emeric heard him from the hall door on his way out, reading the first-years through the lattice exercise in the flat voice of a man who had heard Armand teach it so many times that he knew every word, and who stopped once in the middle of a sentence and did not finish it and started the next.

Then Emeric went back to the palace, because there was a king in the Church's keeping, and none of that arithmetic had changed.

At the end of the night he went down to the gallery with the lamps out and the screens up, and stood at the glass.

Ferra's column said four cartridges since morning. The dome had been taking one a week.

Inside, Imani had fallen asleep on her stool with her chin on her chest, very much as the old man had been sitting when Emeric found him, and he let her sleep. The tree stood in its faint light, ten leaves open flat at Armand's line, the veins holding their blue-white.

Beside the bed the old man lay on his pallet with his hands folded, and the dome's slow patient draw ran down into him the way water runs down into dry ground.

He did not look like a man who was dying. He looked like a man who was very far away, and busy.
