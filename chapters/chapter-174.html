Chapter 174 - The Visitor

He came in at the gate on the last day of March, a little before noon, on foot, and Emeric watched him do it from the line room window in the gate tower, over Tess's shoulder.

He was not what Emeric had expected. Emeric had not known what he expected; something taller, perhaps, or richer, or with a clerk. The man who walked in under the arch with the carters was sixty or a little more, thin, grey, in a priest's dark coat gone brown at the hem with the road and a pair of good boots that had been mended more than once, with a satchel on a strap across his chest and a plain ash staff. He stopped inside the gate and stood aside, out of the carters' way, and looked round the gate yard the way a man looks round a room he has been told about: the lamp on its post, the new tiles on the guardhouse roof, the wire going up the side of the tower to the window where Emeric was standing. He looked at the wire for longer than at anything else.

Then he asked the gate crew something, and Rylan, who was on the gate, pointed him up the street toward the church; and he thanked Rylan, and went.

"Is that him?" said Tess.

"That's him."

"He's small." She went back to her relay. "I thought he'd be bigger."

Emeric did not answer. He had let his sense out as the man walked through the arch, thin, the way he would have touched a stone he did not know, and found him; and the man had a note. Not a mage's note, not the bright clear note of Linnet or the slow heavy one of Paskin. Lower than that, and quieter, and very steady, like a man humming to himself in another room. But a note. And as he had stood in the gate yard looking at the wire, Emeric had felt that note go out from him, not far, a little way round him, over the yard and the guardhouse and the carts, light as a hand passed over a table to feel for crumbs.

It had passed over Tess's chair, in the tower, on the far side of the wall. The chair's stones were shut. Emeric had felt nothing come back off them.

He had held his own note still, down, the whole time, as he had at the receiver's dinner a year ago; and it had passed over him too.



Doran brought him up to the keep the next morning, as a Visitor called on the lord of any parish he came to, and Emeric received him in the long room off the hall in daylight, with the shutters open, as a prince who had hired some mages.

His name was Brother Orme. He gave it at the door, with a small bow that was exactly as deep as a priest of his standing owed a king's son and not a hair deeper, and then he sat where Hale showed him, and accepted a cup of Maren's tea, and drank some of it, and said it was very good, and looked at Emeric over the cup with pale tired eyes that did not seem to be in any hurry about anything.

Doran sat beside him and was happy. That was the thing Emeric noticed first and kept noticing. Doran had come up the hill in his good cassock with his face pink from the walk and was plainly delighted with his guest, as a man is delighted who has had nobody to talk to about his work for twenty-six years and has suddenly been sent a man who wants to hear all of it. They had sat up till one, Doran said, with the book. Brother Orme had read the burials from the plague years and asked about every page.

"Every page," said Brother Orme, mildly. "It's a very good book. Most of the books I read are kept by men who write down what the fee was and not much else." He set his cup down. "Father Doran writes down the cause. In his own words. For twenty-six years." He looked at Doran with something that might have been real affection. "I've learned more about this town from his margins than I would in a month of asking."

Doran went pinker.

"You're very welcome to the town, Brother," said Emeric, "and to anything in it. Father Doran will have told you we're a long way from the capital, and a little strange."

"He's told me you've built a school, a ward, a water works, a railway, and a great many lamps." Brother Orme smiled. "Which I'd heard on the road. And that you're a good lord to the parish, which I hadn't." He folded his hands. "I won't keep you, Highness. I've come to do what I do in every parish on my round. Read the book. Say the office. Sit at the priest's table." He paused. "And this year, at the chamber's request, one thing more. I'm asking every lord on the southern round how many mages he keeps. It's a count, nothing more. The chamber likes to know where the tower's people go when they leave the tower."

"Twenty-one," said Emeric.

Brother Orme looked at him.

"Fifteen students of Master Armand's, who came south to learn from him and stayed to work on my drains and my roads. Two stone mages who charge my lamps, Orrin and Vesk. An earth mage who digs my cuttings, Paskin. Master Ambrose, who puts lightning rods on my towers. Mistress Linnet, who keeps Master Armand on his feet. And Mistress Amesbury, who flies a paper bag over the river on a rope for the fairs." Emeric picked up his own cup. "And Master Armand himself, who was my tutor when I was a boy, and who I don't count, because nobody's ever been able to make him do anything."

Brother Orme had a little book out of his satchel, and a pencil, and was writing. He wrote the number, and then each name, slowly, in a small upright hand, and then looked at what he had written.

"Twenty-one," he said. "That's a great many mages for a barony."

"It's a great many drains."

"Yes." He closed the book. "Father Doran says you pay them the tower's rate."

"Better."

"That's generous." Brother Orme put the book away. "The tower doesn't like generosity in lords. It says it spoils the students." He stood, and bowed the same exact bow. "Thank you, Highness. I'll be a week, perhaps a little more. I walk slowly and I like to see things properly." At the door he stopped, and turned, and said, as if it had just occurred to him: "The fishermen on the road told me about a light. Over your marsh. At night. Moving." He waited. "Have you seen it?"

"Fishermen see a great many lights," said Emeric.

"They do." Brother Orme seemed to consider that seriously. "Most of them are fishermen's lights. Or the moon on the water. Or the marsh itself, which burns blue sometimes, in the autumn, I'm told." He smiled. "I only ask because the fishermen were so sure. Good morning, Highness."

Doran went after him, still pink.

Emeric sat in the long room with his cold tea and let his breath out. Ward, who had stood by the window the whole time without moving or speaking, as a knight stood in his lord's room when there was a stranger in it, came and sat down in the chair Brother Orme had left.

"He didn't look at me," said Ward.

"No."

"He looked at you. And at Doran. And at the window." Ward turned his head toward it. "And at my sleeve."



He counted them all in a week, as he had said he would.

He went to Armand's room on the second day and sat with the students for an hour, and the students told Linnet afterward that he had asked each one where they came from and what their element was and how long they had been in the tower, and had shaken every one of them by the hand, coming and going. He went down to the drains on the third and watched two of them move water for a morning, and asked good questions. He went to the glassworks and stood by the furnace while Orrin held it, and to the stone bank, where Vesk was charging the town's lamp stones in their rack, and to the foot of the tower where Ambrose was up a ladder with a rod. He walked to the second ridge and watched Fennick's lads blow a hole in it, and stood at the edge of the cutting with his hands folded over the head of his staff and his coat white with dust, and came back down the capital road looking, Rylan said, like a man who had enjoyed himself enormously.

He sat with Armand, too, on the second evening, alone, after the students had gone, for an hour; and Armand told Emeric about it the next morning without being asked, which was not like him.

"He asked me how high the old climbs went," Armand said. "As one old man to another. Over the second cup." He had his eyes shut. "He'd read of them, he said, in the chamber's library. A paragraph. That the tower once sent men up to see whether the air was thicker higher up, and the Church stopped it as a waste."

"What did you tell him?"

"The truth. That I was an apprentice and nobody told me anything. That they went about as high as a lark sings, a little higher, and couldn't breathe, and couldn't stay, and came down cold and frightened, and then the order came and they stopped." Armand opened his eyes. "He said that was what the paragraph said too. Then he asked whether I'd ever wondered why." He was quiet. "I said I was an old man and wondered about a great many things, and he laughed, and poured me another cup, and talked about the weather on the coast road."

He did not ask to see Sorrel's balloon. Sorrel took it up anyway, on the fourth day, on its tether over the quay, because it was the day she always took it up, and he stood on the quay among the children and watched it go up with his head back and the same mild interest he gave everything. Then he asked her, when she came down, how it flew. She told him. Hot air. A paper bag and a fire. Any child could do it.

"No mage," he said.

"No mage," said Sorrel, who was one, and said it without a flicker. "That's rather the point of it."

And he asked the eel men about the light. Emeric was on the water stair when he did it, on the fifth day, at dusk, because he had seen Brother Orme going down the quay toward the eel boats and had found he had a reason to be on the water stair. The eel men were mending their traps on the stones at the foot of the quay, four of them, and the Visitor stood among them with his staff and asked them, quite simply, about the light.

"Smugglers, Father," said the oldest of them, without looking up from his trap.

"Do smugglers carry lamps?"

"Not good ones." The old man pulled a withy tight. "This was a good one."

"High up?"

The old man thought about that, and then looked up, at the sky over the marsh, for some while, with his trap on his knees. Emeric, on the water stair, did not breathe.

"Couldn't say, Father," said the old eel man. "It's dark, at night."

Brother Orme thanked him, and gave each of them a copper, and went back up the quay. And as he passed the foot of the water stair he looked up at Emeric, standing there in the dusk, and nodded, pleasantly, as a man nods to someone he has met once, and went on toward the church.



On the evening of the sixth day he went out walking along the river path under the north wall.

Emeric was in the counting room. He felt it. He had been keeping a thin thread of his sense on the Visitor all week, as much of the time as he could without the ache behind his eyes getting into everything else, the way a man keeps half an ear on a child playing in the next room; and he felt the quiet humming note leave the church, and go down through the lower ward toward the river, and turn north along the path at the wall's foot. Toward the shed.

The shed was chained. The ship's necks were shut. There was nothing in it a man could feel from the path, or he did not think so. But the path went right along the shed's river side, under its doors, and the doors were thirty feet high and a hundred long and had no business being on a quiet stretch of river wall unless there was something behind them.

Ward was on the wall walk, on the north stretch, above the shed. Emeric knew it without looking. He had his mother's stone in his coat. He let his sense out through the stone of the keep and down and across the yard and up the face of the north wall, sixty paces, and further, along the wall walk, and found Ward's note, slow and even, a wall on a wall; and leaned on it, the width of a hair.

Ward, he said. He's on the river path. Coming your way. Under the shed.

The quiet went through Ward's note a breath before the words got there. Then, down the long thin thread of it, flat and unhurried:

I see him.

Don't stop him. Don't watch him. Be bored.

I'm always bored, said Ward.

Emeric held it a little longer. On the path below the wall, the Visitor's note came along under the shed's river doors, slowly, as he walked; and slowed; and stopped, under the doors. Emeric felt the man's sense go out, the light hand over the table, and pass over the doors, and through them, and he held his breath in the counting room with Ferra's lamp beside him and his hand flat on her desk.

The hand went over the ship in the dark, over her shut cases, and found nothing it knew what to do with, and came back.

On the wall walk above him, Ward yawned. Emeric felt it go down the thread: not a word, just a yawn, wide and slow, a man on a cold wall at the end of a long day. And then, aloud, on the wall, Ward's voice, carrying down to the path in the dusk, as it would have carried to anybody walking there: "Evening, Father."

And the Visitor's voice, coming up, pleasant: "Good evening. It's a fine big boathouse."

"Fletcher's," said Ward. "Shipwright. Builds the salt boats." A pause. "Sheds for them, anyway. He hasn't built a boat yet that needed one that size. He says he will."

"Ambitious man."

"Talks a lot," said Ward, and yawned again, and walked on along the wall.

The Visitor stood under the doors a breath longer. Then he went on along the path, and round the north tower's foot, and back up into the town by the water gate.

Emeric let the door go. Ferra, across the desk, had not looked up from her book the whole time; but she had stopped writing at the moment he had, and started again at the moment he let go.

"Well?" she said, still not looking up.

"Ward told him Fletcher talks a lot."

Ferra considered that. "Fletcher won't like it," she said, and turned the page.



On the seventh day the Visitor climbed the gate tower stair.

Emeric felt him start up it from the long room. He reached for Tess's note at once, in the line room at the top of the tower, small and quick and very bright, a note he had known since she was fifteen at the back of Bram's forge; and leaned.

He had asked her in February, aloud, at the bench, whether she would let him do this if he ever needed to, because he did not do it to anybody he had not asked; and she had said she would be very annoyed if he needed to and didn't.

Tess. He's on your stair.

The quiet went through her. Then, faint, and slower than any of the six, because she wore no band and had never been in the room, the words came back down the thread, one at a time, like a person speaking through a door with a mouthful of pins:

Good.

Brother Orme came down the gate tower stair an hour and a half later. Emeric did not see him come down. Rylan did, from the gate, and came up to the keep afterward specially to say so. The Visitor had come down very slowly, Rylan said, one step at a time, with his hand on the wall, and stood at the foot of the stair in the gate yard and looked up at the line room window for a little while with an expression Rylan had only ever seen on men who had been kicked by a horse and were waiting to find out where. Then he had gone back to the church without a word.

Emeric went up the stair. Tess was at the bench with three relays in pieces on the cloth in front of her and her book open.

"What did you tell him?"

"Relays," said Tess. "Contacts. Gap. Why brass pits. The posting house at the ninth milestone, where the box leaks. Why a man shouldn't put a relay box on a north wall. Cells. How you clean the zinc." She picked up a screwdriver. "He asked very good questions for the first half hour. Then he stopped asking."

"Did he ask about anything else?"

"He asked about the lights in the school. I told him about filaments." She turned a screw. "Carbon. Then tungsten. I told him about the dies." She looked at him. "I didn't tell him where the dies came from. He didn't ask. He'd stopped asking by then."

"Did he touch you?"

Tess looked at him a little longer.

"He shook my hand," she said. "When he came in. And when he went. He held it a while, going." She went back to the relay. "He's very polite."



On the eighth day he asked to see the keep's undercroft.

It was a proper request, made through Doran, in the proper form: the old castle had had a chapel once, in the undercroft beneath the hall, before the barons built the parish church in the town, and the chamber liked its Visitors to note every place in a parish that had ever been consecrated, even if it was a storeroom now. Doran had mentioned it at supper. Brother Orme had asked whether he might see it. Doran had brought the request up the hill himself, delighted.

There was no refusing it that would not have been louder than letting him see it.

So Emeric took him down himself, with Hale and a lamp, and Doran behind; down the hall stair into the undercroft, which was a long low vault of grey stone, and dry, and full of barrels of Maren's salt pork and Ferra's spare paper and the keep's broken chairs. The chapel had been at the east end, and there was nothing left of it but a stone shelf where the altar had stood and a carved cross on the wall above it that had lost one arm. Brother Orme stood in front of the shelf with his hands folded on his staff and said a short prayer under his breath, and wrote something in his little book, and turned to go.

And at the west end of the undercroft, where the vault went back under the keep itself toward the cellars, he stopped.

Emeric was beside him with the lamp. He felt the man's note change. Not much. A little more attention in it, a little stiller, the way a dog stills when it hears a sound nobody else in the room has heard.

"There's a draft," said Brother Orme.

Hale, behind them, said that there was always a draft in the cellars. The keep stood on the river. When the river was high, the cellars drew.

Brother Orme did not answer. He went a few steps further, into the mouth of the cellar passage, where the vault narrowed and the lamplight went yellow on old stone and new mortar; and stood, and turned his face a little, as a man turns his face to feel which way a wind is coming from on a still day. He put out his hand, flat, toward the passage wall. Not touching it. A hand's width from it.

Behind that wall, past the new mortar and the rubble the renovation had put back in front of it, was the forge's door. Shut. Locked. The forge had been cold for a week. Nothing had been in its chamber since the night of the relay contacts. But the chamber was lined with Ivo's glass, and the glass was sealed in its jacket, and the jacket was as good as Emeric had been able to make it; and it was not perfect, and he had known it was not perfect since the first run. Where the door met the frame, there was a breath. Raw mana, leaking, very slowly, a thread of it. To anybody without the sense, nothing. To a mage, a wind.

He held his own note down so hard it hurt.

Brother Orme stood with his hand out for a long breath. Two.

"It's not in the joints," he said, to nobody, thoughtfully. "It's in the stone."

"It's an old house, Brother," said Hale.

The Visitor lowered his hand. He looked at the wall a little longer, at the new mortar beside the old, in the lamplight; and then at Emeric, beside him with the lamp, quite pleasantly, as he had looked at him on the water stair.

"So am I," he said. "I feel drafts everywhere these days." He turned back toward the hall stair. "Thank you, Highness. That's a sad little chapel. Somebody should mend the cross."

He went up the stair with Doran.

Emeric stood in the cellar passage with Hale and the lamp until the footsteps were gone. Then he put his own hand out, where the Visitor's had been, a hand's width from the wall. He could feel it. Very faint. A thread of cold, moving, like the breath of a sleeping animal through a crack in a door.

"Highness?" said Hale.

"The river's high," said Emeric.

Hale looked at him, and at the wall, and at the new mortar. Hale had been in that house for longer than Emeric had been alive. He did not know what was behind the wall. He had never asked. He had painted STOVE PARTS on seven crates the week before in his neatest hand without asking what was in them, either.

"Very high," said Hale, "for April," and went up the stair with the lamp, and left Emeric in the dark.
