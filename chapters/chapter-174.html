Chapter 174 - Dead Field

Anselm Cray had been a knight for forty years, and in all of that time he had never once been only a man.

He thought about that, sitting his horse in the dark before the dawn at the head of his lord's household on the north edge of the stream ground, because it was the kind of thing a man thought about on the morning of a fight when there was nothing else to do but wait. He had been seventeen when they put him in the chamber in the capital. He remembered the night before it better than the night itself: the bath, and the white shirt, and his father's hand on the back of his neck, and the old knight who had sponsored him saying, After tomorrow you won't remember what it was like to be tired. The old knight had been right. He did not remember. He had been tired since, of course, on long days in the saddle and in the winter wars and in the one real siege he had seen; but not the way a man was tired. Not the way his groom was tired. The tracery had gone up his arms in the chamber like ivy up a wall and across his shoulders and down his back to the waist, silver-grey under the skin, and from that night on there had been a reach in him that other men did not have. A strength that came from somewhere that was not his arms. A quickness that was not his eyes. He had never had to think about it. He had never been without it for so much as an hour in forty years.

He was going to be without it this morning. His grace had explained it to them at the muster, and again last night, round the fire, and the high mage in grey who walked with his grace's household had explained it again in words none of them followed, and Cray had followed enough.

The prince at Kaldo fought with stones. Everybody knew it. He had stones that flew, and stones that burned, and stones that put men on the floor, and stones that talked to each other along a line of towers from one end of the country to the other. The five at Wayford had said demons flew at them in the dark. Dray had said it on the gallows, the story went, until the rope stopped him. And the prince had knights, few but strange, in plate that did things plate did not do.

So his grace had bought stones too. Hundreds. The cheapest in the capital. And the mages would carry them out this morning into the ground between the hosts, and break them, one after another, and the ground would go dead. Everything that ran on a stone would stop in it. The prince's flying stones and his burning stones and his towers and his strange knights: all of it, stopped. And then there would be nothing in that ground but men. His grace's knights, and the prince's farmers.

The dead ground would strip his grace's knights too. Every man at the fire had known that. The high mages had always known it; it was why nobody broke stones in the field. A knight in a dead field was a man in heavy armour on a horse.

But the prince's farmers were farmers in a dead field or out of it. And a man in heavy armour on a good horse, with forty years of the lance in his arms, was still a knight against a farmer, reach or no reach.

That was what his grace had said, round the fire, and they had cheered him.

Cray had not cheered. He had been thinking about the chamber.

The mages went out at first light.

There were fourteen of them, in grey, on foot, with a guard of his grace's foot round them, and behind them a train of handcarts, and in the handcarts the stones. Cray watched them go past the head of the household and out onto the stream ground into the mist. The ground was flat and wet and open, a mile of rough pasture between the woods to the south and the first fields of Wayford to the north, with the stream winding across the bottom of it toward the bridge, and a ridge on the west side with a stone hut on it, and a mast on the hut, and lamps. The lamps were lit. They had been lit all night. He had watched them flicker in the dark, on and off, talking to the next ridge, and the next.

The prince's host was on the south edge of the stream ground with the stream at its back. He could see it now as the mist thinned. It was not much to look at. A long line of men in blue, in three ranks, standing behind a bank of earth that had not been there two days ago, a low bank, the height of a man's waist, running straight along their front for most of a quarter mile. They were standing very still. They had something on their shoulders, long and thin. At either end of the line there were knots of men in plate, on foot, not mounted. Behind the line, a few horsemen. Carts. A cart with a red cloth on it.

"That's all?" said young Harlow, beside him, who was his grace's cousin's boy and had been knighted in the spring.

"That's all we can see," said Cray.

The mages walked out to the middle of the stream ground, where the grey men said the ground was right, a long bowshot short of the blue line's earth bank, and stopped, and spread out along a line from the ridge to the woods, fourteen of them, a hundred paces apart, with their handcarts. Cray watched them take the first stones out. Big pale lumps, the size of a man's head some of them, cloudy, cracked, the kind that lit a tavern or ran a farm pump for a winter and then went in the midden.

The first mage set his stone on the ground and put his hands on it.

It went with a sound like a great pane of glass cracking. A flat white flash, low along the ground, and a crack, and then a thump that Cray felt in his horse's back through the saddle. And then nothing. The mist round the place where the stone had been did not move. It hung there, perfectly still, in a round patch a hundred paces across, as if a hand had been laid on it.

The second mage broke his. Then the third.

They went down the line one after another, crack and flash and thump, crack and flash and thump, the fourteen of them, and then back up it again with the next stones, and then again, and the patches of still mist joined up with each other and spread and ran together, until from the ridge to the woods there was a long band of ground across the middle of the stream ground where the mist lay flat and dead as water in a bowl, and the grass under it had gone a strange flat grey.

Cray felt it come.

He did not see it. He felt it the way a man feels the sun go behind a cloud with his eyes shut: a change in the air on his skin, a cooling. It came toward the household from the band of dead ground in a slow wave as the mages broke the last of the stones nearest the north edge, and it was not cold, and it was not anything he had a word for. It was a leaving.

And he left with it.

He felt the reach go out of his arms. It went the way water goes out of a basin when the plug is pulled, all at once and then the last of it slowly. His arms were suddenly the weight of arms. His shoulders were the weight of shoulders, and under them, on them, round them, was sixty pounds of plate that he had not felt in forty years and felt now, all of it, every plate, every strap, dragging down. The lance in his hand was a tree. His eyes were a man's eyes; the far end of the stream ground went soft and far away. He had a pain in his lower back that he had had for ten years and had never noticed. He noticed it.

He sat on his horse and breathed.

His horse did not notice anything. It stood under him and shifted its weight and blew through its nose, the way it had all morning. It had never had the reach. It was a horse.

All down the line of the household, Cray saw the others feel it. Harlow's face had gone white. Old Brand, who had been a knight longer than Cray, sat very straight with his eyes shut. A man at the end of the line had dropped his lance and was looking at his hand.

His grace's mages were coming back across the dead ground, walking slowly, and two of them were being helped, and one was carried. They had broken more than three hundred stones in a quarter of an hour, and every stone had taken something out of the mage who broke it, and they were grey in the face as their robes.

"Well," said Brand, very quietly, with his eyes still shut. "Now we know."

On the ridge, the lamps on the stone hut went out.

Cray saw it happen. They had been flickering all morning, talking south, and as the dead ground reached the ridge they did not flicker; they went out, all of them together, as if somebody had blown on them. A man came out of the hut onto its roof and stood by the mast looking at them.

At the west end of the blue line, where the line ran up toward the ridge, the knots of men in plate had been standing just inside the edge of the dead ground. Cray had not understood why; it had seemed a strange place to put knights. He saw one of them now, a big man in plate of a pattern he did not know, dark and smooth and close-fitted, with something like a pack on its back, take a step and stumble, the way a man stumbles who has put his foot down expecting a stair that is not there. He saw the big man stand still for a moment. Then he saw him draw his sword and go on standing where he was, with his men round him, all of them on foot, all of them in plate, as if nothing had happened at all.

And behind the blue line, at the edge of the dead ground's reach, sitting a grey horse by the red-cloth cart, there was a young man in a plain dark coat with no helm, and Cray saw him put his hand up to his head as the ground went dead, the way a man does when a door slams, and sit very still in the saddle.

Cray knew who that was. Everybody knew who that was.

The trumpets went.

His grace's horse went forward at the walk, as it had been told to, the whole of it, near a thousand, the household knights in the first line and the vassals' knights in the second and the men-at-arms behind them, out onto the stream ground into the dead mist. Cray's horse went with the rest. He sat it the way he had sat a horse for forty years and felt it for the first time: the jolt of every stride up through his spine into the pain in his back, the weight of the lance dragging his arm down, the helm. The helm was the worst. He had never noticed it before. It closed round his head and cut off half the world, and his own breath roared in it.

They went from the walk to the trot at three hundred paces from the earth bank, and the dead ground was all round them now, the mist lying flat and still on the grey grass, and the thousand horses' hooves sounding strange and dull in it.

The blue line did not move.

Cray could see it now over his horse's ears through the slit of his helm. Three ranks behind the waist-high bank. The front rank kneeling. The second rank standing behind them. The third standing behind the second. The long thin things on their shoulders had come down, all together, and were pointing at him.

He had heard about the tubes. Everybody had. The story from Wayford was of a boom and a whistle in the dark and Dray down in the Staple's yard with his thigh broken, and the grey men had said it was a working of the prince's, air or fire, some trick, and that in the dead ground it would stop like all the rest.

At two hundred paces they went from the trot to the canter, and Cray lowered his lance, and it nearly pulled him out of the saddle.

At a hundred paces, somebody behind the blue line said one word, very loud, in a flat voice that carried across the dead ground as if it were a room.

The front rank fired.

It was not like anything.

He had heard siege engines and he had heard lightning strike a tower and he had heard a high mage's fire go off inside a gatehouse, and it was not like any of them. It was a noise that went on. A roar and a crackle and a roar, from one end of the blue line to the other, not all together, running down the line like fire running down a fuse, and with it a bank of white smoke that jumped out from the whole front of the line at once and rolled forward toward them, and through the smoke, and through the noise, a sound he had never heard in his life, a hard flat smacking sound, everywhere, all round him, like hail on a slate roof.

The horse beside him went down. The one on his other side. Harlow's horse. Harlow, going over its head.

Cray's own horse went on. It was a good horse and it was frightened and it went on, into the smoke, and the voice behind the line said its word again, flat and loud, and the second rank fired.

He did not see what it did. He was in the smoke. He heard it. He heard the smacking sound again all round him, and a horse screaming very close, and a man's voice saying oh in a surprised way, just once, somewhere to his left.

And then the voice said its word a third time.

The ground came up and hit him.

He did not know how long he lay there.

He was on his back on the grey wet grass with his helm half off and his lance gone and something heavy across his legs, and the smoke was lying over him in a flat white sheet, and through a hole in it he could see sky. The noise had not stopped. It went on, farther off now, the roar and crackle and roar, again and again, down the blue line and back again, and under it now a different noise, a long high sound that went on and on, that he understood after a while was horses.

The heavy thing across his legs was his horse. It was dead. It had been shot through the chest.

He got his legs out from under it. It took him a long time. He had never in his life had to get his legs out from under anything; he would have lifted the horse. He could not lift it. He pulled, and pushed with his arms, and his arms shook, and the plate on his legs scraped on the saddle, and he got one leg out and then the other, and lay on his back on the grass with his chest heaving and the sweat running into his eyes inside the helm.

He took the helm off. His hands were shaking so that he could hardly do the buckle.

He sat up.

The smoke was clearing a little, drifting off south and west on a light wind toward the ridge. He could see the stream ground round him. It was covered in horses. Horses down and horses standing and horses running loose with empty saddles, and men on the ground among them in plate, some moving and some not. Brand was ten yards away, sitting up as Cray was sitting up, with no helm, looking at nothing. Harlow was not moving.

The charge had broken. He could see that without being told. What was left of the household and the vassals' line was going back north across the dead ground, fast, in no order, the ones who still had horses, and the men-at-arms behind them had never come on at all; they were standing in a mass halfway across the stream ground, not going forward, not going back. And past them, far off at the north edge, his grace's banner, and round it the grey men, and nobody going to it or from it.

Up at the west end, under the ridge, where part of the vassals' line had swung wide and come in on the knots of plate at the end of the blue line, men were still fighting. On foot, sword against sword, slowly, the way men fight in deep water, knights of his grace's and the prince's knights in their dark smooth plate, all of them as stripped as each other and all of them finding it out. He saw the big man in the dark plate take a blow on the shoulder that drove him down onto one knee. He saw him get up, slower than a man should, and go on.

He tried to stand.

He got to his knees. His armour was the heaviest thing he had ever felt. It was not heavy the way a weight is heavy; it was heavy the way a sickness is, all through him, in every joint. Sixty pounds of plate, made to be worn by a man with forty years of the chamber's reach in his limbs, and there was no reach, and he was fifty-seven years old with a bad back and a man's arms, and he could not stand up in it.

He stayed on his knees on the grass and looked south.

The blue line was forty feet away.

He had not known he was so close. The horse had carried him nearly to the earth bank before it fell. He could see the bank, a waist-high heap of earth and turf, raw and new, and the men behind it. The smoke was thinning off them. They were standing in their three ranks as they had stood before, a little ragged now, with gaps in them here and there where a man was down or had been carried off, and they were doing something with their hands.

The one nearest him was a young man. Twenty, perhaps. Big-shouldered and fair and red in the face, in a blue coat that did not fit him well across the back, with a cut on his cheek that he had not noticed. A farmhand. Cray had seen a thousand like him at every harvest on his grace's lands, in a smock with a scythe. This one had the long thin thing, the tube, stood upright on the ground in front of him with its end in the air, and he was not looking at Cray. He was not looking at anything. His eyes were on his own hands.

He took a paper thing out of the pouch at his side. A small tube of paper, twisted at one end. He put the twisted end in his mouth and bit it off, and spat. He tipped a little of what was in the paper into a pan on the side of the long tube and shut the pan with his thumb. He upended the paper over the tube's mouth and poured the rest in, and pushed the paper in after it, and drew a long thin rod out from under the tube and rammed it down, twice, hard, and drew it out, and slid it back under. And lifted the long tube to his shoulder, and stood.

He did it in the time it took Cray to draw three breaths. He did it without looking. His hands did it. They did it the way Cray's hands had once couched a lance, in the days when he had first learned and had had to think about it, before the chamber had put the knowing into them for him.

Nothing in the dead field had touched him. Nothing had been turned off in him. There had been nothing in him to turn off.

The voice behind the line said its word again, far off down toward the east end.

The farmhand did not fire. He stood with the tube at his shoulder and his cheek against the wood of it, looking north, over Cray's head, at the men-at-arms in their mass in the middle of the stream ground, waiting to be told.

Cray knelt on the wet grey grass in sixty pounds of plate he could not stand up in, with his dead horse behind him and his helm in his lap, and watched a farmhand bite a cartridge, forty feet away, without looking at him.

He had been a knight for forty years, and he had thought all his life that he knew what a knight was for.

He knew now.
