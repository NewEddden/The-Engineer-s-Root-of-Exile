Chapter 34 - One in Two

Armand came up the Wayford road on the fourth of December on a hired cart with a driver he had clearly been arguing with for eleven days, and four crates, three of which were books.

Emeric was on the wall when the boy called it, and got down the stair faster than a lord ought to, and stood in the yard while the cart came in and found that he had no idea what he intended to say.

What he said was, "You're late."

"I am thirteen months late and I have spent eleven days composing an account of why, and I would like to deliver it sitting down, indoors, with something hot in my hands, because I am seventy-one and that cart has a flat wheel." Armand climbed down in stages. "Also I have brought you a great deal of paper and I would like it out of the weather before I am."

He looked round the yard while the crates came off. The new stone at the gatehouse. The graded approach going away down the hill past the whitewashed stones. The smoke standing up straight off the eastern arm in the cold, which was one furnace and looked from the castle like three.

"Well," he said.

"Say it properly."

"I shall say it in about a week, when I have stopped being impressed, because impressed is not a useful state and I have been in it since the ford." Armand pulled his coat straight. "I will say the first part now. I came down this road on a crown circuit the spring before you were sent here, and I did not stop, because there was nothing here to stop for and everybody knew it. I have been sitting in that cart since the junction trying to work out which turning I have taken wrongly."

The account of why he was late took an hour and it had four things in it and the third one was the interesting one.

The tower had not let go easily. Armand had spent a year handing a thing to three men that he had built to be held by one, which was his own fault and he said so, in the tone of a man who has written the sentence out already. There had been a winter of getting a successor confirmed, and a spring of discovering the successor was wrong, and a summer of the second one.

"And I wrote to you," he said. "Four times. The last in July, which was a shorter letter than the others and said, in effect, that I had begun to feel foolish."

"I had one."

"Which one."

"March. The one about the tower and the three men, and you asked whether the offer was still standing and told me not to be gracious about it." Emeric had it in the box in the long room and went and got it, because Armand's face had done something. "That's the only one that came."

Armand read his own letter for a while.

"I wrote in March, in May, in June and in July," he said. "And I had two from you, and the second of them reached me in September and was dated in April, and I concluded from that that the roads were what the roads have always been, and then a boy told me in a Wayford inn eleven days ago that there had been a war on this plain in August, and I concluded that as well." He put the letter down. "That is the sum of my correspondence with you over a year, and I have just heard it out loud for the first time, and it is worse than I thought it was when it was happening to me in instalments."

"There was a war on this plain in August."

"Yes. I have been told about the war eleven times since the junction and in eleven different shapes, and I will want the true one, and not tonight." Armand rubbed his eyes. "Tonight I would like to see the stone."

He stopped in the doorway of the long room before Emeric had got the box open.

He stopped the way a man stops when he has put a foot on a stair that is not there, and he stood in it, and he turned his head slowly the way a man does when he is trying to find a sound in a room.

"Who else," he said.

"Who else what."

"Do not do that. I have known you since you were four and you are stalling, and the stalling took you about a second and a half, which is the fastest I have ever seen you do anything dishonest." Armand had not come in. He had his hand flat on the door frame. "There has been working in this room. Not tonight. For a long time, repeatedly, by more than one person, and it is in the stone of the wall and it is in the table and it is not a class of working I have a name for, and I have had a name for everything since I was thirty."

Emeric sat down on the edge of the table.

"Say what you can read," he said. "Exactly. Not what you think it means."

"I can read that it happened. I can read roughly where in the room and I would say the table and the chair at that end. I cannot read what was done, or by whom, or when beyond a season, and I would not have noticed it at all outdoors or in a room I had not walked into cold." Armand finally came in and sat down heavily. "That last part is not comfort. I noticed it at a doorway after eleven days on a cart. A man who was looking would find it from the yard."

"How many men can do that."

"Read a residue? Any competent mage at close range, if he is paying attention, and most are not, because there is nothing in the ordinary world that leaves that shape and so nobody is in the habit of looking for it." Armand was watching him. "Recognise it as delving, and know what he is looking at, and know who to tell? That is a much shorter list and it is not a list I am on the top of. The people who keep records of such residues are the temple's people. They have a house for it. I have met two of them in fifty years and I found them both unremarkable, and I mention that because you are about to ask me whether they are dangerous and I do not know."

"How long," Emeric said. "Say it as a date."

"Say what as a date."

"How long have I been findable."

Armand did the arithmetic in his face rather than out loud.

"Since the first time you did it," he said. "Whenever that was. I am sorry. There is no version of this where the answer is recently."

It had been a cold room and the second week of November, in his first autumn, and he could put a date on it because Ferra had put a date on everything since.

He had spent thirteen months building in the open. He had taught it to a smith and to a woman who kept the barony's books. He had used it on a man's arm in July and on a broken femur in August and on forty men after that, in a church, with the doors open, in front of a priest who wrote to a diocese.

"I've been careful about it," he said.

"You have been careful about who you told. That is a different thing entirely and I would like you to hear that I am not scolding you, because I have spent one hour in this barony and I can see that nobody told you, and nobody told you because there was nobody to tell." Armand held his hand out. "Give me the stone. I have waited a year for this and I have just spoiled it for both of us, and I would like to spoil it a little further by asking my question first."

"Ask it."

"Eleven tests over nine years on four stones of that class and I found nothing in them, and I wrote that down, and I was correct." He turned the crystal over in his fingers and his hands were entirely steady, which Emeric noticed. "I want to know what I was doing wrong. Not what you did. What I did, and why it was wrong, in the words a man uses to a student, and I would like you not to be kind about it."

Emeric told him.

It took most of two hours and it was the first time he had said the whole of it in order to anybody, and about a third of the way through he understood that he was being taught how to explain it by a man who had spent forty years listening to people fail to explain things.

Then he took him in.

It was the guided kind, with Emeric holding it open the way he had for Bram and for Ferra, and it lasted a short while and Armand said almost nothing at all in it, and when they came out of it the old man sat with his hands on his knees and looked at the wall of a long room in a barony for some minutes.

"It holds," he said at last.

"It holds."

"Without anybody in it."

"Ferra put a thing in there in June. It's been in there since June."

"Yes." Armand's voice was doing something he was not letting it do. "I would like a moment, and then I would like to say the unkind version, because you will get it from somebody eventually and I would rather it was me. I stopped at eleven tests because eleven tests was a schedule I set myself at thirty and completed at thirty-nine, and a completed schedule feels like an answer. It is not one. I have spent forty years telling students to distinguish between having finished looking and having found nothing, and I could not have told you, until an hour ago, that I had done the thing I warn them about." He breathed out. "There. That is the whole of it and I will not say it again, and if I become tiresome about it you may tell me."

Emeric let it sit, and then made a mistake, which was to move to the thing he had been carrying since a courtyard thirteen months earlier because he thought it was the easy one.

"Then here's the other half," he said. "The floating gallery. Ten men at any hour holding a slab of stone in the air with their hands on a crystal, and ten more behind them waiting to step in when a grip goes, and it has been that way for two hundred years, and every one of those men is standing there because the Circle never once considered that a stone might be able to hold a thing on its own." He heard his own voice getting away from him and let it. "That's not a gallery. That's a room full of men spending their lives being a bracket."

Armand looked at him for a moment.

"You have had that sentence in your mouth for a year," he said.

"Thirteen months."

"Then I am sorry about this as well." He set the crystal down on the table between them. "The three floors below the gallery hold themselves. They were bound in the ordinary way in the reign of the third Harrold and they have not been touched since and they will be there when the tower is not. The gallery could have been built the same way in an afternoon by any competent binder in the last two centuries, and about nine of them have written papers proposing it, and every one of those papers is in the library, filed, read, and answered."

"Then why is it held by hand."

"Because holding it is the point of it." Armand said it gently. "It is not architecture. It is the apparatus. A man grips that crystal for an hour, and the hour is at the very edge of what he can do, and he is relieved the moment before he fails, and he does it again in four days. He does that for ten years. At the end of ten years he is not the man who started, and he is not the man who started by a margin that nobody in the Circle considers arguable, and that is what the gallery is for and it is what it has always been for." He tapped the table once. "It is a bench in a training yard, Emeric. You have been looking at a room of men lifting weights and pitying them for not owning a cart."

Emeric sat with that.

He had built a year on a premise and the premise had been that this world had never turned round and looked at its own tools, and it had been correct about wells and about a foot of iron and about a man with his fist in the grain, and he had generalised from three correct instances the way he would have flogged a junior engineer for generalising, and had done it silently, where nobody could catch him at it.

"Mana trains," he said.

"Mana trains. Like a muscle, is the usual comparison, and it is a good one as far as it goes." Armand poured himself the last of the hot water. "It is the least surprising fact in my discipline. Any acolyte will tell you. It is on the second page of the second book I ever gave you."

"Then why isn't everybody strong."

Armand set the cup down.

"Say that again."

"If it trains, then it trains for everyone. There are what, two hundred thousand men in this kingdom who do something with their hands all day. If pushing a thing to your limit and being relieved before you fail makes you stronger over ten years, then the whole of that ought to be visible in the ordinary population somewhere, in somebody, and it isn't, and nobody in this kingdom walks around able to do what a man off that gallery can do." He was doing it out loud now and could not stop. "So either it doesn't train the way you've just said, or something else decides it before the training gets a chance, or somebody is doing the training on purpose and doing it fast, and I've never once heard how."

"You have heard how," Armand said, with the first real puzzlement of the evening. "You have watched it. Twice."

"Then say it, because I haven't got it."

"The rite," Armand said.

He said it the way a man says a word like harvest.

Emeric had a memory arrive, whole, that was not his and was entirely his: a flagged courtyard in the capital in early summer, and himself at seven or eight standing at the edge of it in stiff clothes, and Zale coming out of a low stone building on his own feet with a man on either side of him not touching him, grey to the lips, and the whole courtyard applauding.

"Tell me what it is," he said. "As if I don't know."

"You do not know?"

"I have never once been told and I have never once asked. Tell me as if I'm a foreigner."

Armand did, and he did it flatly and completely, in about four minutes, the way a man describes something so well known that describing it feels faintly absurd.

A stone, flat, of a common class, prepared by the operator. Pure mana pushed through it into the candidate in waves. Load, hold, release. Load, hold, release. The intervals are the operator's to judge and the judging is the whole of the trade. It runs for a season for the highborn, in the year a boy turns fifteen, at a house that has done it for the family for generations. It runs for a few months for the crown's conscripts, in batches, wherever the crown needs men.

"And the loss," Emeric said.

"About one in six of the highborn. A further tenth are left reduced, and are found something to do." Armand said it without any change of tone at all. "The conscript schedule is heavier because it is shorter. One in two."

"One in two."

"About that. It has been about that for as long as there have been records."

Emeric put both hands flat on the table.

"What do they measure," he said.

"Measure?"

"During. What does the operator measure. What's on the paper at the end of a session."

"There is no paper. There is nothing to measure." Armand looked genuinely at a loss. "The operator judges the wave, and the interval, and the man in front of him, and he judges it by feel, which is why it is a trade and not a study, and why it is learned by standing beside somebody for nine years and not by reading. A good operator can tell you a great deal about a candidate by looking at him. He could not tell you a number about any of it, and there is no number, and I am not sure what a number would be of."

"Then how does he know it's going wrong."

Armand opened his mouth, and closed it, and his face did the thing that Emeric would remember about the whole evening afterward, which was that it did not change very much.

"The candidate dies," he said.

The fire moved. Somewhere below, the second watch was changing, and the boots went along the wall in the order they always went.

"Say that again," Emeric said.

"That is how it is known. That is the sign. Before that there is nothing that distinguishes a hard session that a man will come through from a hard session that he will not, and every operator alive will tell you the same and they are not lying to you and they are not stupid." Armand had his hands round the cup. "I understand what you are asking me. You are asking me why it has not been improved."

"Yes."

"Because there is nothing to improve." He said it kindly. He said it in the voice he had used for forty years to explain a settled thing to a bright boy who had got hold of the wrong end of it. "The rite does not kill knights, Emeric. It fails to make them. A man who comes out of it is a man the rite has found. A man who dies in it was never going to be a knight, and the rite found that out in a season, cheaply, before the crown spent nine years and a suit of harness on him and before he stood in a line and got the men beside him killed." He set the cup down. "It is a hard thing and nobody pretends otherwise. It is not a cruelty. It is the most honest test in the kingdom, and the dead are the proof that it is honest, and the men who come through it know exactly what they are and so does everyone else. That is what the rings are for."

"The rings."

"On the arms. You have seen them on your brothers a thousand times." Armand made a small circling motion above his own forearm. "A knight's rings are how the world knows the rite was performed and survived. There is no other proof and there does not need to be one. A man with rings has been through it. A man without has not."

Emeric was quiet for long enough that the old man began to look concerned.

"Can you perform it," he said.

Armand's eyebrows went up, and for the first time all evening he was visibly, faintly, offended.

"I am the head of the royal mage tower," he said. "I was, for nineteen years. I can describe the rite to you in more detail than the man who runs it, and I have read every paper written about it since the Conquest, and I have advised two kings on it." He shook his head slightly. "No. Of course I cannot perform it. It is a trade. It is held by about forty families and it is taught by apprenticeship and a man is credentialed to it by the men who trained him, and I have no more standing to run a rite than I have to shoe a horse. I would not be permitted to stand in the room."

"So the men who know how it works can't do it, and the men who do it can't say how it works."

"That is a strange way to put a perfectly ordinary arrangement," Armand said, "and I have been sitting here for two hours failing to guess which of the things I say is going to be the one you fix on, and I have not guessed right once."

"Last thing." Emeric had not moved. "I never took it."

"No."

"Why."

Armand took a moment over it, and did not soften it, which was the only mercy available and he seemed to know it.

"Because you tested at a tenth of what your brothers tested at, at six years old," he said. "The rite is not run on a boy who tests where you tested. It is not withheld out of cruelty, it is withheld because at that reading the one in six becomes something a good deal worse, and a house does not spend a son that way." He put his hands flat on his knees. "That was the reasoning, and I heard it given, and it was given by people who believed it. And by the year it would have been argued over, your mother was dead, and your father had four other children and a kingdom, and there was nobody in that room whose particular business it was to argue for you. I was in the room. I did not argue. I thought the reasoning was sound and I still think the reasoning was sound, and I have thought about that room a great deal more since your letter reached me in September than I did at the time."

Emeric got the list out that night and left the space empty at the top, and wrote underneath it:

Findable since a cold room in November of my first year. Not since August, not since the church. Since the first night. He read it off a door frame after eleven days on a cart and told me a man who was looking would find it from the yard, and then told me that the people who keep the records of such things belong to the temple, and then told me he did not know whether they were dangerous, which is the only honest thing anybody could have said and is no use to me at all.

The gallery holds itself in every floor below it and has done since the third Harrold. Nine papers proposing the obvious, all filed, all answered. It is a training bench and I have been pitying them for thirteen months and telling myself it was evidence about how this world thinks. It was evidence about how I think. Three correct instances and I generalised, silently, where nobody could catch me.

One in two. Not a secret, not a scandal, not a thing anybody is hiding. Armand said it to me across a table in the tone he uses for the price of wheat, and he is the kindest man I have ever known and he was not being cruel and he did not notice he had said anything.

They measure nothing. There is no paper. The operator works by feel and the signal that it is going wrong is that the candidate dies, and I made him say that twice and he said it the same way both times.

I asked why nobody has fixed it and the answer was that there is nothing to fix. The dead were not knights. The rite found that out cheaply. I have been turning that over for three hours and the thing I keep arriving at is that if a man came to me tomorrow and told me half the boys in a batch die and that it is the test working, I would need about four days and a room and a set of records to take that apart, and I would still be arguing with a man who is not lying, is not stupid, is not cruel, and has a hundred years of dead boys as his evidence.

I am not going to write down what I am thinking about, because I have not thought about it properly and because writing it down makes it a thing I have decided. I will write down the one part that is not a plan. There are forty families in this kingdom who hold the only knowledge that matters here, and none of them can say a word about how it works, and the man who can say how it works is not allowed in the room.
