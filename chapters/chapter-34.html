Chapter 34 - The Yield

Nothing broke.

That was the first thing Emeric noticed about the harvest and it took him most of the first week to stop waiting for it to stop being true.

The drum rode the load. He had spent two days last autumn standing over it while it bound and stalled and bound again, and this year it took whatever was fed into it and did not care, because the correction had been made and the correction had held. The drying shed held its mark. The bins got walked before they were sealed, all of them, by two men with a rod and a list, and the list came back to Ferra with nine ticks on it and nothing else.

Farris ran the threshing floor and did not need Emeric standing on it.

That was the entire point of three years of work and Emeric said so to nobody at all. He came out on the second morning, stood at the edge of the floor for about four minutes while nothing whatever happened that required him, and then went back over the bridge and did something else, and if there was a better day in his life in this world he could not have named it.

The yield was not a miracle and he was careful about that in the book, because the book was going to outlive the year and somebody might read it and get the wrong idea.

Nine divisions instead of five fields worked to death. Manure carted rather than left in a heap where the cattle happened to be. Water where water was wanted and, more to the point, not where it was not. Seed graded through a sieve so that what went into the ground was what should have gone into the ground.

There was nothing in any of that a Kaldo farmer could not have understood twenty years ago, and most of it he would have agreed with, and none of it had been done because nobody had ever had a reason to think the ground could be different from the way the ground was.

It came in at roughly double what this land had given the year Emeric arrived.

The surplus was not his and he was rigid about it.

The land was worked by the men who worked it and the grain on it was theirs. That had been true before he came and he had not changed it and did not intend to. What existed now was the venture, which was a pooled arrangement that men had opted into with their eyes open: the barony carried the risk on seed, on tools, and on water, and took a share of what came off the ground for having carried it. A man who wanted no part of that kept his own field, kept his own seed, kept every grain he grew, and dug his own ditch.

Several had. Ferra had the names of both lists and read neither out.

Emeric never in his life authorised the sale of another man's grain, and the distinction mattered to him in a way that he suspected would have been invisible to any other lord in the kingdom.

Hollis had stayed out.

He had stayed out loudly in the first year and quietly in the second, and he had been right to, both times, for reasons that had nothing to do with being stubborn. He had eleven acres, no sons old enough, and a wife who had been ill the autumn the venture opened, and a bad year inside the venture would have been survivable and a bad year outside it would not, which meant he could not afford to gamble on either.

He came and found Emeric at the end of the season, at the gate, in the rain, and said it standing up with his hat in his hand.

"I'll come in next year."

"Good."

"I want it said that this is not an apology." Hollis had a way of making a sentence sound like it had been carried some distance. "I did what I could afford to do. You would have done the same and I would think less of you if you hadn't."

"I know."

"And I'd want the same terms as a man who came in the first year. Not better. Same."

"You will get the same."

Hollis nodded once, put his hat on, and went, and it occurred to Emeric watching him go that Hollis was the only man in the barony who had ever negotiated with him on a footing and had never once appeared to notice he was doing it.

Ferra's sheet that autumn was the first comfortable one she had ever produced.

She read it out in the small room off the hall with the window open because the kitchen was smoking again. Seed for spring, whole, set aside and sealed. Reserve for winter, at the full number, not the number they had been calling full for two years. The town fed, every hearth, with the pauper allowance standing and no argument about it. And a surplus sitting on top of all three that was not a rounding error and was not borrowed from next March.

She got to the end of it and stopped and neither of them said anything.

Two and a half years earlier this barony had been burying people it could not feed, in rows, in ground the church would not release, four hundred in a book and nine hundred in the world.

"Read the last line again," Emeric said.

She read the last line again.

"Thank you."

"It will not stay like this," Ferra said, which was as close as she came to enjoying anything.

"No. But it did happen."

And then none of it left the barony, and that was the part nobody noticed.

There was no buyer worth the cartage. Kaldo sat a long way from anywhere that wanted grain, the roads were what they were, and every bushel that went onto a cart lost a fraction of itself to the journey and another fraction to the man at the other end. More to the point there was no reason. This was a town that had spent a decade short of food, in living memory, in the memory of the children currently carrying sacks. You do not sell food out of a place like that. You put it in a cellar and you look at it.

So the best harvest in three generations happened inside Kaldo, was eaten and stored inside Kaldo, and appeared in no market, no ledger, no scale house and no conversation anywhere outside the barony's own walls.

Saban, who sold everything the barony sold, sold none of it, and therefore said nothing about it anywhere, because a merchant talks about what he is carrying.

Not one man outside Kaldo learned a single thing about the farm that autumn.

What Emeric did instead was look at the foundry.

It was running at somewhere under half of what it could run at, and it had been all year, and the reason was ore. The diggings above the town thinned a little more every season, which everyone had known for forty years and nobody had ever done anything about because there had never been anything worth digging for. Now there was a building with three furnaces in it and a pattern shop and two apprentices who could pour, and it sat idle four days in seven because there was nothing to put in it.

So he placed the largest ore order in the history of the barony.

He was cheerful about it. That was the honest shape of the thing and he would remember it later with some care. He had money for the first time, the foundry was finally worth feeding, and buying ore from a hill that exists in order to sell ore is the least remarkable transaction available in the region. Men had been doing it in that valley since before there was a Randillia.

Ferra brought the rate to him with the order sitting under it.

"This has moved three times," she said.

"I know."

"I want to be precise, because I do not think you have looked at it the way I have looked at it. It has moved three times and it has moved in one direction. The first was the spring the wheel went in and I accepted it, because the whole region moved that spring. The second I accepted with a note, and the note is in the book and you initialled it. This one I do not accept."

"On what grounds."

"On the grounds that there is no season in it." She put a finger on the sheet without looking down. "A price moves for a reason a man can name. A bad winter. A shaft flooding. A war somewhere buying everything. Every other rate I hold has an explanation attached to it in four words or less. This one does not have four words. It does not have any."

He heard her. That was the thing he would keep coming back to afterward, at some length, in the dark, for years. He did not brush her off and he did not fail to understand her. He heard every word and he understood all of it and he weighed it and he answered.

"It is the cost of the relationship," he said.

"That is not a reason. That is a way of agreeing to something."

"It is a reason. There is one hill in this region with iron in it and one man who owns the hill, and I need what is in it for the next twenty years. He knows that. He is charging me for knowing it. Every supplier in the history of trade has charged a man for knowing it." He signed the order. "I would rather pay four percent I cannot account for and have the ore than argue about four percent and have a cold foundry."

Ferra looked at him for a moment.

"You have been right about a great many things this year," she said.

"Is that an agreement?"

"No. It is an observation about the year."

And he let it go, and she let it go, and the truthful account of why he let it go is not flattering and he was capable of giving it to himself and did not give it to himself until much later.

He had won every argument in that room since the spring. The tower, which the town had laughed at and which had doubled a division. The pipe. The knife and the empty hand. He had been right about the food, right about the recovery, right about the crossbows, right about the pattern shop, right often enough and publicly enough that being right had stopped being an event and become a climate.

The last argument he had lost in that room had been about Armand, on a dark evening on the western main, and he had not had an answer then, and the way he had dealt with having no answer was to stop having the conversation.

A man who has been right eleven times in a row is not more likely to be right the twelfth. He knew that. He could have written the paper on it. He had written something extremely like it in another life for people who did not read it.

He signed the order in the tone of a man agreeing to a minor expense.

The letter went out with it, and the letter was his own idea.

To Baron Renner of Grayrock. Ordinary courtesy, ordinary terms, a neighbour proposing that two men who had been trading through clerks for two years might usefully sit at a table. He offered to travel. He offered a date in the spring and two alternatives. He kept it to one page and had Ferra read it twice for anything that could be taken the wrong way by a man looking for something to take the wrong way.

His private reasoning he wrote down in four lines and they were sound.

A trade partner you need is worth more than a treaty you signed. Renner had a hill full of iron and a hill that grew nothing worth eating. Kaldo had food, a river, boats, and an appetite for ore that was going to increase every year for a decade. Neither man had to like the other. Neither had to trust the other past the length of an invoice. That arrangement holds for twenty years on nothing but arithmetic, and arithmetic does not get bored or change its mind or have a son.

And underneath it, the part he did not write down and half said anyway.

He had been in this kingdom two and a half years. He had Ferra. He had Bram, and Imani, and Doran, and Farris, and four men who had ridden down with him, and a town that had stopped laughing at him some time last spring without anyone announcing it.

Every single relationship he had was inside his own walls. There was not one person outside this barony who would have crossed a room for him, and he had begun to notice it the way you notice a draught, which is not all at once and not at first.

Ferra's caution on the letter was one sentence and she gave it at the door.

"One buyer and one seller is two hands to squeeze with."

"I agree."

"You are sending it anyway."

"I am sending it anyway," Emeric said, "because Grayrock is where the ore is."

She did not argue, because it was true, and because there was no second hill.

She stayed in the doorway a moment longer than the sentence needed. He noticed it, and did nothing with it, and it was gone before he reached the yard.

He went out to watch the carts go, which he did not have to do and which he did.

Four of them, loaded, with the coin boxed and strapped and two of the garrison riding along for form rather than for need, out through the Western Gate and over the Left Bridge in a hard clean autumn light with the ninth division stubbled gold behind them. The road north-east ran up along the water and out of sight inside half a mile.

He stood on the bridge with his hands on the rail and felt, without complication of any kind, good.

The barony had fed itself. The barony had a surplus. The barony had ore coming and a foundry to eat it and forty-four men in harness standing behind a wall that nobody was going to bother testing, and a neighbour who was about to be invited to sit at a table like a reasonable man.

It was, he thought afterward, the last hour of that entire year in which he did not have to hold two things in his head at once.

The carts went up the road and around the shoulder of the hill and were gone.
