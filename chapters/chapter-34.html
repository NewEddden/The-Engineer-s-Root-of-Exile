Chapter 34 - Deeper Workings

The new price went into Ferra's book on the evening the barge came in. By the end of the week it had gone into every other page as well, and she took him through them one at a time.

The iron Bram had ordered for the spring, priced twice, at the autumn rate and at the new one. The plowshares nobody had asked for yet and everybody on the west side would ask for by May. The copper, which the letter had not mentioned and which she had put up by a fifth anyway, in the margin, with a ring round it, beside the ink. She did not say why. A man who raised one price in a civil letter would raise the next one in a shorter letter, and the ring said so for her.

Bram sat by the stove with his boots off and did the iron in his head. His lips moved. Emeric had learned to wait until they stopped.

"That's the plows," Bram said. "And half what I'd put by for the furnace."

"Pay it," Emeric said.

Ferra looked up.

"Grayrock is the only iron on the river." He had worked it out on the bank, watching the bargeman not look at him. "If we argue, the next barge is late. If it's late twice, there isn't a next barge. A fifth is what it costs to keep them sending anything at all."

"That isn't a reason." She turned the letter round on the table so that its clerk's careful hand faced him. "That's a way of agreeing to something."

He had no answer to that which was not the same sentence said again, and he did not say it again.

She had laid out three pages and stopped at the third. With Ferra that meant there was a fourth. He waited, and she drew a page out from the bottom of the stack, one he had seen once before, in September, when she wrote a month at its foot and turned it face down, and turned it over.

May.

It was the pouch, run forward a week at a time in two columns, what went out and what was left. Sela's list had come back with the thaw and gone past where it had been in November: ninety on the benches, forty-seven boys and forty-three girls, twenty-two of them from across the bridge. Every name on that list was a household paid each week for the mornings it had lost, out of the pouch, as he had promised the square in September. Four guards out of the same pouch, as they had been since April. At that rate it paid the households to the last week of May and the guards a month past that, and then it paid nothing to anybody.

"And the shop?"

She turned to January, where the loan sat in her plain small hand with spring in the margin beside it. The arch bars had gone out on Saban's wagons, and what came back for them would come back as ore and salt and tallow off her list, not as coin, and not before summer. The shop's coin came from Kaldo two coppers at a time, and two coppers at a time paid for the yard and the ash and the women at the kettles. It would not pay back January by May. It would not pay it back by June.

"It says spring," Emeric said.

"It was true when I wrote it." She left the word where it was.

He asked for the purse's own page, the wages, because if the money was going to stop in May he wanted to see which line it stopped from first.

She gave it to him. The guards were at the top, four lines, the same sum every week since April. The households under them, one line a week, longer every week since the thaw. And her own line, under the guards', where it had been since July, because she had written her wage into the book on her first day and counted it out to herself every Saturday where he could see it counted.

Until January. From the first week of January the line was there and the sum was not. Where the figure should have been there was a short ruled stroke, every week, as neat as everything else on the page.

He looked at it long enough for Bram to stop pretending to look at the stove.

"You stopped drawing your wage."

"In January." She did not look up from May. "When the ash came in."

"You didn't say."

"You didn't ask for the page."

That was true, and it did not help. He was on his feet, which he had not meant to be, in a kitchen too small to stand up in with any dignity.

"Paid," he said. "Properly. Not in soap. You said it at this table before you'd said anything else to me. It was your first term."

"I know. I wrote it." She drew a line under a total and did not hurry it. "I can strike it."

"Put it back."

"No."

"Then I'll pay it to you. From January. Tonight."

"Then I'll write it back into the purse in the morning, and you'll have paid the purse." She looked at him then. "It's twelve weeks of a fair wage. That's two weeks of the households. I would rather have the two weeks."

Bram stood up, put his boots on without lacing them, said the forge wanted looking at, and went out, though Emeric had watched him bank it an hour before.

The door shut.

Emeric sat down again, because there was nothing to do standing. He was angry at himself for not asking for the page in January, and under the anger was something he did not have a column for. Twelve short strokes, ruled with the same pen and the same pressure. She had done it for Kaldo. Every line on that table was for Kaldo. He went on looking at the strokes as if one of them might say something different from the rest, and when he looked up she was watching him, and she looked down first.

"The households," she said, and turned back to May.

He let her.



On Saturday he went down to the exchange at noon, when the households were paid.

Rylan paid them, at a trestle just inside the door, out of a bag that came down from the keep every Saturday morning and went back up empty. Sela stood at his elbow with her list and said each name before he counted, so that nobody was paid twice and nobody was paid for a child who had not come. That rule was hers. Ferra had only written it down.

Emeric stood at the back, where he usually stood, and watched the line.

It ran out of the door and down the step and along the front of the building toward the bridge. Mothers, mostly, and some fathers, and two or three older sisters sent with instructions, and one very old man who had come for a great-grandson and wanted that understood. They took their coppers and counted them on the step, and most of them went straight across the lane to the baker's or to Sela's counter for soap, so that the money was back in Kaldo's hands before the line was done. The ones from across the bridge took longer. They had come further for it, and they counted it again, and one woman from the dock quarter tied hers into the corner of her shawl and kept the knot in her fist all the way back over the bridge.

Twenty-two of the ninety came over that bridge every school morning. Ferra kept them in a column of their own, because the bridge was a long walk in the wind off the river, and their mornings went down with the weather, and she wanted to know by how much.

"Ninety," Sela said, when Rylan had tied the bag. She did not look at the list to say it. "And there's a girl from over the bridge I've had on the step three mornings who won't come in, because her mother says the money's a trick." She hitched the shawl up on her shoulder. "I told her to come in anyway. The money's not for her. The letters are."

He did not tell her about May. The line had gone home with its coppers believing that this was a thing that happened on Saturdays, and it was not his to take that away from them before he had to.



He wrote to Grayrock the next morning at Hale's table in the keep.

Hale gave him the form of it unasked. The baron at Grayrock was Renner. A letter to him began in a particular way and ended in another, and Hale wrote out both on a slip of paper in his upright hand and left the middle to Emeric.

Emeric kept the middle civil. He set out the old price and the new one. He set out how the two pieces of ore taken off the barge, from the top of the load and lower in the hold, had compared in his clerk's hands: the same ore, of the same grade, as the autumn's. He asked, with every courtesy he knew, what the deeper workings were, and where, and what they had cost, so that Kaldo might understand the figure it was being asked to pay. And he proposed that he and the baron meet, at Grayrock or at Kaldo or at any point on the river between, on any day the baron cared to name.

He did not threaten anything. He did not have anything to threaten with, and Renner would know that better than he did.

The second letter went to the crown. It said that the only iron on the river had gone up by a fifth without cause, against a barony whose output the crown took its share of, and that the barony would be grateful for the crown's view. He sent it north by the road, like every letter since April, and expected for it what he had got for the others.

He took the Grayrock letter down the hill before it went and read it to Ferra at her table. She listened to the whole of it with her pen still and a smear of ink along one freckled knuckle.

"One buyer and one seller," she said, when he had finished, "is two hands to squeeze with."

"I know."

"Then why write?"

"Because writing costs a sheet of paper," he said. "Not writing might cost the barge."

She did not argue. She took a fresh page and ruled two lines on it, one for Grayrock and one for the crown, each with the day's date, and beside them a column headed answered. She left the column empty.

The Grayrock letter went up the river in the bargeman's coat, on the empty barge, poled back along the east bank with two men leaning on the poles and a third asleep on the deck. Emeric watched it as far as the bend. It was the first thing he could remember Kaldo sending up that river, and it was a request.

"And if Renner doesn't answer?" Ferra said, that evening.

"Then he has his deeper workings." Emeric had been turning the phrase over all day, as he would have turned a part that did not fit. "And we have workings of our own."

"Shut ones."

"Shut ones."



Hale did not ask why the prince wanted the mine leases.

He went down to the vaulted room under the old hall and came back in the time it took Emeric to hear two petitions, with a box under his arm that had been dusted on the stair and not before. He set it on the table and opened it, and took the leases out one at a time and laid them in a row, oldest to the left, without being told to.

There were nineteen. They were parchment, folded in three and tied with tape that had gone the color of tea, and they ran back a little over a hundred years. Each one let a working in the barony's hills to the men of the hamlet below it, for a term of years, at a royalty of so much a cartload to the barony, paid at the keep's gate. Most of them were signed with marks. One or two had a name written out beside the mark in a clerk's hand, and once a man had written his own, large and careful, as though it had cost him the morning.

Each one ended the same way. On the back, in a different hand every time across a hundred years, somebody had written why the lease was given up.

Water.

Water in the lower level.

Seam turned down. Water.

One clerk, more forthcoming than the rest, had written: The men will not go lower.

The last one, at the right-hand end of the row, had no reason on its back at all. Only a date, and the date was the first winter of the plague.

"It was not surrendered, Your Highness," Hale said. "It lapsed for want of payment. Nobody came down from the hill that winter, and the keep sent nobody up."

"And the leases now?"

"Lapsed. All of them." Hale squared the row with one finger, so that the edges lined up. "The workings revert to the barony." He paused, exactly as long as the next clause needed. "To whoever the barony answers to."

Emeric looked along the row of folded parchment. A hundred years of men digging down until the water stopped them, and then moving along the hill and digging down again somewhere else, with hammers and bars, never lower than a man could stand and swing.

"Is there anybody still up there?"

Hale looked at the window.

"The hamlet paid its tithe until the plague years. After that it did not pay, and nobody went up to ask why." He took the last lease off the end of the row and put it on top of the others, which was as near as Hale came to a gesture. "Whether there are people in it now, the daybook does not say."

"And the ore? Did anyone say what it was like?"

"I am told it stank, Your Highness."



Bram was drawing a bar out at the anvil when Emeric came into the forge, and did not stop.

"Hill ore," he said, when he had heard what the box held. He turned the bar a quarter and hit it again. "Stinks."

"You told me."

"Then you know." Another quarter turn. "Smiths bought it when they couldn't get Grayrock. Nobody I knew bought it twice if they could get Grayrock."

"Why not?"

Bram looked at the bar, and at the fire, and put the bar back in the fire.

"You'll see," he said.

Tess, at the bellows, had stopped pumping to listen. Bram looked at her and she started again.

"I want to go and look at the workings," Emeric said. "Two days out. I'd like you to come and tell me what you see."

"Two days there."

"And two back."

Bram took the bar out again and looked along it, as he looked at anything he had not decided about yet. Behind him the fire breathed with the bellows.

"Tess has the forge while I'm gone," he said. "And she's paid for it, properly, not in a pat on the head."

"Yes."

"And I'm not getting on a horse."
