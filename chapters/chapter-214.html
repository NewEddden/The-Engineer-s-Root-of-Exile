Chapter 214 - First Blood

Hob Lantry would not pay.

That much everybody agreed on afterward, and it was nearly the only thing they agreed on. He came up the capital road from the south on the third of November a little before noon, with a Kaldo cart and two of Kaldo's horses and nine crates of soap and lamp glass under a tarpaulin, bound for Aster's warehouse in Wayford, as he had come up that road every week since the ship's run began; and at the south end of Harrow Bridge the bar was down across the road on its green and white post, and the old woodward came out of the toll-house in his green coat with his printed forms, and asked him for a Silver.

He had paid it the week before. He had paid it the week before that, under protest, with a receipt, as the house of Kaldo had written in the gazette that it would. On the third of November he got down off the box and stood in the road and said that he would not.

Why he would not, nobody ever found out to anybody's satisfaction, Hob least of all. He was a heavy, stubborn man of forty with a bad knee and four children at Sela's school, and he said later that he had not planned it; that he had looked at the bar and thought of every Copper of it, every week, until the crown's officer came, who was never coming, and something in him had got down off the box before the rest of him decided to.

The old woodward asked him again. Hob said no again, louder. Two of Lisle's men in green came out of the toll-house behind the woodward. There had been three of them all October. There were three of them still.

The men in brown were not there.

They had gone the day before. All fourteen, and the fifteenth with his stone, in their two wagons, north up the road through Wayford at first light on the second, without a word to anybody at the posting house, as they had come. Aster had sent it down to Kaldo by the ship that evening, under the rope prices, and nobody had known what to make of it.

So there was nobody on the bridge on the third but an old man in a green coat and three of Lisle's household men, who were a cowman's son and a groom and a widow's boy from Lisle's own village, and a Kaldo carter with a bad knee who would not pay a Silver.

What happened then depended on who was asked.



Emeric came up on the evening run, with Ward and Imani and her bag, and the ship put down in the clearing past the twelfth milestone at a quarter past five, in the last of the light; and Rylan was waiting under the beeches with horses and a lantern, and did not say anything, and did not need to.

They had laid the four of them in the posting house stable, on straw, under horse blankets, in a row. Rylan had carried all four in from the bridge with three of the column, and he had not put Kaldo's at one end and Lisle's at the other. He had put them in the order he found them.

Emeric lifted the blanket off each face.

Garth Hallam, of the Wayford column, twenty-six, out of Kaldo's levy and the garrison school before that, who had married in the spring; a cut across the side of the neck, deep. A boy in Lisle's green, very young, with yellow hair, who had been hit on the head with something heavy and had no other mark on him at all. Abner Tuck of the column, the oldest of the patrol, with a groom's knife still in his side, because Imani said, kneeling in the straw beside him, that whoever had pulled it would have killed him faster and nobody had wanted to. And another of Lisle's, a big man, older, shot once through the body at close range, with the burn of the powder on his coat.

Imani looked at each of them in turn. She did it quickly, with her hands, and when she came to the boy in green she looked longest, and turned his head a little to the lantern, and put it back.

"Something round," she said. "A rifle butt, or a wheel spoke. Or the post the bar's on." She sat back on her heels. "None of them lived long. That's all I can tell you. I can't tell you the order. Nobody can, off a body."

Emeric stood in the straw with the lantern.

He could feel nothing from any of them, which was what dead meant. He put the blanket back over the yellow-haired boy's face himself.

"Where's the carter," he said.



Hob Lantry was in the posting house kitchen with his arm in a sling of tablecloth and his face out of shape on one side, and two of the column standing at the door, though nobody had told them to and nobody was coming. He tried to stand when Emeric came in. Imani pushed him back down by the shoulder without looking at him and began to unwind the tablecloth.

"I'm sorry, Highness," said Hob. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry." He said it to the table, and to Imani's hands while she found the break in his forearm, and the rest came out in pieces in between.

He'd said no. The old man had asked again. He'd said no. The two in green had come out, and the first one, the big one, had told him to pay or turn round, and he had said the road was the king's road and he'd do neither, which he had heard the column say in the posting house, and liked the sound of. The big one had shoved him. He had shoved back. He was a carter; he had shoved a horse that weighed half a ton every day of his life, and the big one had gone down on his back on the planks of the bridge. And then the other two were on him, and he was on the ground, and there were boots, and the post, he thought it was the post, his arm had gone against the post, and he had heard it go.

"And then the column," he said. "Then they were there. Garth. And Abner. And the others. I heard Garth's voice. I was on the ground." He looked up. "I didn't see. I'm sorry. I had my face in the planks. I didn't see who, Highness. I heard the gun."



The column's patrol had been six men. Four of them were alive, and Ward took them one at a time in the posting house's back parlour with the door shut, by a single candle, and wrote down what each one said in his own square hand, and did not let any of them hear what the others had said.

Emeric sat in the corner and listened.

They had been walking south to look at the padlocked relay post, as they did every day, and had heard the shouting from a furlong off. They had run. Calloway, who had the patrol, said that when they got to the bridge the carter was on the ground and two of the green were kicking him and the third, the big one, was getting up off the planks with a knife out. Calloway had shouted. Garth had got there first, being fastest, and had put himself between the big one and Hob. The big one had cut him.

The second man said the big one had not had a knife out; the knife was the groom's, and it had come out after, when Abner had hold of the groom.

The third man said Garth had hit the boy first, with his rifle, to get him off Hob, and the boy had gone down, and then the big one had gone for Garth.

The fourth man had been last onto the bridge and had seen nothing but backs. He had heard the gun. He had not fired it. Calloway had fired it, into the big one, at a yard, after Garth was down; Calloway said so, and said it again, and did not try to say anything else about it, and then put his face in his hands.

The old woodward said, with his careful face grey, that the Kaldo carter had struck first. That he had seen it with his own eyes, the carter put both hands on young Nye and threw him down on the planks. Nye was the big one. Nye had been Lisle's cowman for twenty years. And then the Kaldo soldiers had come running with their guns, and the woodward had gone into the toll-house and shut the door, because he was sixty-four and had never seen anything like it, and when he came out again it was over.

"Who struck first," said Ward. "When the soldiers came. Between the soldiers and Lord Lisle's men."

The woodward looked at him.

"I had the door shut," he said. "I am sorry, sir. I had the door shut."



Ward put his pages in order on the parlour table at a little after ten that night, squared them, and laid his hand flat on them.

"The carter put hands on Lord Lisle's man first," he said. "That is certain. Two witnesses and his own word. After that I have four accounts from our men and none from theirs who lived, and the four do not agree, and they are honest men, every one, and none of them was looking at the whole bridge." He looked at the candle. "It cannot be known. Not by me. Not by a court."

"They knew it couldn't," said Emeric.

"Yes."

"The men in brown left the day before."

"Yes." Ward did not move his hand. "If they had been on the bridge, Highness, it would have been fourteen and a hedge mage against a carter and six. Our men would not have run onto it. Calloway would have stopped at the furlong and sent to me. It would have been a beating and a complaint." He was quiet. "They left three of Lord Lisle's own people. A cowman. A groom. A boy. And an old man who would shut the door." He took his hand off the pages. "So that it would be Lisle's blood, and Lisle's dead, and Lisle's village. Not Aldane's. And so that nobody in the kingdom could say Aldane's men were on the bridge."

Emeric thought of Saban, in the strongroom under the east tower, with a beer and a toll receipt, and Marrow nodding at the sum.

He'll make you hit first.

"We didn't hit first," he said.

"No," said Ward. "And we cannot show it." He looked at Emeric across the candle, flat, and without any unkindness at all. "It does not matter now who did, Highness. It only mattered before."



He did three things that night, and Ferra did the rest in the morning.

He took the column off the bridge, and off the half mile of road on either side of it, back up to the Wayford end, with orders written in his own hand that no man of Kaldo's was to go within sight of the toll-house armed until he said otherwise, and that Kaldo's carts would pay at the bar. Calloway took it without a word. Rylan read it over Emeric's shoulder and said nothing either, which was how Emeric knew what he thought.

He wrote to Lisle. Not the steward: Lisle, by name, in his own hand, with all his names under it, and sent it by a Wayford boy on a fast horse at midnight with a white flag on a stick. That two of his people and two of Kaldo's were dead at Harrow Bridge. That the house of Kaldo would not say who had struck first, because the house of Kaldo did not know. That Kaldo's men had been withdrawn from the bridge and would not return to it. That the bodies of Nye and of the boy, whose name Kaldo did not yet have, were in the stable at the Wayford posting house, washed, and would be carried to Lisle's gate in the morning on Kaldo's cart under a white flag, by unarmed men, or fetched by Lisle's own, whichever Lisle preferred. That the house of Kaldo would pay what was owed to their families, as it would pay its own. That the house of Kaldo still waited for the crown's officer.

"He won't answer," said Rylan, watching the boy go.

"I know."

"Then why..."

"Because it'll be read," said Emeric. "Later. By somebody."

The third thing he did was go back to the stable, after Imani had gone up to bed in the posting house's best room with her bag beside her, and sit on an upturned bucket by the door with the lantern turned down, with the four of them in the straw, until Lisle's steward came with a cart at two in the morning, without a flag, and four men, and took his two away without speaking. Emeric stood up when they came in and stood while they did it, and the steward looked at him once, over the yellow-haired boy, and did not say anything, and neither did he.



Ferra came up on the morning run with the books.

She brought the small black book she kept for the column, and the pay roll of the Wayford garrison, and a fresh bottle of ink, and she sat down in Sethe's counting room at eight with Ward's pages and the four statements, and read all of it, every line, twice, before she wrote anything at all.

Then she opened the column book at Garth Hallam's page and Abner Tuck's.

Emeric watched her do it from the window seat. She wrote slowly. She wrote the date, and the place, and the cause: for Garth, a knife wound to the neck, received on Harrow Bridge in the defence of a Kaldo carter; for Abner, a knife wound to the side, the same. She wrote the hour, as near as Imani had it. She ruled her line under each. And then she took a clean page at the back of the book, where nothing was, and headed it in the same plain hand. Lisle. And under it: Nye, cowman. And: the boy, name not known.

"They're not ours," said Emeric.

"No." She did not look up. "They're dead on a road we use, in a quarrel about our cart. If I don't write them down somewhere, it'll be as if they weren't." She dipped the pen. "I'll not have a book that says two died at Harrow. Four died at Harrow."

She wrote Calloway's account in, under Garth's line, as the patrol's sergeant had given it, with his name beside it: struck first by Lord Lisle's man Nye, with a knife. Then she sat looking at it, with her pen lifted.

Then, under it, she wrote a second line, and dated it.

4 Nov. Correction. The carter Lantry put hands on Nye first, by three accounts and his own. Between the patrol and Lisle's men, who struck first is not known, and four honest accounts disagree. F.

She did not strike the first line through. She never did. It stood there above the second in the same ink, saying what Calloway believed, and the second stood under it saying what could be known, and anybody who opened the book in a hundred years could read both and see which had been written first, and why it had been corrected.

"That's going to be read too," Emeric said.

"I should hope so." She blotted it. "If it's going to be read, it'll be read true." She turned to the pay roll. "Garth Hallam married Wenna Pask in April. She's in the river street, three doors down from the chandler. Abner Tuck has his mother at Milford, and a sister. Garth's pay runs to the end of the quarter and Abner's to the end of the month." She ran her finger down the column. "The roll says nothing about what a barony owes a widow. It never has. Nobody's ever written it." She looked up at him for the first time that morning. "So we'll write it now. Before Aldane writes something else for us."

"What do you want to write?"

"His pay, for her life, or till she marries again and asks for it to stop. The same for Abner's mother. Schooling for any child, which there isn't yet, but there may be. And the same sum, paid the same day, to Nye's people and the boy's, through Lisle's steward, with a receipt." She paused. "He won't take it."

"No."

"Then I'll enter that he wouldn't, and keep it in the strongbox under their names, and it'll be there when somebody asks." She dipped her pen again. "There's always somebody who asks."



Wenna Hallam was mending a net on her doorstep when they came down the river street, because the light was better on the step, and when she saw who it was she put the net down and stood up and stood very still, with the shuttle in her hand, and Emeric understood that nobody had come to tell her yet. The ship had brought the news; Kaldo had it; the river street had it. Nobody had been to the door.

So he told her.

He had done it before, in the plague streets and after the cellar, and had learned that the long way round was for the one doing the telling. He said Garth's name, and where, and that it had been quick, which Imani said it had been, and that Garth had been standing over a man on the ground who could not stand up.

Wenna listened to all of it. She was small and broad, with a chapped red face, and when he had finished she looked down at the net on the step.

"He was meant to be home Thursday," she said. "Three days. He's got leave Thursday." She looked at the net. "I told him I'd have this done."

Ferra did not say anything about pay. Not then. She stood at Emeric's shoulder with the book shut under her arm, and when Wenna sat down on the step, suddenly, as if her knees had decided it, Ferra sat down beside her on the doorstep in her good grey coat without asking, and picked up the other end of the net, and held it, so that the mesh would not drag in the street. Wenna looked at her. Then she put the shuttle through, once, and again, and went on with the row, and Ferra held the net, and nobody said anything, and Emeric stood in the river street with his hands at his sides.

A woman came out of the house next door with a jug and sat down on Wenna's other side. Then another, from across the way. By the time Wenna had finished the row there were five women on and around her step, and one of them had taken the net off Ferra without any fuss at all and was holding it herself, and Ferra had stood up and stepped back beside Emeric, and the river street had closed around Wenna Hallam.

They came away.

"Tomorrow," said Ferra, at the corner. "The pay. Not today." She looked back down the street once. "Today she'll want to be angry at somebody. Better it's us in a doorway than her own mother."

Abner Tuck's mother, at Milford, was not angry. She was eighty and nearly blind, and she gave them tea, and asked Emeric twice whether it had been the Lisle people or the brown ones, and when he said he did not know, she nodded and said that was a pity, it would have been easier to know; and when they left she gave Ferra a jar of plum preserve for the walk, because she gave everybody something, and Ferra carried it home in both hands on the ship and put it on the counting room shelf and did not open it, and it was still there at Midwinter.

From the ship they had seen the new granary at the head of the bay, two walls up and roofed in sacking for the winter, a twentieth of the barony's grain and no more, the way Ferra had said.



The broadsheet reached Aster's door at four in the morning on the twelfth, in the coat of a rider who owed Saban money, and Cade had it in Kaldo by breakfast.

It was dated 11 November. Aldane's own broadsheet, in Aldane's own fine new fount, with a black border again.

His Grace of Aldane, in grief and in duty, had learned that on the third day of this month the soldiers of the house of Kaldo, under arms upon the king's highway, had fallen upon the peaceful servants of the loyal house of Lisle at Harrow Bridge while they kept their lord's lawful toll under a crown charter, and slain two of them, a man of fifty and a boy of fifteen. That the house of Kaldo had already, this summer, hanged men of the north without a crown judge, and put out the north's lights, and now shed the north's blood upon the north's own road. That the crown's mediation, so wisely offered by the lieutenant, had been mocked by this violence while it was yet pending. That His Grace could not, as a man and a peer, stand by while a loyal house was put to the sword by a neighbour whose demons flew by night. That His Grace therefore took the house of Lisle and all its lands and people under his protection, as a kinsman and a friend; and that from this day His Grace held himself at war with the house of Kaldo, by the old right of the northern peers to defend their own, until such time as the house of Kaldo gave satisfaction for the blood of Harrow and the lives of Wayford.

Under it, smaller, a list. The houses that stood with His Grace in this quarrel.

Sallow. Vane. And three more northern names that Emeric knew only from the peerage rolls, small houses on the far side of Aldane's own country, houses that had never in their history ridden anywhere.

Ferra read it at the long table, the whole of it, aloud, because Ward asked her to. She read in the same voice she read a rent roll in, and did not stop at the boy of fifteen, though Emeric saw her finger stop.

"We wrote to him that night," she said, when she had finished. "Lisle. With the boy in the stable. That we didn't know." She laid the broadsheet down. "This was written before he'd have had it. Or after, and doesn't care."

"Before," said Saban.

Everybody looked at him.

He had a second paper. He had been holding it under the table, flat on his knee, while Ferra read, and now he put it on the board in front of him and did not push it across to anybody. It was in his capital man's small slanting hand, three close pages, and it had come in the same coat as the broadsheet.

"His Grace isn't mustering," said Saban. "He's mustered." He said it in his commercial voice, as he said everything, and his face was quite still. "Two thousand, near enough. Nearly half of it horse. His own knights and his own men-at-arms, and upland men off his sheep walks, and levies out of the abbey towns, Tarnby and Withey and Askel, and the household men of the houses on that list, and the men in brown, who aren't in brown now, by all accounts. A dozen gray mages on hire, perhaps more; my man has a dozen names, and a stone merchant who thinks there were fifteen. Sallow brings a hundred and fifty horse. Vane, a hundred. The three small ones, two hundred between them, mostly on foot." He turned the first page over. "They've been in the field under Highcairn, at Aldane's seat, since the middle of October. Camped. Fed. Drilling. Three weeks before the bridge, Highness. Before Hob Lantry got down off his box. The quartermasters were buying oats in the north in September and sending the bills south to the capital to be paid, which is how my man found it, because the bills went through a counting house he drinks with." He laid his hand on the page. "Three weeks."

"Two thousand men in a field for three weeks," said Ward, at last. "That is smoke you can see from the next county. And cook fires. And a hundred carts a day on the roads." He looked at Saban. "Who was watching the north?"

"Mine." Saban did not look away from him. "A man called Ansley Dunmore. At the posting house at the crossroads below Aldane's seat. He's been mine nine years. He sees every cart on that road. He writes me every fortnight." He was quiet. "He wrote me on the twentieth of October. The roads were quiet, the harvest was in, His Grace was at the capital for the season, and the price of oats was up a little on account of the wet." He took his hand off the page. "He wrote me again on the third of November. The day of the bridge. The same."

"He didn't see it," said Rylan.

"He saw it," said Saban. "He couldn't not."



Saban did not stay for the rest of the table. He excused himself, which he never did, and went out, and Emeric found him half an hour later in the old exchange, in the little room at the back that had been his since the second year, with the window over the bridge foot, sitting at his desk with a ledger Emeric had never seen open in front of him and the small book he never let anybody see open beside it.

He did not shut either when Emeric came in.

"Sit, Highness, if you like," he said. "There's the other chair. It's got a leg." He turned a page. "I'm doing a stocktake."

Emeric sat.

The ledger was names. Not many; perhaps forty, in Saban's neat round trading hand, down the left of each page, and along each line a column of dates and a column of sums and a column of what had come back for them, and in the margin, here and there, a small mark that might have been anything.

"I've people in every town on the round," said Saban, "and one or two in the capital. You've heard me say it. I've said it at that table often enough, and I was proud of it." He ran his finger down a column. "What I don't say is that I've never once asked any of them who else they sell to. You don't, in my trade. A man who knows things knows them for whoever pays, and if you're lucky and you pay well and you're decent to his wife at Midwinter, he tells you first." He turned a page. "Dunmore was decent. Nine years. He sent me the price of salt in the north before the north knew it. He found me a glassblower once." He stopped. "Nine years, I'd have said he was mine."

"Was he paid to stay quiet? Or paid to write it the way he did?"

"I don't know yet." Saban looked at the window. "Either one costs me the same. I'll find out which, because it matters for the next man." He touched the small book. "And I'll find out who else on these pages sells both ways. It'll be more than one. It always is. You learn the one and you start to see the others in the margins, little things you let go by because the man was good company." He was quiet. "There was a northern man came to the river street a fortnight ago and talked to Fennick about the key to the powder house. That was said at the long table and nowhere else. I've been turning that over since, Highness, and I didn't like any of the answers, and now I've got a new one I like less."

Emeric said nothing.

"It's my trade," said Saban, and shut the ledger. "I never priced the network. I never thought to. It's people, and people talk both ways. Every one of them is two doors." He looked at his hands. "I should have priced it."

"What will it cost to find out?"

Saban looked at him, and for the first time in the conversation something came back into his face, the dry thing it usually had.

"Money. Time. Three or four men I'd as soon not lose." He put the small book in his coat. "And a fair amount of being decent to people's wives at Midwinter while I watch them. Nothing you'll see on your books, Highness. It'll be on mine."



The war council sat at the long table that afternoon, and again that night. Ward did most of it. He had the levy roll and the garrison roll and the rifle count and the casks in the powder house all on one page, and he had plainly been keeping it since the black-bordered sheet in September, and he read it down the table line after line.

A hundred and twenty in plate. The column at Wayford, forty. The levy: three hundred and some who could stand in a line and fire Bram's rifle three times a minute without hitting the man in front, of whom forty had Rylan's glyph on their arms. The knights of Kaldo's own houses, a handful. Every stone they had, every suit, every lamp. And the ship.

"Against two thousand," said Rylan. "Half of them horse." He was not joking. Emeric had seldom heard him not joking at a table, and did not like it.

"Against two thousand," said Ward.

"What are the crystals for?" said Ferra.

Ward looked at the table.

"I do not know yet," he said. "I have a guess. I will not give it until I know. It is not a guess anybody should have to carry before they must." He looked at Emeric, briefly. "Your Highness will work it out. You read ledgers."

Ferra wrote every figure down as it came, the casks and the cartridges and the bushels of oats and the carts to carry them and the Gold Rand it would cost to keep three hundred farmers' sons in the field through a northern November, and at about nine, when the lamps had been lit twice, she put her pen down and said what it came to, and it was a great deal, and nobody at the table argued.

"We can pay it," she said. "Till spring. After spring, I'll want to sit down with the books on my own." She picked her pen up again. "Not tonight."



He got two minutes with Imani at about ten, on the stair.

He was going down for the levy rolls and Imani was coming up with a list for Ward, of what a field hospital in the north in winter would need, which was long, and they met at the turn where the window looked out over the dark yard, and stopped, because the stair was narrow and somebody had to.

She looked at him in the lamplight from below.

"You've not eaten," she said.

"I had bread."

"At one o'clock. I asked Sela." She put the list against his chest and held it there with one hand, flat, so that he couldn't take it or go past. "That isn't eating. That's a crust in a corridor."

"There's a war."

"There'll be a war tomorrow. There'll be a war with soup in it." She did not take her hand away. Her palm was warm through the paper and his shirt. She looked at the list under it, and then at him, and then, quickly, down the stair and up it, like a girl looking down a lane before she climbs a fence. Nobody. Ward's voice in the long room above, flat, and the scrape of Rylan's chair.

She stood up on the step above hers and kissed him, once, on the mouth, fast and hard and over almost before it had started, with the list crushed between them.

"That's not the rest of the sentence," she said against his chin. "Don't think it is. That's interest."

"On what?"

"On what you owe me. From the telephone. It's accruing." She stepped back down and smoothed the list on his chest where it had creased. "Ferra's worked it out for me. It's ruinous. You're going to have to come home from this alive to pay it, because she won't let me write it off."

"Imani."

"Soup," she said. "Then the rolls." She ducked past him under his arm with the list and went on up the stair toward Ward's voice, and did not look back, and at the top she said, without turning round, "And I'll know if you don't."

He ate the soup.



Isolde and Jabari came south on the thirteenth, on the morning run from Wayford, with one trunk between them and Jabari's sword in a sack.

Saban had sent for them on the fourth, the hour he put Dunmore's last letter down; Saban's carrier had found them in the north, where they had been living quietly since the wedding, and they had left inside the hour, by night, on two of the carrier's horses, because Isolde had read the broadsheet over the carrier's shoulder in the doorway and seen her father's name on the list. They had ridden to Wayford in two days, and looked it.

Isolde came down the gangway first, very straight, in a riding coat that had been good once, with her hair in a plait that had come half down, and stopped at the foot of it and looked at Ferra, who was waiting on the slip.

"My father's banner is out," she said. "Behind Aldane. We hold of Aldane; when he rides, we ride. I'm sorry. I saw it on the list." She lifted her chin. "You said, under Kaldo's protection. In his own hall. I've come to see if you meant it."

"I'll not say things in a lord's hall I don't mean," said Ferra. "It's bad for the books." She held out her hand, and Isolde looked at it, and took it, and something in her shoulders let go. "Come up. There's a room. And there's tea, and it's hot, which I'll warn you is unusual."

Jabari came down behind with the trunk on his shoulder, as if it weighed nothing, and the sword in its sack under his other arm, and set the trunk down on the slip and looked at the ship, and the river, and the works across it with its chimneys, and the wall, and then at Emeric, and nodded once, as a man nods to somebody he owes something and does not mean to talk about.

"Highness."

"Sir Jabari."

"Her father rides with a hundred and fifty horse," said Jabari. "I know most of them. I trained some of them. Bastian has the second company. He's twenty and he'll ride at the front because he's twenty and somebody once told him to remember." He looked at the works again. "I'm told you're short of knights."

"We're short of everything."

"Knights especially." Jabari looked at him. "I'll stand where Sir Ward puts me. I'll not ride against my wife's father with a lance and I'll not hide from him either." He shifted the sack. "I've got a sword. I've no harness. I came out of the north in a coat."



Bram fitted him that afternoon, at the works, in the long shop, with the presses stopped.

It was the first time Bram had fitted plate to a man who had worn plate all his life, and Emeric, leaning on the bench by the door, saw it bother him for about a quarter of an hour, and then saw him forget about it. Jabari stood on the low block in the middle of the shop in his shirt and breeches, with his arms out, and Bram walked round him with a tape and a stick of chalk, and Tess walked round him the other way with the board and the book, writing down every figure Bram called in her square hand.

"Shoulder to elbow," said Bram. "Fourteen and a half."

"Fourteen and three quarters," said Tess. "You pulled the tape."

Bram looked at the tape. He measured again. He grunted. Tess wrote fourteen and three quarters without looking up.

When Bram knelt to take the length of his shin, Jabari looked across at the rack along the shop wall, where the garrison plate hung in its rows, black, crestless, a hundred and twenty sets of it.

"That's not northern work," he said.

"No," said Bram, from the floor.

"What's it weigh? A full harness?"

Bram told him.

Jabari looked at the rack for a while, at the black plate on its pegs, and Emeric watched him take the figure in, a knight's figure, what he had carried on his shoulders every day since he was fifteen, and come out the other side of it with a third gone. He did not say anything. He only stood a little differently on the block, as if he had already put it on.

"It'll not have any stones in it," said Emeric. "Not a bead. Not a glyph. Ward's rule. It has to work in a field where nothing works."

"Good," said Jabari. "I never trusted the ones with stones." He held his arms out a little further for Bram's tape. "A man's harness should do one thing. Stop the other man's sword. If it's busy doing something clever it's not doing that."

Bram looked up at him from the floor for the first time, with the tape in his teeth, and something like approval crossed his face and went.

"Ten days," he said, round the tape. "For the fitting of it. The plates are cut. It's the strapping and the joints that take the time, on a man your size." He took the tape out. "You'll want to walk about in it after. Up and down the yard. Before you're asked to do anything in it."

"I'll sleep in it if you like," said Jabari.

"Don't," said Tess, writing. "You'll crease the leathers."

Jabari looked at her. For the first time since the slip, the corner of his mouth moved.



Isolde gave Ward the rest of it that evening, in the long room, standing up, because she would not sit.

She knew her father's company better than Saban's man did, and Vane's, because she had grown up at the high table hearing their names. She gave Ward the captains, which had been to a war and which only to tournaments, which drank; that Vane would be at the back because Vane was fifty-eight and had been at the back of every room he had been in for forty years; that her father would be wherever Aldane was, because that was where a man stood who had been shamed in his own hall and wanted it seen that he had not run. That Bastian would be at the front.

"He'll be at the front because of what you did," she said to Emeric. Not unkindly. "You told him to remember. He remembers."

"I know."

"He's my brother," she said. "He's a fool. He used to carry me down to the mill on his back to see the eels." She looked at Ward. "If it comes to it, and he's down in front of your men, I'd like him to be taken. If he can be. I'll pay his ransom myself if my father won't."

Ward wrote it down.

"Bastian Sallow," he said. "Second company. Taken, if he can be." He looked up at her. "It is a long way from a table to a field, my lady. I will tell the men. I cannot promise you what a field will do."

"No." Isolde looked at him. "That's why I asked you and not him." She nodded toward Emeric. "He'd have promised."

Jabari, by the door, said nothing at all, but Emeric felt his note shift, warm, toward her, as it had on the road in the north with the lamp oil burning.



Ward gave his orders on the evening of the thirteenth, at the long table, standing, with his page in front of him.

The levy would go north. Not to Harrow; not yet. To Wayford, which was the better part of three weeks on foot with the carts, and nobody at the table thought Aldane would give them three weeks. So the levy would march out on the capital road to the first ridge and camp in the field below it, and the ship would lift them from there by night, forty at a time, as many lifts as she could make between dark and dawn, and as many nights as it took; the slip was for the garrison and the stores. The carts would come back empty. The column at Wayford would hold Sethe's hall and the bridge at the Wayford end and nothing more. The garrison would go up in the ship in three lifts. The powder would go by cart, up the capital road, under Fennick and under Ward's key, with Ward's own men on the boxes, and nobody else; it would be three weeks on the road, and till it came, the forward depot Ward had started at the Wayford end in October would have to do, with whatever the ship brought. The hospital would go up with Imani in the second lift. Ferra would stay in Kaldo and keep the books and the barony, and the counting room would have a telephone line to Wayford by the long way round, through Milford and the river towns, which Tess's men had begun stringing that morning and swore would be done in eight days.

"His Highness will come with the first lift," said Ward. He did not look at Emeric. "And will come to the quarry tomorrow first. For the drill."

"Every week," said Emeric.

"Every day now," said Ward.

Nobody at the table spoke.

Then Osric, at the end, who had been very quiet all evening, and who had a pencil behind his ear that Emeric had not seen there before, cleared his throat and asked whether the levy would want feeding on the road or whether they'd be expected to eat their boots, because he'd seen the levy eat, and it was a lot; and Ferra said they would want feeding, and told him how much, to the bushel, and Osric whistled, and Rylan laughed for the first time in two days, and the table was a table again.



He went down to the slip at midnight, because he could not sleep and the ship was there.

Ferra was already on the coping, in her grey coat with her collar up, with the column book shut in her lap. She did not look round when he came down the steps. She moved over on the stone so that there was room.

He sat.

The river went by below them, black, with the lamps of the works in it, and the ship rode at her mooring above with her hull dark and her ropes creaking a little in the wind. Across it, from the long shop, faint, came the sound of somebody hammering, steady, one stroke and then another.

"Bram," said Ferra. "On Sir Jabari's plate. He told Tess he'd do the strapping tonight because he couldn't sleep either." She opened the book on her knee, though it was too dark to read it, and shut it again. "I entered Hob Lantry's arm today. Under the bridge. And his wages for the weeks he'll not drive, which come to more than the toll would have if he'd paid it every week till Midsummer." She was quiet. "I worked that out on purpose. I don't know why. To have it somewhere."

"It's a fair thing to want to know."

"It's an unkind thing to know." She looked at the river. "He'll never know it. I'll not tell him."

He took her hand. It was cold, and she let him have it, and after a while she turned it over in his so that her fingers lay along his wrist, inside his cuff, and stayed there.

"You'll write," she said. "From Wayford. Every day. On the wire when the long way round is up, and on paper till it is."

"Every day."

"Not about the war. I'll have Ward's reports about the war." She did not look at him. "About anything else. What you ate. What Rylan said. Whether it's raining." She paused. "Whether Imani made you eat it."

"That's a great many letters."

"I've a great deal of room in the book." She leaned, a very little, so that her shoulder was against his. "I'll enter them. Under the date. In a column of their own." Her fingers moved, once, on the inside of his wrist. "Nobody else will ever read it. It'll be the only column in the book that isn't for somebody."

The hammering went on across the river, one stroke and then another.



The levy marched on the fourteenth, at first light, out of the south gate and round under the west wall onto the capital road.

Three hundred and some, in their department coats, with the rifles on their shoulders and the cartridge pouches at their belts, and their bands on their wrists, and forty of them with Rylan's glyph under their sleeves where nobody could see it. Farmers' sons. A cooper's boy. Carters. They did not march well. They had drilled for shooting, not for walking in step, and the column went out of the gate in a long uneven shuffle with the carts behind it, and Ward rode alongside and did not correct anybody. They had only as far as the first ridge to walk, and the ship would not mind whether they did it in step.

Half of Kaldo was on the wall to see them go. Nobody cheered. It was too early and too cold, and too many of them had a son in the shuffle; they stood with their breath going up white, and now and then somebody called a name, and somebody in the column lifted a hand without turning round.

Sela was at the south gate. She stood at the side of the road by the gate tower, in her shop apron, with her arms folded, and watched the column go past her a rank at a time, and when Arthur came by, in the fourth rank from the end, with his rifle on his shoulder and his sleeve down over his arm, she did not call out. She only watched him. He saw her, and went a little red, and looked straight ahead up the road, and went past.

Emeric stood on the gate tower above her and watched them go round under the wall.

Behind him, down on the slip, the ship was taking on the first lift: Bram's plate in its crates, a hundred and twenty sets, and the garrison filing up the gangway behind it, and Imani at the foot of the gangway with her list and her bag, checking off the hospital chests as Cade swung them aboard, and arguing with Cade about one of them. Across the river the works had not stopped all night. In the long shop Bram would be on the strapping of a black harness for a northern knight, day one of ten. In the powder house Fennick would be waiting for Ward's key.

And north, past Wayford and past the bridge, two thousand men had been sitting in a field for four weeks, waiting for somebody to hit first.

The tail of the column went round under the west wall and out of sight. The carts creaked after it. On the wall the people began to come down, slowly, in twos and threes, back to their houses and their shops and their day, because it was a Friday and the bread still had to be made.

Ward's horse came back round the corner of the wall at a trot, alone, and stopped below the gate tower, and Ward looked up at him.

"Highness," he said. "The quarry. The stone is waiting."

Emeric went down.
