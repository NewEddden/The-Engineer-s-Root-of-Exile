Chapter 214 - Chapel of Ease

Father Doran counted his congregation every Sunday from the pulpit step, during the second psalm, while they were singing and could not see him doing it. He had done it for forty years. He did not write the figure down. It was not a figure for a book. It was a figure for a priest, the way a shepherd counts at the gate without meaning to, and it told him, better than the parish book or the barony's census or anything the prince's clerks could print, how his parish was.

On the first Sunday after midsummer he counted one hundred and ninety.

The church on the island had held six hundred at the Easter after the first quarter was drawn, standing, with the doors open and the rest in the porch and out on the steps. It had held five hundred at the Easter after that, and he had told himself it was the weather. This spring it had held three hundred, and it had not been the weather, and now it was one hundred and ninety on a fine morning in high summer with the windows open and the swallows in and out of the roof, and he knew every face in it, and he knew where every face that was missing had gone.

They had gone four miles.

Some of them still came. He saw them at the back, the first-quarter families, in their good clothes with the road's dust on the hems, who had set out at seven to walk in for the ten o'clock and would walk back after, eight miles on a Sunday with the children on their shoulders by the end of it. Fewer every month. They came at Easter and at Christmas, and for a christening, and for a funeral. The rest of the year they did what people did when their church was four miles off, which was to stay at home and feel bad about it, and then after a while to stay at home and not.

He had been a curate once, forty years ago and more, in a chapel in the hills, and he had seen what that did to a place.

He walked out to the new church on the Tuesday, because he walked out to it every Tuesday now, to see how far they had got.

He could not walk it all. Not since the winter. The winter had got into his left hip and not come out, and Imani had looked at it and pressed it and asked him to walk the length of the ward and back, and then given him a stick of ash with a brass ferrule and told him that the stick was not a suggestion. So he rode out on the carrier's cart as far as the new city's gate, sitting up beside the carter with the stick between his knees, and walked the rest, slowly, up the long avenue and round the ring street to the north side, with the stick going in front of him on the new stone.

It was a good church. He had said so to nobody, because the prince had drawn it and the prince did not need telling, and because he had not got over, and was not sure he meant to get over, where the prince had put it.

The palace was in the middle, on the rise, with its drum and its columns, where the whole city could see it. The church was on the second ring street on the north side, well away from the middle, on the plot he had been shown at the datum stake. He had asked about it once, mildly, the day they cut the datum. He had been shown the drawing. He had said thank you. He had told the Visitor so in the autumn, on a horse, looking up at the drum, and had heard as he said it how it sounded, and had not been able to stop.

The nave was roofed. That was new since last Tuesday: the last of the slates had gone on at the east end, and there was glass in four of the six windows on the south side, plain glass, the city's own, that let the light in white and clean onto the new floor. The tower was a stump. It stood at the west end in its scaffold to the height of the nave's roof ridge and stopped there, with a crane on top of it and a gang of Hewitt's masons sitting in the shade of it at their midday bread, and it would not be finished this year, and perhaps not the next.

He went in and stood in the nave.

It was empty. It smelled of lime and cut stone and new timber. There was no altar yet, only a platform of dressed stone at the east end where the altar would go, and no benches, and no font. The light lay on the floor in long squares. It would hold, he thought, four hundred seated and six hundred standing, the same as the island, which was what he had asked for when the prince asked him how big, and which was, he knew, not going to be big enough in five years.

He could not say a Mass in it. That was the thing. He could stand in it and pray, as any man could pray in any room, and he did. He could not marry a couple in it, or christen a child, or say the Sunday office, or bury anybody out of it, because it was not a church. It was a building. The church of this parish was on the island, and had been since before there was a barony, and the seat of a parish was not a thing a prince could move with a drawing, or a priest with a wish. It was fixed by the diocese.

And the diocese had been cool to Kaldo since the autumn before last.

He could not have said how he knew. Nothing had been said. The letters from the close were what they had always been: courteous, lawful, in the chancery's hand, answering what he asked. But they came later. The oil for the chrism came in the spring a month after it was asked for, with a note regretting the delay. A small dispensation for a marriage in Lent, which the chancery had granted every year he had ever asked for one, came back granted, with a line under the grant asking him to state in future the reason in full. He had stated it in full. He always had.

It was the Visitor. He knew that too, without being told. Benedek Orme had sat at his table for a fortnight and read his book and walked the works and the school and gone away north with a report under his arm, and had been kind at the gate, and something in the report had made the close look at Kaldo differently. Doran did not know what. He had kept his book honestly for forty years and he could not think what in it could have done that. He had thought about it a great deal, on the long nights when his hip would not let him sleep, and had come to no conclusion, and had left it with God, where things he could not understand generally went.

But he knew what the close would say if he wrote tomorrow asking to move the seat of the parish of Kaldo off the island and into a new church in the middle of a prince's new city. It would say no. It would say it courteously, in the chancery's hand, and under the no there would be a line asking him to state the reason in full, and then another asking why a parish that had stood on the island for three hundred years must now stand where a prince had drawn it. And then there would be a Visitor again, and not in two years.

He found the answer that evening, in his own memory, which surprised him, because he had stopped expecting to find anything there that he had not already found.

He was sitting in the presbytery with his leg up on a stool, doing nothing, looking at the shelf where the old parish books stood, the full ones, the ones before his. And he thought of the chapel in the hills.

It had been his first post, when he was twenty-four and had a full head of hair and thought he would be a bishop. A curacy. A chapel of stone with a turf roof in a valley twelve miles up a bad track from its parish church, in a hill village that had grown up round a lead working a hundred years before and was too far from the church to walk to on a Sunday and back before dark in winter. So the chapel had been built, long ago, for the village's ease. It was in the same parish. It had the same priest over it, the rector down in the valley town, who came up twice a year on a pony and was not liked. The village's dead went down the track to the parish churchyard, most of them, on a cart, because the old families would not have their dead anywhere else. The rest was done in the chapel. The Sunday office. The christenings. The marrying, by the rector's leave, which was never refused. It was a chapel of ease. The diocese had licensed it three hundred years ago, and nobody had ever thought it was anything but what it was, a second roof inside the same parish for people who lived too far from the first.

He took his leg off the stool.

He did not have to move the parish. He had never had to. He only had to ask for what had been given, without question, to every village too far from its church since there had been churches: a chapel, in the same parish, under the same priest, with the same book. The seat stayed on the island. The church stayed the parish church. Nothing was conceded. Nobody was asked to agree that a prince's city was anything more than a place where a great many parishioners happened to live.

And the close would have to grant it. Not because they liked Kaldo. Because to refuse a chapel of ease to half a parish four miles from its church, when a hill village twelve miles from its church had been granted one without a question, they would need a reason, and the reason would have to be written down. He did not think they wanted to write one. Not yet. Whatever was in the Visitor's report, it was not yet a thing anybody at the close wanted to put their name under in a letter to a country priest about a chapel.

He wrote the petition that night, in his own hand, at the presbytery table, by the lamp. It took him three drafts. The first was too long, because he had begun by explaining, and a man who explains to a chancery has already admitted there is something to explain. The second was too short. The third said what the chapel was, and where, and for how many, and how far from the parish church by the road, and that the parish church remained the parish church, its seat unchanged, its churchyard in use, and its book kept by its priest as before; and that it asked the licence of a chapel of ease, in the ancient form, for the ease of those parishioners who could not walk to it. He cited the form. He cited the chapel in the hills, by name, and the year of its licence, which he found in his own old commonplace book, because he had copied it out at twenty-four when he still thought such things would matter.

He sent it, as a Visitor's parish did, to the Visitor, to go on with his own hand under it. Benedek was at Grayrock that month. It went up the river road on the carrier.

He did not tell the prince. It was not the prince's to know until it was granted, and if it was refused it would not be the prince's to know at all.

The licence came in the fourth week, which was quick for the close, and he read it standing in the presbytery doorway with the carrier's bag still open at his feet.

It was four lines in a chancery clerk's hand, under the diocesan seal, granting the church of the new town of Kaldo, in the parish of Kaldo, the licence of a chapel of ease, for the Sunday office, baptism and marriage, the parish church and its rights remaining unaltered. It was signed by a name he did not know. That was usual. It was exactly what he had asked for and not one word more, and he had not asked for one word more, and he read it three times anyway, looking for the word.

There was a second paper folded inside it, smaller, in a different hand. Benedek's.

Granted, as you see; I sent it on with my own name under it and a line saying I knew the church and the distance. I was asked at the close, by a clerk I do not know, whether it was the parish that was moving. I said it was not, since it is not. Your chapel in the hills did most of the work. I had forgotten you were ever that young. B.O.

Doran read that three times too.

A clerk he did not know, at the close, asking. Not the chancery that wrote the licence. Somebody else, somewhere in the same buildings, who had seen a country priest's petition go by with a Visitor's name under it and had wanted to know whether a parish was being moved into a prince's city. And Benedek had answered truthfully, because Benedek always answered truthfully, and the true answer was no.

It was no. The parish was not moving. He had made sure of that with every line he wrote. And yet somebody had asked, which meant that somebody had thought it might be, which meant that somebody at the close was reading what came out of Kaldo with a particular kind of attention, the kind Doran had once given, in his youth, to a curate's accounts that he suspected were short.

He did not know what to do with that. He did the thing he did with everything he did not know what to do with. He took the parish book down off its shelf, the current one, his own, and opened it to the page of the week, and in the margin, where he had written for forty years the things a priest notes for his own memory, he wrote in small letters: The new town's church licensed a chapel of ease, the parish unchanged. The Visitor asked at the close whether the parish was moving. He answered that it was not.

And the date. And then he put Benedek's note inside the back cover, with the licence, and put the book back on its shelf.

The first Sunday office in the new church was on the second Sunday of the high summer, at ten, and the nave was full.

He had not told anybody to come. He had told the first quarter's street gangers, and the street gangers had told everybody, and the barony's printing office had without being asked printed a small notice with a picture of a church on it, a church with a whole tower, which Doran thought was a piece of optimism, and pinned it to the tap posts in every street of the first and second quarters. By half past nine the nave was full, and by ten the doors were open and the people were standing in the porch and out on the ring street under the scaffold of the unfinished tower, in the sun, the way they had stood on the island's steps at the Easter after the first draw.

There was no bell. There would not be a bell until there was a tower to hang it in. A boy from the first-quarter school stood in the porch with a handbell from the school and rang it at ten, and it sounded thin and cheerful and nothing like a church, and Doran found as he came up the aisle on his stick behind the cross that he had to stop for a moment by the third row, as if for his hip, before he could go on.

He did not count them from the pulpit step. He tried to, during the second psalm, and lost count at three hundred, and did not start again.

He christened four children after the office, one after another, at a font that was a stone basin borrowed from the island church and carried out on a cart the day before by two of Hewitt's men, and he wrote them in the parish book on a table in the vestry, which had no door yet, with the parents standing round to watch him write. The book had come out with him in its box. That was what moved. The parish book's daily use, and the Sunday office, and the christenings, and the marrying. And the priest.

One of the four was Hanne Pell's daughter's boy, a month old, who had been waiting for a Sunday when his grandmother could get him baptised without carrying him eight miles, and who screamed from the first drop of water to the last and was, Hanne said, going to be a singer.

The prince came to see him the week after, on the island, in the evening, at the presbytery.

He had heard, of course. Everybody had heard. Doran had not told him himself because he had wanted it to be done first, and it was done. The prince did not say anything about that. He stood in the presbytery's front room with the door open behind him and the evening coming in, and looked at the boxes on the floor.

"You're going out there," he said.

"I'm going out there." Doran was sitting at the table with his leg up, sorting forty years of sermons into two heaps, one to keep and one to burn, and finding the burn heap a good deal higher than he had expected. "Two rooms at the end of a first-quarter street, a hundred yards from the church door. The barony's house, at the barony's rent. Mistress Ferra has priced it." He looked up. "A Copper a week. The same as a plague house on the island. I pointed that out to her. She said a Copper a week was what the barony charged anybody who left the island for one of its houses, and she wasn't going to invent a new price for a priest."

The prince almost smiled. "That sounds like her."

"It's where my people are," Doran said. "Most of them. I can't walk four miles on a Sunday any more and I won't ask them to. A priest lives where his parish lives. This church will have its office, every Sunday, the early one, for the ones who stay. I'll come in on the cart for it. And for the burials."

"The burials stay here."

"The ones that want to." Doran put down the sermon he was holding. "The families who want their dead with their dead. That's most of the old families. They'll bring them in from the flat on a cart, the way the hill village brought theirs down the track. The ground out there outside your wall is for the ones who want to lie near where they lived. I'll consecrate it next week. It's a good piece of ground. It drains." He looked at the prince. "You chose it well. You chose it before you had anybody to put in it. That's the only piece of your whole city I've ever been glad to see drawn early."

The prince did not answer that. He looked out of the open door, toward the east, where the churchyard lay under the island's wall in the long evening light.

Doran watched him. Then he got up, with the stick, which took a while, and said, "Walk with me," and went out past him into the evening, and the prince came.

The churchyard ran along the inside of the island's east wall, a long strip of grass between the wall and the backs of the last houses, and at its north end were the rows.

They were not like the rest of the churchyard. The rest had stones, some of them, and mounds, and a yew. The rows were rows. They had been dug in the plague years, when there had been no time for anything but rows, long straight trenches, one after another, filled and closed and filled again, and the grass had grown over them flat and even so that there was nothing to show where one ended and the next began, except, at the head of each row, a small plain post, with a number cut into it, and nothing else.

Doran stood at the end of the third row and leaned on his stick.

"Here," he said. "When it comes. Not out on the flat. Here. By the wall, with these." He did not look at the prince. He looked at the grass. "With the ones I wrote down, in the book. And the ones a man I won't name paid to have written down, when nobody else would. And the ones in the worst of it that went into these rows so fast I never had their names to write." He moved the stick a little on the grass. "There are more of those than there are of the others. Somebody counted them, in the ward, for as long as they could. I didn't. I was burying. I've always thought I should have counted as well."

The prince stood beside him and did not say anything for a while. The evening was very quiet. A gull went over. From the far side of the wall came the sound of the lake on the stones below.

"That will be kept," the prince said.

"I know it will," said Doran. "It's in the book."

The prince looked at him then.

"I wrote it in last week," Doran said. "In the margin, the same day as the chapel. The priest to be buried by the east wall, in the rows. In my hand, with the date. Anybody who keeps this book after me will read it. Whoever that is." He turned on his stick, carefully, and began to walk back along the wall toward the presbytery, and after a moment the prince came after him, slowly, to keep to his pace. "I said it to you once," Doran said, without looking round. "The day I came up the hill about the fees. A man who is not in the book was never here. I was angry with you that day, and I was right to be. It holds for priests."

He consecrated the ground outside the new city's wall on the next Saturday, in the morning, walking the bounds of it with the cross carried before him and the stick in his hand, and the holy water in a bucket carried by the same school boy who had rung the handbell, who took his duties very seriously and spilled almost none.

It was a square of good dry ground on the rise outside the north-west wall, fenced with a low stone wall of its own, with a gate, and a path through the middle of it, and a yew planted at the gate by somebody at the barony who knew that a churchyard needed a yew, though it was the height of a boy and would not look like anything for a hundred years. There was nothing in it. Grass, and the path, and the little yew. Doran walked the four sides of it slowly, saying the words, and a small crowd from the first quarter stood at the gate and watched, and some of them crossed themselves, and at the end he stood at the gate and blessed it, and it was holy ground.

Nobody would lie in it for a while, God willing. That was the strange thing about a new churchyard. You made it ready, and hoped, and waited for the first one, and did not know who it would be.

He went back through the north-west gate and along the ring street to his church, and sat for a while in the nave on the stone step at the east end, where the altar would go, with the stick across his knees and the light coming in white through the new glass.

It was a chapel of ease. It was lawful and small and ancient, and it conceded nothing, and the close had granted it in four lines. He had done nothing that any country priest with a village too far from its church had not done in every century since there were churches. He had followed his people, which was a priest's whole work.

He thought, sitting there, of a clerk at the close he did not know, asking a question of Benedek Orme in a passage somewhere, and of Benedek's answer, which was true. He did not know what the clerk had wanted, and he did not know what was in the Visitor's report, and he did not know why, when he thought of them together, he felt the way he had felt forty years ago in the hills, when the rector came up the track on his pony with the accounts under his arm.

He said a prayer about it, since there was nothing else he could do, and got up, which took a while, and went home to his two rooms at the end of the street. From the window of the front one, if he leaned, he could see the stump of his tower in its scaffold against the evening, with the crane standing on top of it, waiting for the stone.
