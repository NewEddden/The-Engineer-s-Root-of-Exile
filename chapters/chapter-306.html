Chapter 306 - Ostrel Silver

By nine in the morning on the third of July the king had found the switch by the door, and worked out what it did, and done it, by Ward's count from the passage, forty-one times.

Ward was on a chair outside the door at the head of the west stair, not sitting in it. He had put it there at five, when he and Saban brought the king up the water stair to the postern with the castle still dark, and had stood beside it since with his helm on the seat, as if the chair were for the helm. Under the door a line of light came and went. On, and a long while, and off. On.

"Since six," said Ward. "He slept three hours on his back with his boots on and his hands on his chest like a man on a tomb. Then he got up and found that."

Emeric had not slept. He had been on the ramp in the field after midnight, and in the line room before five with Imani looking at his throat, and at the ward until seven with Rylan, who would not stop talking; and somewhere in there the night had turned into a morning without asking him, and his father was in the keep, in the rooms that had been the baron's guest rooms in Wexler's time and nobody's since.

He let his sense go through the door, out of habit. His father's note was low: a note kept down the way a voice is kept down, very steady, close in to itself, with nothing at its edges for anybody to take hold of. It was sitting by the window. Not at it; beside it.

He knocked.

"Yes," said the king, at a volume that would have died at arm's length, and the light went off.



Harrold was in the chair to the left of the window, where the wall hid him from the inner ward, in one of Ward's shirts, which fitted him across the shoulders and nowhere else. His hair was wet. He had shaved with something, badly. The coat he had come out of the Church's keeping in, the one Rylan had lain on across the ferry's deck, was folded over the other chair with its sleeves crossed on its breast, and smelled even from the door of incense and river.

He did not get up. He looked at his son the way he had looked at him on the ferry, as if checking a thing was where it had been put.

"The water in that room is hot," he said.

"Yes."

"It comes out of the wall hot. You turn the brass and it comes." He looked at the bathroom door, which stood open on wet tiles. "I let it go to see when it would go cold. It didn't. I stopped after a while because it occurred to me that somebody would have to carry it, somewhere, and I'd be keeping them from their breakfast."

"Nobody carries it. It comes from the works, under the river and up the hill in a pipe."

His father considered that.

"Ward wrote that you'd put water in the houses," he said. "At the bottom of a letter about the garrison's boots. Five words. I took it to mean a pump at the end of each street. I thought, that's a good thing, and thought no more about it, because I had a council the next morning about the Isle."

He put his finger on the switch without looking. The lamp in the ceiling came on. He looked up at it as though it had done something rude.

"He wrote about these later. Two lines. The long hall lit without fire." The lamp went out. "It doesn't hum. A lamp stone hums; you can feel it in your teeth under the big ones in the throne room. This doesn't do anything." On. "It's a wire."

"It's a wire," said Emeric.

"Ward said so. The cipher has no word for wire, so he spelled it, and it took me an evening." Off. "I thought it was the cipher going wrong."

Emeric had come across the room without meaning to, because at the door he could not hear, and found he had stopped a foot from the chair, which was where his father had always wanted him.

"There's nobody at the door but Ward," he said.

"I know who's at the door."

The voice did not get any louder. He understood after a while that it was not going to: that it had been at that volume for twenty-eight years in rooms where it had needed to be, and had nowhere else to go.

His father had said very little to him since the ferry, and most of it had been practical. He had not said anything about the bank. He had not said the cardinal's name. Neither had Emeric.

"I'd like to see it," said Harrold. "The town. When it's convenient to you. Not today, and not in that." He looked at the folded coat. "And not as anything. I'll not walk about a duke's streets being a king, a season after the council put me in the Church's keeping for being one badly. That would be a gift to a great many people." "I'll not be a difficulty in your town."

"You're not a difficulty."

"I'm the largest difficulty in the realm," said his father, without any expression at all. "I'd like to be a small one in your house." He turned his head and looked at his son. "There were two of the household's riders on that bank who didn't get up. I'd like their names. I'll write to their people. Under no seal, and not in my hand. But I'll write."

"Saban will have them."

His father did not ask how they had known the hour. He did not ask who had counted forty-two horses, or how a duke in the south had known the colour of a carriage's painted-out panels. Emeric watched him not ask it. It went by in the room like a man walking past an open door and looking straight ahead.



Imani came up at ten with her bag, from the ward, where Rylan had been explaining to her orderly since dawn that a cracked rib was a rib with an opinion and no more.

She looked at the king across the room, the whole of him, as if he had been brought in on a door and she was deciding which end to start at.

"Majesty," she said. "Sit there. In the light."

His father looked at the light, which was the window; and Ward came in without being asked and closed the shutter on the ward's side and opened the one on the river's, and went out again. Harrold sat where she had said.

She was a quarter of an hour: pulse, eyes, gums, a thumb pressed into each nail. She had him breathe out, and smelled it, without apology. She asked what he had eaten, every day for three months, and he told her at arm's length, very exactly, the way a man gives evidence.

"Bread," she said, at the end. "Broth."

"The same broth for eleven weeks. On Sundays a little fish." He considered it. "They were very attentive to my mind. They sent devotions."

"Ward read us your letter. The first queen told you to watch what you ate. Who tasted it?"

"Nobody. I watched it, as I was told. I ate it slowly and I watched it." He said it without weight. "I don't know what I'd have seen."

"Nothing, until it was too late, and nor would I." She sat back on her heels. "There's nothing in you. Your gums are clean and your nails are clean and there's no smell on your breath but bad broth. You're a stone light. You've been eating like a man counting what's on his plate in case it's evidence, and sleeping like a man on watch, and that's all that's wrong with you."

"Two things," said his father.

"The first I can mend. Maren will mend it by Friday; she tastes everything in that kitchen before it goes up a stair, because that's how she cooks." Imani picked up her bag. "The second's yours, Majesty. I can't do anything for a man who doesn't want to sleep."

At the door she stopped and looked back; not at the king.

"You've not slept either."

"I will."

"Tonight. Or I'll put you in the bed next to Rylan's and you can hear about his rib." She glanced at Harrold. "He's like that too, you know. Counting. He gets it from somewhere."

She went out. His father sat with the river's light on one side of his face. After a while he reached for the switch, and stopped with his hand an inch from it, and put the hand back on his knee.



Ferra sat him at supper, because somebody had to and nobody else would.

The long table in the hall had never had any order to speak of, except that the head was Armand's and had been since the ninth year. She met the king at the foot of the west stair at seven, in his dark coat, which Maren's girls had taken away at noon and brought back smelling of nothing, and walked in beside him, and stopped, and looked at the head of the table, where Armand was already sitting.

Armand stood up.

It took longer than it once had, because there was more of him now: in a coat of Ward's, with his cropped hair coming in dark at the roots under the white, and his hands, which had shaken for ten years, quite still at his sides.

"Majesty," he said.

The king looked at him for a long time. The hall had gone quiet the way it went quiet when a dish was dropped. Emeric felt his father's note do nothing at all. It was the most careful nothing he had ever felt in a man.

"Secretary," said Harrold.

"Not for some years. Your Majesty last saw me at the tower's dinner in the year of the Isle levy, I think, when I was sixty-seven and told the Circle's master at his own table that his lightning was ornamental. I'm told you laughed. Nobody saw it." Armand inclined his head. "I was ill in the winter, Majesty. A stone mended me. It was rather more thorough than I'd asked it to be."

The king looked at the hands, and the hair, and the coat too short at the wrist. Then he looked away, the way a man looks away from a letter on a desk that is not addressed to him.

"Then I'm glad of it," he said, and asked nothing else.

"That's your chair, Majesty," said Armand, with his hand on its back. "I've outlived three kings at tables in my time and never once given a chair up to any of them. I'd like to be able to say I did it once."

"No," said the king.

He went down the table and sat on the bench halfway along the far side, between Bram and the wall, where the lamp did not quite reach and nobody coming in at the door would see his face first.

Bram looked at the king beside him the way he looked at a casting he had not poured. Then he reached over and moved the salt from his own side of the board to the king's.

"You'll want that," said Bram. "Maren doesn't put enough in."

"I heard that," said Maren, from the door.

"You were meant to," said Bram, and went back to his bread.

After a while the hall's noise came back, the way water comes back into a footprint on a beach. His father ate everything Maren's girls put in front of him, slowly, and watched none of it.



The coin came up on the Sunday, in a glove.

Mira brought it to the study a little after eleven, with the bells going across the inner ward for the end of the service. She put the glove on the desk, one of the stealth suit's, and shook it, and a disc of silver fell out of the cuff onto the blotter and rang once.

"At the landing," she said. "When Ward's stone let go, the Church men's wagon was down to its hubs in the gravel, and everything that had been in the men on it was in the gravel round it. That was by the off wheel. I picked it up because it shone. Then the ship came and I forgot it was in my glove until last night, when it fell on my foot."

Emeric picked it up.

It was the size of a Silver Rand and twice as thick, heavy with the weight good silver has and bad silver pretends to, and nobody had clipped it. On one face, a lamp: the old shape of the Church's Light, a flame in a bowl. On the other, a walled house with a tower at its end, cut sharp and deep; and round that face's rim, in the narrow square letters the Church used for things that were meant to last, a line of words worn soft at the high points by somebody's thumb.

What the Light holds, it keeps.

Saban, in the window seat, weighed the disc on his palm and said nothing, which from Saban about a piece of silver was a long time. Mira went. On the stair she met somebody coming up and said "Father," in the voice the whole town used to Doran; and the slow steps came on, an old man's, with a book under his arm.

It was the brown one with the cracked spine that lived by the parish house's kitchen door. He stood in the study door with his surplice still on under his coat, and looked at the thing in Saban's hand, and his face changed.

"That's a house token," he said. "May I." He took it to the window. "The brothers carry them on the road. You show it at any house of the order and you're fed and lodged and nobody asks your business, and the inns on the brothers' roads take them for a night's hay. That's Ostrel's house. The little houses strike in lead. Ostrel strikes in silver because it can." He turned it to the rim, and his lips moved. "And those are the cardinal's words. Off his seal. They go with the office, not the man. I've had them at the foot of every letter the close has sent me in forty years." He gave it back to Saban with the tips of his fingers, as if it were warm. "Where did it come from?"

"A river bank," said Emeric. "On Friday night."

Doran stood very still.

The town knew. A knight had walked up the hill to the ward at five on Saturday morning held up between two others, singing, and a man nobody knew had come up the water stair to the postern with Ward and Saban, and the postern's night man had told his wife. The town had decided, Saban said, that it was the king, and was waiting very politely to be told so.

"Then I'd best show you this," said Doran, "and you can decide whether I've wasted your Sunday."

It was the lodgers' book, which every parish kept by a rule older than Doran: every brother of the Church who slept under the parish roof, his house, where he was going, the date. The entries were very few. Brothers came to Kaldo only in April, in twos, for the coast's fishing blessings.

The last entry was dated the second of July. Brother Iven, of the northern houses, to the coast. And beside it, in Doran's small upright hand: alone. Came on foot at dusk. Asked for the room over the kitchen. Ate; did not take wine. Gone before I rose; the bed not slept in; the window open.

"I don't get northern brothers in July," said Doran. "Not alone. He was civil and had a good road coat and clean hands, cleaner than a man's who'd walked from Wayford. He asked for the room over the kitchen because it was cool, he said." He put his finger on the line. "It isn't cool. It's over the kitchen. It's the hottest room in the house. It's the one with the window over the roofs to the lake."

Nobody said anything.

"I keep the book so that somebody can decide what's in it," said Doran, and picked up his hat. At the door he stopped. "I'll pray for him. Whoever he is. I pray for all of them in the book." He went down the stair, slowly.

Ferra had written the date and the name and the house and nothing else.

"The Church moved the king on Friday," she said. "It would want to know if Kaldo did anything about it. So it sent a brother to sit up at a window over the lake and see what came home before dawn. That's one way to read it."

"Mine's that the Church has never once in twelve years sent a brother to sit in Doran's kitchen at night," said Saban, "and on the one night that it matters, one comes, on foot, and asks for that room." He turned the token over on his knee, lamp, house, lamp. "Somebody told him which night and which room, or told somebody who told him. And that somebody's in Kaldo, or talks to somebody who is."

"Or the Church can count to two."

"Or that. I'd like it to be that." He put the token in his waistcoat. "I'll find out which. It'll take a while. A man who talks both ways listens hardest when he hears somebody asking."

Emeric looked at the open book and thought about the K Discovery coming down onto the lake at twenty to five on Saturday morning, dark, on her stones, into the reeds by the secret dock with a king aboard and Rylan on a door; and about a man sitting up all night at a hot window over the roofs, waiting to see what came home.

"Both," he said. Ferra wrote it.



He drew the hull at the long room's table on the Monday night, because Ward asked him to, and because it had been in his head for three days and would not go away.

The six were there, and Armand at the head with a cup of Imani's permitted wine, and the lamp pulled low so the light fell in a circle on the paper. The king was not. Nobody had asked him, and he had not asked to be asked; he went up the west stair at eight every night, and the line under his door went out at nine.

It was not much of a drawing. His eyes had seen the thing for the space of ten breaths, at night, over trees, with his father's ferry going away downriver and Rylan's note gone ragged on her deck. Something long and white, the way a gull is white at dusk; pointed at the front; and along its back a row of tall thin curved ribs, like the stone ribs that hold a church's roof up from outside. A rail that had caught some light and given it back yellow. A third of the K Endeavor's length, perhaps less.

Then he drew what his sense had seen.

"Stones here," he said, and put a ring of marks along its belly. "Lift. Eight or ten; it was going away. And in the nose, bigger. Two, I think. All of them working. I felt them go up out of the trees the way I feel ours go up off the lake."

"Then somebody was pouring," said Bram.

"There was a seat, where the casting seat is on a capital hull. I felt the brass and the plate. There were people aboard, four or five, at the back, and the man from the bank, and another with him." He did not say who the other was. "Nobody was in the seat. Nobody had a hand on anything. She was held. The way ours are held."

The lamp hissed.

"No," said Armand, pleasantly, in the voice he had always used to tell a third-year he had got a figure wrong. He leaned into the lamp's circle and looked at the drawing upside down. "There's no such thing. Not outside this town. Every ship in this kingdom is a job. I've watched the capital's hulls come in to their moorings every evening of my life with the casters carried off them on boards. A hull that holds itself is a thing you did, in a cellar, with a stone you wrote from the inside."

"Somebody else writes from the inside."

"The high Church does. A handful of old men at Ostrel, for their altars and their seals, and they keep the knowing of it to themselves. And the cardinal's one of them; you've told me so, and I believe you, and I watched him for forty years and never once saw it." He tapped the drawing's belly with one long finger. "Writing a stone for an altar is one thing. One man doesn't build a ship. One man doesn't fit ten lift stones and a seat nobody sits in, in a ship with gilt on her rails, and have it waiting in a wood for the one night he needs to leave quickly. That's a yard, and a crew, and years, and a great deal of money. That's..."

He stopped. He sat back out of the lamp's light, slowly, and was quiet long enough that Ferra looked up from her book.

"My master came down from the last climb with his nose bleeding," said Armand at last, in a different voice, "and told the high table there was something up there. Thick. And the Church shut the climbs the next spring, every tower in the realm, by one letter from the close." He turned the cup in his hands. "I was an apprentice. I thought it cowardly, and said so to anybody who'd listen, and I've thought so all my life." He looked at nothing. "I never once asked what they were protecting. A man doesn't, about a thing he's already decided is stupid."

Ward had not sat down. He stood at the table's end with his arms folded, where the lamp caught his hands and not his face.

"I felt it too," he said. "Going up. I thought my head was ringing from the bank." He unfolded his arms and put one finger on the white ribs. "Then somebody's been lying for a long time."

Saban made a small noise that was not quite a word.

"One ship," said Ferra.

"One that I saw."

She wrote it. Then she looked at Armand, and at Ward's finger, and Emeric watched her do the thing she did with a column that would not come right: go back to the top and begin again, every line. "Armand said a yard," she said. "A yard's not for one ship."

"No."

"Then where is it?"

"West first, clear of the hills. Then north."

Imani had been watching Armand with her chin on her hand. "So we're not the first," she said, to nobody, and it was not a question; and Armand, in the dark beyond the lamp, laughed once with no pleasure in it at all.

"We're the first who'll admit it."



The slate on the study wall had been written in the same order since December. The K Amara's lift stones first; her trim and steadies and held stones; then her shell, from the second shard stone out of the bucket. And at the very bottom, under a line, the twins' pin stones, which would hold an escort still in the air so she could fire the big guns in her promises without going backward across the sky.

He had put them at the bottom because every gun Kaldo had ever drawn had been drawn against the ground, and a ship that fought the ground could sit on the sea and do it.

He stood in front of the slate at midnight with the cloth in his hand, and rubbed out the whole of it, and wrote it again. The K Amara's shell, first. The pin stones for escorts one and two. Then everything else, in the old order, below a line. And under the shell, smaller, so he would see it every morning: against something that flies.

Ferra had come in behind him with two cups and the gravity mug, which she had given back to him on condition that he keep it in the middle of the desk. She read the slate, both orders, every line.

"You've put the guns ahead of the ship."

"I've put the shell ahead of the ship, and the pins ahead of the trim. A ship that fights a ship can't fire from the water. She has to be up where the other one is, and take a hit from her own height, not just from below."

"That's Bram's cutting for a month. And all of yours. You'll be down the stair every night with the shard stone till September."

"Yes."

"For one white ship."

"For a yard," said Emeric.

She did not argue. She wrote in her own book, not the works book, and turned it so he could read the line. 5 Jul. Slate re-ordered: shell and pins first. E. says: a white hull, and a yard. F. Then she gave him his tea.



Rylan had been on the ward four days and had been moved twice, once for singing and once for the beer.

The first night he and the night nurse had done four verses of the drovers' one with the grey mare in it before the woman in the next bed but two asked, quite civilly, whether one of them might be killed; so Imani had moved him to the room at the end of the passage, where he could sing to the wall. On the third day the orderly's boy had come out of it with an empty jug and a copper, and Imani had moved him again, to the bed under her own office window where she could see him through the glass, and had taken the copper.

He was propped on four pillows with his ribs strapped and his arm on a board on the blanket, dressed from the shoulder to the knuckles in Imani's linen and yellow salve; and across his knees lay a ferryman's pole three feet long, cut down from a broom handle, with RYLAN burned into it in Wren's careful capitals and a small tin crown nailed to the top.

"Tess did the crown," he said. "Tess says I'm to have it over the door. Nessa says over her dead body." He looked past Emeric to the passage. "Is that him?"

The king had come up the hill through the back lanes at eight with his collar turned up, before the town was about, and stood in the ward's door with his hands behind his back, looking at the long clean room and the tall windows and the morning in them.

Rylan tried to get up.

"Down," said Imani, through the glass, without looking up.

Rylan went down, slowly, with a face he made into a grin halfway.

"Majesty. Forgive me not getting up. I'm told I'm not allowed."

"So I heard." His father stopped at the bed's foot. "You were the one with the pole."

"I was. I'd always wanted to be a ferryman." Rylan considered it. "I'd not want to again, I don't think. It's a great deal of standing about in the wet, and then a fellow on the bank takes against you."

"That has been my experience of most posts," said the king.

Rylan laughed, and stopped very suddenly with his good hand pressed flat to his side and his face gone the colour of the pillow; breathed through his nose, short, twice; and grinned up through it.

"Don't make me laugh, Majesty. She'll have me moved again. There's only the cellar left."

"I'll try not to," said Harrold, with a perfectly straight face. "It isn't generally a difficulty."

He came round the side of the bed and stood a foot from it, and looked down at the arm on its board: the arm he had put into the river himself, on the ferry, with his hand on Rylan's chest.

The dressing stopped at the knuckles. Below it, on the back of the hand and up the inside of the wrist where the linen did not reach, the skin was marked: red going to brown, very fine, branching and branching again out of a single line, the way frost branches on a window, or the roots of a tree show in a river bank where the water has cut it. It went up under the linen and did not come out again. Emeric had seen marks like it once on Earth, in a photograph of a man who had stood under a tree in a storm; they had a long name, and faded in a week. He did not say so.

His father looked at the marks for a long time.

His note did not change. It went, if anything, quieter, and stiller, the way a pool goes still when something large goes by underneath it; and stayed so. His face told Emeric nothing, and Emeric did not ask.

"Does it hurt?" said the king.

"Not a bit," said Rylan.

"It hurts a great deal," said Imani, through the glass.

"It hurts a bit," said Rylan.

The king nodded, as if that were the answer he had expected. Then he laid his hand, very lightly, on the broom handle across Rylan's knees, just below the tin crown, and left it there a breath.

"Thank you for the crossing," he said, at the volume that died at arm's length, so that only the bed heard it; and went back down the ward and out without looking round.

Rylan lay looking at the crown on his pole. He did not say anything at all for a while, which in four days, Imani said afterward, he had not once managed.



Abloh made the coat in two days and complained about it for four.

"Majesty's got a king's shoulders and a prisoner's waist," he said at the fitting, with the chalk in his teeth and the king standing in the middle of the sail loft with his arms out like a scarecrow, being measured. "I can cut for one or the other by Thursday. Not both. I'll cut for the shoulders. Maren'll do the waist."

"Will she," said the king.

"She's started," said Abloh darkly.

It was plain brown wool from the Milford mill, the kind every second man in Kaldo wore to work, without a thread of gold or a button that meant anything; and on the Thursday, the eighth, at ten, the king came down the west stair in it and stood in the hall's door and looked like nobody. Almost nobody. There was nothing Abloh could do about his eyes.

Ward had arranged the rest in four sentences at breakfast. The king would walk where he liked, with the duke; Ward behind, as he had walked behind the duke for thirteen years; and ahead and on either side, in their own clothes, never closer than twenty yards and never further than forty, Arthur, Mira and Bren, who had walked up the face of a lord's house in May and could certainly walk through a market.

"They'll not be noticed?" said the king.

"Bren will. He's tall and he walks into things. Anybody watching will watch him." Ward looked down the table at Bren, who had gone red. "That's why he's there."

They took the tram from the South Gate. It came up the Gate Avenue under its wire between the young limes, cream and green, with the brass wheel ticking along the wire over its roof, and stopped at the platform under the gate's arch, level with its own floor. The king watched it come the whole length of the avenue without saying anything.

"Ward wrote me about this," he said. "A cart that goes on its own, drawn by the wire. I thought he'd been drinking. I nearly wrote and asked."

The conductor, a girl with a clipper on a string, looked at the man in the brown coat and at the duke and kept her face very straight indeed.

"Copper each, sir."

The king put his hand into his coat.

Emeric watched it go into the pocket of a coat that had been made on Tuesday and had never had anything in it, and stop, and come out empty; and understood, a breath after the king did, that he had come out of the Church's keeping with the clothes he stood in and nothing else, not a ring, not a seal, not a coin, and that it had not until that moment occurred to him that a coin might be wanted.

Before Emeric could move, a copper went into the girl's palm from the side.

"Two," said Mira, from the platform's edge, where she had not been a breath before, in her grey work dress with a market basket on her arm; and paid the second, and was gone toward the tram's back door without looking at anybody, a carter's daughter going to market.

"I owe that knight a copper," said the king.

"You don't, sir."

"I'll owe it anyway." He sat down by a window on the slatted bench, between a woman with a crate of eggs and a works apprentice asleep against the glass. "I'd like to owe somebody something small," he said, very low, to the window, and Emeric did not think he had been meant to hear it.



They got down at the civic ring and walked, because on foot nobody looks at anybody.

He walked as he had walked the long gallery in the palace, and looked at everything and asked almost nothing. At the six glass towers along the civic ring, the tallest at twenty storeys with the morning in its windows floor over floor, he stopped in the middle of the pavement and put his head back and looked up the whole height of it, while a man with a barrow went round him.

"He wrote me twenty floors," he said. "I thought that was the cipher."

On the east avenue, near the wall, the ring line went over. The train came along the viaduct outside the bastions with no warning but a long rising sigh in the rails, white, five cars, its windows full of people, and was gone round the town in the time of ten breaths. Nobody else on the avenue looked up. It went every twenty minutes, and had since April.

His father stood and watched the place where it had been, the empty viaduct and the wire still humming faintly, long after there was anything to watch.

"I signed a paper once," he said at last, very low, "for the crown's share of the toll on the Wayford line. Keller brought it with sixty others and I signed it between the Isle wardens' pay and a petition about swans. It came to more than the toll on the south road. I asked Keller why, and he said because people used it, Majesty, and I said that would be all." He was quiet. "I didn't come and look."

"You couldn't have."

"No." He started walking again. "That's not the same thing as not having."

In the market hall a woman selling cheese looked at him over her board, and at Emeric, and at him again, with her knife stopped in the wheel.

"You'll be family, sir. You've his eyes."

The king looked at her long enough that Emeric felt Ward, behind, shift onto the front of his boots; and twenty yards off by the fish, Arthur put down a herring he had been pretending to want.

"So I'm told," said the king.

"Uncle, is it?"

"Something like."

"Well, you'll want to try that, then," said the woman, and cut him a corner of the hard yellow one from the Milford dairy and held it out on the knife; and he took it off with his fingers and ate it standing in the middle of the market hall, and told her it was very good, which was true, and she was pleased, and charged Emeric for a whole pound.



He asked to see the lower ward. He asked it coming out of the market hall into the noon light, without a reason, the way he gave instructions; and Emeric did not ask why, and took him.

The lanes of the lower ward still ran down toward the wall as they had when the rows were full and the wells were bad, narrow and crooked, with lamps at their corners now and water at every door. The king walked down them under somebody's washing and looked at the doors.

At the bottom, by the north well, the school was coming out: forty children through one door at once, shouting, scattering up the lanes; and behind them, last, as she always was, Sela came out onto the step with the empty bread basket on her hip and pulled the door to.

She saw Arthur first; she always saw Arthur first, in any crowd, by the back of his neck. He was at the well's lip being a man drinking from a well, and he went, very slowly, red from the collar up. Then she saw Emeric. Then the man beside him in the brown coat.

She was greyer than she had been in the first year, and thinner, and she looked at the king's face the way she looked at a coin somebody had put on her counter and called silver. She looked at the eyes.

"You'll be the father," she said.

"Yes," said the king.

Ward, behind, did not move. Arthur had stopped pretending to drink and stood with the dipper in his hand, very straight, the way he stood in the yard when Ward came out of the armoury.

Sela came down off the step, one step and then the other, and stood in the lane in front of the king of Randillia with her basket on her hip, a head shorter than he was.

"Your war took my eldest," she said. "When the crown came choosing men. He was a boy. I wasn't told where he'd gone, or how, in all the years after. I thought he was dead in a ditch." She did not raise her voice; she had never needed to. "He came home. Off the Isle, out of Kesrath's camp, where he'd been half his life with the others nobody sent for. The duke brought him. His chest is no good; the camp did that. He mends nets in the loft by the quay now, slow. He's glad of it. He says so."

The king stood in the lane.

"What's his name?"

Sela looked at him.

"I signed the levy that year," said the king. "For the Isle. In the small office with the window. And the list of how many from each barony, and Kaldo's was a number." He did not look away from her. "I'd like to know his name."

It was the same low voice. But it had gone, for once, a little past arm's length; enough for Sela to hear, and Ward, and Arthur at the well.

"Edric," said Sela.

"Edric," said the king.

She looked at him a breath longer. Then she put her hand into the basket, which was not quite empty, and took out the heel of the last loaf, and held it out.

"You're thin," she said. "Here. They'll have fed you on nothing up there." And, not looking round: "Arthur. If you're going to stand at that well all day you can draw me a bucket while you're about it."

Arthur drew her a bucket. She took it off him without a word and went back up her step with it, and in, and the door shut.

The king stood in the lane with the heel of bread. After a while he ate it, standing there at the bottom of the lower ward by the north well in the noon light, with the crumbs going down the front of Abloh's coat; slowly, the whole of it, and did not watch it at all.



Saban had the rest of the knife man on the thirteenth.

He sat in the study's window seat with his hat on his knee and did not begin for a while, which was how Emeric knew it was good.

"The posting-house driver. You'll remember I had a man going after him slowly."

"Going on four years."

"He's a careful man, and so am I. He went over the border in March buying wool, and in June he found the driver drinking on his wife's people's money, which is running out, and bought him a better drink, and told him a grocer in the south would pay for a story about an old fare and never put his name to anybody. Which is true. I'd like it kept true."

"It's kept."

"He drove the man with the scar north, the winter of the coach road. Four days, to a town past the border on the old salt road, which I'll still not name in a room with a window, and to a chandler's yard in it. It sells rope and tallow to the salt carters, honestly, at the front. At the back, over the stable, a man sits at a table with a lamp and a strongbox. That's the door. The purse in the north that the road calls a network: you go in at the back of a chandler's and up a stair."

"You've seen it."

"My man's seen the stair. Nobody's seen the table but the men who've been up it." Saban paused. "The driver set the man with the scar down in that yard at dusk, and was told to wait and take him on south in the morning. So he put up his horses in the back stable. And there was another horse in it already: a good horse, with the brothers' plain bridle and a road blanket with the Light sewn in the corner. In the morning the yard's man came to that stall and the brother paid him for the night's hay, and the yard's man bit what he was paid and laughed and said it was the only honest silver ever came through his gate."

He took the token out of his waistcoat and laid it on the desk, lamp up.

"My man drew him one of these on the back of a wool bill. The driver said that was it."

Ferra had put her pen down. "A brother in a stable, paying for his hay, on the night the knife was sent south."

"I can't put him at the table up the stair. Nobody saw him go up it. But I'll tell you what I think, and you may price it as you like." Saban looked at the token. "The purse doesn't take the Church's money. It doesn't need to. The money comes from whoever wants a thing done: a house that lost a war, a man with a grudge. The purse pays the men short, in clipped silver. And the Church sends a brother up the stair with a name. That's all. A name, and a quiet word that a certain duke would be no loss to the realm and the Light would think no worse of whoever saw to it." He turned his hat once. "It pays nothing. It writes nothing. It signs nothing. It rides the purse the way a man rides a horse he doesn't own, and pays for nothing but the hay."

"In Ostrel silver," said Emeric.

"Because a man on a holy road doesn't carry coin; it's against his rule. He carries his house's tokens. And it never once occurred to anybody that a stable man would bite one." Saban stood up. "I'm going down to Marrow. It's Tuesday."

Emeric looked at the token on the blotter.

"I'll come," he said.



He had sat in the old strongroom under the east tower often enough, two doors along, with the man from the wood; but never once at Marrow's door. Not since the ash at Wayford, and the room going cold, and the man on the floor curled round his ledger with blood coming out of his nose.

Saban did not ask why. At the bottom of the stair he stopped outside the second door and looked back, and Emeric stopped where he was, by the passage wall, out of the door's line.

"Here," he said.

Saban nodded, as if that were the right price, and went in with the beer and the book, and left the door a hand's width open.

Emeric stood with his back to the cold stone. He could hear a great deal more through a hand's width of door than an ordinary man would have. He heard the beer go down, and Saban say it was warm out and the herring were in. He heard Marrow not answer. Marrow's note through the wall was what it had been for years: quiet, careful, mild, holding very still, the note of a man in a small room who has been paid short all his life and stopped expecting otherwise.

Then a small sound. Silver, on wood.

Saban did not say anything about it. He poured the beer.

And Marrow's note moved. Not much: for the space of a breath it went toward the table, the way a man leans without getting up to read a thing put down in front of him upside down. The token was turned over. Lamp to house. Then a long stillness, which was Marrow reading the rim.

"He never lets it out of his hand," said Marrow.

That was all.

Emeric stood very still against the wall.

He was twenty-two in a receiving line in the great hall, and a man whose face had not aged a line in fifteen years stopped in front of him and raised his left hand to bless him. The right stayed closed at his side.

And he was on a river bank eleven days ago, with the reeds hissing and his father's ferry going away downriver behind him, and the grey bead at his wrist, cut small from the first of his mother's stones, answering. One string of two, out of that same closed hand, the way one bell in a tower answers another across a valley when it is struck. And under the note, something that was not a note; that he had no word for, and did not believe in, and that had been there anyway.

Inside, Saban waited out the hour, as he always did, and drank his beer, and talked about the herring; and Marrow said nothing else.

When Saban came out he shut the door and looked at Emeric by the wall in the lamp's light.

"You heard."

"Yes."

"He'll mean the cardinal. It's the cardinal's words he read, and he's never once said a man's name to me in all these years; he wouldn't start." He weighed the token in his palm. "He never lets what out of his hand?"

Emeric did not answer.

Saban had sat across a counter from a great many men who did not answer him, and could price every silence to a copper. Emeric watched him price this one, and decide not to say it.

"Well," said Saban, and put the token away. "When you've a price for it, I'm a buyer."

He went up the stair with the lamp, and Emeric followed him in the dark.



The capital had said nothing yet. That was Saban's word at the table on the fifteenth. Thirteen days, and no proclamation, no rider, no word from the council or the close; only the household cavalry coming back through the north gate on the fourth with two men across their saddles. "It's deciding what it saw," said Saban, "and what to call it. When it's decided, we'll hear it called." Ferra wrote that, and under it the two riders' names he had brought, and their mothers' names; and gave the page to Harrold at supper, folded once. He read it twice and put it inside Abloh's coat, which Maren had by then made fit.

He had been in Kaldo nearly a fortnight. He went up the west stair at eight and his light went off at nine. He did not stand in windows. He had ridden the ring line once round the whole circuit and walked back without a word. He had been to the ward twice more, and Rylan had started calling him the passenger, to Imani's horror, and been allowed to. He had drunk his tea one morning out of the gravity mug while Ferra told him what it had cost, and asked her if it was true that it bounced, and looked at it a long time and not dropped it, and Emeric had seen him want to.

He had not asked about the bank. Not once; not of Emeric, and not, Ward said, of Ward.

"He'll ask me first," Ward had said on the stair. "He never asks the man who has to answer. He asks the man beside him." He had looked at Emeric flatly. "When he does, I'll tell him to ask you."

He did not ask Ward.



He came up onto the wall walk on the night of the seventeenth, a little after ten, when his light had been out an hour; along it from the north stair with his hands behind his back, between the electric lamps on their posts, past the masons' scaffolding at the bastion, to where Emeric stood at the south-east corner over the lower ward with his elbows on the coping.

Emeric had felt him from the stair's foot. He did not turn round.

His father stopped a foot off and put his own hands on the coping and looked out with him: the lower ward's roofs, the dark of the river, and the long lit curve of the viaduct going away round the town with a train on it, small and white and full of light, with no sound at that distance. Below in the terraces a woman was calling a child in by name, over and over, crossly, and getting no answer.

"I have stood beside that man," said the king, "since before I was king."

He said it at arm's length, to the river.

"At my coronation. At every feast. At my wife's funeral. He blessed my sons." His hands did not move on the coping. "I did not know he could do what he did on that bank. I made it my business all my reign to know what every man in that palace could do and what he would do with it, and I did not know that." He was quiet. "You did."

Emeric said nothing.

"Ward stood on that bank with a rope in his hand and looked at the road, not at me. I've known Ward since he was a boy at my gate. I know what he looks at when he's afraid. And you came out into the river looking like a man who'd been told something." He turned his head and looked at his son along the coping, the gold eyes in the lamplight. "And neither of you has said one word to me since about what it was you knew."

Below them the woman had found the child. A door banged.

Emeric felt the old instruction coming, the one that had always come at the end of a thing like this in a small room with the door shut: say nothing; trust nobody; do not come back until you are sent for. He stood at the coping and waited for it, as he had waited for it all his life.

It did not come.

"I'm not going to ask you," said the king. "I've watched you not tell me for a fortnight. You do it well. You do it the way I'd have done it." Something happened at one corner of his mouth and was gone. "That's not a compliment."

"I know."

"Then you'll tell me when you've decided how." He took his hands off the coping. "I'd like you to decide soon. I've waited half my life for most things, and I find, since the river, that I'm out of the habit."

"Yes," said Emeric.

His father nodded, once, as he nodded at the end of an audience, and went back along the wall walk the way he had come, between the lamps, without hurry, with his hands behind his back.

Emeric stayed at the coping. After a while he turned his left wrist over on the stone and looked at the band in the lamplight: the fine steel wire and the beads strung on it, and at the clasp the grey one, faceted, no bigger than the end of his little finger, cut from the first of his mother's stones with the old shape still in it. It had not done anything since the bank. It sat on his wrist and was quiet.

He put his other hand over it, and kept it there, and watched the trains go round.
