Chapter 328 - One Round

The tracing Teale's team carried into the cloth market on the twelfth of December had been drawn in Kaldo in the last week of November, by Tess's boy, off the light sand table, in brown ink on oiled paper that would not run in rain.

Emeric had watched the boy lay his paper over a print of the ground layer and ink in the lanes, one team's road at a time, while Tess sat in the head chair and raised each street for him. The cloth market's was a short road. Down the dyers' lane from the south, two carts wide with a gutter down its middle, into the square at its south-west corner; along the west side under the timber fronts to the cloth hall on the north side, where Saban's walkers said a trebuchet stood in the yard, throwing over the roofs; and the east side of the square, all of it, one long block of grey, drawn solid, as one roof. The boy had shaded it with the side of his pen. There was no lane there to ink.

It had rained in the night. The capital lay under the K Amara with its roofs shining and the smoke going straight up out of ten thousand chimneys, as if nothing were happening down there but a December morning. Emeric's eye still went to the summit every time he came out onto the tower's walk, and found a raw pale scar where the close had been, and the castle beside it on the east shoulder, empty.

The trebuchet had been throwing since the sixth. Every time a shore team came down outside the towers' reach, onto a tenter field or the bare ground behind the south suburbs, a dead-zone stone came over the roofs a minute after and burst among them, and they did the rest of the day on their feet with plain steel and powder; and Ward had put his finger on the cloth market at the chart table on the night of the eleventh, and said that one.

Teale took thirty-one men of the ward companies and two knights. The knights were Mira and Bren. Arthur was with Osric's people on the other side of the hill. Teale was a thick quiet sergeant from the coal quay who had taught half the ward companies to load a self-loader with their eyes shut, and he went down at a quarter to eleven off hull two's ramp onto a dyer's drying yard south of the market, in the lee of a long brick wall, and was in the dyers' lane by eleven.

Emeric listened from the radio room under the tower, the cupboard with two clerks and the set and a porthole. Teale's set was in Mira's helm, the MK3's two short antennas on its crown sending her voice up four miles to the horn, faint and close at once. She gave the road in the cipher's numbers at first, and then, when the clerk's pencil got behind, in plain words, low, because nobody on the hill had a receiver and a great deal on the hill could kill them while a clerk looked up a group.

"Dyers' lane. Gutter's running. Nobody in it." A pause. "Shutters down both sides. Doors barred. Smell of alum." Another. "Corner. Square's in front. Stalls up, nobody at them. Hall on the north side, big doors open. I can see the arm over the hall roof. It's cocked."

From four miles up the lower town was a roar of notes packed so close that he could not have told a stallholder from a soldier from a cat. He listened to the horn.

"West side," said Mira. "Going along. Teale's on the hall door. Bren's with the back six." A pause. "The east side's an arcade."

The clerk's pencil stopped.

"Say again," said Emeric, into the mouthpiece.

"The east side." Her voice had not changed at all. "The whole east side of the square. It's not a roof. It's a row of arches with a roof on, and a passage behind them, and doors out of the passage into the street behind." A breath. "There are men in it."

And then the horn was full of noise.



Mira saw them before she heard them, which was the way it went with men who knew what they were doing: the arcade's shadow, that had been empty when she looked along it from the corner, was not empty, and the thing that was not empty about it was moving, the length of the square, toward the place behind Teale's back six where the dyers' lane came in.

They had come through the passage. She understood that afterward, sitting on a cot with her side on fire, and at the time she did not need to understand it; she saw where they were and where the lane was and that they would be in the lane's mouth before Bren's six were. Men in the Lieutenant's coats, a score, two score, coming out from under the arches at a run with their heads down. And among them, taller, not running, three men in long pale coats to the boot.

"Back six," she said, into the helm. "Behind you. The lane."

Bren turned. She heard him say the word he was not allowed to say in front of Wren.

The first burst stone came over before anybody had fired. She saw it go up out of the arcade's shadow, a dark thing the size of a man's fist turning over against the grey sky, and saw where it would come down, short, among the stalls between the arcade and the back six; and it came down short, and burst, and the heat of it came across the square like the door of a kiln opened in her face. A stall went up. Canvas, cloth, the poles. And something in a Lieutenant's coat that had been running in front of the stall and was now lying beside it on the wet cobbles, very still for one breath and then not still at all, with his left arm under him in a way she did not look at twice.

His own side had thrown it. She knew that, and filed it, and went on.

Then the rifles. The self-loaders in a square that size were a noise she would hear in her sleep for a year, the brass going off them in bright arcs onto the cobbles; and the Lieutenant's men went down behind the stalls and the water troughs and fired back, crossbows and the bad copied fire-tubes Saban had sent drawings of in the summer. Bren's six were in the lane's mouth, just. The men from the arcade were between them and the lane's far end, in the doorways, on the far side of the gutter.

Mira went up.

Not far. That was the rule in a street: never higher than the eaves, never longer than a count of three, because a wind crossbow bolt would go through her plate at fifty paces and the capital's guard had been shooting them since before she was born. She went up off the cobbles with the suit's stones under her, a count of two, to the height of a first-floor sill, and came down on the far side of a cart in the middle of the square with her spear in both hands and its head lit, the charged edge humming in her palms, between the arcade and Bren.

And the square went dead.

She felt it in her feet first. That was what everybody said and it was true: as if the ground had dropped half an inch under her boots and come back. Then the suit was nothing. The held stones at her hips and shoulders and knees went still, all at once, and the plate was only plate, black and heavy, a great deal heavier than she remembered it ever being; the spear's head went out in her hands like a lamp turned down; and across the square she saw a man in the Lieutenant's coat get up from behind a water trough, a plain man with a plain grey face and a plain iron box open in his hands, and drop the box, and sit down against the trough as if his legs had gone, which they probably had. A breaker. He had forced it in his two hands, right there among his own men, so that nobody in Kaldo's black would fly out of that square.

The radio still worked. It was the only thing in her helm that did. She heard Teale say in her ear, quite calmly, "Powder only. It's knives now," and heard the self-loaders go on firing, because they had never had a stone in them to lose.

The three in the pale coats came out from under the arches and walked into the square.

They had lost nothing. She could see that from the way they walked. A dead zone took a knight's casting and left him his body, and these were knights, the old kind, made on the floor; the pale coats were open over mail and the mail over silver, tracery to the jaw on the one in front, Full-traced, the whole body. He was old. Older than Ward. Grey, cropped close, a long face with the bones standing out of it, a sword in his right hand that had been old when he was young; and he looked at her across the wet cobbles, a small black figure with an unlit spear beside an overturned cart, and changed his line by a step, and came to her.

She knew she was stronger than him.

She had sparred with Ward, twice, and lost both times on something she could not see, and felt under the losing that hers was the better body: the bone in her packed and heavy and certain, that Imani had no word for, and her channels clean where theirs leaked. Ward was strong while he drew. She was strong standing still. And this man was older than Ward.

The first time their steel met, she proved it. His blade came down on her haft and she took it on the haft and pushed, from the ground up, the way Rylan had taught them in the gravity hall on oak at half again their weight, and the old man went back a full yard on the wet stones with his boots skidding. She saw his face. It did not change. It looked at her the way Bram looked at a casting that had come out of the sand a different shape from the pattern.

So she went at him.

She was stronger and he was old and there was no time to be clever; she came in low with the spear's point at his belt and the whole packed weight of her behind it, to drive him into the cart and pin him there, and it would have worked on anybody she had ever met.

He was not there.

He had not moved much. A step. Half a step, sideways and back, and his left hand came off his hilt and took her haft just behind the head, not to stop it, to help it, so that the drive she had put into it went on past him into nothing and took her with it. And while she was going past him, all of her weight on her front foot and nothing left to take it back with, he turned her. One hand on the haft. As if he had known for a long time exactly where she would be and had only been waiting for her to arrive.

She felt the sword go in under her right arm, where the MK3's plate was cut away for the arm's swing.

It did not hurt. Not then. It was cold, and then wet, and then she was on her knees on the cobbles with her spear gone somewhere and the old man standing over her with his sword back for the second one, at her neck, under the helm's rim, and she thought, very clearly, in Ward's voice: you read the spear. He read you.

Bren hit him.

He came from her left, from the lane's mouth, where he had no business leaving, across ten yards of open square in a dead zone with no stones in his suit and his blade out, and he did not use the blade. He hit the old knight with his shoulder, the whole of himself, low, the way Bren hit everything, the way he had hit the Hod's hatch coaming the night it jammed; and the old man went sideways off his feet into the overturned cart so hard that the cart moved.

Bren stood over her. He did not look down at her. He had his blade up in both hands and his feet set and he was breathing like a bellows.

"I'm all right," said Mira, from the cobbles, which was not true.

"Shut up," said Bren.

The old knight got up. He got up slowly, out of the cart's spilled bolts of cloth, and stood, and looked at Bren; and then at her on her knees with her hand clamped under her arm; and at Bren again, a long careful look, up and down, the way a man looks at a horse he has been told is sound. She saw him decide. She did not know what he decided. She never found out.

Because the square was going white.

It came in from the south, from the dyers' lane, low, over the cobbles, rolling: a bank of white so thick she could not see the lane's mouth through it, nor the troughs, nor the man who had broken the stone; it came in over the back six and over Bren's boots and over her, wet and warm and smelling of the dyers' gutter, and the old knight's long grey face went into it and was gone.

"Teale." It was Emeric's voice in her helm. He sounded tired already, and it was not noon. "Come south. Into the white. The lane's yours for a count of a hundred. After that it isn't anybody's."



He had come down off the K Amara's after apron in his suit with Ward's voice in his ear saying a thing Ward only said once, and he had not answered it.

He came down onto the dyers' lane's roofs a hundred yards below the square, onto the ridge of a dyer's house with a cistern on its leads, and read the square; and found the edge of the dead ground forty yards short of him, a clean line across the lane like the edge of a shadow, and inside it nothing. A hole in the city's roar the shape of the cloth market, with Teale's thirty-three somewhere in it, and he could feel none of them at all.

So he could not cast into it. Nobody could. That was what a dead zone was.

But a dead zone stopped a spell. It did not stop what the spell had made.

The gutter down the middle of the dyers' lane was running from the night's rain and the dyers' vats, a hand deep and grey-blue and warm from the vats already, steaming a little in the cold, all the way down past him to the river. He put his heat into it. Not his, the fire's: a long strip of it along the gutter for forty yards, up to the dead line and no further, as he had put it along the airship yard's channel in the night over the black keel; not enough to boil it, enough to make it smoke. Then water, lifted off the gutter's face in a fine breath and thrown up into the heat. And then air, and he was careful with the air. He leaned. He did not push. The morning was cold and the square was the lowest place on that side of the hill, a bowl among the roofs, and cold wet air wanted to go down into it the way water wants to go into a hollow; and he gave the white to the cold, and let it go where it would rather be.

The glyphs came up off his hands in the lane for anybody on a roof to see, the fire's and the water's and the air's, three rings of light turning over the gutter one after another. He let them. Everybody on the hill was meant to see a mage.

The veil went up the lane like a tide coming in over a flat, and crossed the dead line, and went on. The air he had leaned it with stopped at the line; he felt it stop, the way a rope goes slack when somebody lets go of the other end. The white did not stop. It was only water now, warm water in cold air, and it rolled on over the cobbles into the market square because the square was lower, and lay there, and filled it.

"Teale," he said, into the set under his helm. "Come south."

They came out of the white at the dead line in ones and twos, running bent, the way Ward had drilled them to run out of smoke: the back six first, then the rest, Teale last but two with his self-loader across his chest and a man over his shoulder. Two men carrying a third between them who was in the Lieutenant's coat. Then Bren, with Mira's arm over his neck and his other arm round her, half carrying her, her feet going and her head down; and behind Bren, last of all, the team's medic, a big Wayford woman with her sleeves soaked red to the elbow, walking backward with her pistol out, looking into the white.

Emeric counted them past. He counted thirty-four, and then counted again, and it was thirty-four, because one of them was not Kaldo's.

The white behind them moved.

They were coming after. He could not feel them inside the dead line, but he could hear them, boots on wet stone, a lot of boots, coming down the lane in the fog toward the place where his team was strung out along the gutter for fifty yards with its wounded; and then the first of them came over the dead line into his sense, and the next, a score of notes, the sour grey of men run hard, and three that were not grey at all, that were tall and dense and silver-cold, coming at a walk.

The lane was wet from wall to wall.

He had learned this one on Maren's ice cream cone and a plate of ice that had tipped him into the Mere, and Armand had given it its old name: the heat must have somewhere to go that is not you. He pulled. Fire set the other way, the heat coming up out of the wet cobbles and the gutter's water and the film of rain on every stone, out of the lane between the dead line and the last of his own people, forty yards of it, all at once; and he did not take it into his hands. He threw it up into the veil over the square, which was the one place on that hill that wanted it.

The lane went white a second way. Not fog. Frost, then glass. He heard it crack as it took, a long splintering sound like a pond in January, from wall to wall, and the gutter stopped running in the middle of a ripple.

The first of the Lieutenant's men came out of the fog onto it at a run and went down as if somebody had cut his legs. The two behind him went down over him. The rest stopped at the edge, which was what he wanted; and the three in the pale coats, behind them, stopped too, and stood looking at the ice in the grey light, and at the man on the dyer's roof below it with the last of the glyph going out over his hands.

The oldest of the three looked up at him for a long time. Then he turned round and went back into the white, and the other two went with him.

Emeric sat down on the dyer's leads beside the cistern.

It had cost what the black keel's night had cost him, in four minutes instead of four hours. His arms ached to the shoulder, and behind his eyes, and under that was the thin cold hollow in the middle of him that he did not feel often any more.

It was a quarter past noon.



Hull two came down for them at one, over the roofs, to the dyer's drying yard where she had put them off.

Her master brought her in low from the south with her shell up and her keel a man's height over the chimneys, and hung her over the yard with her ramp down, and Teale's people went up it with the wounded first. Emeric watched from the dyer's roof: the man in the Lieutenant's coat on a board, with the medic beside him holding his good hand; Bren carrying Mira up in his arms, because she had stopped being able to walk at the ramp's foot.

The tower above Tanner's Row was four hundred yards off, on the second wall, uphill. He had felt it all morning: its chest on the floor under Venn's two upright bombards, cold and held, its breaker asleep on his tick, a crew of eight, two grey mages on the leads. The drying yard was out of its reach by a hundred yards on the table and on Ward's chart and in Fletcher's figures. A bombard pushed only inside its stone's field; after the trough the ball was a thrown shot, and fell off with distance.

He felt the stone in the nearer bombard wake.

And then he felt it go on waking. Past where a stone stopped. Past where a stone ought to be able to go. The crew were pushing it, all eight of them, every bracer on that roof pouring into the one stone at the breech, and the gravity mage at the breech, whoever he was, forcing it, as a man forces a door that has been locked against him; and Emeric knew what it was, because he had stood beside his father on the proving field at fifteen and been told it by an officer in a low voice. Forbidden. Except at the last.

"Hull two," he said. "Climb. Now. The Tanner's Row tower's..."

The ball came.

He did not see it. Nobody saw it. He heard it, after: a sound in the air over the roofs like a sheet torn the length of a room. And hull two's shell took it, the way her shell took everything that came at her fast, and stretched it, and slowed it, and her shell stone drank and drank to stop it and could not drink enough; he felt the stone empty, all of it, in one long draw, like a man emptying a cup in one swallow, and the ball came on through what was left at a walking pace with the whole weight of a bombard's stone behind it, and went into her hold below the ramp, through her side, and out of the other side, and fell into a cooper's yard two streets off and lay there on its own dent with steam coming off it.

Hull two lurched. Her stern went down. Somebody on her deck screamed, a long way off and very high. Then her lift caught, and held; her lift stones were her own and the shell's stone was another thing, and the lift was all there; and her master put her nose up and climbed out over the roofs, slowly, with a hole in her that a man could have crawled through on both sides, a hold full of wounded, and no shell at all.

And the tower above Tanner's Row went out.

The stone at the breech had thrown its ball with everything it held, and then it burst, as the officer had said it would on the proving field; and the dead zone came off the tower's roof in a small clean ring. The second bombard's stone stopped. The chest under them stopped, with every sleeping stone in it that nobody could break now. The grey mages' lightning in their belts. And the crew: eight men who had poured everything they had through their bracers into the one stone, and had nothing left in their own bodies to stand up on, and were lying on the leads round the bombard in the cold, as he could not feel and could imagine very well.

"It's dead," said Ward, on the set, from somewhere above the second wall. "That tower. All of it. Its own crew's on the floor." A pause. "Going in. Osric."

"Going in," said Osric.

They went in on foot, twelve knights with their suits gone heavy and their blades unlit, up the tower's outside stair to the roof. The crew could not lift their arms. The grey mages had nothing in their hands and knew it. The breaker had slept through the whole of it and had to be woken twice. And Ward stood on the roof of the tower above Tanner's Row with Venn's two dead bombards beside him, pointing at the sky, and the chest at his feet with its lid open on a row of grey stones that would never be broken now by anybody, and said into the set, "That's the one," and nothing else.

That left two.



He went into it at dusk, because he wanted to see the chest, and because he was the only man in the fleet who could tell from the outside what was inside a stone wall.

The dead ground would not close for most of a day; mana came back into it from the edges like water into a footprint on a beach, and slower on the ground in the cold. The tower stood in its hole in the dusk, round and squat, its stair going up inside the wall's thickness; and from the street, outside the dead line, with his sense laid flat against the stone, Emeric read it.

Ward's people on the roof. Ward's people at the door. And halfway up, in the thickness of the wall, off the stair, a small dark room he had not been told about, that the stair passed on a landing; and in the room, sitting still on the floor in the dark with his back to the wall, a man. Dense. Silver-cold. Not asleep.

Ward's people had gone up the outside stair, onto the roof, from the street. Nobody had come up the inside one.

"There's one in the wall," he said to Ward, at the door. "Halfway. On the landing. A temple knight."

Ward looked at him in the dusk. He looked at the way Emeric was standing, and at his face, and at his hands, which were not quite steady and which Emeric had put behind his back as soon as he saw Ward look.

"Stay here," said Ward.

"I know where he is. You don't."

Ward said nothing. He said nothing for long enough that Emeric understood that he was deciding not to say it twice; and then he went in at the tower's door with his blade drawn, and Emeric went in behind him.

His sense went out at the door like a lamp carried into fog, until there was nothing but his own heart and breath and the ache in his arms. He had counted the steps to the landing while he read the wall, and he counted them now, in the dark, behind Ward's back, up a stair worn into hollows by four hundred years of the city's watch.

Twenty-six. Twenty-seven. The landing. Ward was past it, on twenty-nine, his back to the little room's door, because the door was a black slot in a black wall and nobody without a sense could have seen it was a door at all.

The temple knight came out of it at Ward's back.

He came fast and quietly, big in the slot, an old sword in his hand; Emeric could not feel him and did not need to, because the wall had told him where the man would be an hour ago. Emeric was a step below the landing, and the man's head was level with his shoulder, in a steel cap with cheek plates, half turned toward Ward.

He hit it with his right hand.

The weight from the ground up, and in the blink before the knuckles met the steel, the rings: down into the bone and the flesh over it, the outermost to give first and the next a hair after, the joints left free. Three hundred a morning on the cart shed's post with Ward counting. He was thinking of nothing else, and there was nothing in him to do it with.

The rings came late. The first of them had barely packed when the steel was already there, and the rest never came at all.

The temple knight's head went sideways into the stair's wall with a sound like a bell dropped on a flagstone, and he went down on the landing; and Emeric felt his own hand go the way it had gone on the golem's shoulder, the same wrongness across the back of the fist, the same two knuckles that had been there and were not quite. No pain yet. Only knowing.

Ward was down the two steps and had his knee on the man's back and the man's sword arm turned up behind him before the man had finished falling. He bound the wrists with a strap off his own belt, by feel. Then he looked up at Emeric in the dark, which he could not see, and Emeric could not see him.

"Hand," said Ward.

"Yes."

"Same one."

"Same two."

Ward got up, and took the temple knight up off the landing by his collar and belt as if he were a sack, and went on up the stair with him to the roof, to put him with the others. He did not say anything else, then or later. Emeric stood on the landing in the dark with his right hand held against his chest in his left, and felt the pain arrive, slowly, punctually, like a man who has been sent for and is not in a hurry.

From the roof, when he got there, he could see both of them: the last two. One on the crown wall under the empty palace, on the hill's east shoulder above the gardens; one on the gatehouse at the town end of the long bridge, over the river, where nobody from Kaldo went. Their chests were cold and held in his sense, beyond the dead line, a long way off across the lit roofs; and he stood at the parapet in the last of the light between Venn's two dead bombards and pointed them out to Osric, so that Osric could put them on his slate as they stood at dusk, with his left hand.



Imani strapped it at nine, on the K Endeavor, in the end bay of the hospital hold, on a stool, between a Kaldo regular with a crossbow bolt through his calf and a lay brother from the cathedral's crypts who had a cough and would not take his broth from anybody but her.

She did it the way she had done it the year before. Felt each knuckle with her thumb, watching his face and not the hand. The splint, the strap over it, the bandage over that. She did not say anything while she did it. When it was done she took her book out of her apron, and turned back through it to a page near the front, and wrote under a line that was already there, so that he could read both. The golem, two knuckles, right hand, last November. And under it, in the same hand: 12 December. A helm. The same two.

"Twice the body's rate," she said. "If you aim at it. Which you will. It'll still be strapped when this is over." She shut the book. "You'll point with the other one."

"I've been pointing with the other one since four."

"Good." She looked at him. She looked at him the way she looked at a man on a ward who had told her he was fine, reading him, the room in him; and he knew what she found, because he could feel it himself, the floor under everything and not much over it. "Why were you on a stair?"

"I knew where he was."

"Ward knows where everybody is."

"Not in a wall."

Imani considered that, and put it away somewhere, and did not argue with it, which was worse than arguing. "Eat something," she said. "Then go and see her. She's been asking whether you're cross."

Mira was in the next bay, on her side on a cot with her right arm up over her head on a pillow, because that was the only way the side could lie. Bettany had come across from the K Amara at three to do the stitches and done nineteen, in a long curve along the ribs under the arm where the plate had been cut away; and Imani had read it after, through the hollow view, and said that the blade had gone in to the bone and the bone had not cared, and that everything round the bone had cared very much. Bren was asleep on the deck plates beside the cot with his back against its leg and his head on his knees, in his suit's undershirt, with his boots still on.

Mira watched Emeric come in. She did not move anything but her eyes.

"He read me," she said.

"Yes."

"I was stronger than him. I knew it when we touched. I pushed him a yard." Her voice was very flat and very careful, the voice she used with a book open in front of her. "He let me be stronger. And I went where he wanted, because I was stronger, and he knew I would."

Emeric sat down on the end of the next cot, which was empty, with his strapped hand in his lap.

"Ward told us," said Mira. "In the yard. Every time. You'll lose to us on what you can't see." She looked at the deck plates, at Bren's boots. "I thought he meant it'd be close."

He did not tell her anything. There was nothing to tell her that Ward had not told her already, and she knew it, and was lying on a cot with nineteen stitches in her because she had not believed it until today; and anything he said now would be a speech. He sat with her. After a while Bren said something in his sleep, a word that sounded like "coaming", and shifted against the cot's leg, and Mira put her left hand down without looking and laid it on the top of his head and left it there.

The man in the Lieutenant's coat was at the far end of the hold, in the bay by the hatch where Imani put the ones she wanted to see every time she went past.

He was young. Twenty, perhaps, with a city face, pale and narrow, and his hair stuck to his forehead with sweat; and his left arm stopped a hand above where his elbow had been, in a white bandage, and he kept looking at the place where it stopped as if it might have changed while he was not looking. The burst stone had taken the forearm and the hand off him on the cobbles, mostly; the team's medic had put a tourniquet on him in the square, under fire, in a dead zone, while Teale shouted at her to leave him, and she had said no, once, the way Ward said things, and not looked up. Bettany had taken the rest of it off above the elbow at four o'clock, clean, because there had been nothing below the elbow to save.

One of Ferra's clerks was sitting by his cot with the prisoners' book open on his knee, the way Ferra's clerks had been sitting by every Church and capital man in this hold since the morning of the second, writing them in.

"Name," said the clerk.

"Danner." The young man looked at the clerk and then at the bandage. "Private. The Lieutenant's foot. The fourth company."

"Where from?"

"Here." He meant the capital. "The lower town. Behind the tanneries."

The clerk wrote it, and then again at the back of the book on a page ruled in Ferra's columns, with the date and the surgeon's name and what had been done; and in the last column, without looking up, something short.

Danner was watching him do it.

"What's that?" he said.

"The bill."

The young man's face went still: the face of a man from behind the tanneries who has never once been able to pay a surgeon and has always known what happens to men who can't.

"I've nothing," he said. "I've three weeks' pay owing. I've nothing at all."

"You're not paying it," said the clerk. "You're a subject of the crown. You were hurt in the crown's city. It goes to the crown." He turned the book round so that Danner could see the last column, if he could read, and tapped the word. "There."

Danner looked at it for a long time.

"The Lieutenant'll never pay that," he said at last.

"It's not made out to the Lieutenant," said the clerk, and turned the book back, and closed it, and went on to the next cot.

Imani had come up behind Emeric while the clerk talked, with a bowl of Hester's broth in her hands that she gave him without asking whether he wanted it. She stood beside him and watched Danner look at the place where his arm stopped.

"He'll send it on," she said, very low. "Ferra's man. To the K Discovery. Tonight." Her mouth moved. "Your father'll get a bill for a capital boy's arm, with Bettany's fee and my broth on it, from a hold his son paid for."

"He'll pay it."

"I know he'll pay it. That's why it's funny." She did not laugh. She went on standing there after the broth was gone, longer than she needed to, with her eyes on the bandage and her hand an inch off it, not touching, reading, the slow hollow way she read everything; and then she took her book out again and wrote something under Danner's name that was not a dose and not a date, and that Emeric did not try to read.



He called Tess at eleven, from the radio room, on the K Amara, with his hand on his knee.

She was in the line room in Kaldo with Ambrose; it was her watch. The clerk read her the arcade in four sentences of cipher; and then Emeric told her what he wanted in plain words, himself.

"Every roof the table can't see under," he said. "Every one. Hatch it. Stripes, or whatever you like that nobody will ever mistake for a roof."

The horn hissed.

"Every one in the capital?" said Tess.

"Every one on the table."

"That's Kaldo too."

"Yes."

He could hear her thinking about it in the order it would have to be done in.

"It'll be every covered lane in Kaldo," she said. "Every porch. Every shed." A pause. "Half the works."

"Yes."

"All right," said Tess. And then, in a slightly different voice, the one she used when she had found the fault in a thing and was angry that she had not found it sooner: "I drew that lane."

"The boy drew it."

"Off my table," said Tess, and put the set down.

Her count came up at six in the morning, in cipher, on a slip the clerk brought to him on the tower's walk with his tea. In the capital, eleven hundred and four roofs the table could not see under, hatched; on the Isle, nineteen; in Kaldo she had not finished, and would by noon. There was nothing else on the slip. He folded it and put it in his coat, in the place where the leaflet was, and drank his tea left-handed.



The thirteenth was a long grey day and nothing fell.

The two towers stood. Two shore teams went down on the tenter fields in the morning and no dead-zone stone came over the roofs at them, because the cloth hall's trebuchet had gone in the night on rollers down some lane the table had a hatch on now; and the teams walked up as far as the second wall and sat in the lanes in the drizzle with the Lieutenant's men at the far end of the same lanes, and neither side went forward. Hull two lay alongside the K Endeavor with a canvas patch lashed over each hole, and no shell. Saban's people said the hill house above the falls had smoke going up from four chimneys and its gate shut, and nobody had gone in or out all day but a baker's boy at seven with a basket.

Augustine had not come out. He had not come out on the second, when his cathedral came down. He had not come out since.

At a quarter to four in the afternoon the clerk in the radio room put his head out of the cupboard's door onto the tower's walk, where Emeric was standing at the rail with his strapped hand inside his coat, and said, "The Isle, Your Grace. Captain Rylan. He's asking for you. He's off the cipher."



Rylan's voice came out of the horn flat and small and very close, like a man talking in the dark beside a bed.

Emeric had heard it every night for twelve nights. The landing, the boats with no lamps, the beach that was already somebody else's. And then the mage.

He had come down off Kesrath's tower within the week and had not gone back. He did not walk. He hung in the air over whatever he wanted to hurt, sixty feet up, a hundred, inside a skin, the way a gunship hangs, and threw down at it whatever he liked. Kaldo's rounds went into his skin and slowed and dropped out of it into the sand like flies off a window, and every one cost him something, and he could stand the cost for an hour at a time; then he went back toward the tower to eat, and came back. None of the escorts could touch him from the Band, because he never once hung over anything but Kaldo's own men.

"He's over the wells," said Rylan. "Since noon. Sixty feet. Right over the drum." A breath. "We're in the gully. The rock's holding. The levy's in the pipe trench. He can't get at the trench without coming down to forty, and he won't come to forty."

"The divers?"

"Under the jetty. All of them but two. Since ten this morning." Rylan's voice did not change. It never changed; that was what it was for. "They went in at the village to cut Kesrath's two boats off the jetty's piles so they'd drift. He came over the water while they were under. Sennet came up at the jetty's end. His canister. He came up on the outside of the piles." A pause the length of a breath. "Fenn went out for him. Off the bottom of the ladder. Fenn had him by the harness." Another. "The mage was right over them. Lightning, into the water. Then fire."

Emeric stood in the cupboard with the clerk's pencil not moving beside him and the porthole full of grey.

Sergeant Ade Fenn, who had come out of Wicket's shallows in September unable to float and taught sixty others to swim a mile in the dark, a long bony man with a red neck and a voice like a gull's, touching each swimmer on the head at the gangway's foot. And Robin Sennet, the youngest of the divers, carrying his bag up the same gangway in both arms like a sleeping child.

"The rest are under the jetty," said Rylan. "Eleven. On their canisters. Kesrath's levy is on the jetty over them and on the shore both ways. Every time one of them puts his head up at the piles, the mage feels it, or sees it, and he comes off the wells and goes and has a look. So they don't put their heads up." A breath. "The canisters are good for three hours from ten, Tess said. It's a quarter to four."

And if the detachment broke at the wells, Kesrath had the wells and the drum and the Claim; and the boats from Kesrath's roads, forty sail, were sitting off the Claim's west end now, waiting for that.

"Your Grace," said Rylan.

Emeric waited.

"Declan's on the bluff over the gully. Three hundred and ten yards. He's had him in Ivo's glass since noon." Rylan's voice was exactly as flat as before. "He's got it in the magazine. The last one. The one."

The dead-zone round. A chip of stone in a bullet's nose, forced past what any stone could hold the instant it struck, so that it burst and blew the air round it dead. The rarest round; only Emeric could cut the chip, and each one was issued by the round, in a numbered tin, under Ward's key. Every one of them was a dead zone that somebody in Kaldo would have chosen to make.

"Target is Kesrath's high mage," said Rylan, "over the dune drum." Every target named aloud before it falls. Ward's rule. "It'll take him. It'll take his skin and it'll take his flight. He's over the drum, so it'll take the drum's stones, and the jack, and every suit in the gully, and every glyph, and the levy's crystals. Ours. All of ours in the zone." A pause. "We go to powder. We go to our hands. So do they. And I've got thirty knights in that gully and they've got none."

The horn hissed.

"I'm asking leave," said Rylan.

Emeric had his strapped hand inside his coat, against his chest, and he could feel the two knuckles in it, each one, with every beat of his heart. He thought of the drying yard, and the hole in hull two that a man could crawl through, and the eight men on the leads of the tower above Tanner's Row with nothing left in their bodies. He thought of the old man in the pale coat looking at the ice. He thought, privately, that Earth had built a weapon that went off in the air and killed every machine in a ring, friend's and enemy's alike, and had meant it for the very end of everything; and that he had cut these by hand at a bench in the Dust House with the window open.

"Yes," he said. "You have leave."

"Thank you," said Rylan. And then, away from the set, not to him: "Declan."

Another voice, further off, a man lying down with his cheek on walnut. Emeric had heard it say three words in a row perhaps twice in thirteen years.

"On," said Declan.

The horn carried it.

There was the crack of the rifle, small, almost polite, the air crystal's flat smack, that would have been the last sound that crystal ever made. A breath. And then a noise that he felt rather than heard, through the horn's brass into the cupboard's bulkhead and the soles of his boots, a deep soft thud like a door slammed in a very large house three rooms away; and then shouting, a great many men, all at once, in two languages.

"He's down," said Rylan.

His voice had changed. Not much. A man running.

"He's down. He came down on the drum. On the sand. He's moving." Breath, and boots on rock, and the set knocking against something. "The suits are dead. All of them. Three of ours were up. Two came down in the gully on sand. One came down on the pipe." A pause, and the sound of the pause was a sound Emeric would remember. "His leg's wrong. He's talking. The jack's stopped. I can hear it's stopped."

And then the powder.

It came out of the horn so loud that the clerk jumped and dropped his pencil: the levy in the pipe trench, going over to powder all along their line at once, as Ward's book said, the dual-modes switched and the self-loaders already firing, a long ragged roar of it and a bank of white Emeric could not see but could hear, the way he had heard it roll over the stream ground in Aldane's war. Twelve days of silent rifles and boats without lamps; and Kaldo on the Isle had just made the loudest noise anybody on that island had ever heard.

"Their glyphs are out," said Rylan, under it. "Every mage on the dune. They're standing there looking at their hands." A breath. "Their levy's got nothing. Crossbows. It's knives and our powder." Something that was nearly not flat. "We're going down the gully."

The clerk had picked up his pencil and was not writing anything with it.

It was twenty minutes. Emeric stood in the cupboard for all of it with the horn a foot from his face. He heard the gully taken back a word at a time; the knights going down it on foot in their dead suits with only their own bodies under the plate, which was all a knight had ever had and was enough, against men who had never once fought without a glyph. He heard Kesrath's men go back off the drum, then off the dune, then off the beach toward the village. He heard Rylan send twelve down the shore to the jetty. And a long while after, a different voice in the background, high and breathless and outraged, said something about a scarf; and Rylan said, flat again, entirely flat, "Divers are out. All eleven. Abery went in off the jetty for the last one and lost his scarf. Lorne fished him out by the collar."

"And the mage," said Emeric.

"Alive. Both legs and his back, Declan thinks. He's on the drum's lid on a hurdle with four men on him and his hands tied, in case." A breath. "He keeps asking what it was. In their tongue. Over and over. He doesn't know what it was."

"Don't tell him."

"No," said Rylan. Then: "The zone's the gully and the drum and a bit of the dune. The jetty's clear of it. The boats are standing off. They saw." The hiss. "It'll be morning before the jack goes again. Before the suits do. We're sitting in the dark till then, on our hands." A pause. "Fenn and Sennet are on the jetty. The divers brought them up."

The porthole had gone darker. Four hundred miles north it would be dusk on the Claim, and the detachment sitting in a dead gully in the cold without one lamp that worked, with a broken mage on a hurdle and two of their own on a jetty under a blanket, waiting for the air to come back.

He had cut it at a bench, and said yes into a mouthpiece, and it had taken a man out of the air and everything of Kaldo's with him, and been worth it. He would have said yes again in the same breath. That was the thing about it.

"Rylan."

"Here."

"There won't be a second."

The horn hissed for a while across four hundred miles of sky.

"No," said Rylan.

Emeric took his left hand off the mouthpiece. He went out onto the tower's walk and stood at the rail in the cold with the capital below him lighting its lamps, ward by ward, as it had lit them every December evening of his boyhood; and two towers on the hill with their chests cold and held, and a hole on the summit where the cathedral had been, and a hill house above the falls with its gate shut. Then he went down to Fennick's store under the after-castle, where the tins were, and told the man on Ward's key to open it; and counted the tins that were left, with his left hand, one at a time, and had the number written down.
