Chapter 302 - The Hour

The K Discovery came down on South Sentinel a little before five in the morning, out of a sky going from black to grey over the east bay, with nobody aboard who had slept.

She had not gone home. Fletcher had taken her out over the coast with the can still red behind her and kept going, south and east over the empty water, because the rule Emeric had made over the fields an hour before was already a rule: no burn near anybody's eyes, and no ship of Kaldo's coming in over the town at first light smelling of hot iron with half a capital's watchmen still awake behind her. So she would lie up the day on the island under the yard's sheds and go home after dark, quietly, on her stones, the way she always went home.

The yard came up under her out of the half dark: the long sheds on the bay's south shore, the slipway lamps, the moorings, the K Endeavor lying at the deep berth with her hatches shut. Fletcher put her down on the yard's hard beside the shed where she had been built a second time, and shut her down stone by stone, and sat at the helm with both hands flat on his knees.

"Well," he said, to the dial.

Nobody answered him. Arthur and Mira and Bren were asleep in their stealth suits on the cabin floor with their hoods down, in a row, like three cats in the sun; Bren had gone first, before they cleared the coast, with his head on Mira's boots. Saban was in the corner seat with his hat over his face. Ward sat upright on the bench by the hatch where he had sat the whole way, with his helm on his knee, looking at nothing.

Emeric let his sense out over the yard as the hatch came open, from habit, the way he did coming home anywhere. The yard's watch at the gate. The night crew in the plating shed, tired. A cook already at the long hut's stove. And along the works shore to the east, half a mile off by itself behind its earth bank, in the Dust House where no lamp should have been lit at five in the morning, a note he knew better than almost any in the world, low and banked and patient, sitting very still beside two small hot points he did not know at all.

He stood in the hatch with one boot on the ladder.

"Bram's here," he said.

Ward looked up. "He's in Kaldo."

"He's in the Dust House." Emeric went down the ladder. "And so is Ferra."



The Dust House stood by itself at the end of the works shore with a bank of earth piled round three sides of it to the eaves, so that whatever happened inside would go up and not out, and a door of riveted plate in the fourth side facing the sea. Bram had built it in the winter to Tess's drawing. The mill was in the back, under canvas, shut since Imani had shut it; and along the front wall, under the one window, stood the press.

It did not look like much. A frame of four steel columns as thick as a man's leg, a great screw down the middle of it with a wheel on top that two men could just turn, and between the platens a steel block the size of a bread oven with a gauge on its side and a pipe coming into it from a lagged flask on the floor. A good deal of it had been a hay press in Milford in the spring.

Bram was sitting on a crate beside it with his elbows on his knees and his hands hanging between them. He had been there all night; it was in his shoulders. On the bench beside him there was a clean cloth, one of Maren's, folded square, and on the cloth two stones.

Ferra was at the bench's other end with her book open and a pencil through her hair and her coat buttoned to the throat, because the Dust House was cold at five in the morning in May and she had not, evidently, thought about it. She looked up when the door went. She looked at him in the black suit with the hood down and the mesh at his collar, and at his face, and he watched her read the night off it in about the time it took to read a line; and then she looked at her father, and did not say anything, and went back to her page.

That was how he knew it was Bram's.

"Grier," said Bram, without getting up.

"He agreed. He was ashamed of himself before we were through the door." Emeric came to the bench. "He gave us a name. We burned on the way out. A sky-ship's mage put a light on us over the fields. Fletcher lifted the guard before I'd opened my mouth."

"Did he." Bram looked pleased about that for a breath, and then put it away. "Then you'll want to sit down."

Emeric did not sit down. He looked at the stones.

They were both fire stones, the red-brown kind, each about the size of the top joint of his thumb, and they did not look like anything that had ever come out of a hill. Wild stones came out of the ground with a skin on them, the dull rind of the rock they had grown in, and a mason's hammer or a mage's patience took it off. These had no skin. They were clean all over, smooth as a river pebble and as even in colour as a drop of wine held to a lamp, and one of them was a shade darker than the other.

He put his hand out over them, not touching, and let his sense down.

The pale one was a fire stone. Ordinary, quiet, full; a small warm note, what any fire stone that size would have given him. The dark one was a fire stone too, and it was more. Not hotter; fuller, the way a jug is fuller when it is filled to the lip instead of the shoulder. It sat on Maren's cloth humming at exactly the note of the best stone he had ever held out of the Isle's fields, with nothing in it anywhere that was not fire stone: no flaw, no seam, no cloud, no grain of the host rock it had come out of.

He took his hand back.

"Tess's cube," he said.

"Tess's cube." Bram reached into his coat and brought out the black book with the Dust House's mark on its cover, the one she had put into his hands on the morning she swallowed, and opened it at the page with the corner turned down. Her small fast writing; a flask knocked over on the threshold bench in January; a rack of fire-dust cubes set in quartz grit soaked through with liquid mana; a week later one of them faintly warm with no keystone in it. And under that, in her hand, the line she had written on the morning of the stone, in the dispensary, sitting in her chair in her shift with Imani waiting: If it matters while I'm out, run it. And under that, much larger, in Bram's hand: RUN.

"I read it in April," said Bram. "The week she went in. I'd nothing to do with my hands but the mill, and Imani had the mill shut." He shut the book. "So I read it every night, and I thought about it, and one night I thought: that wasn't the dust. Dust doesn't come back. You said so, she said so, it's in her book in three places. The cube didn't come back to life. Something grew in it." He nodded at the press. "Grit, and dust, and the mana soaked through and nowhere to go. So I thought, well. Give it nowhere to go, harder."

"You built a press."

"I bought a press. Off Paxton's brother in Milford, for hay. Then I built it." He rubbed his face. "Quartzite for the body, ground fine. That's the host. Tess's grit was quartz and I didn't see why I'd change what worked. A pinch of fire dust off her sorted tins in the middle, for the seed, so it knows what to be. And the mana in the flask from the plant, forced in under the screw till the gauge says it won't take any more." He looked at the press. "Then you leave it. You don't touch it. It's like setting a bell. You leave it in the pit and you go and do something else and you don't open the pit to look."

"How long?"

"Four weeks, that one." He pointed with his chin at the pale stone. "I put it in on the twentieth of April. I gave it what I thought a stone wanted. I had the figure off the cartridges, what a fire stone that size takes to fill." His jaw moved. "I opened it on the eighteenth. It was a stone. It was a fire stone, out of a hay press, in a shed, made. I sat on this crate for an hour." He was quiet. "Then I filled it, and it was full at a third."

"A third of a mined one its weight," said Ferra, without looking up. "I weighed it. I filled it off a cartridge with the gauge on, three times, and drained it three times. It holds a third, every time, to a hair."

"So I'd made a bad stone," said Bram. "A good-looking bad stone." He shrugged, a mountain moving. "So I did it again, the same night, only I didn't give it what a stone wants. I gave it what the flask would give. I kept the screw on it and kept feeding it till the gauge stood on the pin and the pipe started to sing, and then I gave it the same again over the week, every time it took a swallow." He looked at the dark stone on the cloth. "I was going to open it Saturday. Then Ferra came out on the Endeavor yesterday with the quarter's book, and the Hod three were gone, and you were gone, and Ward, and Saban, and nobody would tell her where except up, and she sat in this shed with me till midnight not asking." He paused. "So I opened it. To give her something to do."

"I had plenty to do," said Ferra.

"You'd added the same column four times. I watched you."

Ferra wrote something.

"It came out at half past one," said Bram. "Like that. That colour. I filled it, and she drained it, and I filled it again." He stopped. He looked at Emeric, and then down at his hands, and then up again; and his face did a thing Emeric had seen it do perhaps three times in thirteen years, the last of them on a frozen lake under a dead sky with a little steam engine knocking on the ice in front of him.

"It's a mined stone," said Bram. "Its weight. To the hair. Ferra's got it written. It holds what a stone out of the Isle holds, and it's cleaner than anything out of the Isle, and I made it." He sat back on the crate. "That's mine. I want that written down. You weren't here. Tess wasn't here. I read her book and I built the press and I worked out the second dose on my own, sitting on this crate, and it's a stone." He folded his arms. "I solved it."

"You solved it," said Emeric.

"I know I did. I'm telling you so you'll know."

"It's written down," said Ferra. She turned her book round on the bench so that he could see the line: 28 May, half past one in the morning. Second press run, B. Equal. And under it, underlined twice, the same in her hand as on the morning she had ruled a line under an old man in her stone book: B. did it.

Emeric looked at it for a while.

A grown stone. He had a word for it ready, and it was not the crews' word, and he did not say it: cultured. Pearls on a string at a market stall in another life, a grain of grit put into an oyster on purpose so that it would do slowly what the sea did once in ten thousand years. A crystal grown on its seed in a steel bottle under pressure for weeks, in a factory he had walked past and never once gone into. He had known it could be done the way he knew most things, from the outside, and he had not done it. A blacksmith with a hay press and a sick girl's notebook had done it in a month, because he had nothing to do with his hands.

"Cultured stone," he said, when he said anything, because it was going to need a name in a book and Bram's would be worse.

"It's a grown stone," said Bram. "I grew it."

"Grown stone," said Ferra, and wrote it, and put cultured beside it in brackets, smaller.

Ward had come in behind them and was standing in the door with the grey light coming past him off the sea. He looked at the two stones on Maren's cloth.

"What's it cost?" he said.

Ferra had been waiting all night to be asked. He could see it in the set of her shoulders. She turned back two pages.

"The mana's what costs. The press is a press, the quartzite's gravel, the dust is sweepings." She put her finger on a figure with a box round it. "To make the second one he forced into that block three times what the finished stone holds. Near enough three. I've the gauge readings every night of the week. And the first one, the bad one, cost very nearly as much and gave back a third." She took her finger away. "Liquid mana off the plant, carried down by the Hod, at what it costs us to carry it. Four weeks of a press and a man. Against a mined fire stone off the Isle field, at what Saban pays at the coast with the crown's cut in it." She looked up. "That stone cost us three mined stones. A little over."

Nobody said anything.

"So it's no good," said Ward.

"It's very good," said Ferra. "It's dear. Those aren't the same."

"It's insurance," said Emeric.

They looked at him.

"Every stone we've got comes out of a hill or a river or the Isle," he said. "Every fire stone, every water stone, every light stone, every air stone in every ship. And every hill runs out. But Grayrock's old seams were stripped before the plague and they never came back, and the crown takes its cut of every stone that's dug, and the Isle's half Duvrenne's whenever Duvrenne remembers it." He looked at the dark stone. "While the hills are open we dig. It's cheaper. Ferra's right, and she'll be right every year she runs the figure. But the day somebody shuts a road on us, or a war sits down on the Isle, or the price at the coast goes to three times what it is now..."

"Then that shelf pays," said Ferra. "Yes." She wrote. "I'll run it every year. Against the coast price. The year the coast price is three times today's, I'll tell you."

"You'll tell me before that."

"I'll tell you the year it's twice, and you'll say not yet, and I'll write that you said not yet." She drew a line. "It goes on the shelf. Oiled."

Emeric picked up the dark stone. It was warm from the cloth and the bench and the night, and perfectly smooth, and so even under his sense that it was almost dull to hold, the way a perfect sphere is dull to look at; nothing anywhere for the eye to catch on.

He put it back on Maren's cloth.

"Go to bed," he said to Bram.

"I'm going to sit here."

"Then sit here." He sat down on the crate beside him, in the black suit with the mesh at his collar, and Bram moved over by an inch to make room for him, which on that crate was a great deal; and they sat side by side in front of the hay press with the sea getting light in the door, and neither of them said anything, and after a quarter of an hour Ferra put her pencil down and came and sat on the bench behind them with her knee against her father's shoulder and her other knee against Emeric's, and shut her eyes.



The first things the Dust House made that anybody could use came out of its kiln in the second week of June, after the K Endeavor had carried the Hod three's report and the grown stones' figures home and come back with Tess's sorted tins; and they were not, whatever Ferra's book said, the winter-concrete form liners.

The form liners were real. So were the gravity-dust test slabs, six of them, cast in lifts like the dockhands and keyed with a small gravity stone each, which went to the Works yard on the mainland under canvas and were loaded until they broke, and broke later than the plain ones, and were written up in Bram's hand on a sheet that said LATER at the top in letters an inch high. So was the first square of shard-dust glass, the size of a book, which Ward took into the knights' yard and threw a stone at, very hard, and then a heavier one, and then stood looking at in a way that meant it was going to be in the Hod's cockpit by midsummer whether Fletcher liked it or not.

But the first things that came out of the kiln were three mugs.

They were plain and heavy and grey-glazed inside and out, with a small red-brown chip of keystone set in the foot of each, and the tea in them stayed hot. Not warm: hot, at the first sip and the last, from breakfast to the lamps. Emeric took one up to the study on the twelfth with his morning tea in it and found the same tea in it at six in the evening, untouched, too hot to drink.

Ferra priced them.

She did it at supper, on the thirteenth, at the end of the long table in the hall with the three mugs in front of her in a row and her book open beside them; and she did it properly, from the dust in the glaze to the sorting of it to the keystones to the kiln's fuel and the potter's wage and his nephew's time, while Maren's girls cleared round her and nobody else at the table dared reach for the salt.

"These are the most expensive mugs in the world," she said at the end of it.

"In Kaldo," said Saban.

"In the world. There aren't any others. I'm counting the world." She turned the book round. "Each of those cost what a carter makes in eleven weeks. For a mug."

"It's a very good mug," said Emeric.

"It's a mug that costs eleven weeks of a carter."

"Then nobody's allowed to drop one," said Imani, and took one, and put both hands round it, and looked at both of them over the rim with her eyes half shut like a cat on a hearth; and that was settled.

He dropped his on the twentieth.

It was in the study, at seven in the morning, with the window open and the Port Town's bells going and a letter from Paxton about the Milford wind towers in his other hand; he reached for the mug without looking and caught its handle with the side of his finger instead of the inside of it, and it went off the edge of the desk and down onto the oak boards, full, from the height of a man's waist.

It bounced.

It did not break. It did not even chip. It hit the floor and went back up off it, gently, about a foot, as a ball of wet wool would bounce if wool could bounce, and came down again on its foot, upright, and stood there, with the tea in it slopping very slightly and then going still.

Emeric sat looking at it.

Ferra, at the study table across the room with her own mug and the quarter's book, had seen the whole of it. She put her pen down.

"That's not mine," said Emeric.

"It's yours. It's the one you took up on the twelfth."

"It's not a fire mug." He got down on the floor and picked it up. The tea was hot. The glaze was the same grey. He turned it over, and there in the foot, beside the red-brown chip, was a second chip, dull and dark and heavy, that had no business in a mug at all. "There's a gravity key in it."

"So you've been drinking out of a test tile for a week," said Ferra, in the evening.

"It's a very good test tile."

"It's a test tile that costs fifteen weeks of a carter. The gravity dust is dearer." She wrote it down. She wrote, he saw over her shoulder, the date and the hour, and the height of the desk, and the height of the bounce, and under it: Mug, gravity-keyed, by mistake. Dropped by the duke, who was told. Did not break. And under that, smaller: Ask the potter if he can do it on purpose.

Imani laughed for most of the evening, at intervals, whenever she looked at the mug, and once in the night, in her sleep.



Grier had given them the right man and the wrong day.

It was in Saban's first sheet after they came home, on the thirtieth of May, in the wine store at night with the six round the table and Armand at its head in his chair, with his knees up rather higher than the chair had been built for and his robe riding at the ankle over a stocking Abloh had found him that was nearly long enough. He had come to the table every night since the stair, and sat in the chair he had always sat in, and been very pleased with himself about all of it in the same voice he had always been pleased with himself in; and the table had nearly stopped looking at him.

"He gave it us freely, and he gave it true," said Saban. "As true as he had it. A man in the prince's household, by the badge, the silver thread at the collar, who wouldn't say whose he was. A paper with the quittance on it. A cup of the vintners' wine that tasted of the cask. It's what he remembered." Saban turned his hat on his knee. "He remembered a Thursday. The Thursday before the vote. Raining. He's sure of the rain."

"It wasn't a Thursday," said Ferra.

"It was a Tuesday. It was raining on the Tuesday too. It rained that whole week in the capital; I've the House's carriers' sheets." Saban looked at Emeric. "You went into the man's house with his leave, and walked through his rooms with him holding the lamp, and saw what he saw. And he'd walked that evening so many times since in the dark, being sorry about it, that he'd moved it two days to the right and never noticed."

"A given read's what a man remembers," said Armand. "Not what happened. I said so in the tower forty years ago and nobody wanted to hear it."

So Saban had gone the slow way, which was his way. He had put the name into the north on the night express and let it go round the people he paid to sit in the right kitchens. His man in Corbin's household had the under-purser's face by the end of the first week, and the man's lodging, and the girl he walked out with, and the stable book. And the stable book, which nobody in any household ever thought to hide because it was only horses, showed the under-purser's own cob signed out on the Tuesday evening before the vote, at seven, against the household's warrant, for an errand in the lower town, and back at a little before eleven with the groom's mark beside it and a note that she had come in lame on the near fore from the cobbles by the vintners' hall.

"Not the Thursday," said Saban. "On the Thursday he was at table with the prince's steward, from six till past midnight; there are four men who'll say so, and a butcher's bill. The Tuesday he was out, on the household's warrant, at the door of a lord who owed the vintners eleven hundred, and came home on a lame horse." He put a slip on the table: his man's pencil, on the back of a list of the household's candle stubs. "And a vintner's boy at the fountain by the upper market, who carries for the hall, remembers a household man in the silver thread who gave him two coppers to hold the cob's head in the rain on a Tuesday because he was going to be an hour, and told him if he told anybody he'd have the boy's ears. He's eleven. He was very proud of the two coppers and he's told everybody, including my man."

"So he's proved," said Ward.

"He's proved to me. He's a man in the prince's own household, on the prince's own warrant, at Grier's door on the night the paper was signed, with a horse's book and a boy and a butcher's bill to put him there." Saban folded his hands. "I can't put it in front of a court. Not yet. Not while the courts are what they are. But if ever I can, I can do it without one word of the House's book in it, and without anybody's head but Grier's own mouth." He looked down the table to where Vantic sat at Emeric's left hand in her grey with her gloved hands folded. "Which was the price."

"It was," said Vantic.

"You found a man," she said. "You didn't find a book." She unfolded her hands. "My eldest brother's crown book has never been in a court in its life, and it will not go into one on your account or anybody's. A stable book is the prince's. A vintner's boy is his own. And the shame in Lord Grier is his own as well, and he may do what he likes with it." She looked at Emeric. "I gave you a road on a condition. You kept it. I'll say that once."



The leak came up out of Grayrock on the fourth of June, and it was not, when it came, anything like what any of them had been afraid of for a year and a half.

Saban brought it to the study himself, with his own man's docket, and with the face he wore for a cargo that had come in wet.

"A works hand," he said. "One of the forty. On his leave. It's his first leave home since February; his mother's in the pit rows at Grayrock, and her chest's bad, and the island foreman gave him ten days and the K Endeavor's run to the mainland and a ticket on the Grayrock line." He put the docket on the desk. "On the Saturday night he's in the Miner's Rest by the top station with three men he was at school with. He's paid for two jugs. He's brown as a boot and he's got coin, and they want to know where from. And he's a good lad, and he's sworn, and he tells them he's on the Works in Kaldo, which is true." Saban paused. "And then he's had the third jug, and one of them says the Works never browned anybody like that, and he tells them there's an island."

Emeric sat back.

"Out at sea," said Saban. "A great green island nobody knows. With wild birds you can pick up off their nests and seals on the sand, and a whole shore of it his to build on, and the duke's ships going in and out, and the best land he's ever stood on. And a stone in the sea that pulls boats in." He looked at the docket. "He told it well. My man says the table was quiet by the end of it. And then he heard himself, and laughed, and said it was a story the crews tell, and paid for the fourth jug, and went home to his mother."

"How do you know which crew?" said Emeric.

Saban almost smiled.

"Because he said the north shore."

And there it was: the oldest trick Saban had, the one he would not let anybody call by its stupid name. Every crew that had gone out to South Sentinel since February had been told, by the foreman, on the first night, round the first stove, some small thing about the island that was not true, a different small thing for every crew, and no crew had ever been told what another had heard. One crew had been told the yard was on the north shore. One that there were wolves in the hills. One that the stone in the reef was iron. One that the island was three days out, not one. One that the old quay was the duke's grandfather's. It had cost the foreman three nights of practising with Saban to keep them straight, and it had cost the island nothing at all; because the yard was on the east bay, and anybody who had worked in it knew that the north shore was marsh and nothing else, and so nobody had ever thought twice about a thing they had been told round a stove on their first night and never had cause to repeat.

"The north shore was the third crew," said Saban. "March. Twenty-two men. Four of them on leave this month, and only one of them gone home to Grayrock." He put his hand flat on the docket. "Ellery Cotter. Twenty-four. His mother's in the pit rows with the bad chest, and he sends her half his pay."

Emeric did not say anything for a while.

"Where's the story now?" said Ferra.

"Ah." Saban turned his hat. "That's the other half."

"And on the second of June," said Saban, "that line was copied, by the brother, onto a sheet of the chancery's best, in a fair hand, and the sheet went up the stair to the prince's old private clerk. The careful one." He took a slip out of his coat: his chancery man's pencil, this time, on the back of a list of the chancery's wax. "Who read it twice, my man says, and put it in a folder he keeps in his cupboard, with the other things about Kaldo that he's been told and can't prove. It's a thick folder. It has a dragon in it, from the Isle, and a man in Wayford who swears the duke can fly, and a woman in Milford who says the duke's bread makes you live to a hundred and ten." He put the slip on the docket. "He wrote nothing on it. He put it in and shut the cupboard."

"So he has it," said Ward, from the door.

"He has a sentence. That the duke keeps an island. No where, no what, no who. It's in a folder with a dragon." Saban looked at Emeric. "He'll keep it. He's that kind of man. He keeps everything. One day he'll take it out and look at it beside something else. But he can't find an island with it. Nobody can. There's no island on any chart in the realm and no road to one, and the man who said it went home to his mother and said it was a crew's tale."

Emeric looked out of the window at the town.

"And Cotter?" he said.

Ward took it down the hill himself the next morning, and told the table in four sentences: the lad had said it all back, word for word, because he thought lying would be worse; then he had cried, and asked whether they would take his place off his mother. Nobody was taking anything off his mother. He was going back on the Endeavor, his mother was going out to a house on the east bay in August, and the foreman would tell the next crew the yard was on the west shore. "And stop paying them three jugs at once," said Ward.

Ferra wrote all of that down, and then, at the end of it, for no reason anybody could see, one more line that she covered with her hand.



Harrold's last letter came on the ninth, by the Hod's charge run, in the spine of a binders' ledger as they all came now; and Ward read it in the study after supper standing, in the flat voice, with Ferra at the table and Emeric at the desk and the window open on a warm night.

He read the cipher's dates and formulae first, as he always did, the buckram and the gilding; and then the king.

The king was well. He had been well since April, in the Church's keeping, which was not a cell; it was a set of rooms on the cathedral close, three of them, cold in the morning and warm in the afternoon, with a window on the cloister garden and a lay brother at the door who brought his meals and took them away and could not be talked to about anything, and had been told not to be. He was allowed books. He had read all of them. He had asked for more and been sent devotions.

"There's a thing I should set down," Ward read, "because I have not decided what it means, and you will have more time than I have to decide it."

Ward stopped for a breath, the way he stopped at a turn in a road to see what was round it. Then he went on.

The first queen had come to see him. Once, in the second week of May, by the council's leave and the close's, with one of her women, for the quarter of an hour the leave allowed. She had not come in all the weeks of the council's keeping before the verdict, when she might have come any day; she had come now, to the close, which she had always said she would not set foot in.

She had stood the whole quarter of an hour. She had not apologised for her son. She had not, the king wrote, mentioned their son at all, which in thirty years he could not remember her doing in any room they had both been in. She had told him that she had never wished this for him. And then, at the door, with her woman already in the passage, she had turned round and said that he should watch what he ate.

"That is all she said," Ward read. "I have known her since I was seventeen and she was sixteen and neither of us had been asked. In all that time I do not think she has ever said a thing to me she did not mean twice over. I have been eating what I am given, because there is nothing else to eat, and I have been watching it, as she told me, and I am still well. I do not know whether she meant the lay brother, or the kitchen, or a man she did not name, or only that a king in the Church's keeping should not trust the Church's bread. She has distrusted the close all her life. She refused it something once, a long time ago, and she has never told me what." Ward paused. "I tell you because Ward will want to know who tastes my supper, and I cannot tell him. Nobody does."

Ward's voice did not change at that. Emeric felt his note go very steady and very still, the way a held breath is still.

There was a line more. The council meant to move him, the king had heard, though not from anybody who would say so; the close was the Church's house, but it was in the capital, and a king in the capital had friends in the capital, and windows. He would not be able to write from wherever it was. This road would stop.

"Keep the books," Ward read. "All of them. I am proud of you. I have not said that in your hearing since you were seven years old, and I find it is easier to write than to say, which I should have learned sooner. H."

He folded the sheet in half, and in half again.

"The rest is gilding," he said.

Ferra had put her pen down some time before. She did not pick it up.

And she had gone, after all those years, into the one house in the capital she had sworn never to enter, and stood for a quarter of an hour, and told her husband to watch his bread.

"She never liked my mother," he said.

"No," said Ward.

"She never liked the cardinal either." Ward put the folded sheet inside his shirt. "She wouldn't sit at his end of a table. Thirty years. I watched her not sit there." He looked at the window. "I don't know what that is. I'd like to."

"It's a woman who's angry about something," said Ferra, "and is choosing very carefully who she's angry at." She looked at the place on the desk where the letter had been. "That's not the same as being on anybody's side."

"He says nobody tastes it," said Emeric.

"I know what he says." Ward went to the door. "I'll write tonight. That we heard, and that I'll see to it." He stopped with his hand on the latch. "I can't see to it. Not from here. He knows that." He was quiet. "I'll write it anyway. He'll want to read that it's being done properly even when it isn't."

He went down the stair. His note went all the way to the bottom, steady, and out across the ward to the wine store, and stayed there, at the table, until well past two.



"They'll move him," said Emeric, the next night.

The six were at the table in the wine store with the lamp low and the door shut, and Armand at the head with a cup of something Imani had allowed him in his long new hands, and Saban with the week's slips from the north squared in front of him.

"He says so," said Ward.

"He says he's heard it. I'm saying they will, and I'm saying when, near enough, and where." Emeric put his hands on the table. "Corbin's careful. He was careful before anybody got to him, and whoever's got to him has made him more careful. And in the last three weeks somebody's pulled a thread. A lord who owed the vintners eleven hundred and voted the king down had three of his own watchmen wake up on the floor of his gate passage on the twenty-seventh of May with sore heads and no idea who'd put them there. The same night a sky-ship crew came home swearing they'd put a light on a ship in a field that went off like a scream over three villages. And this week Saban's walkers have been asking in the prince's household about a Tuesday in the rain." He looked at Saban. "Somebody in that household will notice."

"Somebody already has," said Saban. "The under-purser's been given a month in the country, my man says. For his health."

"The mother house," said Armand, slowly. "Up the river road."

They looked at him.

"The northern brothers' house, a day and a half out, over the ferry at the crossing." Armand turned the cup in his hands. "I spent a winter there at twenty, copying, because the tower sent me. It has a wall the old kings' tax-men never got through and the Church will tell you so at length. Nobody goes there who isn't sent." He drank. "If I wanted to keep a king where nobody could come at him, I'd keep him there. I'd have done it in April."

Nobody spoke for a while. Ferra wrote one line and did not cross it out.

"There's another thing from the walkers," said Saban, "and it goes with this, so I'll say it now." He turned the top slip over. "The capital's towers. The old gate towers and the four on the palace wall and the two either side of the cathedral porch. Since the winter my men have watched the crown's people putting iron on them. Frames. They went up in March and April, by night, with canvas over them by day, and the canvas came off a fortnight ago." He looked at Emeric. "Bombards. The crown's own. Gravity, the old silent kind, the ones that push a ball. Two to a tower. Only they're not on their carriages the way they sit on a proving field. They're set up on end. Pointing at the sky."

Emeric sat very still.

"My man says the crews call them Venn's," said Saban. "After a mage of the tower, who drew the mounts. They've been firing them up the river at kites, at dawn, for practice. My man watched one take a kite at four hundred feet without a sound and the line coming down slack across a barge." He put the slip down. "Since the lift hull, they say. Since she came up the river to her mooring in the year nine with the whole city on the quays to watch her, and went home again every season since on the Milford route with paying passengers, where anybody with a glass could see her."

Emeric was not in the wine store.

He was fifteen, in a long hot summer of dust, on a burnt brown valley floor with his father beside him and his hands behind his back; and at the end of a line of carriages there was an empty frame, iron, new, with its trunnions set not level but upright. And he had asked what it was for, and the officer had looked at the king, and the king had looked at the frame and then at his son.

Nothing yet.

"He told me," said Emeric. "My father. He told me at fifteen and I didn't hear it."

Ward looked at him.

"It doesn't matter," said Emeric. He made himself come back into the room. "It matters for later. Write it for later. Every tower round the capital, two upright, silent, with the dead-field stones Aldane's war taught them. Nothing of ours goes low over that city on stones and comes home without a hole in it." He looked at Ferra. "Not this summer. The road's what matters this summer."

"And we don't know the road," said Ward, "or the day."

"We know the road," said Armand. "There is only one road to that house that a carriage can take." He put the cup down. "You don't know the day."



The day came in a letter about a horse.

It came on the nineteenth of June, by the night express to the railhead and the morning train south, in the ordinary mail, in an ordinary cover addressed in a square untidy hand to the Duke of Kaldo at the castle, private; and the porter brought it up to the study with the rest, because it did not look like anything, and Ferra sorted it with the rest, and stopped.

She held it out to him without a word.

There was straw in the fold. One short piece of it, gold and dry, that fell out onto the desk when he opened the cover, and lay there.

He looked at it for a long breath before he read anything.

The letter was one sheet, written on both sides in pencil, with a good many words crossed out and written again, by a man who did not write letters and had not wanted to start.

The grey had a filly, it said. In April. I said I'd send word. She's grey too, or she will be, they come out dark. She's got a white foot. I haven't named her. I thought you might.

Then a line crossed out so hard the pencil had gone through.

Then: I keep horses in the mews. You know that. The master of horse sends down to me what's wanted and when, because nobody else here can read his hand, and nobody looks at what I read.

On the sixteenth he sent down for remounts. Forty-two. Saddle, not carriage, the heavy sort that can stand a long day. To be at the posting stations on the river road by the night of the first of July. Six at each, out to the ferry at the crossing, and a pair for a closed carriage to go over on the ferry and be changed on the far bank. Nobody's told me what for. The close's grooms came to the mews on the seventeenth for the harness, which they've never done in my time. A closed carriage. The close's own, with the panels painted out.

They'll go out at the north gate on the second, early. I can count. Forty-two horses is forty knights and the carriage pair. They'll want to make the ferry by the evening of the second and the brothers' house by the next night.

Under that, another crossing out, longer. And then, at the very bottom, very small, as if he had come back to the sheet after he had meant to stop:

I've done this because I want to, not because anybody asked me. I don't know what you'll do with it. I'd rather you did something than nothing. Don't send anybody to the mews. W.

Emeric read it twice. Then he turned it over and read the front again, the filly with the white foot, and put it down on the desk very carefully beside the piece of straw, and put his hand flat on it.

"It's from your brother," said Ferra, quietly. She had not read it. She did not need to; she had read his face.

"The grey had a filly."

"Oh," said Ferra.

She came round the desk. She did not touch him. She stood beside his chair and read the letter over his shoulder, both sides, the way she read anything that came into the study, every line and every crossing out; and at the end of it she put her hand flat on his back between the shoulder blades and left it there.

"Forty-two horses," she said, after a while.

"Forty knights."

"And the close's carriage, with the panels painted out." Her hand did not move. "The second of July. The ferry by the evening." She was quiet. "He counted."

"He was always good with what was in front of him," said Emeric. "He was bad at holding the pieces."

"Was he."

"He was three."

Ferra laughed, very softly, once, and took her hand away; and then she picked up the piece of straw off the desk between finger and thumb, and looked at it, and took the small book out of her pocket that was not Kaldo's, and laid the straw in it between two pages, and shut it.

"Ward," she said, not loudly.

Ward was already on the stair. Emeric had felt him start up from the wine store a minute ago, steady, not fast, the way he came when Ferra's voice in the study changed and he did not know why yet. He came in and shut the door behind him and stood. Emeric held out the letter. Ward read it standing up, both sides, as Ferra had, and the front again.

His face did not do anything at all. At the end he folded the sheet in half and held it, and looked at Emeric over it for a long time.

"That's the boy in the mews yard," he said.

"Yes."

"He said nobody looks twice at a man who keeps horses." Ward looked at the sheet in his hand. "Nobody did."

"He says don't send anybody to the mews."

"Nobody's going to the mews." Ward put the letter down on the desk, square to its edge, in front of Emeric. "That's his. You'll answer it yourself, by Saban's road, as a man writes to his brother about a horse, and there'll be nothing in it but the horse." He paused. "Name the filly."

"I don't know anything about horses."

"You don't have to. He doesn't want a horse's name. He wants you to have named something of his." Ward turned to the door. "I'll have the table at nine. All of it. And Fletcher, and Declan, and Rylan. And Arthur, and the two." He stopped with his hand on the latch. "The second of July. The ferry. That's thirteen days."

"Twelve and a half," said Ferra.

"Twelve and a half." Ward looked at her. "Then we'd best not waste the half."

Emeric sat with the letter.

After a while he took a clean sheet and a pen, and wrote at the top of it, in his own hand, not Ferra's office hand or the duke's, Dear Waylon; and sat looking at the two words for some time, because he had never in his life written them. Then he wrote that he was very glad about the filly, and that a white foot was lucky, he had heard, though he knew nothing about it and Waylon would know better; and that if she was going to be grey, he thought she should be called Ash, because she would come out dark and go pale, like the thing that was left after a fire, and that was what the two of them were.

He crossed it out. It was too much for a letter about a horse.

He wrote it again underneath, exactly the same, and left it.

At nine they went down to the table.
