Chapter 112 - Under a Roof

Fletcher came up the river in the first week of the late autumn with a roll of drawings under his arm, and put them on the table in the long room, and said that he had been thinking about the deck.

He had been thinking about it, it turned out, since the morning he stood at her wheel two miles above the lake with his hands inside his sleeves and his breath being taken out of his mouth by the wind of her own going. He had not said anything at the time. He had come down, and gone back down the river with her, and put her on the stocks, and sat in his cottage in the wind off the bar for three weeks with a pencil.

"Three things," he said.

Emeric had been thinking about three things too. He waited to see if they were the same three.

"You can't stand on an open deck up there," Fletcher said. "Not for long, and not at any speed. It was a trot and it took the skin off my face. At a gallop it'd take a man off the deck." He put his finger on the first drawing. "So she gets a roof."

It was a turtle deck, a curved shell of iron plate over the whole of her waist from the stack to the transom, riveted to her sheer, with the helm and the levers under it and a row of small ports along each side, each the size of a man's two hands, with a shutter that closed on the inside and a pane of the glassworks' glass behind the shutter. At the bow the shell came down to a blunt point. Along her sides, below the shell, there were two long shallow recesses let into the plating, and into them, folded, went the battens and Abloh's sails, flush with her side, so that nothing stood out of her anywhere for the wind to take hold of.

"Two," Fletcher said. "It's cold. It's cold enough up there to stop a man's hands, and a roof won't stop that on its own, but it'll help. And three." He looked at Emeric. "I couldn't breathe."

"No."

"Nor could your man at the levers. I saw his mouth. I've seen that colour on a man pulled out of the sea." Fletcher sat back. "A roof keeps the wind off. It doesn't make the air any thicker. I don't know what does. That's yours."

It was the same three. Emeric had come down off the lake on his knees on a hatch cover with Ward's blue lips very close to his face, and he had spent three weeks on the third of them, and he had the answer on a sheet in the notebook, and it was the same answer as the ship's thrust, and as the cells, because in the end they were all one thing.

He had been looking for it since the band.

The cells had filled in the band because their rails lay in the air, and the air up there was thick with the medium, and the rails drank it. And the feed had died above the band for the same reason, because the rails lay in the air, and the air above the band was nothing, and the rails bled everything they carried into it. The necks had held, because the necks were narrow. The cells had held, because they rode in iron. The rails had not, because nothing lay round them.

So the rails went into iron, every one, sleeved in iron tube from the rack to the deciding stone and from the deciding stone to every station, so that whatever the sky was, the rails would not know it. And the cells would not drink from the sky through the rails any more.

They would drink from the veins.

He unrolled his own drawing on top of Fletcher's.

It was the whole of her, from the bow to the stern, and running through her from end to end, under the turtle deck and above her hold, there were two channels of iron, one each side of her centreline, as wide as a man's shoulders at the bow and narrowing aft. At the bow each one opened to the air at the blunt point of the shell. At the stern each one opened again, low, under the transom, where the screw's arm was. And in each of them, at intervals along their length, there were air stones in housings, the kind that had breathed at the backs of Ward's calves, facing aft.

"Air in at the bow," Emeric said. "Pushed along by the stones. Out at the stern. That's the push. Nothing on the outside of her at all."

"And the cells?"

"In the veins." He put his finger on the channels where they passed over her hold. "The necks go through the wall of the vein into the air that's running down it. Everything she flies through goes past every neck she carries."

Fletcher looked at the drawing for some time.

"In the band," he said slowly, "that air's full."

"Yes."

"And the faster she goes, the more of it goes past."

"The faster she goes, the faster they fill."

Fletcher did not say anything for a while. He looked at the veins, and at the necks, and at the air stones in their housings facing aft, and Emeric watched him follow it round the way Bram followed a drawing, and come back to the start of it, and not like where he had come back to.

"That's a thing that pays for itself," he said. "Every shipwright alive has had a man come to him with a thing that pays for itself."

"It doesn't. It pays for the push with what's in the band. The band pays for it. Below the band there's nothing to pay with and she runs on what she brought, the same as she did at the coast." Emeric did not soften it. "And the cells give back no faster than their necks let them. They'll fill fast and pour slow. It won't run a forge. It'll run her."

Fletcher thought about that.

"And the third thing," he said. "The air."

Emeric moved his finger aft along the port vein, to a place above the helm where he had drawn a small branch off it, like a twig off a bough, running up into the space under the turtle deck.

"A little of what the stones push down the vein goes up into her," he said. "Into the room under the roof. More goes in than can get out round the ports, so the air under the roof is thicker than the air outside it. Not as thick as the lake. Thicker than the sky." He took his finger away. "Like a bellows into a bladder."

"You're going to blow her up."

"A little."

"Rivets," Fletcher said. "Every one of them's a leak."

"Every one of them's a leak. So it has to go in faster than it comes out, all the time, and it will, as long as the stones are pushing." He looked at Fletcher. "It's the same rule as everything else on her. If the stones stop, the roof's still a roof, and the air under it goes back to being the sky's, and you breathe the way you breathed over the lake. It isn't worse than that. It's only better while it works."

Fletcher rolled up his own drawings, slowly, square at the ends, and left Emeric's on the table.

"I'll build the roof," he said. "Your smith'll do the veins. I'll want him at the lower water for a month." He stood. "And I'll want to be aboard the first time she goes faster than a horse. I built her. I want to feel what she does."

It took them five weeks.

Bram went down the river with the veins in sections on a barge and stayed the whole of it, and so did Jorrin, and so did the forge, in a sense, because every rod and every neck-socket and every length of iron sleeve came down the river from the pattern store in crates, where the forge made them through the nights with nobody's hands on it, and Bram's second writer's stone, the first he had ever made alone, told it the shape of a vein socket and told it right. Emeric had checked it three times in the delve before he let it near the plate, and found nothing wrong with it, and said so, and Bram had nodded once and gone back to work and said nothing, and Ferra had written it in her book with the date.

The turtle deck went on over her waist a plate at a time. The ports went in. Abloh's sails went into their recesses and lay there flush. The veins went through her, bow to stern, and the air stones went into them, and the rails went into their iron sleeves, and the necks of the cells went through the walls of the veins in rows. And at the end of the five weeks she stood on the stocks at the lower water with a roof on her like the shell of something that lived in the sea, black and closed and smooth, with nothing standing out of her anywhere but the stack and the screw on its arm, and two open mouths at her bow like the nostrils of a horse.

The six men who had walked her deck in the early autumn came and stood in their row and looked at her, and the oldest of them, the one with the burn on his hand, said that she looked like she meant it.

They took her out on the last calm day of the autumn.

It was four again: Emeric, Ward at the levers, Fletcher at the helm, and Vesk, who had asked. He had asked Emeric outright, at the etching floor, three days after the lake, with the sieve in his hands and a steadiness in his voice that had not been there before the band. He wanted to feel it again. He wanted to know if it was the same the second time. He wanted it written down beside Emeric's, as it had been. Emeric had looked at him for a long moment and said yes, and had put a line in the notebook, in the column for things he could not answer, that said Vesk, full, and had not yet written anything under it.

Ferra's margin said over water, the whole way. So it was the coast, and the sea, and no boat under them, because no boat on the sea could have kept station with what Emeric meant to do.

They went up off the lower water under the turtle deck, with the ports shuttered and the lamps lit, and it was the strangest thing Emeric had yet felt her do, because he could not see her do it. He could feel it, in the medium and in his feet, and he could see it through the glass of the port beside the helm, the sea going down and away, grey, and the coast going down and away, and the dunes becoming a line and the line becoming a thread. But under the roof it was a room. It was cold, and the air stones in the veins breathed on either side of them like a sleeping animal, and the lamps swung very slightly on their hooks, and it was a room.

He watched the splinters. He watched the probe stone, which he had hung inside the port vein this time, behind a pane of glass let into the vein wall, where anybody at the helm could see it. And he watched Ward's lips.

At the height where the lake had given him a headache, he had none.

He breathed. It was not the lake. It was not even Kaldo's keep. But it was not the sky outside the ports either, and he could feel the difference in his chest, and Ward's lips were pale and not blue, and Fletcher was breathing through his nose at the helm with his hands on the wheel, and nobody was on their knees.

The probe stone behind its pane began to glow.

"The band," said Vesk. He had his hands flat on the deck, as he had at the hatch over the lake. "It's the same. It's the same as before."

"Ward," said Emeric. "Level her here. Not a foot higher."

Ward levelled her there, at the bottom of the band, where the medium came in on every side dense and full and the splinters in the rack had stopped dimming and begun to brighten, and nobody in her was going to put their head up through the top of it again today.

"Push," Emeric said.

Ward moved the right lever off its stop.

It was not the bellows. It was not the roar in the foundry either. It was a sound that came from everywhere in her at once, from the veins either side of them, low, steady, the sound of a very great deal of air going a very long way through iron in a hurry, and it went up and up as Ward brought the lever forward, a notch, and another, until it was a note held in the whole of her hull, like the note in a bottle when a man blows across the neck of it.

She went.

There was almost nothing to feel. That was the strangest of all of it. When a horse goes from a walk to a gallop a man feels every stride of it, and when a ship goes from a crawl to full steam he feels it in the deck and the engine and the water against her. She did not go against anything. She went the way a thing goes on ice when it has been pushed and nothing stops it, and there was only the note in the veins going up, and the lamps leaning very slightly aft on their hooks, and a pressure in Emeric's back as though a hand had been laid flat between his shoulders and was pushing, gently, and did not stop.

He looked out of the port.

The coast was going by. It was going by the way the banks of the river went by from the steamboat, a thing you could watch, and then faster than that, and then faster than anything he had ever seen a coast go by from anywhere, the dunes and the marshes and the creeks sliding under them two miles down in a long brown and grey unrolling, like a map being pulled off a table.

"The headland," said Fletcher. His voice was very level. "The one below the bar. The coasting men count from it."

Emeric had the glass in his hand. It was a quarter-hour glass out of the finishing shop, the one Neta turned for the rolling, and he had checked it the week before against the sun at noon, on two days running. He watched the headland come up under the bow, far below, a dark finger of land into the grey sea, with the white of the surf round its point.

It went under them.

He turned the glass.

"How far," he said, "to the white head? The one they call the Chalk."

"Fifty miles," Fletcher said. "By the coasting men's reckoning. They've been running it for three hundred years. It's fifty miles, give or take the tide."

The sand ran. The coast unrolled under them. The note in the veins held, high and steady, and nobody under the roof said anything at all for a long time, because there was nothing to say that the coast going by was not saying better. Vesk was sitting on the deck with his back to the bulkhead and his hands on his knees and his eyes shut, breathing slowly, as a man breathes in a warm room after a cold road. Ward had one hand on each lever and his eyes on the splinters in the rack, which were bright, and brighter, and brighter again, because every breath of the band that went past every neck in her was paying for the one after it. Fletcher stood at the wheel that did nothing and held it anyway.

The sand in the glass ran down to its last thread.

Far ahead, at the edge of the sea, something white came up out of the haze. It was a cliff. It came on very fast, the way nothing on the coast had ever come on, growing out of the haze into a head of white rock above a grey sea with the surf at its foot, and it went under the bow as the last grains went through the waist of the glass.

"The Chalk," said Fletcher.

Emeric looked at the glass. It was empty. He held it up so that Fletcher could see it, and Fletcher looked at it, and at the white head going away behind them under the stern, and did the arithmetic, and did it again, the way he would have done it twice for a price.

"Fifty miles," he said, "in a quarter of an hour."

"Two hundred in an hour."

"Two hundred miles in an hour." Fletcher said it slowly, as though he were reading it off a bill he did not believe. "Nothing goes two hundred miles in an hour. A horse goes forty in a day. A good ship goes two hundred in a week, with the wind behind her."

"She isn't sitting in the water," Emeric said. "She isn't standing on the ground. Nothing's holding her up but stones. So every push the veins give her goes into going. None of it goes into holding her up, and none of it goes into dragging her through the sea. Only the air takes it back." He looked out of the port at the coast unrolling. "And up here, the air pays the cells for it, as she goes."

Fletcher took that in. He did not argue with it. He looked at the splinters in the rack, bright as lamps, and at the glass in Emeric's hand, and at the coast, and then he took one hand off the wheel that did nothing and put it flat on the curved iron of the roof over his head, the way he had put both hands on her rail on the beach, feeling how she lay.

"Take her home," Emeric said.

Ward took the push off, and brought her round in a long wide curve over the sea two miles down, and she came home along the coast in the same quarter of an hour, with the Chalk going by under her the other way and the headland below the bar coming up under her bow, and she came down out of the band, and down out of the cold, onto the lower water, into the sea, as gently as she had ever come down onto anything.

The splinters in her rack were brighter when she came down than they had been when she went up.

They came to the table in the long room four nights later, the six of them, and Emeric told them.

He told it plainly, as a figure. Fifty miles, by the coasting men's reckoning, from the headland below the bar to the Chalk, in a quarter-hour glass checked against the sun. Two hundred miles in an hour. The cells fuller at the end than at the start. The air under the roof thick enough to breathe. Ward's lips pale and not blue.

Nobody said anything for some time after he had finished.

Then Ward said it, because Ward always said the thing first, in the tone he used for the number of men on a road.

"A warship."

He said it flatly, and he did not say anything more, because he did not need to. He was the Secretary of War. He had carried the Crown's harness for a Crown that had sent him south with a prince nobody at court expected to see again, and he had just flown two hundred miles in an hour two miles above a sea, and he knew better than any man in the barony what a thing that went two hundred miles in an hour two miles up would be to anybody standing on the ground underneath it.

"A road," said Ferra.

She said it without looking up from her book. She had been writing in it the whole time Emeric was speaking. She put her pen down.

"Grayrock is a day by the river when there's a river, and two by the road, and the iron's four miles up the road and six summers short of the hill. Wayford's further. Milford's further again. There's ore to come down and grain to go up and letters to go both ways and men to go wherever men are needed, and most of it still goes by carts at the pace of a horse, and all of it costs the pace of a horse." She looked up. "A road that doesn't need to be built. That doesn't want fifty-five tons of iron to the mile. That doesn't wash out. That goes over the wood and the hill and the Mere. That's what it is before it's anything else."

"It's a price," said Saban.

He had been turning his cup round on the table. He stopped.

"Not what it costs. What it's worth. Every man in the kingdom who needs a thing somewhere else in a hurry, and there are a great many of them, and most of them have money." He looked at Emeric. "And every cell she brings down out of that band is full of sky, and costs nothing to fill. You could sell the sky by the cellful."

"You couldn't," said Bram.

Everybody looked at him. Bram did not often speak at the table.

"Narrow bucket," Bram said. "They fill fast and they pour slow. You'd get a lamp out of one, or a lift, or a forge's hold. You'd not get a furnace." He folded his arms. "You couldn't smelt with sky. You'd want a fire for that. Same as always."

Saban considered that, and nodded slowly, and said that it was still a price, only a smaller one, and Emeric saw him put it away to think about later, the way he put away every price he meant to come back to.

Imani had said nothing yet. She had the log of the flight in front of her, and she had been reading it the way she read a history, and when she spoke she did not look up from it.

"A surgeon," she said. "In any barony in the south. By noon." She put the log down. "I've lost people because the man who could have saved them was two days off by the road. So have you. So has everybody at this table." She looked at Emeric. "That's what it is to me. I'd like it written down that I said so."

"It's written down," said Ferra, and it was.

Armand did not say anything. He sat with his hands folded on the table in front of him, where anybody could see them, and looked at the log, and at nobody, and Emeric looked at him for a moment and let it be.

Then they looked at him.

He had known they would. He had known since the Chalk went under the bow what he would say when they did, and he had turned it over for four days to be sure of it, and he was.

"All of those," he said. "Ward's right, and Ferra's right, and Saban and Bram are both right, and Imani's more right than any of us. And all of them are going to happen, whether I want them to or not, because a thing like this doesn't stay one thing for long." He put his hands flat on the table. "So the first thing she does is going to be the thing I choose. And the first time she goes anywhere, she carries nothing that can hurt anybody. Not a weapon. Not a man in harness. Nothing anybody on the ground could look up and be afraid of."

"Where?" said Ferra.

"Emberfell."

There was a pause. Ferra knew the name. So did Saban, who had heard it from every coasting man at the lower water for a month. The others knew it the way anybody in the south knew it, as a word off the edge of the map.

"Why there?" Imani said.

Emeric opened his mouth, and Saban was there first.

"Sulphur," Saban said. He said it the way he said a price he had been waiting for somebody to ask. "The coasting boats bring it back, when they can get it, which isn't often, because it's a long way along the coast in a small boat. There's a mountain there that smokes. It puts sulphur on the ground round it the way a hearth puts ash. And pumice. And it's hot." He looked down the table. "Bram says you can't smelt with sky. You can smelt with a mountain. Charcoal in this barony's been scarce since the war, and every ton of iron Kaldo pours eats a wood. There's a place along the coast where the ground does the burning for you."

He turned his cup round once more.

"And it isn't the Crown's," Saban said. "It's held by a woman who came to Randillia as a treaty, and went home when the treaty stopped needing her. Whatever comes from Emberfell comes down a coast the Crown doesn't own, to a barony that sold the Crown no stone this season and told it so in writing." He did not smile. "If I were choosing the first place a ship went that nobody could stop from going anywhere, I'd want it to be a place that wasn't anybody's to stop."

Emeric closed his mouth. He had had most of that. He had not had all of it, and not in that order, and he found he did not mind in the least.

"Rowena's," said Ferra quietly.

"My father's second queen." He said it plainly. "She knew me when I was twelve and not worth knowing. I'd like her to see what I've been doing since."

Nobody at the table said anything to that. Ferra wrote something, short, in her book, and he could read it upside down, and it was only the name.

He sat up after they had gone, with the notebook and the lamp.

Under Cells, above Flight, there was a line now that said three times, and under Flight there were more lines than he had ever meant to write under anything, and in the column for things he could not answer there were three: the reserve, set by a man; Vesk, full; and eleven buildings in a room above Kaldo that nobody could prove. He looked at them all for some time.

Then he turned to a clean page, at the back, where he kept the figures that did not belong anywhere else, and did one more sum, because it was the one that had been in his head since the Chalk went under the bow.

He had come south from the king's city in a carriage, on the road, and the road had taken what the road took, every day of it, and he remembered every day of it, because it had been the longest journey of his life and he had spent it working out how he was going to survive the end of it.

At two hundred miles in an hour it was a morning. Less. He could leave Kaldo after breakfast, over the lake and up into the band and along it, and stand in the king's city before the bells rang noon.

He looked at the figure for a long time.

Then he closed the notebook and put out the lamp and sat in the dark, in a room at the southern end of the kingdom, which had been the end of the world when he was sent to it, and was now a few hours from the king's door.
