Chapter 112 - Lime and Clay

The lime kiln had stood outside the south-east wall since before anyone in Kaldo was born, a squat round tower of fieldstone with a hole in the top and an arch at the bottom, and for most of that time it had not been lit. Emeric had lit it in the first spring for soap. It had taken nine days to bring back, and since then it had burned more or less without stopping, a fat column of white smoke going up beside the road to the burial hollow, eating limestone off the carts at the top and giving back quicklime at the bottom in white crumbling lumps that hissed and swelled and cracked apart when the rain got at them.

The cement kiln went up beside it in March, and it did not look like anything.

That had been on purpose, at first. Then it had stopped being on purpose and become simply what it was, which was a second kiln, taller and narrower than the first, of the same fieldstone, with a lining of the firebrick that had been left over from the furnace, and a little roof over the top of it where the charging platform was, against the rain. Anybody who walked past on the road to the hollow saw two kilns where there had been one and thought nothing about it, because nobody in Kaldo had thought about the lime kiln in forty years.

The difference was in what went into it, and how hot.

Lime was limestone burned. You put the stone in the top, and burned it with wood or charcoal at the heat of a good fire, and the stone gave up something into the smoke and came out lighter and white and hungry, and if you slaked it with water and mixed it with sand it made a mortar that set, slowly, over weeks, by drinking the air. It had held the curtain wall together for three hundred years. It also washed out of a joint in the rain, a little every winter, and would not set at all under water, which was why the drain under the east wall had been built with its joints packed in clay, and why Bram wanted cement at the toe of the slip.

Cement was the same stone, with clay in it, burned much hotter.

That was all. The book in his head, a builder's manual with a cracked spine that he had read one wet summer on a building site on his other world because there had been nothing else in the hut, said it in two paragraphs and then went on for forty pages about kilns, and grinding, and the proportions, and what went wrong. Limestone and clay, ground together, wetted into a paste, dried in lumps, and burned until the lumps half melted and fused into small hard clinkers like grey glass gravel. Then ground again, as fine as flour. Wet that flour and it did not drink the air. It drank the water. It went hard in a day and harder in a week and harder still for a year, and it went hard under the river as well as it did on the bank.

The trouble was the heat. The clinker wanted a fire far hotter than lime did, hotter than charcoal gave easily, and it wanted it evenly, all through the charge, for hours. Coke would do it. Coke and a draft. They had both now.



Vesk came to the kiln on the second day of the first burn, at noon, and stood at the edge of the clearing with his hands in his sleeves, and watched.

He had been in Kaldo nearly two years. He was a narrow brown man of no particular age with grey coming in at his temples, and he had been taken at the South Gate in the war with a ring on his finger that said he was a mage and a rope on his wrists that said he was a prisoner, and he had sat in the guardroom under the gate for most of that summer with Orrin talking at him from the next bench, and then Emeric had hired them both. Orrin had never stopped talking since. Vesk had hardly started. He was not mute, Emeric had found; he simply did not seem to see what talking was for, when there was a thing in front of him that could be looked at instead.

He looked at the kiln. He looked at the boys on the platform tipping the charge in, the lumps of grey paste dried in the sun like cakes, and the coke on top of them. He looked at the second boy at the arch at the bottom, feeding the draft. Then he went to the heap of clinker that had come out of the bottom that morning, small dark lumps cooling on a sheet of iron, and crouched over it and put two fingers on one.

Emeric saw him frown.

"It's not right," Vesk said. He did not look up. "The middle's not gone. This one, and that one. The outside's glass and the inside's still clay."

"I know. The kiln's cold at the edges. We're losing heat out of the sides."

Vesk put his fingers on another lump. Then he stood up, and looked at the kiln, at the curve of its wall, the way he had once looked at a crack across a hearth stone.

"I could make you the block," he said. "The test one. Out of the clinker, or out of the lime, or out of nothing. Shape it. It would come out of my hands hard as that wall and it would stay hard in the river." He did not say it as a boast. He said it as a man says he could carry a sack for you. "I could do the tank in the tower, the whole lining. A month. Less."

"I know you could."

"But you won't have me."

"Not for this," said Emeric. "For the slip, I had Paskin. For the boiler I had you look at the seams, and you found one Bram missed, and I'd have you do it again tomorrow. But this is the thing that goes into everything after. The tank. The slip. Bridges. The footings of houses that don't exist yet. Every wall in this barony for a hundred years." He looked at the kiln. "If it only comes out of a mage's hands, then every one of those is a mage's, and when the mage goes, or a stone cracks in the wrong place and the air goes dead for a day, it's all a wall nobody knows how to mend."

Vesk did not answer. He stood with his hands in his sleeves and looked at the boy at the arch.

"I sat in your guardroom," he said, after a while, "nineteen weeks."

Emeric waited.

"With my hands tied. They tied them in front, so I could eat." Vesk took one hand out of his sleeve and looked at it. "There was a cup on the bench. Clay. With a crack in it, down the side, so it leaked when you filled it, and the man who brought the water every day said he'd get another and never did. Every day I sat and looked at that crack. I could feel it. I knew the shape of it better than my own face." He turned the hand over. "And I couldn't close it. Not with my hands tied. Not a crack in a cup. Nineteen weeks I drank out of that cup with water running down my wrist."

He put the hand back in his sleeve.

"Burn your clinker," he said. "Teach the boy at the arch how hot is hot. I'll not make you a block." He looked at the kiln again. "But I'll tell you where your heat's going, if you want. Not fix it. Tell you. And your mason can fix it with his hands."

"Yes," said Emeric. "Tell me."

Vesk walked round the kiln. He walked round it twice, slowly, with one hand held a little way off the stone, and stopped at three places on the curve, low down on the north side, and put his palm flat on the stone at each and held it there.

"Here," he said. "And here. And there. The old stone's cracked behind the firebrick. Not much. A finger. The draft's going out through them instead of up." He took his hand away. "Pull the brick there, and pack the crack with clay and the brick back over it, and she'll burn even."

The mason did it that afternoon, with the kiln let down, swearing at the heat of the stones. When they lit her again the next morning the clinker came out of the arch at noon dark and hard all through, and Vesk came by at noon and crouched over the heap and put two fingers on a lump and did not frown.

He did not say anything else about it. He went back to the forge. But on the way past the arch he stopped beside the boy feeding the draft, and spoke to him, which Emeric had never once seen him do to anyone of his own accord, and the boy listened, and nodded, and when Emeric looked again the boy was standing a little further back from the arch and watching the colour of the fire in it instead of the smoke at the top.



The grinding was the worst of it.

The clinker had to go to flour, and it did not want to. It was harder than any grain the barony's mill had ever ground, harder than the millstones themselves, and the first week it went through the mill's old spare stones on the lower floor it took the face off one of them and Hollis, who had a share in the mill, came across the bridge to say so at length. In the end they did it the slow way, in a long iron drum Bram rolled from plate, lined with the furnace's first rough castings, with a load of round river cobbles inside it and the clinker with them, turning on its side off a belt from the works' wheel, day and night, the cobbles tumbling over the clinker and the clinker over itself, until what came out of the end was grey and fine and as soft between the fingers as the flour in Maren's bins.

A ball mill. Emeric told Bram the name. Bram said that there were no balls in it, they were stones, and Emeric said that in the book he had it from they were balls, iron ones, and Bram said that in Kaldo they were stones, and that was the end of it, and it was the stone mill ever after.

Into the flour, at the end, went the slag.

He had told Imani it would, standing at the edge of the pit outside the bay wall, and he had been right, though he had not been sure of it until he said it. Ground fine, the furnace's slag set up in the cement as if it belonged there, and the block that had slag in it was harder in a week than the block that had none. He made both. He wanted to be able to show it.



The test blocks went into the river on the second of April, at the mill race, where the water came off the wheel fastest and coldest and would find any weakness in a thing that was put into it.

There were three of them, each the size of a loaf. One of plain lime mortar, the barony's oldest kind, the kind that held the curtain wall together. One of the new cement, sand and water and grey flour. One of the new cement with the furnace's slag ground into it. Bram had made a mould for them of planks, and they had been filled and tamped and left a day to go off in the mould, and then knocked out and tied each in a net of rope, and lowered into the race off the wheel house's step and left there, with the ropes tied off to the rail.

A week, Emeric said. It would take a week before anybody could tell anything.

Half the works came to see them pulled out.

It had not been announced. It did not need to be. The boys at the kiln knew the day, and the boys at the furnace knew it from them, and Bram knew it because he had been counting, and Tess knew it because Bram had, and by the middle of the morning on the ninth of April there were twenty people on the bank of the race below the wheel, standing round in the thin spring sun on the wet stones with their hands in their pockets. Haddon was there, black to the elbows. Vesk was there, at the back, with his hands in his sleeves. Fletcher had walked down from the shed, which was a long way, and stood with his arms folded beside Hollis, and the two of them were agreeing about something, which was so unusual that Tess kept looking at them.

Vantic was there too.

She had not been invited, any more than anyone else. She stood a little apart from the rest on the stones at the edge of the race, in her grey coat and grey gloves, with her book under her arm, and nobody had seen her come. The cement kiln was a new work under the covenant. Emeric supposed she had come to see what the fifth had bought.

Bram hauled up the lime block first.

It came up out of the race on its rope, and the water ran off it, and half of it went with the water. It came up rounded, like a bar of soap that has been left in a basin, its corners gone and its top gone soft, and when Bram put it on the stones and pressed it with his thumb, the thumb went in.

Somebody laughed. Not unkindly. It was the wall's mortar, and everybody had seen what the wall's mortar did in the rain.

The second block came up whole.

It came up out of the water square, with its edges sharp, grey and wet and dark, and Bram set it on the stones beside the lime one, and stood looking at it with the rope still in his hand. He did not press it with his thumb. He knelt, after a moment, and rapped it with his knuckle, and it gave back the sound a stone gives back.

Nobody said anything.

The third came up, the one with the slag, and it was greyer still, nearly blue, and when Bram rapped it the sound was sharper, and he looked up at Emeric with his eyebrows up and said nothing.

Vantic was the first to touch them.

She came across the wet stones from where she had been standing, unhurried, in the middle of everybody not knowing what to say, and crouched beside the slag block with her coat gathered in one hand off the wet, and put her gloved hand on the top of it, flat. She held it there. Emeric watched her press, a little, and then a little harder, the way a person tests a step they mean to stand on.

It did not give. It was a week old and it had been in the river every hour of the week and it was a stone.

She took her hand away and looked at the glove. There was nothing on it but water.

Then she did a thing he did not expect. She drew the glove off, finger by finger, and held it in her other hand, and put her bare palm down on the cement, flat, where the glove had been.

He had never seen her hands. It came to him only then, standing on the bank of the race, that in the year and more she had been in Kaldo he had never once seen her without the grey gloves, at the trestle or in the gate yard or at the shed or on the river bank in December. Her hand on the block was narrow and pale, with long fingers, and very still, and there was an ink stain on the side of the second finger where a pen would rest, which was the most ordinary thing he had ever seen and made him look away from it, because it felt like reading her book over her shoulder.

She held her bare hand on the cement for a long breath. He could not see her face. She had it turned down to the block.

Then she stood up and put the glove back on, finger by finger, and turned round, and her face was exactly as it always was.

"It's cold," she said. To him, or to anyone. "Colder than the stones."

"It's been in the river."

"So have the stones." She looked at the block. "It's colder than they are. And it's harder." She looked down the bank at the lime block, slumped beside it like a dropped loaf. "That's the wall."

"That's what's holding the wall together, yes."

"And this," said Vantic, "is what will." She tucked her book back under her arm. "The House has lent against walls in a great many towns. It has never once asked what was between the stones." She looked at him. "It will now."

She went back up the bank toward the bridge. At the top she stopped and looked back, not at him, at the block on the stones, grey and square and wet. Then she went on.

He did not know what she had felt with her bare hand that she had not felt through the glove. He did not know why she had wanted to. He found that he wanted to know, which he had not expected, and that he was not going to ask, which he had.

Bram was still kneeling by the block.

"The toe of the slip," he said. "And a wall each side. Before the summer floods."

"Before the summer floods."

"And the tank." Bram got up. He looked up the river, at the town, at the round towers of the inner ward with their blue roofs standing up above the roofs of the lower ward. "That's a lot of stone flour."

"Then we'd best start grinding," said Emeric.



They chose the tower by walking round all of them.

There were six round towers on the curtain of the inner ward, the old castle wall that had stood there before the town grew up round it and the great wall was built outside the town. They had blue conical roofs of slate, and most of them had been empty for a hundred years, except for pigeons, and one of them had been the old baron's dovecote and was still full of doves, which Maren wanted kept. The tower they took was the north-west one, the highest, standing on the highest point of the inner ward's rock, with nothing in it but three floors of rotten joists and a great deal of what the pigeons had left.

The aqueduct's channel came in under the inner ward's wall not forty feet from its foot. That was why.

Water ran downhill. It was the only thing about water that Emeric had ever met that was entirely reliable. If you lifted it up into a tank at the top of a tower on the top of the town, it would run out of the bottom of that tank and down a pipe to any tap in the town lower than the tank, and push out of the tap with the whole weight of the tower behind it, without a pump, without a mage, without anybody doing anything at all but keeping the tank full. Pressure. The upper town had never had it. The upper town carried its water up from the standpipes in buckets, the same as the lower, and the women of the inner ward had legs like porters.

So they gutted the tower to the stone, and shovelled out a hundred years of pigeons, which took four days and a great deal of complaining, and Imani came up on the fourth day and stood at the bottom looking up the empty shaft of it and told them to lime the inside before anybody so much as laid a hand on it again, and they limed it. Then the masons went up inside it on a scaffold of poles, and from the top floor down to the third they lined the whole of the inside with a skin of the new cement, two fingers thick, over a mesh of Bram's wire, trowelled smooth, and went down a floor, and did it again.

It took the whole of April. It would be May before the tank was hard enough to fill.

Emeric went up the scaffold on the last day of the month, to the top, where the masons had finished, and stood inside the cement skin of the top floor, a round grey room with no floor yet but the joists, and looked up at the blue slate cone of the roof, and down through the joists at the shaft of the tower going down into the dark.

It was a water tower. It did not look like one. It looked like what it was: an old round tower with a blue roof on the top of a castle wall, the same as five others, with pigeons on its roof again already. Anybody who came up the high street would see six towers on the inner ward and think nothing of any of them.

Inside, it would hold more water than every bucket in the upper town carried in a week.

He put his hand flat on the cement, as Vantic had, and it was cold, and hard, and smooth as a plate.

"That'll hold," he said, to nobody.

Then he went down the scaffold, carefully, because he was not Ward and did not trust poles, and went home to write to Bryda for more coal.
