Chapter 141 - Your Transport Will Be With You Shortly

He wrote the summons five times, in the Ambry yard, an hour before light, at the bench under the dovecote with a bottle of the test ink at his elbow and a lamp on a hook over his head.

It was the same page each time. He had drafted it the night before at the inn and cut it twice, and Ward had read it and handed it back without a word, which from Ward was an opinion, and in the end it was four lines. He wrote it on a sheet of Kaldo's paper in the ink marked T2 and let it dry under the lamp, and took the next sheet, and wrote it again, and it did not get any shorter or any easier the second time or the fifth.

You are hereby called to answer for your crimes. If you accept, come to the counting house by the end of this day. If you do not, remain where you are. Your transport will be with you shortly.

He did not sign it. There was a line under it for the barony's seal, and Hobb would put the seal there, and that was the signature.

Behind him, through the back room's one window, he could hear the printers.

He had thought about it for most of a night in the spring and again last night, and it came down to this: nothing that left Wayford with the five houses' names on it could be in Kaldo black. The ink was a secret that dried. A page written in it and sealed and carried across the square to a lord's door was a page some mage in some lord's pay might one day hold near a stone for no reason at all, and feel it answer. So he wrote the five sheets where the dovecote knot could hear them, and the knot passed them on to the root in the back room, and the root put each one out on a printer in his own hand, line for line, pause for pause, in plain black pen ink that carried nothing and would never carry anything, and Wenna took each one off the carriage as it came and cut the head from it with the rule and the knife, so that no lord in Wayford would ever sit looking at a knot's name and a number and wonder what they were.

The originals he burned in the Ambry copper, one at a time, and watched each one go.

The machine that had printed their pages printed their summons. He noticed it and did not enjoy noticing it, and put it with the rest.

They went out at dawn in the barony's colours, five of Pennock's runners from the counting house, one to each door, with the seal on the fold and orders to put the sheet into the hand of a servant and say nothing and wait for nothing. Hobb went with the runner to Strade's house, because Strade's was the one Ferra had said should have a witness, and came back and said that the girl who took it had shut the door before he was off the step.

By the second bell the square knew that something had gone to the five houses. By the third it knew what. Pennock's runners had said nothing, and the servants who took the sheets had said nothing at the doors, and it made no difference, because a household is a room full of people who read over each other's shoulders, and five households are a town.

Lisle was not summoned.

Saban had the rest of it inside the hour. A servant in brown had come out of Lisle's town house on the lane behind the stream ground at a little after the second bell, on a good horse, and taken the south road at a canter toward the woods, which was where Lisle kept himself and always had. Nobody had gone to Lisle's door. Nobody from the Staple had been seen near it. Lisle had simply known, which meant Lisle had a man in one of the five kitchens, or in all of them, the way Saban did.

"He's telling himself," Saban said, "that it's nothing to do with him."

"It may not be."

"No," Saban agreed, and did not sound as if he believed it, and did not say anything more, because he had nothing more to say that was not a feeling, and he did not deal in those.

Nobody came.

Emeric sat in Pennock's inner room through the morning with the toll books, and at noon the counting house's big door stood open on the square in the sun with a clerk at the table inside it and a ledger open in front of him and a pen, and nobody came through it but a carter wanting his toll chit and an old woman wanting her son's.

He had not thought they would. He had written if you accept knowing that none of them would, because accepting meant walking across the square in daylight to the barony's own door, in front of their own town, and putting themselves in the hands of a boy who had eaten at their tables and let himself be taught. They would not do it. They would not do it because they did not believe the boy had written the page. They would think somebody had written it for him, some clerk from the south with a grudge, or Ferra, whose name they had heard, and that the boy had signed where he was told, and would be as frightened as they were by nightfall.

He wanted them to think that. He had worked all summer to make them think it.

At the first bell of the afternoon he went out into the yard of the inn and had the horses brought round, and he and Ward rode out of Wayford by the south road, openly, at a walk, in daylight, the prince going home.

People watched him go. He felt them do it. The square was full, the way it was always full in the afternoon, with carts at the beam and men at the Staple's arches and women at the pump, and the talk went down as he rode across the top of it and came up again behind him, and at the corner by the gate a boy ran along beside his stirrup for a dozen yards for no reason except that it was the prince, and then stopped and stood in the road and watched him out of sight.

The Staple's lamp was lit in the upper window. It was always lit. He did not look at it.

"They'll say you've run," Ward said, when the last house was behind them.

"Yes."

"Some of them will be glad."

"Some of them," Emeric said, "will be very glad." He looked at the road ahead, pale and empty, running away under the ridge with Lisle's woods dark along the left of it for most of a day. "Let them."

The Staple had a room at the top that nobody who did not keep its books had ever been in, and Aubrey Placit had been keeping its books, in the sense that mattered, for nineteen years.

It was the warden's room. It was on the square side, with a long table and a hearth and a strongroom door in the end wall, and the window that held the lamp. The lamp sat in its bracket in the window with a man on a stool beside it, the way it always did, and the man did not look round when the lords came up the stair one after another in the evening in their cloaks. It was not his business to look round. His business was the lamp and the square below it, and he had been doing it for six years, and he had never once in those six years been asked what he saw.

They came in by the stable gate at the back, not across the square. Placit had sent word by his own man to each house an hour after the sheets came, and his word was that it would be the usual night, tonight, brought forward, and that they would come in by the back, and bring their men.

They brought their men. Placit heard them in the yard below and on the stair, boots and voices, Hollin's big steward swearing at somebody's horse, the weighing hall's doors being barred on the square side and the bar going across with the sound it made. By full dark there were more armed men in the Staple than there had been in it at one time since the war, and fewer than there should have been, because Dray had brought four and one of those was his groom.

"Four," Placit said.

"Four's what I have," said Dray. He was sitting at the end of the table with his cloak still on and his hat on the table in front of him, turning it round and round by the brim. "You didn't say how many."

"I didn't think I had to."

Hollin said, "Leave him," without looking up from the summons in front of him, which he had read, Placit supposed, some dozens of times since the morning, and which he was now reading again with his finger under the line. "Remain where you are. Where is that? My house? This?"

"This," said Placit.

"It says the counting house."

"It says come to the counting house if you accept. We don't accept." Placit took his own copy out of his coat and laid it on the table. "And it says if we do not accept, we remain where we are. We are where we are. Here. Behind a wall, with a gate, and forty men."

"Thirty-eight," said Vennor, from the window.

Vennor had come in from his manor at noon on a lathered horse and had not taken his coat off. He had not sat down. He stood at the side of the window away from the lamp, looking down into the square, which was dark now except for the lamps at the inn door and the one over the counting house, and he had said almost nothing since he came in except that.

"Thirty-eight, then," said Placit. "The boy's gone home. The whole town watched him go. Rode out at the first bell with the one guard he never goes anywhere without, and didn't look back." He put his hand flat on the summons. "That is a boy who has had a letter written for him by somebody in Kaldo with more stomach than he has, and has been told to sign it, and has signed it, and has found that he does not want to be in the same town as what he has signed. Whoever wrote this will send somebody. A clerk and a dozen men and a writ. And we will be here, and we will talk to the clerk."

"Talk," said Hollin.

"Treat. It's what one does with a clerk." Placit looked round the table. "Eleven years we paid Sethe the tenth out of our own charter and the Crown never asked where it went. The boy's clerks have been through Sethe's books, and they've found some things, and they want some of it back. Very well. We'll find out how much. There's nothing in this," his hand on the page, "that a clerk can't be talked down from, if the clerk's reasonable, and if he isn't, there's the Crown, and the Crown is not the boy's friend. Everybody knows that."

"He wasn't running," said Strade.

She had been sitting at the far end of the table with her hands folded in front of her and the summons square to the table's edge in front of her hands, and she had not touched it once all evening. She did not raise her voice. She never did. It was a small dry voice and it went down the table like a rule laid on a page.

"He rode out at a walk," she said. "He didn't look at this building. He didn't look at anything. A boy who is frightened of what he has signed goes fast and looks back." She looked at Placit. "He was a different man at the judgment. I told him so at my own table. He answered me like a fool, on purpose, and I let him, because I wanted to see how long he would keep it up. He kept it up all summer. He was not running."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Oriel," said Placit, gently, "he eats what he's given."

"Yes," said Strade. "I noticed that too."

Placit let it go. He had known her thirty years and she had been right about a great many things in that time and wrong about some, and on the whole he had found it cheapest to let her say what she thought and then do what he thought. Hollin went back to his page. Dray turned his hat. Vennor, at the window, did not move.

The door at the end opened and the book-man came in.

He came in the way he always came in, without knocking, with the Staple's big ledger under his arm and his coat buttoned to the throat though the room was warm, a man of no particular height and no particular age, grey at the sides of the head, with a face that people forgot while they were looking at it. He put the ledger down at Placit's elbow. He did not sit. He never sat at that table.

"Up to the quarter," he said. "As you asked." He spoke quickly, in a low hoarse voice with the flat vowels of somewhere north, and never said a word more than he needed to.

Placit looked at the book, and at him.

"Burn it," he said.

The book-man did not move.

"Not this one. This one's the charter's, and the Crown can read it any day it likes." Placit put his hand on the ledger. "The others. All of them. Everything in the strongroom that has a figure in it that isn't the beam. Oriel's books." He did not look at Strade while he said it. "Tonight."

"Aubrey," said Strade.

"Tonight, Oriel."

"Those books are the only thing in this town that says who owes what to whom." Her voice had not changed. "Burn them and you'll never collect a Copper of it again. Nor will I. Nor will any of us, and every man in the barony who's paid us for eleven years will know by spring that he needn't have."

"Then we'll write new ones." Placit turned back to the book-man. "Up there. The strongroom grate. Start with the old ones and come forward. And the other thing," and he did not say what the other thing was, and did not need to, and the book-man knew it, "stays as it is. Tell Crisp. The lower door stays shut and the lamp stays lit and nothing moves in or out by the back while there are men in this building who aren't ours. Nothing is different. Nothing has ever been different."

The book-man looked at him a moment longer. Then he nodded once and took the strongroom key from the nail by the door, where it always hung, and went into the strongroom and pulled the door to behind him.

Presently they heard him at the grate.

It was a small grate. It had been put in a strongroom to keep the damp off parchment, not to burn anything, and it drew badly, and a ledger is a tight thing that burns from the edges in and chars and goes out and has to be broken open with a poker and fed in a quire at a time. They could hear him doing it. They could hear him, now and then, stop, and the pages turn, and then start again. He was reading them as he burned them. Placit supposed that was natural in a man who had kept half of them.

Hollin said, "And Lisle?"

"I sent to Lisle this morning." Placit reached into his coat again and took out a half sheet, folded once, and put it on the table where they could all see it. His man had brought it back from the woods at dusk. There was no seal on it and no name, and one line of writing in a hand none of them could have sworn to.

I have no part in this and will take none.

Dray laughed, a short high sound that he stopped almost at once.

"That's Lisle," Hollin said.

"That is Lisle," said Placit, and put the half sheet in the fire, and watched it go. "We don't need him. We never needed him. We'll hold this house and we'll treat with whoever comes, and in a month this will be a question of what the tenth comes to over eleven years and who pays how much of it, and in a year it will be nothing at all."

At the window Vennor said, without turning round, "The boy wrote transport."

"Yes."

"Not a clerk. Not a writ." Vennor was looking out at the square, at the lamp over the counting house door, at the road beyond it going south into the dark. "Transport. As though he'd already sent it."

"He's a boy," said Placit. "He likes a good word."

Nobody answered that. Behind the strongroom door the book-man fed another quire to the grate. Down in the yard somebody's horse stamped and was quieted. The man on the stool by the lamp sat as he always sat, facing the square, and did not turn round, and the lamp burned in the window over the Staple's arches the way it had burned every night for six years, and nobody in the room said what they all knew and none of them had said aloud since the morning, which was that somewhere under their feet, under the weighing floor and the old wool cellars and the lower door that stayed shut, there were people who could not be moved in the dark with the town watching, and could not be left if the town ever came in.

The column was camped where the road came down off the ridge to a ford a long day south of Wayford, in a meadow by the water, and Emeric and Ward came down to it at dusk with the horses blown.

He smelled it before he saw it. Horses and woodsmoke and the pitch the carters used on their axles, and then the fires coming up in the dusk one after another along the meadow's edge, and the pickets, who had seen them a mile off and let them come, and a knight at the ford with a lantern who knew Ward's horse before he knew Ward and said so.

They were all there. He had not let himself believe it until he saw it. Ward's list, called in a morning, on the road by first light, four days up the only road there was with the carts behind and the horses fed, and nobody lost and nobody lame and nobody gone back. He walked down the line of fires with Ward beside him and men got up from them as he passed, and he did not know what to say to them and so he said their names, the ones he knew, which was most.

Declan was at the last fire with his boots off and his feet to the flames, and got up too fast and swore at the ground.

Rylan was beside him, and did not get up at all. He was sitting on his saddle with his back to the cart that carried the suits, four of them in their crates under oilcloth and a knight sleeping across the tailboard, and he looked up when Emeric came into the firelight and then looked back at the fire.

Osric was not there. Osric was in Kaldo, with the ship, where Ward's orders had put him, and would stay there until the fourth night of the road, which was tonight.

Ward sat down and had the map out before anybody had given him anything to eat. It was Saban's bad map, and it went on the ground between the fires with a stone on each corner, and Declan and two of the knights who had been rehearsing the Staple's house men on Kaldo's island all spring came and crouched round it, and Ward told them, plainly and in order, what they had all known since the spring would happen, which was that the five houses were not in five houses.

"They're in the Staple," he said. "All five. Since dark tonight, or near it. With their men in, and the doors barred on the square side, and the stable gate at the back." He put his finger on it. "Forty men, Saban's count, give or take the ones who weren't worth bringing. House men. Not soldiers."

"The plan's gone, then," said Declan.

"The plan's gone." Ward did not sound as if it troubled him. "We march in the morning. Openly, up the road, into the square, in daylight, with the colours. We put a ring on the Staple and we sit down. They'll want to treat. Let them. Osric talks to them, and says what the paper says, and nothing else, and doesn't let them make it a conversation." He looked at Declan. "You and Osric have the front. On foot. Nobody puts a suit on in that square. Nobody. If a man in Wayford sees a suit tomorrow, he sees a knight in black plate standing in a square, and that is all he ever sees."

"And then?"

"Then we rest. The day, and the night after, and the next day. Let them look at us out of the windows. Let them count us." Ward's finger went to the back of the building. "The night after that, we go in."

He did not say who went in at the back. Everybody at the fire knew. Declan looked at Rylan, and away.

Emeric said, "I'm going home tonight."

The knights looked at him. Declan said, "Home?"

"To Kaldo. There and back." He did not look at the knights, who did not know, and would not be told tonight, how a man went from here to Kaldo and back in two nights on a road that took five days. They were Ward's men. They knew that the prince came and went, and that Osric stayed behind in Kaldo when the column marched, and what that meant they had decided among themselves a long time ago not to ask. "There's a thing I should have built in the spring and didn't. I'll be back the night we go in."

"What thing?" said Declan.

"A thing for a man who runs."

Declan considered that, and apparently decided it was an answer, and went back to his boots.

Rylan had not looked up from the fire. When the knights had gone back to their own fires and Ward had gone down the line to see the horses, Emeric sat down on the other side of the cart's wheel from him, on the grass, and for a while neither of them said anything.

"Is he there?" said Rylan.

"He goes to the Staple on the usual night. Placit's letter brought the usual night forward. It'll be tonight." Emeric looked at the fire. "Saban's man saw a man in a buttoned coat go in by the stable gate at dusk with a ledger. He's there."

Rylan nodded, once.

He did not say anything else and Emeric did not ask him to. They sat on either side of the cart's wheel in the dark with the fire between their boots and the suits in their crates behind them, and after a while Rylan took something out of his shirt and looked at it in the firelight, a letter folded small, and put it back without unfolding it.

At the fourth bell of the night Ward walked out of the column's camp with a shuttered lamp and Emeric behind him, north along the ford road for half a mile and then up off it onto a bare shoulder of hill that the pickets had been told to leave alone, and set the lamp down in the grass on the hilltop, and opened its hood a hand's width, facing south.

They stood and waited.

Below them and a mile off, the column's fires lay along the meadow by the ford, a long uneven row of small orange lights going down to embers one by one as the men turned in. Somebody was singing, badly, and somebody told him to stop, and he stopped. A horse whickered. The river went on over the ford.

Then the sky over the hill was not empty.

She came down out of the dark over the Wayford road the way she always came, without a light on her anywhere and without a sound that could have been told from the wind, a long shape blacker than the sky with no stars where she was, lower, and lower, until the grass round the lamp began to lie down flat in a wide slow ring and the air under her was cold. Her legs came down out of her belly. Ward put his hand on the lamp's hood, ready to close it.

A mile away along the meadow the fires burned down, and a knight on picket at the ford stood with his back to the hill, watching the road, and nobody looked up.
