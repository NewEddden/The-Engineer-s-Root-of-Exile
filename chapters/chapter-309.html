Chapter 309 - Treason

The council sat on the eleventh of August, for the first time since the river road, and Saban had known it would for nine days, because the chancery had sent down to the chandlers' hall for sixty-four candles of the long sort, the kind that burned from noon into the night, and the chandlers' hall kept a boy who could count.

"Sixty-four," Saban had said at the table, turning his hat on his knee. "Not a box of a hundred, which is cheaper by the candle and what the chancery always buys. They mean to have every seat lit, and they mean to sit late."

So the line room was full again by nine on the eleventh, with the shutters open on a hot still night and the river low under the castle and the whole town lit to the walls, and the hiss in the box.

It was nearly the room of April. Imani was on the floor against the wall with her shoes off, Ward on the bench under the window, Saban on the stool, Bram in the door, Ferra at the operator's shoulder with her pad and Tess at the dial; Osric had carried her up at half past eight with Declan on the bottom end of the chair and gone back down to sit on the stair. Armand was at the window in a dark coat Abloh had cut for him at last with sleeves that came to his wrists, looking pleased about the sleeves.

And in a chair by the door, brought up from the wine store that afternoon because there was nothing else in the room that was not a stool or a bench, sat the king.

Nobody had asked him to come up. Ferra had told him at supper that the council sat today and that the line room would have it tonight, and at a quarter to nine Emeric had felt his father's note leave the old guest rooms and come across the inner ward and up the keep stair, unhurried, past Osric, who stood up so fast he hit his head on the turn of the wall.

"Sit down, Sir Osric," Harrold had said, without stopping.

Osric had sat down. He was still sitting there, with one hand on the top of his head.

Emeric took the sill, which gave him the box in front of him and his father's profile at the edge of his eye. He had not decided yet whether he wanted it there.

At a quarter past nine Tess put her fingers on the dial.

"There," she said, before anybody else could have heard it; and then they all heard it, the hiss changing its mind, and the patient small voice of Saban's man coming up through it from seven hundred miles away.

"Two. Nine. Nine. Four."

"Begin," said Ferra.

Harrold did not move. But Emeric felt his father's note go very still and very attentive, the note of a man who has listened to councils from the dais all his reign and has never once heard one from the far end of a wire.



Keller's notes came first. The crown's magistrate went into the hall at the ninth bell and wrote as it sat, and his clerk carried the pages to the drapers' street at noon and again after the candles were lit, as he had done since the winter, because nobody in the chancery had yet thought to stop him.

"Every seat," Ferra read. "Sixty-four. His Grace of Aldane on the first bench, with Tarrowmede and Dunholt and Halverne along it, and Tarrowmede's cousin of Penhallow at his shoulder, and the Marquis of the Cocan March at his elbow, down from the passes. Lisle and Coldharbour on the bench behind, and Brask. The Princess Maeve in the gallery." She wrote. "The king's chair on the dais, empty. The Lieutenant standing beside it, as in April, with his hand on its back."

Nobody looked at the chair by the door. Emeric felt everybody in the room not look at it.

"Who said the prayer?" said Harrold.

It was so plain a question that Ferra had looked back up her pad for the answer before she had registered who was asking.

"The dean of the chapter," she said. "Keller says so in a line by itself." She read it. "The prayer was said by the dean. The chair at the head of the right hand was empty for the whole of the sitting. Nobody in the hall looked at it."

"He has not missed a council," said Harrold, "since I was crowned."

He said it to nobody, in the voice that died at arm's length. Then he put his hands back on his knees.

His note did not move. It moved very little, that Emeric had ever felt; he had felt it go once at Ferra's table, when his father laughed and was surprised by it. He thought about the bank at the crossing, and the pressure that had opened over it, and the two strings in it, and the right hand; and made himself stop, because Ferra was reading again.



The Lieutenant had spoken for the better part of an hour, without notes, Keller wrote, and he would give the substance only, because the gallery would have the words.

"That last's for me," said Saban quietly. "My man was in the gallery. His groups come after Keller's."

The substance was this. That in April the council had found the king a soul in question and committed him to the Church's keeping, and the king had gone into it without a word, and the council had honoured him for it. That on the second of July, at the crossing, the king's carriage had been taken off the ferry in the middle of the river by armed men who came up out of the water; that the men at the near landing had been struck down out of the dark by others never seen; that the escort had been broken with a light like noon and a stone that put horses and men flat in the road; and that two of the crown's own household riders had been killed on the bank, a trooper named Aldo Ivers and his corporal, Simm Harker, whose names the Lieutenant had read into the record with their parishes and their years of service.

Ferra read the two names in the same voice as everything else. Then she stopped writing for one group, and Emeric saw the pencil sit still on the pad, and go on.

Imani had sat up off the wall.

"We didn't," she said. "We killed nobody. Ward said nobody, and it was nobody."

"It was nobody of ours," said Ward.

He did not say the rest of it. Every person in that room who had been on the bank had felt it or seen it: the strike that came off the man the others gave the road to, too fast and too wide, that had burned Rylan's arm and cracked his ribs; and on its way, because they were in it, two notes in the escort's own line that had simply gone out, like lamps in a door when the wind takes it. Emeric had felt them go. He had read their names in July, on the page Ferra tore out of her book for his father, with their towns and their mothers' names; he heard them now read into a council's record as Kaldo's work.

Aldo Ivers. Simm Harker. By the door, his father's note did not move at either name. It had had them a month.

"There's more," said Ferra.

There was more. That the men who took the king had gone off the far bank into the air, in a ship the ferry's boy had seen from the reeds: a ship that came up out of the trees past the crossing without a light and without a sound, and went north. That there was one house in the realm which kept ships that flew without a sound, and men who could come up out of a river, and that every man on the benches knew which.

"North," said Bram, from the door.

"North," said Ward.

"The boy saw the white hull." Emeric's own voice sounded perfectly ordinary, which surprised him. "He saw the cardinal's ship go up, and they've put it in our hands."

"The boy saw a ship," said Saban. "The hall knows who has ships."

At the window Armand made a small sound that was nearly a laugh and was not one.

"It's very neat," he said. "You don't have to like it. You do have to admire it. He ran off the bank himself and left the bill on our table."



Then the gallery's groups came, Corbin's own words, and Ferra read them flat, the way she read a contract back to the man who had signed it.

"I said in April that I would keep it for him until he came home," Ferra read. "He has not come home. He was offered the road home by this council, in the keeping the law gives a soul in question, and the Church kept the door of it for him. And on the second of July he was offered another road, by armed men out of a river, and he took it. He chose." A group. Another. "I will not insult this hall by pretending that I do not know where my father is tonight, or whose roof he sleeps under."

The hiss came up between two groups like water over a weir and Tess's fingers moved by a hair, and it went down again.

"My father chose one son over the crown," Ferra read. "I am sorry for it. I am sorrier than any man in this hall. But I am the crown's. And I will not."

Nobody in the line room said anything.

Emeric did not look at the chair by the door. He could feel his father's note, close and still at the edge of the room, and it did not change by so much as a breath when the line was read; and that stillness, which every courtier in the capital had read for twenty-eight years as a man who felt nothing, he knew now for what it was. It was the hardest thing Harrold did. He was doing it now, in a chair from a wine store, while his eldest son's voice came out of a box at him through seven hundred miles of hiss.

"It's well put," said Harrold.

That was all.



The rest was the law, and it went fast, because Corbin had drawn it carefully and in advance.

He had named it treason, and not the king's; he had not once in the hour, Keller noted, said the word king and the word treason in the same sentence. The treason was the house of Kaldo's: to have levied force against the crown's keeping of the king's person, which the old law had a name for and had last used three reigns ago, and to have shed the crown's riders' blood in it. And a house that had levied force against the crown held nothing of the crown. Its patent was void. Its lands came back to the crown that had given them.

"Escheat," said Ferra. She wrote it, and looked at it. "He's commanded the chancery to draw the instrument. The duchy, and the four baronies under it, Kaldo and Grayrock and Milford and Wayford, by name, with every parish. To be sealed under his hand as Lieutenant and read in every market square in the realm."

"When?" said Ward.

"When it's drawn. He didn't say." She read on. "The council gave its voice standing. Keller says he didn't count. There was nothing to count; the left side stood, and then the right."

"Aldane?" said Emeric.

"Stood with the first bench." Ferra's pencil did not stop. "The Cocan March was up before him. Keller says Tarrowmede and Halverne were the last two up on that bench, and took their time about it."

"Last up is still up," said Ward.

"Last up is a man waiting to hear a price," said Saban.

Ferra wrote on. "Keller rose once, to ask under which article the instrument would be drawn, so the crown's table might prepare its answer. The Lieutenant said it would name its article when sealed, and that the magistrate should have the first copy, out of the respect the council bore him." She paused. "Keller writes: I was thanked."

"Ah," said Armand.

"He'll have to break the patent first," said Ferra, to her pad. "The duchy's. The one Keller stood up in that hall and made them seal in front of everybody." She wrote. "You can't take back a thing you sealed in public because the law said you must. Not for a breach. You need something bigger than the statute." She looked up for the first time since the begin. "That's what the treason's for."

There was one more page of Keller's, short, at the foot of everything. He had headed it to His Majesty. The king was his client and the duchy was not, and Keller had never in his life written a line to Kaldo he could not have read aloud in the hall; tonight, knowing perfectly well whose house his client slept in, he had written the king's style at the head of a page and sent it to the drapers' street.

"The settlement of Harrow Bridge," Ferra read, "was made in the king's name between the crown and the house of Kaldo, and bound each of them to keep the king's peace with the other. The Lieutenant has this day named the house of Kaldo a traitor to the crown. A settlement does not outlive its parties calling each other that. Its terms bind nobody now, either way. I make no remark on it. K."

She wrote it out, and under it, smaller, the thing it meant.

"That's the three hundred gone," said Ferra.

Ward did not say anything at all. He turned his head and looked out of the window behind him, across the dark ward to the knights' quarters, where the levy's rolls were kept in a chest; and turned back.



The voice gave the end, twice, and the hiss came up and filled the box.

Tess turned the gain down to a breath, and then off. Nobody moved. It was the same quiet as April's and not the same at all.

Harrold ended it. He stood, with one hand on the chair's back, the way a man stands who has sat too long in a chair not built for him and will not say so, and looked round the room at all of them, and last at his son on the sill.

"Thank you," he said, to the operator.

The operator did not know what to do with that at all, and got up, and sat down again.

Osric was flat against the stair wall before the door was open. Harrold stopped on the top step.

"He was always the best of them at putting things," he said, not turning round. "I paid for the men who taught him. They were very good."

Then he went down. Emeric felt his note go all the way to the bottom of the keep and back across the ward to the guest rooms, and up; and stop at his window, and stay there.

"Somebody go and sit with him," said Imani.

"He doesn't want anybody," said Emeric.

"I know he doesn't. I said somebody." She was putting her shoes on. "Not you. You'll both sit there being quiet at each other till dawn and call it a conversation. I'll take him something for the heat. He hasn't slept since the island. He thinks nobody knows."

She went. Ward watched her go and did not stop her, which was Ward's way of agreeing with something.

Ferra was still writing. She wrote the two riders' names at the foot of the last page, by themselves, and drew no box round them, and shut the pad.



The slips came south on the night express of the twelfth, which was forty minutes late, and Saban brought them up to the study at half past seven on the morning of the thirteenth with his hat on.

"A box on the train held it up at the junction," he said. "The shunters were arguing. I'll find out." He took the hat off and laid three slips on the desk in a row, in the order he meant to talk about them, as he laid out samples for a buyer.

Ferra was at the table by the window with the quarter's book and her tea in a fire-glazed mug, held in both hands though the morning was already warm. Ward came up behind Saban without being sent for. And at a quarter to eight Harrold came in and took the chair by the empty grate, which had become his chair in that room without anybody saying so, and nodded to Saban, and waited.

He looked as though he had slept, a little. Imani had sat with him until one, Ferra said later, playing cards. Imani had won, and Harrold had asked whether that happened to everybody, and Imani had said yes.

"The chancery first," said Saban.

His man in the chancery was an old copying clerk at the third desk from the stove. The instrument was being drawn at the first desk by the chancery's best hand, with the rolls of the four baronies carried up from the strongroom in their boxes, every parish in the duchy copied in by name. Kaldo's were done. He had started on Grayrock.

"My man says ten days to the seal," said Saban.

"And you say?" said Ferra.

"A week. They'll put another desk on it when the Lieutenant asks how it goes, and there's a man from the tower in the chancery every afternoon looking up the old reigns for the article. A week, then the seal, then the riders. A few days after that it's read at every market cross in the north, and somebody brings us a copy down the line."

Ferra wrote the date, and under it a second date, a week later, and put a small mark beside the second that Emeric did not know.

"The second," said Saban.

He did not touch this one. He looked at it.

"The cardinal left the close by night," he said, "in the third week of July. North. Not in his own carriage and not by any gate my men keep. A carter watering his team at the third posting house on the north road saw four of the close's horses changed at two in the morning for a closed carriage with nobody's arms on it, and the lay brother who paid wore a ring on a cord round his neck the carter didn't like the look of." He paused. "The close says the cardinal is in retreat. The posting houses have the carriage past the fourth. Then nobody has it."

"North," said Ward.

"North, and on."

"Ostrel," said Harrold.

They looked at him. He was sitting as he always sat, back from the grate with his hands on his knees, and his face did nothing.

"There is one house north of the channel," he said, "that a cardinal of this realm goes to in person. He goes when he is sent for, or when he has something he does not trust to a letter. Since I was crowned he has gone twice, and both times he came back with the same face he went with. I did not know then what to look for." He was quiet. "Nobody has sent for him. Nobody at Ostrel knew there was a reason to, until he told them."

Emeric said nothing.

He had stood on a bank at the crossing with a door open in his chest, in front of the one man in the realm who could feel it, and Ward beside him with a door of his own, a knight's, which nobody in the Church had ever written down could happen. Augustine had felt both. He had looked at Emeric across the bank for the first time in Emeric's life, really looked, the way a man looks at a figure in a column he has been adding for twenty-eight years and finds was never the figure he wrote down; and then he had gone up in a white ship and taken it north to somebody. What that somebody would make of it, nobody in the room knew, and nobody in the room was going to find out by sitting in it.

"Then he'll be some time," he said.

"It's three weeks to Ostrel on a good road," said Saban. "Less, for a man who doesn't stop." He did not look at anybody, and Emeric understood that he had thought about the white hull too, and decided not to say so in front of the king.

Harrold looked at Saban for a breath, and then at his son.

"You need not do that," he said. "Either of you. I was on the ferry. I saw it go up."



The third slip was old, Saban said, and it wasn't.

"The night we read Grier," he said. "The watch on the capital's north gate tower keeps a book: every light it sees after dark, fires in the fields, lanterns on the river, sky-ships, falling stars. It's been kept since a beacon went up on the hills once and nobody saw it." He turned the slip toward Emeric. "On the night of the twenty-seventh of May, in the small hours, the watch wrote down a red star, low in the south-east over the fields, that went the wrong way. Up the sky and along it toward the coast, too quick and too even for anything falling, and shrinking all the way."

Emeric did not need to read it. He had been in the cabin with the can red behind them and the floor shaking under his boots.

"And there's a sky-ship crew out of the tower's yard," said Saban, "whose casting mage put a light on something in a field that night. They swear it was a ship. Long and dark, with legs, and a noise like the end of the world, and a red eye going away. Their captain laughed at them. The tower wrote falling star in the margin of the watch's book, in a good clear hand." He folded his hands. "Nobody believes them."

"Then why is it on your slip?" said Ferra.

"Because the morning after the sitting, the prince's careful clerk sent a boy to the gate tower for a copy of the watch's book for May, and another to the tower's yard for the crew's names." Saban looked at the slip. "Nobody believes them yet."

"It'll go in the folder," said Ferra. "With the dragon."

"With the dragon, and the man in Wayford who says the duke can fly, and now a white ship off a ferry." Saban took the slip back. "And one of these nights he'll lay them all out on his table side by side. He's that kind of man."

"He'll have a white ship and a red one," said Ward.

"He'll have two ships nobody's ever seen," said Saban, "and one of them was ours."

Emeric thought about the rule he had made over the fields that night with Fletcher's hand still on the guard: no burn near anybody's eyes. It had held. It had held at the ferry, where Ferra had made it hold. He had thought it cost them nothing to keep, and saw now that it had cost nothing only because it was made in time, which was not the same.

"It holds," he said. "Until the war. Tell Fletcher I said it again."

"Fletcher knows," said Ward.

"Tell him anyway. He'll like hearing it."

Harrold, by the grate, said nothing at all. But Emeric saw him look at the slip as it went back into Saban's coat, and then out of the window at the empty sky over the town; and understood, with a small cold jolt, that nobody had yet told his father what the K Discovery could do with her can open, and that he had just been told anyway.

His father looked back from the window.

"I shall not ask," said Harrold, "until I am told I may."

"You may."

"Then I shall ask later." He settled back. "Go on, Master Saban."

But Saban did not go on. A boy in the station's blue was coming up the study stair two at a time with his cap in his hand, and stopped in the open door, out of breath, and looked at the room, and at the king by the grate, whom he did not know, and at Ward, whom he did, and decided on Ward.

"Captain," he said. "Mister Anders says sorry, sir, and there's a man in the goods yard with a horse box off the night express who won't come out of it, and won't let the horses out of it, and says he's the duke's brother." He took a breath. "Mister Anders says he's covered in straw and smells of horse, sir, and he wouldn't credit it, only he's got His Grace's face. Same eyes. Gold, sir. So Mister Anders thought he'd best ask."



The goods yard lay along the river below the station, a long hot strip of cinders and sidings and cattle pens, and the horse box stood by itself on the end road by the loading ramp: a tall brown van with louvres along the top and a groom's door at one end, with every man in the yard finding something to do within twenty yards of it and nobody inside those twenty.

Emeric felt the note before he saw the box, and knew it at once. He had stood beside it in a lamp-lit mews yard for perhaps a quarter of an hour, four years ago, and it was the same, low and stubborn and warm, with something in it that pulled tight and let go like a man worrying a knot. Beside it were two more, both new to him: a big slow animal warmth, uneasy, shifting from foot to foot; and against it, a small bright one, asleep.

Anders, the yard foreman, met them at the ramp with his hat in his hands. He had the look of a careful man who had spent two hours deciding between two kinds of trouble and had chosen the one with a duke in it.

"He paid for Milford, Your Grace," said Anders. "At the capital's railhead. Then at the junction he got the guard to take the box off the Milford car and put it back on ours, and paid him in silver and wouldn't take any change. That's what held the express." He turned his hat. "Then he said he'd stay in it with them till the shunter was off the coal, because he'd not have the mare come down a ramp with steam blowing at her. And then he said who he was, and asked was there anybody here who'd know him, and I said there was the duke, and he laughed."

"He does that," said Emeric.

"Yes, Your Grace."

"Thank you, Mister Anders."

The groom's door at the end of the box was open on its catch. Emeric went up the ramp and looked in.

The box was lined with straw a foot deep and smelled of horse and hot iron and the long night. At the far end a big grey mare stood with her head up and her ears going, sweated dark on the neck; and in the straw at her feet, with his back against the partition, sat Waylon, in a riding coat with straw on it, and in the beard, with a dark long-legged filly lying across his boots fast asleep.

He looked up.

"I said you'd know me," he said.

"I know you."

"Your man thought I was a horse thief." Waylon looked down at the filly. "Fair enough. I look like one." He did not get up. "I'm not coming out till that engine's gone. She's been in this since nine last night and never in a box in her life, with this one at her heel, and every time we stopped she tried to come over the partition. She's done in. I'm done in."

"It's on the coal till nine."

"Then I'll be here till nine." He looked past Emeric, at the door. "Is that Ward?"

Ward had come up the ramp behind him and was standing in the doorway with the yard's light behind him, filling it.

"Your Highness," said Ward.

"You've got old."

"You were sick on my boots," said Ward, "in the long gallery, when you were three. You'd eaten a whole dish of something nobody had given you leave to eat. I had to go and change them. I was on the door."

Waylon looked at him for a long breath.

"I don't remember that," he said.

"I do."

And Waylon laughed, the way Emeric had heard him laugh in the mews yard, short and surprised, as if something had been let out of him that he had not known he was holding; and put his head back against the partition and shut his eyes.

"Seven hundred miles," he said, with his eyes shut, "and the first thing anybody says to me is I was sick on him."



The engine came off the coal at a quarter to nine, and Emeric felt the mare's note change as the hiss of it went up the yard, all at once, the way a held breath goes. Waylon was on his feet.

"Right," he said. "Stand there. Both of you. Don't do anything."

"I could..."

"Don't do anything." Waylon took his coat off. "I don't know what you can do and I don't want to find out with her." He folded the coat lengthways and went up to the mare's head talking to her, a low running nonsense with no words in it, and laid the coat over her eyes and tucked the ends under her headcollar, and stood with his hand flat on her neck until she put her head down. Then he turned her in the stall and backed her out, and down the ramp, blind, a step at a time, saying the nonsense all the way, with the filly pressed against her mother's flank like a child against a skirt; and on the cinders he took the coat off, and the mare blew, and shook her head till the headcollar rattled, and looked round at the goods yard of Kaldo as if she had a great many complaints about it and was saving them for later.

The yard, which had been finding things to do twenty yards off, went back to doing them.

"Done that before?" said Ward.

"Off a ferry. Off a ship once, at the river quays; a stallion. He went in the river." Waylon put his coat on. "She's got sense. She just didn't want to." He took the mare's rope in his left hand, and then, as if he had decided a thing before he could think better of it, held out the filly's to his brother: a short soft rope on a little headcollar of red leather that somebody had made by hand.

"Here," he said. "Hold her."

Emeric looked at the rope.

"I don't know anything about horses."

"I know." Waylon did not take his hand back. "Hold it anyway. You named her." He paused. "Badly, if you like."

Emeric took the rope.

The filly turned her head on the end of it and looked at him. She was dark all over, the colour of a wet slate, with a short brush of a mane standing straight up and a long foolish tail, and one white foot, the near hind, white to the fetlock as if she had stepped in a pail of lime. Her face seemed to be mostly forehead. She stretched her neck out as far as the rope would go and took the top button of his coat in her lips, very gently, and pulled.

"She does that," said Waylon.

"She's eating my coat."

"She's not eating it. She wants to know what it is. Keep hold of the rope. She's quick, and she doesn't know where she is."

Emeric kept hold. Under his hand the filly's small bright note sat against the mare's big one and did not stray from it, and her lips worked at his button; and he stood on the cinders of his own goods yard with his brother's horse on the end of a string and could not think of anything to say, which had not happened to him in a very long time.

"Ash," he said, at last.

"That's her," said Waylon.



They walked them up.

It was not quick. The mare would not be hurried and Waylon would not hurry her, and the filly stopped to look at everything: the tram wire on the station road, which hummed, and the tram itself when it came down the hill full of people going to the works, which she stared at with her ears so far forward they nearly touched. The people in the tram stared back. Emeric could not tell whether at the filly or at the duke holding her.

Waylon said nothing about the tram. He looked at the wire, and the lamp standards, and the clean water running in the gutter, and a cart going by with a pair of heavy browns in it that he watched until it turned the corner, the way Ferra watched a column; and on the long climb up through the town he asked one question, and it was about the cart.

"Who shoes your carters' horses?"

"I don't know. There's a farrier by the brickfield."

"He's good. The near brown had a set on her that'll last her the summer." Waylon walked a few steps. "The off one's cast a shoe behind and nobody's noticed. You can hear it."

Emeric could not hear it. Ward, beside him, said, "I'll send word," and Emeric understood that Ward could not hear it either and had decided to believe it anyway.

It was on that climb that Waylon told it, the way he had written his letter: a piece at a time, with things crossed out, looking at the horse and not at anybody.

Maeve had come down to the mews the night after the sitting, late, without a lamp, as she had come once in the spring to talk to him about the filly and nothing else. This time she had not talked about the filly.

"She said a council that's found a king in breach and a duke in treason looks next for the rest of the line." Waylon looked at the mare's ears. "I said she was the rest of the line as much as me. She said she's Rowena's, and Rowena's in Emberfell with an army in the yard, and nobody's going to look for Rowena's daughter in a hurry. And I'm Amara's. And I'm a man who sleeps over a stable." He was quiet for a few steps. "She said it all at once like that. She does. I said I'd think about it. She said no, I wouldn't; I'd go on the express tomorrow night, and her man would buy the box and a ticket to Milford in a dealer's name, and I'd get off where I liked. Then she went back up. She didn't wait for me to say yes."

"You said yes," said Emeric.

"I'm here." Waylon looked at the filly. "I wasn't going without them. She knew that. She bought a box with two stalls."

"Was anybody watching you?"

"There's been a knight on my door since April. Corbin's. For my safety." He said the last three words in a voice that was very flat and not his, and it took Emeric a breath to understand it was an imitation, and of whom. "A good man. Polite. Stands all night outside my rooms in the east wing." Waylon shrugged. "I sleep over the mews half the year. There's a loft over the grey's box with a bed in it and nobody's ever thought to ask. He's been minding an empty room since April."

Ward, on his other side, made a sound that might have been anything.

"He'll know by now," said Emeric.

"He'll know I'm not in it. They'll ask the mews, and the mews'll say I took a pair down to a buyer in Milford, because that's what I told them. Then somebody will check the buyer." He looked at the mare. "Three days, maybe. Maeve said four."

"And when they know?"

"Then they'll know I'm here." Waylon looked up, for the first time on the climb, at the castle walls on the hill, and the gold rose hanging limp in the heat over the gate arch, and the high curve of the dome's glass behind the walls, catching the sun like water. He stopped. The mare stopped with him and the filly walked into her, and Emeric, on the end of the filly's rope, was pulled a step and a half and stopped too.

"That's the glass thing," said Waylon. "Everybody talks about the glass thing."

"That's the dome."

Waylon looked at it for a while.

"Somewhat," he said.

It took Emeric two whole breaths to understand that it was a joke, and whose, and from when: a lamp-lit mews yard, and I hear you build bigger things now, and his own voice saying the one word back. By then Waylon was walking again.



The castle's stable yard lay on the north side of the outer ward behind the old granary, and had not had much in it for nine years, because the duke went everywhere by tram or train or in the air and the carts were the works'. Twelve boxes along two sides under a tiled roof, a pump in the middle, a loft above; four boxes with the castle's own horses in them, and the rest with sacks and timber and, in one, a stack of Tess's spare tram wheels.

Waylon looked at the yard and did not say anything for a while, and then said, "Who drains this?"

"The yard drains into the north sewer," said Emeric. "There's a grating under the pump."

"There's a grating under the pump with a sack over it." Waylon hung the mare's rope on a hook, took the sack off the grating and threw it into an empty box, and stood looking at the boxes, the doors and hinges and hay racks and the roof over them, the way Bram looked at a machine on his floor for the first time. "The two at the end are dry. The rest want their floors up. You've got rats in the loft; I can smell them. Which can I have?"

"Any of them."

"Then the two at the end." Waylon went to look at them. "And I'll want the loft swept, and a cat."

"There's a bed for you in the keep. Imani'll want..."

"I'll sleep in the loft. Over them. The first nights, anyway; she'll not settle if I'm not." He went into the end box and kicked through the old bedding and came out again, and said, as if it followed, "Who's Imani?"

Emeric was opening his mouth to answer when he felt his father's note come out of the guest rooms.

It came across the inner ward and through the inner gate and along the foot of the north wall, not quickly; and it did not stop at the arch into the stable yard, where a king's note would stop, to be met. It came through. Harrold walked into the stable yard in his plain dark coat with his hands behind his back and looked at it: the pump, the tram wheels, the big grey mare on her hook with the sweat dried white on her neck and the dark filly at her side, and his youngest son in the door of the end box with straw in his beard.

Waylon did not move.

Emeric had wondered, on the climb, what his father would say, and had decided it would be something a king said, which was the only kind of thing Harrold had ever said to any of his sons with anybody else in the room.

Harrold looked at the mare for a long time.

"She has travelled badly," he said.

Waylon looked at the mare too.

"She hates trains."

"The ship," said Harrold, "is worse."

And Waylon laughed: once, short, as if it had been punched out of him, and stopped, and looked astonished at himself, and at his father, who had not changed his face at all.

Harrold came across the yard and stopped at the filly, and she put her head up and looked at him, and stretched out her neck and took the top button of his coat in her lips, very gently, and pulled.

The king stood in a stable yard and let a filly eat his coat.

"What is she called?" he said.

"Ash," said Waylon. Then, after a breath, because it was true and he was the kind of man who said the true thing even when there was a better one to say: "He named her."

Harrold did not look round. But Emeric, by the pump with the filly's rope still in his hand, felt his father's note do something it had not done in the line room for all of Corbin's hour, or at the grate for all of the cardinal's road. It did not move very far. It moved.

"You wrote to each other," said Harrold.

"Once," said Waylon. "Each."

"Yes." Harrold put his hand down, carefully, the way a man does who has not touched a horse that was not already saddled for him in a long time, and laid it on the filly's neck under the brush of mane. "He showed me yours. Afterwards." He looked at the filly. "You can count."

Waylon said nothing.

"Forty-two horses," said Harrold. "Forty knights and the carriage pair. I sat in that carriage all the way up the river road with the windows painted out, and I could not think how anybody had known the hour." He took his hand off the filly's neck. "Thank you."

Waylon looked at him. His face did something complicated in the straw-coloured beard, and settled on nothing, and he looked at the mare instead.

"I didn't do it for you," he said.

"No," said Harrold. "I know."

The yard was very quiet. Up on the loft's edge a sparrow came and went. The castle's own four horses had their heads over their doors, all four, looking at the grey.

Emeric thought he should go. He thought it very clearly, and did not move; because Waylon had turned his head and was looking, not at the mare now, but at his father, straight, the way he had looked at Emeric across the carriage lamp in the mews yard, with something in his face that had been in it since he was four years old.

"He told me in the mews it had to be everybody," said Waylon. "The door. His door, when I was four. He said it had to be everybody or it wouldn't have worked." He did not look away. "Was that you?"

Emeric stopped breathing.

Ward was in the arch. Emeric had not felt him come, which meant Ward had come very quietly and on purpose, and he stood there now and did not come any further.

His father did not answer for a long time. He stood with his hands behind his back and his face did nothing, as it had done nothing for twenty-eight years in every hall of the realm; and then, because there was nobody in this yard to see it but a horseman and a captain and one son who already knew, it stopped doing nothing.

"Yes," said Harrold.

Waylon looked at him.

"Me as well," he said. It was not quite a question.

"You as well."

Waylon nodded, slowly, twice, the way a man nods at a sum he has finished in his head that came out where he had thought it would, and did not like.

"Right," he said.

He went to the mare. There was an old dandy brush on the shelf by the pump with half its bristles gone, and Waylon knocked it on his boot and started on the mare's neck where the sweat had dried, in long strokes with the lie of the coat; and after a while, without looking round, he said, "There's another one on the shelf."

Harrold looked at the shelf. There was another one on the shelf.

He took it down. He held it wrong, and went to the mare's other side and began, slowly, against the coat; and the mare turned her head round and looked at him.

"Not like that," said Waylon, across her back. "With the hair. The way it lies. You'll have her up the wall."

"I was never shown," said the king.

"No," said Waylon. "You weren't." He reached across the mare's withers and turned his father's wrist a quarter round with two fingers, and let go. "Like that."

Harrold did it like that.

Emeric gave the filly's rope to Ward at the arch, because Ward held out his hand for it without a word, and went out into the outer ward and stood in the sun by the granary wall for some time, with his hand still closed as if the rope were in it.



Imani found Waylon at noon, asleep in the loft on a horse rug with his boots on, and told the table afterwards that the duke's brother was dehydrated, filthy, eleven hours without food and had a sprained thumb from the box's partition, where the mare had pinned it at the junction; that she had strapped the thumb and stood over him while he drank a quart of water with salt and honey in it; and that he had watched her the whole time as if he were waiting for her to bite.

"Then he asked whether I'd look at the mare's off fore, because she's warm in the foot and he thinks it's the box floor. I said I'm not a horse doctor. He said, no, but you've hands." She took a piece of bread. "So I looked at the mare."

"And?" said Ferra.

"It's the box floor. He was right. I told him so, and he looked as if nobody had ever told him that in his life." She looked down the table at Emeric. "Your family."

"I know."

"I don't think you do," said Imani. "But you're learning. It's very slow to watch."

Ferra wrote the stable yard into the book that afternoon: two boxes, floors up, the loft swept, a cat to be found, the drain cleared of a sack, a farrier to the brickfield carter's off brown. She costed it, and wrote sundry under the total, and then crossed out sundry and wrote Waylon, and left it.

Emeric saw her do it. She did not look up.

"It's a line," she said. "He'll want one. People do."



He went down to the stable yard that night at eleven, because it was too hot to sleep and because he could feel from his window that Waylon was not asleep either: the low stubborn note awake over the loft, beside the two notes of the mare and the filly below it, which were.

He stood at the foot of the loft ladder in the dark and said, not loudly, "You said you'd like to see."

There was a pause in the loft.

"I said not yet."

"That was four years ago."

Another pause. Then the straw moved, and Waylon came down the ladder in his shirt and boots with the strap on his thumb pale in the dark, and looked at the filly asleep on her side with her long foolish legs out straight; and came.

They went up the keep stair together, past the line room's shut door, to the hatch, and out onto the lead, still warm from the day.

Waylon walked to the parapet and put his hands on it and did not say anything at all.

It was all there. It was always there at night, and Emeric had stopped seeing it years ago except in pieces, the way a man stops seeing his own house; he saw it now because his brother was seeing it beside him. The town ward by ward to the walls, and the walls lit along their tops; the river under the castle hill with the quays' lamps doubled in it; the ring line's white trains going round outside the walls, small and steady and lit end to end; the works shore with the furnace glow over it and the slipway lamps. And below them in the inner ward, close enough to drop a stone on, the dome in its ribs, dark tonight, with the tree a shape against the glass and the faint violet of the old room at its heart, the way a lamp shows through a hand.

Waylon looked at it for a long time.

"Is it always like this?" he said.

"Every night."

"Who pays for the light?"

Emeric nearly laughed. "The river. The works. Coal. And everybody, by the month, at a meter. Ferra could tell you to the copper."

"Ferra's the one with the pencil."

"Ferra's the one with the pencil."

"She looked at me in the yard like she was adding me up." Waylon kept his eyes on the town. "I didn't mind it."

"She was. She put you in the book. Under your own name."

Waylon thought about that for some time.

"What was I under before?"

"You weren't in it before."

"No," said Waylon. "I wouldn't have been."

They stood there. The last tram of the night went down the hill to the station, nearly empty, and round the corner, and was gone. Over at the works the furnace door opened and the glow came up red on the smoke, and shut again.

"What happens now?" said Waylon. "To this."

Emeric looked at the town.

Seven hundred miles north, by a chancery lamp, the best hand in the realm was copying the parishes of Grayrock into an instrument by name; and after Grayrock, Milford, and then Wayford, and then a seal, and riders, and a herald who would rather be anywhere else reading it out at a market cross while somebody cut a copy down to carry south. North of the chancery, on a good road or not on any road, a man with his mother in his closed right hand was going to a house past the channel to tell somebody what he had felt on a riverbank. And in the knights' quarters, Ward had got the levy's rolls out of their chest at seven that evening and had them on the table under the lamp, and had said nothing to anybody, and was still sitting there with a pen. Emeric could feel him from here.

"They're drawing it up," he said. "In the chancery. It'll be read in the market square."

"And then?"

"And then I have to decide something."

Waylon did not ask what. He looked at the town a while longer, and at the trains going round.

"You'll decide it," he said. "You always did. On the floor. You'd sit there with the pieces and decide what it was going to be, and then it was that." He pushed himself off the parapet. "I'd only hold them."

"You held them badly."

"I was three."

"You were very bad at it."

"I've been practising," said Waylon. He went to the hatch. "I'm going down. She'll wake and I'm not there, she'll have the door off." He stopped with one foot on the ladder, and looked back, at the town, once more, and at his brother. "He brushed a horse," he said. "Today. Did you see?"

"I saw."

"Badly," said Waylon, and went down.

Emeric stayed on the roof. He felt his brother's note go down the stair and across the ward to the stable yard, and up the loft ladder, and settle in the straw over the grey's box beside the two warm notes below it. In the guest rooms his father's note was awake at a window, as it had been all evening. After a while Emeric felt it go from the window to the bed, which it had not done at this hour once in six weeks; and lie down; and, some time after, sleep.

He went down himself at one. Ferra was awake with her book, sitting up against the bedhead with the lamp low and Imani asleep across the foot of the bed with one arm flung out over the edge as if she had been in the middle of telling somebody something; and Ferra moved her knees over to make room for him without looking up.

"He went back to the loft," she said.

"He said he would."

"I've written him down for a bed in the guest rooms anyway. The one next to your father's." She turned a page. "He'll not sleep in it. I've written that down too." She looked at the page for a while. "Ash," she said. "You named a horse after the two of you, in a letter, and crossed it out, and wrote it again."

"You read it."

"I read everything that goes out of the study too." She shut the book. "It was a good name. I'd have told you, if you'd asked." She put the lamp out. In the dark she found his hand on the sheet without looking for it and held it, the way she held her pencil, firmly, as if it had something to write. "You've a brother," she said. "In the stable."

"I've a brother in the stable."

"Go to sleep, Emeric."

He did not, for a long time. But he lay there, and it was not the worst way he had ever lain awake.
