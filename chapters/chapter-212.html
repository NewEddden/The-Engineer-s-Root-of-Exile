Chapter 212 - Gift or Leash

Armand gave them a season, if nothing was done.

He said it in the dispensary off the end room on the second morning, with the door shut, sitting on Imani's stool with both hands folded on the head of his stick and his eyes on the floor, because he had spent the night going from bed to bed in the end room with his eyes shut and his hand held an inch above each blanket, and he was an old man, and it had cost him. Emeric felt what it had cost him. Armand's note was the widest he knew, wide as a room and deep as a well, and in the middle of it, where his heart was, the old catch was catching more than it had in the summer.

"They fill at night," said Armand. "All of them. A body does. It's what a body is for." He looked at the floor. "But not as far as they did the night before. A little less, every night. The way a pond fills after a dry summer: the rain comes and the water rises, but the bed's cracked, and it never quite reaches the old line." He was quiet. "Twenty years of the same drawing, every morning. They've learned to be empty. The body's forgotten where full is." He looked up at Imani. "A season. Perhaps two, for the young ones. Then the line goes under the floor, and they don't wake."

Imani stood by the dispensary bench with her arms folded.

"And if something is done," she said.

"Then you feed them." Armand lifted one hand off his stick. "Slowly. A little, every day, less than a body makes on its own. What Emeric's town has been doing to itself for seven years by living beside its own stones; what I've been measuring on the band wearers. Slow enough that the body takes it as its own, and doesn't fight it." He put his hand back. "It won't grow them. They're past growing. But it may hold the line where it is."

"Feed them with what?"

Armand looked at her, and did not answer, because she knew.

"No," said Imani.

"Imani."

"No." She did not raise her voice. "You didn't see her. Hild. On the first day. A light stone in a cage, on a wall, twenty feet off, and she screamed until I'd carried every one out of the hall with my hands." She looked at the shut door of the end room. "I'll not put a stone in her hand. Not in any of their hands. Not in their beds, not under their pillows, not in a box at their feet where they can't see it and I can tell myself it isn't there." She looked at Armand. "I'll not lie to them. Everybody who ever had them lied to them. The cart was a season. The chair was the rag. It's the one thing in that room I can give them that nobody else has. I'll not take it back to keep them alive."

Armand looked at her a long breath, with his hands on his stick.

"Then they'll die honestly," he said. "In a season." He did not say it unkindly. "I'm not telling you you're wrong. I'm telling you the arithmetic."

"I know the arithmetic," said Imani.

Emeric, in the doorway, said nothing. He had nothing to give either of them. He had thought about it all night and had come to the same place Armand had and the same place Imani had and could not stand in both, and he did not have a third.



It was the night nurse who found the third, and she did not know she had.

She was a Grayrock girl named in Imani's book as the second of the night, a big quiet collier's daughter who had come down to the hospital at fourteen and stayed, and she did what night nurses do at three in the morning in a room full of people who cannot sleep: she made tea. There was a kettle on the end room's own hearth, and she used it for the first fortnight. Then the end room's chimney began to smoke when the wind came round to the east, as it did in September, and Imani had the fire let down until the sweep came, and the sweep was slow; and the night nurse boiled the end room's kettle on the dispensary hob instead, next door, through the wall, and carried the tea through.

The dispensary hob stood against the dispensary's back wall. And against the other side of that wall, in a tall iron cabinet with a padlock on it, Imani had locked every stone that had come out of the end room on the first day: the light stones from their cages, the warmers from the beds, the beads from the nurses' wrists, the fire stone from under the hearth. Forty-odd stones, all charged, all Kaldo's, all locked in an iron box a foot from the back of the hob, where nobody in the end room would ever see them.

Armand noticed on the ninth day of the smoking chimney.

He had been taking his readings every night, the hand an inch above the blanket, going down the beds one after another, and writing them in his book by a shaded candle in a hand that had got smaller and shakier every year since the tower; and on the ninth night he sat back on Imani's stool at the end of the row with his book on his knee, and turned back through the pages, and turned forward again, and frowned at it for a long while, and sent the night nurse for Imani.

The line had stopped going down.

Not in all of them. Not by much. But for nine nights, in twelve of the fifteen, the night's reading had not been less than the night before; and in four of them, Tansy and Robin and Perrin and Jem, the four youngest, it had been, by a hair, more. Armand showed Imani the columns. Imani looked at them by the candle with her arms folded and asked what had changed. Armand did not know. Nothing had changed. The same beds, the same broth, the same fires, except the end room's fire, which was out. The same tea.

"Not the same tea," said the night nurse, from the door.

They both looked at her. She went red to the hair, and told them.



Emeric had them do it three times, because once was an accident and twice was a coincidence.

Three kettles, on three hobs, in the dispensary, filled from the same jug of water from the same pump in the yard and brought to the boil at the same time. One on the hob against the cabinet. One on a hob Bram brought over from the works on a barrow and set up at the far end of the dispensary, thirty feet from any stone in the building. And one on a hob beside which, on a stool, a foot from the kettle's flank, Emeric set a single Kaldo stone the size of a hen's egg, charged that morning by Orrin, with nothing else near it.

Then he poured a cup from each and put his hand flat on the cups, one after another, and let his sense down into them.

The first had something in it. Faint. So faint that if he had not been looking for it he would never have noticed it was there, any more than you notice the warmth in a cup that has been standing a while: a thread of mana, untold, shapeless, held in the water the way the water held the heat. The third had more. The second, from the far end of the room, had nothing at all.

He stood in the dispensary with his hand on the third cup and thought about ink.

Kaldo black. The pot of ink he had boiled in March at the mill's ink bench, half asleep, on the size plate, over a fire stone that pushed a set heat into whatever sat on it; with its pinch of drained stone dust in it, as every pot of Kaldo ink had, and the dust had sat on the charged stone at a boil and drunk from it, and come out black, and carried a pulse while it was wet. It had broken the Staple. He had thought it was the dust, and it was, mostly; the dust drank the way dry earth drinks rain. But it was the boiling too. Water at a boil, close to a charged stone, took up a trace, the faintest trace, and held it as it held the heat, and did not let it go while it was water.

He had had it in his hand since March and had not thought to put it in a kettle.

"How much?" said Imani, behind him.

He told her. He had to work it out to tell her: a cup from the hob by the cabinet had in it, he thought, about as much as a person of ordinary capacity made in an hour of an ordinary day. A cup from the hob with one stone beside it, perhaps a little more. Less than a body made. Much less than the chair took in a minute.

"Slow," said Armand, from the stool. He had his eyes shut, and his hand flat on the table beside the cups; he had been feeling them too. "Slower than a body makes it. In water. In tea." He opened his eyes. "In tea, Imani."

Imani looked at the three cups on the table for a long breath.

Then she picked up the third one, and drank it, all of it, standing in the dispensary, the way she tasted every new thing that went into her ward before it went into anybody else; and put the cup down, and wiped her mouth with the back of her wrist.

"Tastes of tea," she said.

Armand had not moved from the stool. He had his hand flat on the table still, beside the empty cup, and his eyes were open now, and he was looking at nothing, the way he looked when a column in his head would not come right.

"The upper town," he said.

They looked at him.

"Every year since I began the readings," said Armand slowly, "the upper town has read a shade higher than the lower. Every sheet. From the first. Not much. A shade. I wrote it down every year and put it down to meat; the upper town eats better." He turned his head and looked at Emeric. "What does the upper town drink?"

Emeric stood very still.

The tower on the hill. Bram's grey cement tower, from the fourth year, with Imani's sand bed on its top floor; and under the sand bed a copper pan the size of a cart bed, where the water boiled before it ran down into the tank, over eight fire stones in an iron grate, Orrin's, charged by Orrin every week. Boiled beside charged stones, every gallon of it, every day, for four years, and down the pipe to every standpipe in the upper town and every kettle beside every standpipe.

"Four years," said Armand. He took his hand off the table. "We've been doing it for four years. To the upper town. And none of us knew." He was quiet. "Not them. Not us."



The kettle went on the dispensary hob every night after that, against the cabinet, and nowhere else. Imani told them what it was.

She told all fifteen, herself, bed by bed, the first morning: that the tea came from a kettle that was boiled near the stones locked in the next room, and that a little of what was in the stones went into the water, as it went into Kaldo's ink, and that it would go into them a little at a time when they drank it, much less than the chair took and much slower, so slowly that they would never feel it go in; and that she wanted them to drink it, because Master Armand had measured them and they were going down, a little every night, and the tea had stopped it in most of them. And that if they did not want to, they need not, and she would bring them water from the far hob instead and not think the worse of them.

Perrin listened to all of it, propped on his elbow, with his white hair flat on one side from the pillow.

"Will I look like me again?" he said.

"No," said Imani.

He thought about that.

"The other lot said something else," said Perrin. "The keepers. On the straw. They said we'd be right as rain by harvest, once the season was done, and go home." He looked at the cup on the stool by his bed. "I liked you saying no better." He picked the cup up and drank it.

Fourteen of them took the tea that week. Hild did not. She listened to Imani with Tansy asleep against her, and looked at the cup, and turned her face to the wall; and Imani brought her water from the far hob, every night, in a cup with a blue band round it so that the night nurse would never mix them, and said nothing about it to anybody. Tansy drank hers. Hild watched her drink it, every night, with her hand on the girl's hair, and did not stop her.

In the third week Hild held out her hand for the cup without the blue band. She did not say anything. Imani gave it to her. After that she drank the tea like the rest.

The line held. Armand's book showed it, night after night, in his small shaky columns: the readings flat, most of them, from one night to the next, where they had gone down; in the four youngest, rising by a hair, and then holding. Nothing came back. Perrin's hair stayed white. Hew did not remember his wife's face. Senna asked whether the lamp would go out soon, in the middle of the day, every day. Wat lay by the window and did not speak. But they stopped going down; and Armand closed his book one night in the third week of September and sat with his hands folded on it, and said that the season he had given them was perhaps a year now, for most, and perhaps a good many years for the young ones, and perhaps more, and that he did not know, because nobody had ever done this before, and that he would like very much to live long enough to find out.



He told Sela on the first Saturday in October, in the school room, after the children had gone.

She was at her table under the window with a stack of slates, wiping them one at a time with a wet rag and setting them in a row to dry, as she had every Saturday for seven years; and she looked up when he came in at the door, and saw his face, and put the rag down. She had seen that face before. He had worn it into her shop on a summer morning in the first year, and she had put down a bar of soap, square with the others.

He told her plainly, in a voice the empty room could hear, because she would want to know it was true and that nobody had softened it. He had promised himself on the ship home from Wayford that he would tell it to her in the words Garrin had used, and he did. The docks, and Cobb's coppers. The gate, and the door, and the name through the door. The boy going down to the docks that evening, not to run, not to sell anybody: to tell Cobb he was done. Cobb's word. The mill road, going home. Once, from behind. And the name on the roster for the gate change, beside Tam's, of a man who did not exist, so that he walked it alone.

Sela listened without moving, with her wet hands flat on the table on either side of the slates. When he had finished she did not say anything for some time. Outside the window, down in the street, Ward's levy was coming back from the butts in a column of twos, forty of them, in the department's coats, with Rylan calling the step; and at the head of the second file, tall, with his collar turned up against the wind, her youngest. She watched him go by. Emeric watched her watch him.

"Is he alive," said Sela. "The man."

"Yes. In the strongroom under the keep."

"Will you hang him?"

"He's asked me to." Emeric looked at the slates. "He told me he'd not hang in Wayford beside the five. He said if he hangs, he hangs for the boy. In Kaldo. By Kaldo's law. For what he did." He was quiet. "I think he thinks it's owed. I think it's the only thing he has left that he thinks is his."

Sela looked out of the window at the empty street, where the levy had gone round the corner.

"No," she said.

He waited.

"He doesn't hang for my boy." She picked up the wet rag and folded it, once, and put it down. "He doesn't get to. He doesn't get to stand on a box in this town with Tam's name in his mouth and go off the end of a rope like a man who's settled his account, and have everybody say afterward that at least he hanged for it." Her hands were quite steady. "He doesn't get Tam. Not even that. Not even the end of him."

She looked at Emeric across the table.

"Make him live a long time," said Sela.

Emeric looked at her for a long breath.

"Yes," he said.

She nodded, once, as if he had passed something, and picked up the next slate and began to wipe it. He stood in the doorway. After a while, without looking up, she said:

"Say his name."

"Tam."

"Again."

"Tam."

Sela wiped the slate, and set it in the row with the others to dry, and took the next.

"You know it now," she said.



Armand was in his tower room, which was not a tower room, at the top of the keep's west stair, where he had his books and his instruments and a bed he slept in less than he should. Emeric went up on an evening in the first week of October, because there was a thing he had not asked anybody and Armand was the only person in the south who could answer it.

He told it from the beginning. The strongroom and Marrow, the door forced, the blood from both of them and the kitchen with the geranium that had come away with him and would not leave. And then the cellar: the door opening on its own, without his leave, and going out along his own mana in every direction at once, to every note it touched, forty and more, and taking hold of all of them; and all of them afterward with the same pain in the same place, a nail behind the eyes, and he with it too, as if forty nails had gone into him. Vantic. Ward. The column in the yard with their heads in their hands.

"Was it the door?" he said. "Was that the backlash? Forty of it, at once, both sides, every one of them paying and me paying for all of them?" He was quiet. "Or was it only the anger. A surge that size. Would that do it, on its own?"

Armand sat in his chair by the cold hearth with his stick across his knees and his eyes shut, and did not answer, while the candle on the table burned down a finger's width.

"I don't know," he said at last.

Emeric waited.

"I don't know," said Armand again, "because nobody has ever done what you did and been able to tell me about it afterward. Forced entry, yes. I've read of it. The tower kept it out of the books for a reason: the man who forces a door pays, and the man behind it pays more, and sometimes there isn't a man behind it afterward, only a body that breathes." He opened his eyes. "One door. One man forcing it. That's in the old books. Forty doors at once, opened by nobody, by a door that was meant to be shut and came open of its own accord?" He shook his head slowly. "That's not in any book I've read. I'd say it was impossible, if you weren't sitting in my chair telling me about it with a face like that."

"Then which?"

"Both, I'd guess. If you want a guess." Armand looked at the cold hearth. "A surge that size hurts. Everybody who's ever been near a mage losing hold of himself says so. Teeth ache, eyes ache. That's old." He was quiet. "But a surge doesn't give forty men the same pain in the same place at the same moment as the man it comes from. A surge is weather. It hurts everybody differently, the way a storm does." He looked at Emeric. "What you describe isn't weather. It's a bill. Forty people paying the same price for the same thing, and you paying it forty times. That's what a forced door costs. I'd say the door opened on them, and they paid for it, and so did you."

Emeric did not say anything.

"That isn't the part that frightens me," said Armand.

"What is?"

"That it opened on its own." Armand put both his hands on his stick. "Your door. Your room. The rules you wrote in the back of my notebook in your first autumn: contact, consent, nothing forced. You've kept them for eight years better than any man I ever taught. And when you were angry enough, the door didn't care what rules you'd written. It opened. It went out. It took hold." He looked at Emeric. "That means it can. Without you. If you're angry enough again, or frightened enough, or grieving." He was quiet. "And next time it might not be your own people in the yard. It might be a town."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, in the keep, somebody was laughing in the kitchen.

"So I learn to keep it shut," said Emeric.

"So you learn to keep it shut," said Armand. "Not by holding it. You can't hold a thing like that by strength; you've tried; it went past your teeth. You learn to know the moment before it opens. The way a man learns to know the moment before he loses his temper, and walks out of the room." He shut his eyes again. "That takes years, for a temper. I don't know how long it takes for this. Start now."



The argument with Imani came in the second week of October, over the letters.

The priests' answers had come in from the barony by then, every parish, and from Hollin's holdings and Dray's and the rest, and Ferra had the Served column matched against them to the last name she could find: a hundred and thirty-one dead within five years of coming home, and the dates, and the causes in forty different priests' hands, the cough, the wasting, the decline. Sixty-two in the Losses, by name, with the date and the holding. And for every name a family, somewhere, that had been told for twenty years that it was the rag.

He wanted to print it. He said so at the long table, on the second Monday in October, with Ferra's sheets in front of him. Every name, every village, every date, on one sheet, from Ambry's press in Wayford's new type, posted on every church door in the north of the barony and every door where the notice had gone. The truth, in one place, where anybody could read it, so that no family would ever again have to wonder whether their man had died of the rag. They had a right to it. Nobody had ever given them the truth about anything. He would give them this.

Imani heard him out. She was at the far end of the table, between Armand and Doran, with her elbows on it and her chin on her fists, and she let him say all of it, which was a bad sign; and then she said no.

"They've a right to it," she said. "They've not an obligation to read it off a church door between a notice for a cow sale and the date of the fair." She sat back. "You'd have a woman go to the church on a Sunday and stand in front of a sheet with three hundred names on it, with her neighbours behind her, reading over her shoulder, and find her husband's name halfway down in Ambry's new type. And find out, there, on the step, with the whole parish watching her face, that it wasn't the rag. That it was a chair. That he came home to her for two years knowing what had been done to him and coughed and said it was the rag, because he'd been told to." She was quiet. "And then she goes home. On her own. With that."

"They have a right..."

"I said they've a right. I'm not saying hide it. I'm saying how." She put her hand flat on Ferra's sheets. "This isn't a notice. It's a hundred and ninety-three deaths. Every one of them gets told at the door. By a person. Sitting down, in the kitchen, with the door shut. Somebody who knows how to tell a woman her husband's dead, and how to stay after." She looked down the table at Doran. "The priests know how. Most of them. The ones that don't, Doran can teach, or I'll send a nurse." She looked back at Emeric. "And the family asks what it wants to know. All of it, or the name and the date and nothing else, or the cellar, or not the cellar. Whatever they ask, they get, true. What they don't ask for, they don't get handed on a step." She was quiet. "And the list's in Ferra's book, the whole of it, and anybody can come and read it, any day, if they want to. Want to. Not have it put in front of them."

"That's a year's work," said Emeric. "A hundred and ninety-three kitchens. All over the north."

"Yes," said Imani.

"Ambry could print it in a day."

"Yes," said Imani. "That's what's wrong with it."

He looked at her down the long table. He opened his mouth to say the next thing, which was about truth, and about what the five had done with secrets, and about a town that had been lied to for twenty years; and he heard, before he said it, how it would sound, and to whom. He had read Aster's letters for four years. He had read every line. He had never once gone to Wayford and stood in the square and looked at who was in it.

You might ask one of them how it's done, Ottoline had said. You've got two.

He shut his mouth.

"Doran," he said, after a while. "How many priests in the north could do it? Sit in a kitchen."

Doran, who had kept Kaldo's death book for thirty-four years and had sat in a great many kitchens, considered the question with the seriousness it deserved.

"Half," he said. "The other half I'd not send to tell a woman her cow had died." He folded his hands. "I'll write to the half. And I'll go myself to the ones nearest, if Her Grace of the ward will lend me a nurse to drive the cart."

"Not a cart," said Imani. "A trap. Open." She looked at Emeric. "Well?"

"You're right," he said.

"I know," said Imani, and took her chin off her fists and pulled Ferra's sheets toward her, and began dividing them by parish.



He found her at the hospital's side door at dusk.

It was the door she used when she wanted ten minutes and could not have them anywhere else: a stone step at the end of the east range, facing the river, with a boot scraper and a rain barrel and nothing else, where the nurses went to cry when they needed to and Imani went to stand. She was standing on it now, in her ward dress with her sleeves down for once and her shawl round her, with her arms folded, looking at the river going grey. The lamplighter was coming along the river street below with his pole and his ladder, one lamp at a time, the way he did every night.

Emeric came out of the door behind her and stood beside her on the step.

"You were right," he said.

"I know." She did not look at him. "You said."

"I'm saying it again. Out here. Where there's nobody to hear it but the rain barrel." He looked at the river. "I'd have put it on a church door. I'd have thought I was being honest. I'd have been reading a ledger to a town, out loud, and calling it the truth."

Imani was quiet.

"It is the truth," she said at last. "That's the trouble with you. You never say anything that isn't. You just say it to the wrong people at the wrong time in the wrong voice, and then you're surprised when they cry." She unfolded her arms. "I'll teach you. It takes about twenty years. I'll charge you for it."

And she leaned her head sideways onto his shoulder.

The lamplighter came along the street below them. He set his ladder against the first lamp, and climbed, and lit it, and came down, and moved his ladder to the next. They watched him do it. Imani's head was warm and heavy on his shoulder, and her curls were coming out of their pins against his neck, and she did not say anything, and neither did he; and when the lamplighter had lit the lamp below the step, and the last one at the end of the street by the bridge, and shouldered his ladder, and gone round the corner out of sight, she lifted her head off his shoulder exactly then, not a breath sooner or later, and went back in.



The geranium went first.

He noticed it in the second week of October, sitting at the long table one morning with a cup of tea, that he could not see it any more: the red flower in its pot on a kitchen sill that had come up behind his eyes every day since the strongroom, at odd times, at the table, on the stair, in the square under the beam with a man dead on a rope in front of him. It had gone. He went looking for it, carefully, the way you put your tongue to the place a tooth was, and found the kitchen still there, and the window, and the sill; and on the sill an empty pot.

The bread went next, a few days after. The smell of it. Then the kitchen itself, the shape of the room, which way the window faced. He did not notice those go. He only noticed, one evening, that he could not remember which side the door had been on.

The woman's voice was the last.



Wat died in the eighth week, on the night of the fourteenth of October, between the third bell and the fourth.

Emeric was there because he had been there every night that week. He had not meant to be. He had come down to the end room on the first night of the week because Imani had said, at supper, in her ward voice, that Wat's readings were not going down and not going up, that the tea had held him as it had held the others, and that he was going anyway; and that she did not know why, and Armand did not know why, and that she thought it would not be long. And Emeric had gone down after supper and sat on the stool by Wat's bed by the window, and Wat had looked at him once, and then back at the window, and Emeric had stayed. He came back the next night, and the next.

He did not talk. Wat did not talk. They sat, the one in the bed and the other on the stool, with the river going by below the window in the dark and the tallow candle on the sill, and the other fourteen asleep down the room behind them, breathing, and the night nurse going softly up and down with her kettle.

On the last night, toward the third bell, Wat's hands began to move.

They had lain on the blanket all week, still, the way they had lain on the bench in the square. Now they moved. Slowly, both of them, on top of the blanket, in the candlelight: the right hand reaching out and gathering something, and bending it down, and the left hand coming over to hold it while the right reached again. Over and over. Reaching, bending, laying down, holding. Emeric watched them a long time before he understood.

The night nurse had stopped at the foot of the bed with her kettle, and was watching too.

"He's laying a hedge," she whispered. "My grandda did it. Up home." She watched the hands. "You cut the stem most of the way through, low down, and bend it over, and lay it along, and weave the next one in. So it grows sideways, thick, and the beasts can't get through." She was quiet. "He's laying a hedge."

Best hedger on the hill, the old man had called in the square. Old Wat, at the Ridings. Wat's hands went on laying it on the blanket, stem after stem, carefully, a good hedge, close and even; and Wat's face on the pillow was turned to the window and to the dark river and did not change at all.

And somewhere in the middle of it, sitting on the stool by the bed with his hands between his knees, watching a man lay a hedge on a blanket, Emeric heard the woman's voice for the last time.

It came up out of nowhere, the way it always had. A woman in another room, at dusk, calling a child's name, twice, rising at the end. Marrow's mother, perhaps, or his wife, or nobody; a kitchen he had never stood in, in a town he had never been to, in a life he had torn a piece out of with his own hands in a strongroom full of ash because he had wanted an answer and had not been willing to wait for it. She called the name. He listened for it to come again, the way it always came, twice.

It did not come again.

He sat very still. He went looking for it, carefully, the way he had gone looking for the geranium; and the kitchen was gone, and the window, and the voice, and there was nothing behind his eyes at all but his own room, his own long quiet room with its light from nowhere, empty, and the door of it shut. Shut, and his.

He had broken his rule once. Contact, consent, nothing forced. Once, in his whole life, in a strongroom, on a man who had been paid forty Gold to have him taken. And it had cost him eight weeks of a stranger's kitchen and a woman calling a child he would never know, and it had cost Marrow more, and he did not know yet how much. He sat by Wat's bed and knew, without deciding it, the way he knew the turn in Ward's yard, that he would never do it again. Not to an enemy. Not to save a life. Not for any answer in the world.

When he looked up, Wat's hands had stopped.

They lay on the blanket, one over the other, the left holding the last stem down where the right had laid it. Wat was looking at the window. He was breathing; slowly, and then more slowly, and the night nurse put down her kettle very quietly on the floor and went to fetch Imani without being told, and Emeric sat on his stool and watched Wat breathe, and counted it, as Imani counted a wrist, until there was nothing to count.

Imani came, with her shawl over her nightgown and her hair down, and stood at the foot of the bed, and looked, and did not need to touch him to know. She touched him anyway. She laid two fingers on the side of his neck and waited, and took them away, and drew the blanket up, not over his face, only to his chin, and smoothed it, over the hands.

"No cause," she said, very low. "His readings didn't change. Armand's book will say they didn't change." She looked at Wat's face. "He'd just had enough."

They sat with him till it was light, the two of them, one on the stool and the other on the edge of the next bed, while the fourteen slept on down the room. None of them woke. When the light came up over the river Imani got up and went to tell them, one at a time, as they woke, sitting on the edge of each bed, in her ward voice, with the door shut.



Doran wrote him in the book that afternoon.

The parish death book of Kaldo, the third volume, with Doran's own entries running back through the plague and before it, in the same small upright hand for thirty-four years. He wrote the name, and the date, and the place, the Ridings, past Coldwell, in the barony of Wayford; and where the book had a column for the cause, he wrote nothing, and drew a line. And in the column for the age, after he had looked at Emeric across the vestry table for a breath, he wrote: thirty-one.

"He looked seventy," said Doran, blotting it. "I'll not write what he looked."

They buried him the next day in the churchyard, in the new ground at the top of the slope, looking down over the river. It rained. Most of the end room came, those who could walk, in coats from the town's baskets, with Imani's nurses holding umbrellas over them; and Perrin, who could not walk that far, came on a litter, because he had told Imani that he would, and she had stopped arguing with Perrin in August. Robin held his doll under his coat to keep it dry. Hild came with Tansy by the hand, and stood at the back, and did not look at anything but the grave.

Somebody from the Ridings came, too. An old man in a wet hat, who had come down from Wayford on the ship's regular run because Aster had found him and put him on it, and stood at the edge of the grave with his hat in his hands and did not speak to anybody, and when Doran had finished, bent down and put a handful of something on the coffin lid, out of his coat pocket, before the earth went on. Emeric saw what it was. Hawthorn, a bundle of green whips cut that morning, the kind a hedger lays.



Doran came up to him afterward, at the lychgate, in the rain, with his book under his cassock to keep it dry.

"The stones," he said. "From that cellar. Ferra says they're in your strongroom, in a box, in trust." He looked at Emeric from under his wet hood. "In trust for whom?"

"I don't know."

"No." Doran looked back up the slope at the new grave, and the old man in the wet hat still standing beside it, alone now, with the rain running off his brim. "You told me once that a stone keeps whatever's put in it. That it doesn't leak, or wear, or fade. That a stone filled today will hold it a thousand years." He was quiet. "Two hundred and eighty-seven stones, and nobody knows whose is whose. Sixty-two people in the lime at Wayford with no grave. A hundred and thirty-one in forty churchyards with the wrong thing written against them." He looked at Emeric. "And the stones are all of them have left that anybody could hold."

Emeric did not say anything.

"Let me bury them," said Doran. "Here. Up there, by him. In the ground, with the words said over them, and a stone on top with no names on it, because we don't know the names, and a line under it, the way I drew it in the book." He was quiet. "Nobody draws from them. Nobody sells them. Nobody ever puts a hand on them again to take anything. That's what you said, in Wayford. Ferra wrote it down." He held his book tighter under his cassock. "That's what a grave's for."

Emeric stood at the lychgate in the rain for a long breath.

"Yes," he said.



Ferra came to the long table that night with Armand's book and her own, and the dispensary's kettle accounts, and sat down across from him, and asked the question he had been not asking since the third cup.

She had been doing sums. She had Armand's columns for the fifteen, and the dispensary's record of the tea, how many cups, how many nights; and she had laid them beside Armand's other book, the one with the barony's readings in it, the ones he took every year from everybody who consented: the upper town and the lower, side by side, every year since the first sheet, the upper a shade higher every time. And beside those, Bram's figures for the tower on the hill and the second tower by the baths, and the gallons a day that went down the pipes to every street.

"The tower on the hill's been doing it for four years," said Ferra. "To the upper town. Nobody asked them. Nobody knew to." She did not look up from the sums. "The second tower doesn't boil. It could. A copper pan and eight of Orrin's stones. A month's work for Bram." She turned a page. "Then the lower town. Then Grayrock's tank, and Milford's. Every kettle in four baronies. Every child in them growing a little, every year, slowly, without the rite, without the Circle, without anybody's leave." She put her pencil down. "Without knowing. That's what you've been thinking since the dispensary. I watched you think it."

He did not deny it.

"A whole barony," said Ferra. "Rising." She looked at him then. "Is it a gift or a leash?"

He opened his mouth, and shut it.

"Whoever puts the stones under the pan," said Ferra, "can take them out. Or put bigger ones in. Or smaller. Or ones that aren't Kaldo's, filled by somebody who isn't Orrin, with something in them nobody's thought of yet." She was quiet. "Does a town that's grown on a dose need the dose? Armand doesn't know. Nobody's ever taken it away from anybody to see." She looked at him. "If it does, then a town that's grown on your water is a town that needs your water. Every kettle. That's a leash. Even if it's a gift. Even if the man holding it is you." She was quiet. "And you'll not always be the man holding it."

He looked at the columns on the table between them: Armand's small shaky figures, the fifteen, held; the upper town and the lower, a shade apart, four years running; Bram's gallons a day. Armand had looked at the same columns in the dispensary in September, on the morning of the three kettles, and had said only one thing more before he went up to bed that night, with his hand on Emeric's arm on the stair: that the proof of it, written down anywhere outside that room, was a manual for the next cellar.

"I don't know," he said.

Ferra looked at him a while longer. Then she picked up her pencil and wrote it down, in her book, under the day's date, in her ordinary hand: E. asked, gift or leash. Said he doesn't know.

"Good," she said. "Neither do I." She closed the book. "Don't do anything about it till we do."
