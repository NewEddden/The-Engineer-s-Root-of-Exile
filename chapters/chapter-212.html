Chapter 212 - Oil First

The pools smelled before they could be seen.

Emeric had smelled them on the first night, three months back, with Osric and Ward, and had thought then that it was only strangeness: the stink of a place nobody had ever lived in, ground that had been rotting quietly under the sand since before there were kings. On the first working night, coming down on Lighten over the island's western shore with four knights strung out in the dark above and behind him, he smelled it from three hundred feet, through the slit of his helm, over the cold, and knew it was going to be the hardest part of the whole thing to hide.

It was not like lamp oil. Lamp oil off the Emberfell boats smelled of smoke and a little of fish and was, after a year in Kaldo, the smell of an evening. This was rotten eggs and hot iron and something under both that had no name he could give it here, sweet and wrong, and it got into the back of the throat and stayed there.

They came down on the sand between the pools and the sea. Five of them: Ward, Osric, Declan, Rylan and himself, which was every flying suit Kaldo had, and that was the first cost of the thing, before anybody had lifted a ladle. If a suit failed on the island there was no sixth. If a man went down on the island there was nobody to come for him but the four who were already there.

Osric had brought the ladles. They were Bram's, long-handled, iron, with a lip like a jug's, and they rode in a canvas roll on Osric's back under his furled cape with a stack of folded oilcloth and two short spades. Declan and Rylan each carried a cask on a sling, lashed empty against their chests: small casks, eight gallons, the size a man in armour could hold against himself and still fly. Ward carried nothing but a sword, which he did not expect to need, and did not leave behind.

The pools lay in a hollow behind the dunes, a dozen of them, black, still, scattered like spilled ink across ground that had gone dark and greasy for a hundred yards round. Most were no bigger than a cart. Beyond them, where the hollow opened toward the island's middle, lay the great one.

He had seen its edge on the first night and cut a slab off it. He had not understood then how big it was. It went on in the dark further than the sense could easily reach, flat, black, faintly shining where the starlight came down on it, with a crust on top you could walk on if you walked quickly and a softness under the crust that took a boot and did not want to give it back. A lake. Not of water. Of pitch, laid down over more years than he wanted to count, a skin of it over a bowl in the ground that went down a long way. Along its northern edge the seeps ran thicker and slower than here, and the black came up in them stiff and heavy, like cold treacle, and set on the air.

"That's the one," said Osric, low. "That's what we want."

"Not tonight," Emeric said. "Not like that."

He had said so at the table. The lake was the most valuable thing on the island after the glass stone, and nobody would ever miss a cask of it, and a hundred men with spades could have cut it for a year and made no more mark on it than a child with a spoon. But five men could not cut it quietly, and a cut in it stayed. A pool bailed down would fill again from underneath within the month. A block taken out of pitch was a square hole with square sides, the only square thing on the whole island, and it would still be there in daylight and next year and in ten, for anybody who walked this way with a lantern and wondered who on earth had wanted pitch badly enough to row out to Ashendeep for it. So: the light oil. The small pools. And off the lake, nothing. Not yet.

They set the casks in the sand, and knocked the bungs out, and began.

It was slow. He had known it would be slow and had not known how slow.

The oil in the pools lay on water. It lay in a skin on top, thin in some pools and a hand deep in others, and under it was a brackish seep that came up from somewhere below with the oil and was no use to anybody. A man could not simply dip a ladle in. He had to lay it on the surface, tip it a finger's width, and let the black run in over the lip and nothing under it, and then tip it into the funnel and hold it there while it went down, which it did at its own speed. Then again. Then again. Rylan found the knack first, the way Rylan found the knack of most things, with his mouth shut and his shoulders down. Declan swore at it softly and steadily for an hour and then stopped swearing and was quick.

Emeric worked beside them. He had thought about whether he should, on the ship, and had decided that he would not stand on a dune with a sword like a man watching other men work, and Ward had said nothing to that, which meant Ward agreed and did not like it. So he knelt at the edge of the second pool in his thin plate with a ladle, and the smell came up off the black into his helm with every tip, and within a quarter of an hour it was in his hair and his mouth and the padding under the plate, and he stopped noticing it, which was worse. That was how it would go ashore. Not on the casks. On the men. You stopped smelling a thing yourself long before anybody else did.

The first cask took them two hours. The second took an hour and a half. By the fourth, the pool nearest the dunes was a black crust over wet sand, and Rylan went to the next one without being told.

Ward did not ladle. He walked. He walked the lip of the hollow all night, slowly, round and round it, with his cape furled over the plate and the hood up so that in the starlight he was a darker lump against a dark dune, and every so often he stopped and stood with his head turned toward the island's middle, toward the ridge they could not see from here, and listened. Then he walked on.

At the second hour past midnight they left the pools and went to the flats.

That was a walk of a mile, northeast, along the back of the dunes, with four full casks slung between pairs of men on poles, because a full cask was too heavy for a suit to carry on its chest and fly. On Lighten the men could take most of the weight off themselves, but not off the cask, and the sand took every step and gave it back grudgingly. Emeric had written in his notebook a year ago that the whole difficulty of every covert thing in history was not the secrecy but the carrying. He had not known, writing it, that he meant this.

The flats came up white under the stars, crunching. The grey shrub stood on them in its scattered way, knee high, a plant every few yards, with nothing between. They cut it with sickles, low, a hand above the root, the way Imani had shown them on the one in her pot, which had put out six new shoots from its stump in the time since the first night and was now a small grey bush on the sill of the ward, and which she watered herself.

They cut in a line, because Emeric had said so, a row across the flat from one dune to the next, every plant in it, and left the flat on either side of the row alone. Next time, the next row. By the time they came back to the first it would have grown again, or it would not, and they would know which. Osric bound what they cut in bundles with cord, grey stems and silver leaves in bales the length of a man, and the leaves smelled faintly bitter, like crushed sage, which after the pools was almost pleasant.

They cut for an hour and a half. It was hot work in plate even on a desert night, and the sweat ran into the padding and went cold at once, and every man's hands were raw through the gauntlets' lining by the end of it, because the stems were woody and the sickles were new. When Emeric stopped and straightened and looked back along the row, it ran away behind them across the white flat for perhaps two hundred yards: a line of stumps, grey, low, very straight.

Too straight. He looked at it a long time. There was nothing on the whole of the flats that ran in a line, no path, no wall, no track; the shrub grew where the wind had dropped it. From the ground, at night, a row of stumps was invisible. From the ridge in daylight, if anybody ever stood there and looked west with the sun behind him, it would be a line drawn with a ruler across a page where nothing else was ruled.

Nobody had any reason to look west. Kesrath's men wanted the glass. They had been working the ridge for years and had never, as far as he could find, walked the flats at all, because there was nothing on the flats a Kesrath man wanted.

That was what he told himself. He told the table, afterwards, the same thing, and it was true. He also made them, from the second night on, cut the rows along the lie of the ground, following the flat's own low swells and hollows, so that a row bent where the ground bent and did not look so much like a thing a man had done. It cost a quarter hour a night. He paid it.

On the third night they heard Kesrath.

It was not a tenth night. Emeric had not chosen it to be. They had been on the flats an hour, cutting, when Ward came in off his circuit and stood beside him without a word and put a hand on his shoulder, and Emeric stopped, and the others stopped when they saw him stop.

From the east, from the ridge, a mile off, faint and clear on the still air: iron on stone. A pick. Then another, not quite with it. Then a third. And under the picks, so faint that for a moment he thought he was making it up, a man's voice singing, a working song, the kind that went with a tool so that the tool came down on the beat, in the thick speech of the other shore, too far off to follow.

They stood on the flat with the sickles in their hands and listened.

He let the sense out toward it. It did not reach a mile, not to see anything by, but the medium over the island was not quite even, and toward the ridge, at the far edge of what he could feel, it was a little thicker than it should have been, and a little warmer, the way the air in a room was thicker when there were people in it. Life gave off the medium. A crowd read heavy. A mile off, on the ridge, there were men enough to lie on the sense like a hearth felt through a wall. Thirty, he thought. Perhaps forty. Cutting the glass stone out of the ridge a bench at a time, a splinter at a time, and singing to keep the picks together, and never once looking west, because there was nothing west but a flat nobody wanted and a stink of oil.

"They can't hear us," Rylan said. Not quite a question. His voice was barely a breath inside his helm.

"Not over the picks," said Ward. "Not at a mile." He did not move his hand from Emeric's shoulder. "We can hear them because they're making the noise."

Emeric thought about that. Two crews on one island in one dark, a mile apart, both stealing from two kings, both keeping their heads down, both paid for by somebody who kept a book. And only one of them knew the other was there. It was the whole of the advantage and it was the whole of the danger, and it would last exactly as long as Kaldo's crew was the quieter one.

"We go on," he said. "Quieter."

They went on. The sickles went into the stems slower and softer, and the bundling cord was drawn tight by hand instead of with a jerk, and nobody spoke at all. The singing on the ridge went on for another hour and stopped, and after it stopped the picks went on alone, and at some point in the fourth hour after midnight somebody up there shouted, long and angry, and somebody else shouted back, and every man on the flat went still and down on one knee in the dark with his hand on the ground, and waited. It was a quarrel. It went on for a while, too far off for words, and ended the way quarrels between tired men at the end of a shift ended, with a last word and a laugh from somebody who was not in it. The picks began again.

Declan let his breath out through his teeth, very slowly, and Emeric heard it across three yards of flat and thought, if I can hear that, and did not finish the thought.

The ship came down for them an hour before dawn, as she had on the first night.

That was the other cost, and the one Fletcher never stopped talking about. A hull full of casks and bales did not go up a line. It went up the winch, and the winch was slow, and the ship had to hang low over the flats for the whole of it, in the dark, where anybody who looked up would see her. They had a gravity stone in a sling for the loads, on Lighten, so that a cask weighed a third of itself on the way up and the winch could take it at a walking pace, and even so it was the best part of half an hour, cask and cask and bundle and bundle, with the knights on the ground hooking on and Ned on the winch in the belly counting under his breath and Fletcher at the levers holding her still against a breeze off the sea that came and went and would not settle.

Half an hour, low over a flat, with a crew of forty working a mile off. It was the worst half hour of every night, and they could not make it shorter without carrying less, and they could not carry less without making it not worth the coming.

On the third night the picks were still going when the last bale went up. Emeric went up after it on the line, last as he always was, and on the way up he looked east toward the ridge as he had looked east on the first night, and saw nothing: a darker line on the dark, a glow on it that might have been a shaded lamp or might have been nothing, and the sea beyond, and the Kesrath shore, and no boat.

The smell came home with them.

They had tried. Fletcher had rigged a hose in the ship's belly on a hand pump from a tank, and the knights stripped out of their plate in the band on the way home and washed as well as men could wash in cold water three miles up, which was not well. The padding went into a sack and the sack went into a locker and the locker never came off the ship. The plate was wiped with lye and wiped again. Nothing that had touched the pools went ashore but the casks, and the casks were tarred shut and went straight from the winch in Fletcher's yard to a shed at the works that had been a paint store and that nobody went into but Bram.

It was not enough. Ferra, who had been on the ship all night in the deckhouse with the log and had not gone near a pool, came down the gangway at dawn and said her hair smelled of it. Imani, when Emeric came into the ward that morning to see the shrub on her sill, stopped in the middle of what she was saying to a nurse and turned her head and looked at him.

"You've been in something," she said.

"I've been in the band."

"The band doesn't smell like a tannery that's caught fire." She came across the ward and stood close to him, which she never did, and breathed in once, and made a face. "It's in your skin. Scrub it with the grey soap, the strong one, and then scrub it again. And your hair. And don't go down the lane smelling like that, because every chandler on it will know it's oil and not ours."

That was it exactly. That was the thing he had known at three hundred feet on the first night. Kaldo bought its lamp oil off the lane, off Saban's chandlers, off the Emberfell boats, and every man on the lane knew what that smelled like. This was not that. A chandler who got a breath of it off a knight in a tavern would not know where it came from, but he would know it was not from the lane, and he would wonder aloud where else a Kaldo knight could have got into oil, and the wondering would go down the lane faster than any cask.

So there were rules, after that, and Ward made them, and they were Ward's kind of rules, which were short. No man who had been on the island went off the yard for a full day after. Everybody scrubbed at the yard's wash house, twice, with the strong soap, before he went home. Nobody went into a tavern for two days. The wives were told the knights had been working with tar on the ships, which was true, since they had.

Declan's wife said that was the worst tar she had ever smelled, and Declan said it was foreign tar, and that was true too.

It was Ferra who showed him the calendar, and she did not mean to.

It was the sixth week. They had been out seven nights. She had the log open on the deckhouse table on the way home from the seventh, in the band, in the cold, with the hooded lamp over it and the pencil on its string, entering the night: the date, the hour they left the yard, the hour they were over the island, the hour the ship came down, the hour she went up, the casks, the bales, the weather, and at the bottom, as she had on every night, Kesrath at the ridge, or Ridge dark, whichever it had been.

Emeric was sitting across from her with a blanket round his shoulders and his hair still wet from the hose, and he was not really reading it. He was watching her write. Then he was reading it, because of the way the dates stood in their column, one under another, all down the left side of the page.

He reached across and turned the log round, and she let him, and he turned back the pages to the first night and ran his finger down the column.

Seven nights. Every one of them in the dark of a moon. Two runs of three nights together and one of one, and each run on the same nights of its month as the one before, give or take, because the moon came round when it came round and they had gone when it was darkest. Every one of them leaving the yard at the same hour on the ebb behind the Marsh Hen, because that was when the tide served. Every one of them over the island a little past midnight, down an hour before dawn, home by the first light.

It was a very good log. Anybody with the log could have told exactly when the next three nights would be.

"What?" said Ferra.

"We have a calendar."

She looked at the page, upside down, and then turned it round again the right way and looked at it properly, and he watched her see it, the way he watched her see a figure that was wrong in somebody else's accounts. It did not take her long.

"We go in the dark of the moon," she said. "Of course we do."

"So does everybody who doesn't want to be seen. So does Kesrath's boat. It's the first thing anybody would guess." He put his finger on the column. "That's how I found them. Not the glass. Not the barrels. The board on the hut door with ten strokes in a row and a boat at the end of it. I looked at it and I knew there'd be a boat on the tenth morning, and there was." He looked at her. "If anybody ever sees us once, even once, even a man on a boat who doesn't know what he saw, and he writes down the night, and then somebody sees us again and writes that night down, then all anybody has to do is lay the two nights side by side the way you just did. And the third time, they'll be waiting."

Ferra was quiet. The ship hummed under them in the cold.

"The dark of the moon is the safest night," she said at last. "You'll be trading that."

"Some of it. Cloud's as good as dark, when there's cloud. A thin moon low in the west is nearly as good, if we're down before it sets." He had been thinking about it since the column, and it had come together while he talked, the way things did. "And not the tenth night either."

"Their tenth night's the best we'll ever get. Nobody on the ridge."

"Which is why we can't have it. If we only ever came when their crew had gone to the boat, then our calendar would be theirs, one night in ten, as regular as the board, and anybody who knew their board would know ours." He turned the pencil on its string between his fingers. "We found them because they were regular. We can't be. No two runs on the same nights of the month. No two gaps the same length. Not the same hour off the yard twice running, even if it means waiting for the next ebb. Some weeks two nights, some weeks none. Never three in a row again."

"That will cost," Ferra said. "Cloud nights, a thin moon. The tide."

"Yes."

"And somebody has to choose the nights."

"You," he said. "Nobody else. You choose them, and you don't tell anybody until the afternoon. Not me. Not Fletcher. Not Ward. If nobody knows the night before, nobody can let it slip the night before."

She looked at him for a moment longer, and he could see her weighing it, the way she weighed a figure before she wrote it. Then she took the pencil back off him, and turned to a clean page at the back of the log, and ruled it, and at the top she wrote Nights, and nothing under it.

"I'll want an almanac," she said. "The moon for the year. And Fletcher's tides. And the weather off the lake, every morning, from whoever at the yard reads it best."

"You'll have them."

"And I'll keep this page in the box, not the log." She did not look up. "The log says what we did. This says what we're going to do. They don't go in the same book."

He did not argue. He sat back against the iron of the hull with the blanket round him and watched her, while the ship went home through the band in the dark, and she sat bent over the ruled page with the lamp's hood down low over it, not writing anything yet, only looking at the empty lines the way she looked at a sum that had not been done, as if she could already see what it would come to.

Under them, in the hold, four casks of foreign oil and eleven bales of a shrub that nobody in Randillia had ever seen rode home in the dark, tarred and roped, with the stink of the pools still on them. Behind them, on a flat nobody wanted, a row of stumps bent along the lie of the ground. And a mile east of it, on a ridge of glass, forty men who had worked the same night a mile off would go to their board in the morning and cross out a stroke, and never know that anybody else had been there.
