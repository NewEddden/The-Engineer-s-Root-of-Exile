Chapter 182: A Sister's No

Maeve's note came folded small and sealed with plain wax, and said only: The long gallery. Fourth bell. M.

The long gallery ran the length of the king's house on the garden side, a hundred and forty feet of polished boards and portraits and tall windows, and in wet weather the whole court walked in it because there was nowhere else to walk. It was wet. Emeric came up the west stair at the fourth bell and found it full: ladies in pairs with their hands in their sleeves, two young men arguing about a horse, a secretary going the length of it with papers at a pace that meant he was late, a small brown dog that belonged to nobody and followed everyone. The windows streamed. The portraits looked down at all of it with the patience of people who had been dead for a long time.

She was halfway down, in front of a painting of a king Emeric could not name, standing with her back to the windows so that the light came from behind her and her face was hard to read. He wondered whether she had chosen the place for that.

He did not know her well. He had never known her well. She was his sister, and the court had always known exactly what that meant and he had never been sure, and in the years since the exile they had exchanged perhaps a dozen letters, most of them about nothing. The last one he had kept. Four lines, the week the broadsheet went up on the chancery steps: she had been asked to sign a letter condemning him and had refused, and had been asked to sign a letter commending him and had refused that too, and she had no opinion she was entitled to. M. He had read it more times than it deserved.

"Maeve."

"Emeric." She did not offer her hand. She was in grey again, a dark wet-day grey with no ornament, and gloves. "Walk with me. It's easier if we're walking."

They walked. The dog came with them for a few yards and then lost interest.

"Mother wrote to me," Maeve said. "In the winter. Before the summons went out, when it was already known there would be one."

He waited.

"She asked me to speak for you at the settlement. Not in the hall. She knows I don't speak in halls. She asked me to speak to the people who would be at supper with the people who would decide it. To say, in the places where it would be heard, that my brother was a just man and had done a hard thing well." Maeve looked straight ahead. "She wrote it very well. You know how she writes."

"I know."

"I didn't do it."

A pair of ladies came towards them and passed, and one of them looked at Maeve and then at Emeric and then at the floor, and said good afternoon to Maeve and nothing to him.

"I'm not going to tell you why," Maeve said, when they had gone.

"I didn't ask."

"You were going to." She said it without heat. "You'd have been entitled to. I'm telling you so you don't. I'd rather you didn't wonder whether I'd give you a reason if you asked it the right way. I won't."

He thought about that. They reached the end of the gallery, where a window looked down the hill to the chancery roofs and the river, and turned, and started back.

"Why tell me at all?" he said.

"Because you'd have heard it from somebody. Mother's letters are not as private as she thinks they are. Her secretary has a sister in the Countess of Brede's household, and the Countess of Brede's household is where Aldane's men had their supper the night I said no to your friend last spring." She did look at him then, briefly. "I'd rather you heard it from me than from them. That's all. It's the only thing I can give you and I've given it."

"Thank you."

"Don't." She looked away again. "It has cost me with her. She hasn't written since. She will. She'll write to me at midsummer about something else entirely, and it will be very kind and very long, and she won't mention it, and neither of us will ever forget it." A small breath through the nose that might have been a laugh in somebody else. "I expect she's written to you instead."

"Not yet."

"She will."

The secretary came back the other way at the same late pace and went past them without looking up.

That was three times. He counted them the way Ferra would have: the two letters she would not sign, after Wayford; the word she would not say for him at the Countess of Brede's, which Aldane's men had quoted all over the city that evening; and this. Each time she had said no in a way that cost her something and gave nobody else anything. Each time she had told him, or let him hear, that she had said it. He did not understand it and he did not think she wanted him to.

There was a thing between them that neither of them had ever mentioned. A banquet, the last one before he went south, the night the court said its farewells to the king's younger son with a great deal of wine and a great many speeches, and Maeve's chair empty at the high table the whole evening. He had looked at it all night. He had never asked her about it and she had never said. It was there now, walking beside them down the gallery, as solid as the dog.

He did not ask about it. He thought, if he asked, she would tell him she would not give him a reason, and he did not want to hear that about the banquet too.

At the stair head she stopped.

"You looked very young in the hall," she said. "On the floor. When they read the terms. Everybody said so at supper. They said you looked like a boy who'd had his toys taken away."

"That was the idea."

"I know." She looked at him, and for a moment the light from the windows was at her side and he could see her face, and it was not a face he could read, but it was not a cold one. "I didn't think anybody else did. Be careful who does."

She went down the stair. He watched her go, grey on grey, one gloved hand on the rail.

The cardinal's house stood inside the cathedral close, behind a wall and a gate with a porter, and the porter had Emeric's name before he gave it.

Rylan waited in the porter's lodge. A young priest took Emeric up a stone stair and along a passage with a runner of red cloth down the middle of it and into a room lined with books to the ceiling, with a brazier and a desk and a window onto the cathedral's north side, where the rain was running off the gargoyles in ropes.

Cardinal Augustine rose from behind the desk. He was smaller than he had looked on the east bench, and older, with a narrow clever face and a great deal of white hair and very clean hands. He did not wear the red in his own room. He wore black, like any priest, with a plain cross on a cord.

"My lord," he said. "Thank you for coming. Sit, please. You'll take something warm."

Emeric sat, and took something warm, a cup of spiced wine that the young priest brought and then withdrew, and let the young man in the tight coat hold it in both hands like a boy who had been called to his tutor. Augustine sat down across the desk and looked at him with polite, entire attention, and Emeric understood within about three breaths that it was not going to work. Augustine had watched him on the floor of the hall for two hours. He was being courteous to the young man, which is a different thing from talking to him, and underneath the courtesy he was talking to whoever was wearing it.

"I wanted to meet you," Augustine said. "I have heard a great deal about Kaldo. Nearly all of it from people who have never been there."

"It's a long way south, Eminence."

"So I understand." He smiled. "I had a letter from there, some years ago now. From an old friend of the Circle. You'll know him."

The letter was on the desk. Emeric had seen it the moment he sat down and had not let himself look at it. It lay squared to the desk's edge, unfolded, with the creases of three folds in it, and the hand was Armand's hand, the upright careful hand of a man who had written his letters on steady mornings. Emeric knew what it said without reading it. He had never read it. Armand had written it alone and sent it alone and told the table afterwards. It proposed that the rite be slowed, to the same total over three nights instead of one. It gave no reason that Armand could not defend in public. It said nothing of what was in Armand's box.

"Master Armand," Emeric said.

"Master Armand. He asked me to consider a change to the chamber. I considered it. I answered him." Augustine touched the edge of the letter with one clean finger, the way a man touches something he is fond of. "He is with you now, I'm told. And a great many of his students."

"He came south for the work. There's a good deal of wall to build."

"So I understand," Augustine said again, pleasantly.

Beside Armand's letter lay a second sheet. It was smaller, a single printed leaf, and it had been folded many times and very small, into a square the size of a man's palm, and then unfolded and pressed flat. One edge was stained brown. The stain had soaked in and dried and gone stiff, so that the paper there stood up a little from the desk. It was Kaldo paper. Emeric would have known Kaldo paper across a room. And it was set in Nevin's type, which he would have known across a field.

Keep us, lord, in the hour of the fight.

"This came to me in the winter," Augustine said. He did not pick it up. "One of the duke's knights took it from the coat of a man who had been hanged at an oak on the Wayford road. The knight kept it for a while. I think he did not know why. Then he took it to his confessor, and his confessor, who is a sensible man, brought it to me." He looked at the brown edge. "It's a soldiers' prayer. A good one. Plain and short. Whoever chose it chose well."

Emeric said nothing. The wine was hot in his hands.

"It was printed in Kaldo, I think."

"Yes, Eminence."

"Does Kaldo print scripture, my lord?"

It was asked in exactly the tone of the questions about the wall and the weather. Courteous. Interested. There was no edge on it anywhere. Emeric would have preferred an edge. A man who is angry has shown you where he is. A man who is interested has not finished deciding.

"Nothing of scripture is printed in Kaldo," Emeric said, "without the parish reading the proof first. Father Doran reads every sheet. It's written in the finishing-room rules. It has been for two years."

Augustine looked at the prayer for a while.

"I can see that," he said.

He did not say anything else about it. He did not need to. The line lay on the desk between them, Keep us, lord, with its small l, and the parish had read the proof of it, and it had gone out to two hundred men at a muster, and one of them had died at an oak with it folded in his coat. Emeric could have explained. Nevin's boy, the proof read clean, the fourth line dropped after, reset in a hurry, the capital taken from the wrong box. It was true. He had the whole of it in Ferra's book. It was the kind of error that is always an error, and he could see Augustine knew that, and he could see that it did not matter. It did not matter whose hand had taken the letter from the wrong box. What mattered was that a barony now printed its own prayers, and printed them for its own soldiers, and that two hundred of those soldiers had gone into a field with a sheet of paper in their coats that asked their lord to keep them, and nobody on that field had been in any doubt which lord was nearest.

"It was a compositor's error," Emeric said. "A boy dropped the line after the proof and reset it. Father Doran never saw the reset."

"I believe you." Augustine's face was kind. "I'm sure it was exactly that." He moved Armand's letter a quarter inch, so that it lay square beside the prayer. "Master Armand's letter was a very good letter too. Very carefully written. I read it several times."

The rain ran down the window.

"You'll give my regards to Father Doran," Augustine said, rising. "I've never met him. I hear he keeps a very good parish book."

The next morning Emeric went to the chancery to put his name to the licence application, because the terms said within the year, and the young man in the tight coat would do it at once and sulkily, to show his father that he could.

There was a wait. There was always a wait at the chancery. He sat on a bench in the outer room under the clerestory with the form on his knee, and Saban went through to the rolls room to find a clerk he had made friends with in the spring, and the outer room filled and emptied around him with the ordinary business of a kingdom: stewards with rolls, a widow with a petition, a merchant arguing about a seal.

Two men came out of the inner office talking, and stopped by the door to finish, as men do, and one of them was Keller.

The other wore the grey of the Circle, with a master's black bar on the sleeve, and was angry in the controlled way of a man who has come to complain and been listened to politely.

"It's not a matter of numbers," the Circle master was saying. "I don't say the numbers are large. I say they are a pattern. We have a clerk in the tower, a careful young man, who keeps the postings register. He noticed it in the autumn and brought it to me. Posts vacated. Walls, cisterns, drains, the crown works on the north road. A mage leaves his post, as he's entitled to, and the lord he served writes to the tower to ask for another, as he's entitled to. Ordinarily we'd have three or four a year. We've had nineteen since the spring before last, and the year before that another fourteen, and when the clerk went back through the letters, every one of them that said where the man had gone said south. Wayford road. Kaldo."

"Has any post failed?" Keller asked.

"No." The master's mouth tightened. "No. We've filled them. The capital hasn't lost a cistern. That's not the point."

"What is the point, master?"

"That they went," the master said. "That thirty-odd mages of the Circle, trained in the tower, licensed by the tower, walked away from their posts and went south to work for a baron, and not one of them wrote to the tower to say why. That's the point. The Circle doesn't lose its people. Its people don't leave without a word. It has never happened. And I should like the Crown to know that it's happening."

"The Crown knows now," Keller said. "Thank you, master. I'll see it goes into the day book."

The master nodded shortly and went out. Keller stood a moment at the door with his hand on the frame. Then he looked across the outer room, and saw Emeric on the bench with the licence form on his knee, and looked at him for exactly as long as it takes to recognise someone, and went back into the inner office and shut the door.

Emeric sat with the form. Thirty-three, the master had said, and meant it as a large number, and it was a large number. It was Tessaly at Carrow Ford and Bastian at the cisterns and Paskin who had been Armand's assistant, and the twenty-six earth mages who had raised the footing, and Dorren from the south coast who had raised the bank on the Tarrant nephew's pegs. It was the wall, at half height and rising. It was the reason the wall had not stopped when a third of the gangs went to the levy.

He noticed that the master had not been worried about the walls. The capital had not suffered. The master had been worried that the Circle's people had gone somewhere without asking. That was a different worry, and a worse one, because it could not be answered by filling posts.

He said nothing. He filled in the form. Under the substance to be made he wrote powder, for the barony's company and for blasting in the Grayrock workings, and under the quantity sought he wrote the figure Bram had given him for a year of the beds, not a pound under it, and under the place of making he wrote the works beyond the beds, at Kaldo, because that was where it was. A lie on a chancery form was a thing that could be found, and then it would be the only thing anybody read.

Saban had found his clerk.

His name was Pell. He was about twenty, with ink on both hands and a habit of looking over his shoulder, and he liked Saban because Saban had once sent him a ream of Kaldo paper as a gift and the chancery's paper was grey and furred and spat its ink.

"You're not supposed to see this," Pell said, in the rolls room, with the door ajar.

"Then I won't," Saban said. "I'll only look at it."

The rolls room was long and low and lit by lamps even at noon, with the rolls in pigeonholes to the ceiling and a table down the middle. Pell laid three things on the table and went to stand at the door.

The settlement, engrossed, the full terms in a fair hand on four skins, with the clauses Saban had heard read in the hall and three he had not: the Crown's rider to have the right of entry to any place where powder was made, at any hour; the licence to be examined by an officer of the Crown's choosing; the private company's rolls to be sent to the chancery each quarter day with each man's name, parish and trade.

The day book, open at the Circle's complaint, entered by Keller's own clerk that morning, three paragraphs, with the numbers in them.

And a copy of the duke's file. The whole of it, the five articles and the sworn statements behind them, Garret Oakes and Sir Lewin Marle and Dray's groom and a dozen others Saban had not seen in the glove-maker's version, copied fair in a chancery hand. The duke had filed it at the chancery in the winter, and the chancery had copied it for its own rolls before the original went to the hall. The copy should have gone into the locked press the day the matter closed. It had not yet, Pell said.

Saban sat down and put his sling on the table and his good hand flat beside it.

He did not want to read them. He wanted to have them. He wanted to walk out of this room with every page in his head whole and exact, so that he could lay them out at the long-room table in Kaldo in a fortnight and not have to say I think it said. He could not do that by reading. He had tried, once, in a quartermaster's tent at the border ford with ten thousand pages in eleven chests and the burning already started, and he had pushed it, and pushed it, and Osric had carried him out at the fourth hour with a pulse like a child's and his arm cold to the elbow, and Imani had said she did not know whether it would mark him.

It had not marked him. Not that anyone could see. But he still did not sleep on his left side, and he had not opened the room alone since.

He opened it now.

He did it the way Bram had told the table he did it, the winter after the boiler: not by picturing a room and trying to walk into the picture, but by building what he knew. What Saban knew was a counting house. He had sat in one since he was fourteen. He built the high desk and the tall stool with the worn place on the seat, and the ledger shelf behind it, and the brass rail along the desk's front where you rested your cuffs so they did not smudge the ink, and the lamp. The lamp he built last, and slowly, because it mattered most: a brass lamp with a green glass shade that threw its light down onto the desk and nowhere else.

The counting house was there around him. It had been there in the tent, too. The difference was that in the tent he had flung the doors open and shouted the pages in by the armful, and the room had filled faster than it could hold.

This time he did not push.

He looked at the first skin of the settlement and let it come. He did not reach for it. He sat at the high desk in the green light and waited, the way you wait for a slow clerk to bring a roll up from the cellar, and it came, and he took it, and laid it on the desk, and looked at it until it was whole, every letter and every blot, and then he got down off the stool and put it on the shelf. Then the second skin. Then the third. The clauses he had not heard in the hall came onto the shelf in their places. An officer of the Crown's choosing.

He was aware of his body a long way off. His good hand flat on the chancery table. His wrist in its sling, aching a little. Pell's breathing at the door. The lamp.

The day book. Three paragraphs. On the shelf.

The duke's file took longer. There were thirty-eight pages of it, and some were dense, and the sworn statements had been copied in a small hand that crowded the margins. He took them one at a time. Garret Oakes: longer than a tithe barn. On the shelf. Sir Lewin Marle: and I do not laugh now. On the shelf. Dray's groom, who had fled over the Staple's yard wall and was living in the north on the duke's charity, and who swore that one of the men who came up the wall had a face like the underside of a toad, which Saban thought was probably the mask. On the shelf. He felt the room fill, and hold. It did not strain. It was not cold. The green lamp did not dim.

When he had the last page on the shelf he sat for a while at the desk with his hands on the brass rail and looked at it. All of it there, in order, whole. Nine chests and a page, the last time. This time four skins, three paragraphs and thirty-eight pages, which was not much, and which was all of it.

He closed the room.

He opened his eyes in the rolls room and it was the same noon. Pell was at the door. The lamps had not been trimmed. By the chancery's water clock on the wall, which Saban had not been able to see from inside, he had been gone something under a quarter of an hour.

His hand was warm. He turned it over and looked at it. Warm to the fingertips.

"Are you well, sir?" Pell said, from the door. "You went very still."

"I was reading," Saban said. "Slowly." He gathered the three things and squared them and slid them across to the clerk. "Put those in the press. I never saw them."

"No, sir."

He went out into the outer room, where Emeric was sitting on the bench with the licence form, and sat down beside him. He did not say anything. He did not know yet how he would say it, or to whom, or whether. Bram had told the table. Armand had told the table twice, once that his room would not open on the ground and once, with some violence, that it had. Saban did not know of anyone else. He found that he wanted to keep it for a while, the way you keep a coin in your pocket and touch it.

"All right?" Emeric said.

"I've got it," Saban said. "All of it. I'll give it you at Kaldo."

Emeric looked at him sideways, once, and then back at the form, and did not ask.

They left the capital in the rain two days later, in the same hired Milford carriage, with Rylan up beside the driver in an oilskin and the ledger case on the seat.

The road went south through the wet market gardens and the long straggle of the city's outer villages, and then through open country, grey and green and streaming. The carriage rocked. Saban was asleep before the second village, with his head against the window and his sling in his lap and his mouth a little open, and slept as Emeric had not seen him sleep since the tent, deeply and without moving.

Emeric watched the rain.

He thought about the settlement, and Corbin's satchel, and a prayer with a brown edge on a cardinal's desk. He thought about a Circle master saying that they went. He thought about three sentences under his father's breath and a price he was not to ask about. He would give Ferra most of it. He would give the table most of it. There were things he would give nobody.

But what he found himself thinking about, as the carriage went on south into the dark and Rylan's oilskin creaked on the box, was none of it. It was a chair at a high table, empty all evening, the night of the last banquet. And a sister in grey at the top of a stair, telling him to be careful who else could see through him, who had not come to say goodbye.
