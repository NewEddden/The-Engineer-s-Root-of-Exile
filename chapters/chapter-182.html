Chapter 182 - The Last Thatch

The last thatch inside Kaldo's walls came down on the ninth of September, at ten in the morning, with a noise like a man sitting down heavily in a chair.

It had been coming down all summer. The plague houses in the low rows below the churchyard had stood empty for five years under their old grey hats, and every spring somebody at the long table had said they should go, and every spring somebody else had said that a house was a house and the town might want it, and nobody had wanted to be the one to say out loud that the town would never want it, because everybody who had lived in it was in the churchyard behind. In July Ferra had put the rows on a page of their own in the barony's book, with the name of the last household against each door, from the census and the burial register both, and laid the page on the table at a Monday sitting and said nothing at all. Imani had read it. Doran had read it. And that had been the end of the argument, because nobody at the table could look at the page and say the town would want those houses.

So Fennick took them down.

He did it his way, which was with a great deal of string and very little powder, and Imani's way, which was the rules she had written for him in June after the quarryman, in a fair hand on a sheet that Fennick had nailed to the inside of his powder store door and could now recite. A rope round every house, a full house's length out. A boy at every corner of the rope. A horn, three times, and then a count to sixty, and then the horn again, and only then the match. Nobody inside the rope but Fennick, and Fennick only to light it and walk away, and walk, not run, because running men fell over things. And Imani on a chair at the corner of the churchyard wall with her bag at her feet and her watch in her hand, counting every one of them out and every one of them back.

Fennick hated the walking. He said so every time. He walked anyway.

Before each house, Doran came down from the church and stood at the rope with his book, and read the names that had lived in it. Not a prayer. Just the names, off Ferra's page, the way a clerk reads a roll. A man, his wife, four children, a mother. The last name on the last line. Then he closed the book and nodded to Fennick and went back to the wall, and the horn went, three times, and the count began.

Emeric came down for the last of them. The town did too. It had not been asked to, any more than it had been asked to the lanterns, and it came anyway, and stood along the churchyard wall and up the lane in a long quiet line, the way it stood for a burial; and some of the faces in the line he knew had a name on Ferra's page, a brother, a cousin, a grandmother. The house was at the end of the lowest row, where the lane stopped at the town ditch. A long low cottage of wattle and daub, gone soft at the corners, with a roof of thatch so old it had grass growing on it and a young birch tree, waist-high, rooted in the ridge.

Doran read four names. Then he closed the book and stood at the rope a while longer than he had at the others, and Emeric saw that he was not reading from the page, and had not been for the last two.

The horn went. Three times. Imani counted to sixty under her breath with her watch open in her palm, and the line along the wall counted with her, quietly, without meaning to; Emeric heard it go along the wall like a ripple, forty, forty-one, the whole town counting under its breath. The horn again. Fennick went in under the rope with his match and his slow fuse and bent at the corner of the house, and lit it, and turned round, and walked back out, with his long jaw set and both hands in his coat pockets and every line of him wanting to run.

The charge was the size of a fist. It went with a flat thump that Emeric felt in his feet more than heard. The near corner of the house went out from under it, and the house leaned on its broken knee, slowly, and sat down into itself, the walls folding in under the roof, and the roof came down whole on top of them, thatch and birch tree and all, and lay on the ground in a long grey heap with a cloud of old dust going up off it into the sun.

That was the noise. A man sitting down in a chair.

Nobody cheered. The line along the wall stood and looked at the heap a while, and then people began to go back up the lane in twos and threes, talking in ordinary voices about ordinary things, and somebody's child asked what the tree would do now, and was told to hush.

"Back," said Imani, closing her watch. "All of them." She looked at Fennick across the rope. "You walked."

"I walked," said Fennick. He took his hands out of his pockets. The two missing fingers on the left showed. "I'll tell you, Healer, it's the hardest thing I do. I'd rather carry the powder." He looked at the heap, and his face did the thing it did at the end of a good display, the wide grin with a great many teeth. "That came down sweet, though. Didn't it. Like a lady curtseying."

"Like a house falling down," said Imani, and picked up her bag.



The brick went up on the other side of the lane before the dust had settled.

It had been going up since May, row by row, as the plague houses came down: long terraces of two-storey houses in the new brick from the kilns past the brickfield, with tile roofs and iron gutters and a chimney at each party wall, four rooms to a house and a privy at the back, and a tap in every yard off the lane main, standing in straight lines along the lanes the old rows had wandered down. The worker housing. Bram had built the first one with his own hands in March, to see how long it took and what went wrong, and what had gone wrong was the chimney, and he had built the second one with a better chimney and handed the drawing to the masons. Under each yard he had laid a drain to the lane and stopped it at the wall with a clay plug, against the day the sewer came down the lane to meet it. There were sixty of them now. There would be a hundred and forty by the spring. The first families had moved into the first row in June, from the dock quarter and from the hamlets and from the coast, the families who had come for the works' wage and had been sleeping four to a room in lofts and sheds and the old wool store; and the first thing every one of them had done, Ferra said, was go out into the lane and look back at the roof.

"It's the tile," said Sela, who had walked down from the school with a dozen of the small ones to watch the house come down, and was walking them back. She looked at the new row with her hands on the shoulders of the two smallest. "You can't sleep under thatch in a plague town and not lie awake listening to it. Every rat in the valley lives in a thatch." She steered the children round a puddle. "They'll sleep, in those. First time in years, some of them."

Behind the terraces, on the square, the grey buildings stood in their line: the records hall, which had been the first, and the watch house beside it, finished in August, with its lamp over the door and the town watch's names on a board inside, and the market hall at the end, which opened on the twelfth.

It was a long hall of the same grey cement, open at both ends, with an iron roof on iron columns and stalls down both sides under the roof, so that the market could go on in the rain, which nobody in Kaldo had ever seen; and on its outer wall, facing the square, at a height a child could reach, there was a thing that was Ferra's idea and nobody else's.

A plate of bronze let into the cement, a yard long and a hand high, with a groove cut in it, and the groove's two ends stopped by bronze lips: a yard. Beside it a foot, and beside the foot an inch, each in its own plate, each groove cut from the forge's master set and checked against it with Bram watching. Under them, an iron case with a glass front and a lock, and in the case a set of weights, a pound and its halves down to the ounce and a stone and a half-stone, brass, on a little shelf, and a hook below the case where anybody could hang a balance. The key to the case hung in the watch house next door, and the watch would open it for anybody who asked, at any hour the market was open, for nothing.

"So any buyer can test any seller's scale," Ferra had said, at the table, in March, when she had first put it on a page. "In the square. In front of everybody. Without asking anyone's leave."

"The sellers will hate it," Saban had said.

"The honest ones won't."

The honest ones did not. On the morning the hall opened Emeric stood at the end of the square and watched a fishwife from the lower ward come out of the hall with a cod by its gills in one hand and a stallholder's balance in the other, and go to the watch house and get the key, and open the case, and hang the balance on the hook and the brass two-pound weight in its pan, in front of half the market; and the balance came down true; and she looked at it, and then at the stallholder, who had come out after her with his arms folded, and said loudly that she had only wanted to see; and the stallholder said loudly that she could see all she liked; and they went back in together, arguing about the price of the cod, and the case stood open on the wall until the watchman came out and locked it again.

By noon four balances had been tried. One had been light by an ounce in the pound. Its owner had taken it home at a run and filed the counterweight and come back and tried it again, sweating, with the whole square watching, and it had come down true, and the square had cheered him, which nobody had expected, least of all him.

Ferra watched the whole of it from the records hall steps. She did not smile. She wrote something in her book, a line, and went back inside.



The fire company drilled in the square on the afternoon of the twentieth, and Emeric went down to watch, because it was the first time anybody in Kaldo had put water on a fire from a pipe in the ground.

Every lane inside the walls had a main under it now. That had taken two years, and Bram's pipe works, and every ditch in Kaldo dug up twice; and the mains ran from the round towers, so that wherever a man stood in the town he was standing within forty yards of a column of water a hundred feet high, pushing down. At every crossing of the lanes there was an iron post with a cap on it and a wheel under the cap, painted red. Ferra's design: the posts, the caps, the spacing, the list of which house was nearest which post, kept in the watch house and pinned up in every hall. And the company was Osric's.

Ferra had asked Ward for a knight to run it, because a fire company was a thing men had to be made to do on the worst night of their lives without thinking, and that was what a knight was for; and Ward had given her Osric. Emeric had wondered at it, at the time. He did not wonder now. Osric stood in the middle of the square in his shirtsleeves with his red-flowered cuffs rolled to the elbow and a speaking trumpet in his hand, and twenty men of the town ran at his voice the way the garrison ran at Ward's. Two carts with a hose reel on each, a hundred yards of canvas hose on each reel, Abloh's, sewn and tarred and bound with copper wire at every joint. Brass branches at the ends. A wheel key for the posts. And a gauge on every post's cap, Bram's, a brass dial under glass, so that the man at the post could read the pressure off the main and shout it.

The drill was a fire in the old cooperage yard by the north well, which was a heap of broken staves and wet straw that Fennick had set alight with great pleasure and then been sent away from. The near cart ran to the near post. Osric's man put the key on the wheel and turned it, and the hose filled, kicking, the whole length of it going hard and round across the cobbles like a snake that has been trodden on, and the branch man braced and the water came out of the brass in a white rope and hit the burning straw forty feet off with a sound like a slap, and the square cheered.

The far cart ran to the far post, at the top of Cooper's Row, to come at the fire from the other side.

The water came out of that branch in a gush, and then a dribble, and then a feeble arc that fell short of the fire by a dozen feet and pattered on the cobbles.

"Pump," said the man on the far post, at once. "It's the pump. The tower pump's gone."

"There's no pump," said Osric, through his trumpet, walking across. "It's a tower. It's water falling down a hole."

"Then the tower's gone."

"The tower's there. I can see it from here. It's the big round thing." Osric reached the far cart and looked at the branch, dribbling, and at the hose, which lay on the cobbles soft and half-round, like a hose that has given up. "Kink," he said. "There's a kink."

There was no kink. Three men walked the hose back to the post looking for one, and did not find one, and stood round the post with the key and looked at it, and the man on the key turned the wheel another half turn and nothing changed, and the near cart's water went on hitting the fire from one side and the fire went on burning merrily on the other, and somebody in the crowd along the square said something about Kaldo's famous water, and somebody else laughed.

Tess was at the post.

Emeric had not seen her come. She had come up the lane from the works on the motor, wheels down, the way Ward's rule said inside the walls, and stopped her chair at the far post, a little behind the knot of men round it, and was sitting there with her hands in her lap, looking at the gauge on the cap. The men round the post had not noticed her either. She did not say anything to them. She leaned forward in the chair and looked at the dial for as long as it took the needle to settle, and then she turned her head and looked down Cooper's Row, back toward the town, and then she sat back.

"Boy," she said.

A boy came. There was always a boy at a fire drill.

"Two streets back," said Tess. "The crossing at the tannery. There's a valve under the plate in the road, the square plate, with a K on it. Somebody's shut it halfway. Probably the tannery, to wash their yard down without it flooding the lane, last week, and forgot." She held out her hand, and the man with the wheel key, who had turned round at her voice, put the key in it without being asked, as if his hand had decided. She gave it to the boy. "This fits it. Open it all the way, anticlockwise, till it stops. Then run back and tell me."

The boy ran.

Osric had turned round too, with the trumpet in his hand, and was looking at her.

"How do you know it's half," he said.

"The gauge reads forty," said Tess. "The near post reads ninety. They're on the same main and the same tower and the near post's further from it. So something's in the way between, and it's not much, or it'd read nothing." She looked at the dial again. "Forty out of ninety. Half shut. Near enough."

Osric looked at the dial, and at her, and at the dial.

The needle moved. It went up off forty, slowly, and then fast, sixty, eighty, and the hose on the cobbles at Osric's feet came up off the stones and went round and hard and kicked, and the branch man, who had been holding the branch pointed at the ground like a man holding a dead hen, nearly lost it, and swore, and got it up, and a white rope of water went out over the cobbles and hit the burning staves from the far side with a roar of steam. The square cheered again, louder, and the fire went out in about the time it took to say so.

The boy came running back up Cooper's Row with the key held over his head.

"It was half," he shouted. "It was the tannery. The tanner's wife says she's sorry."

Tess held her hand out for the key. The boy gave it to her. She gave it back to Osric's man on the post, and put her hands on the chair's arms.

"You'll want a list of every valve in the mains," she said, to Osric. "Which way they turn and who's got a key. They'll all get shut by somebody, one time or another." She moved her thumb on the stick, and the chair began to turn. "I'll draw you one."

"Come to the drills," said Osric.

The chair stopped turning.

"All of them," said Osric. He had the trumpet under his arm and his face had gone a little red, though that might have been the fire. "Every drill. For the gauges. Nobody else here can read them like that." He looked at the post. "I can't. I can read them. I can't do what you did. Nobody else here could have done what you did."

Tess looked at him from the chair for as long as it took the steam to clear off the cooperage yard.

"For the gauges," she said.

"For the gauges."

"Thursdays," said Tess. "After the shift." And the chair turned, and went away down the lane toward the works, wheels down, at a walking pace, and Osric stood at the post with his trumpet under his arm and watched it go until one of his men asked him what came next, and he had to think about it.

She came to the drill the next Thursday. And the one after. Emeric found that out from the watch house's book, which recorded every drill and who had stood it, in the watch sergeant's square hand; and he found, going down the book's columns in October, that every drill since the twentieth had the same line at the bottom, under the twenty men of the company. Gauges: T.

He did not say anything to anybody about it. He did not think, at the time, that there was anything to say.



Milford's grain came down the line all through October, in the goods wagons, behind the works' second engine, which had come off the floor in March and which the crews called Dunnel, because they had taken to naming them for Kaldo's dead; the first, which they called Wick, kept to Grayrock's ore and coal. Ferra counted it.

It was the second harvest on Linus's drains, and the first with the drains in everywhere: the wet bottoms that had been rushes and snipe four years ago, under barley; the benches above the river in their square watered fields, under wheat; the four-field rotation in its second turn, with the clover in where the fallow had been and the sheep on the clover. Paxton had sent the figures before the grain, by the wire, a page a day, and Ferra had pinned them up on the counting room wall in a column that got longer every morning; and by the end of the month the column said that the four baronies would eat, between this harvest and the next, everything Kaldo and Grayrock and Milford and Wayford could put on a table, and that when they had eaten it there would be eleven thousand bushels left in the stores, over, that nobody needed.

Eleven thousand bushels. Five years ago Kaldo had not been able to feed itself through a winter.

Emeric put it to the long table on the last Monday of October.

"I want to offer it to Emberfell," he said. "To Rowena. When we go."

The table looked at him.

"Not all of it. A shipload. More, if she wants it, at a fair price. She'll have had the letter about the visit by midwinter, and she'll be wondering why the son of a woman she knew is coming to her harbor in the middle of winter with no reason he'll put in a letter. I want to come with something in my hands." He looked down the table. "And Milford wants a buyer outside the four baronies. Paxton's said so twice. If the price of grain drops in the four baronies because we've too much of it, every farm on the bottoms takes the loss. A buyer on the coast keeps the price up."

"Emberfell has a coast," said Ferra.

He looked at her.

"Emberfell has a harbor that takes the southern grain ships every month of the year," said Ferra. She did not look up from her book. "The southern grain comes up the coast from the wheat islands on the long boats, and it costs less at Emberfell's quay than ours will cost at the end of Milford's line, before we've carted it over the hills or shipped it round the cape. I have the prices. Saban had them from the coaster's master in August." She turned a page and put her finger on a line. "There. If you offer Rowena Milford's grain, you're offering water to a fisherman. Either she'll laugh, or she'll be kind about it, and I'm not sure which is worse." She looked up at last. "And it tells her you think she's short. In front of her own clerks."

"It tells her I came with something."

"It tells her you came with the wrong thing."

Saban, at the end of the table, turned his hat once on his knee and said nothing, in the way that said he had an opinion and was charging for it.

"I want to offer it," said Emeric.

Ferra looked at him for as long as it took to read a line. Then she dipped her pen, and wrote, and blotted it, and went on to the next item, which was the watch house's lamp oil.

He read it upside down. He had read it upside down three times before, in five years, at her table and at Bram's and in the counting room, and he knew the shape of it before he had made out the words, the way a man knows a face across a street.

Grain to Emberfell, at the holder's wish. Said so. Overruled. And the date.

She did not look up again while the table sat. When it rose, and the others had gone out, he stayed in his chair, and she went on writing for a while at her end of it, and then closed her book and stood up and put it under her arm.

"You'll be wrong about this one," she said, at the door, without turning round.

"Probably."

"I'll want it said." She opened the door. "When you are. Out loud. Not just in the book."

"I'll say it out loud."

"Good," said Ferra, and went out; and he sat on at the long table in the empty hall, alone, with the column of Milford's grain on the wall behind him, and found that he was smiling, and could not have said exactly why, except that she had already decided he was going to be wrong and had already decided what she wanted for it, and that was more than most men got from the people who loved them.
