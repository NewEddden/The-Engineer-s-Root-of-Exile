Chapter 42 - Next Time Will Be Different

The account rode north inside Sethe's coat, folded in four along the creases it had come with, and by the second night he had read it four times.

It had been handed up to him in the yard at Kaldo, already marked settled, by a clerk who waited until his fingers had closed on it before letting go. He read it the first time on the Left Bridge, which was a mistake, because the two men behind him could see him doing it. The rest of the readings he did at waystations, with the shutter closed. It did not improve.

He had not expected it to. He read it the way he read everything, which was to find out what it knew.

The two men had brought the money south. They had watched it counted out on a trestle by a clerk who counted it twice, the second time aloud, and had signed for the receipt, and had waited in the yard for him to be brought down, and neither of them had said a word to him about any of it since.

They were not insolent. That was the difficulty. They were exactly correct the whole way north, a length behind him, and whenever he turned his head one of them was already looking at something else.

It was five days to Wayford at a walk. With fresh horses it was three, but fresh horses meant a waystation yard in daylight and a keeper coming out to hold the bridle, and from the third morning every waystation on that road was his.

He could not avoid them at night. The keepers stood when he came in and gave him the good room, and their wives brought the food themselves, and every one of them knew to the coin what his share of the baron had come to. There is only one way a junction town raises money in five weeks, which is by assessment on the road.

Nobody mentioned it. Nobody mentioned anything.

At the fourth station a keeper's son was splitting wood in the yard, and Sethe knew the face. The young man had been in the second rank on the right, one of the forty who had individually and privately declined to be next, and he had walked home. He looked up at his baron, whole, in a good coat, on a good horse, a month late. Then he looked back down at the block and went on splitting.

That was all of it. It was the worst quarter of an hour of the journey, and Sethe had been on the field.

A man who comes home dead has paid. A man who comes home wounded has paid something. A man who comes home unmarked, on a horse, bought back with money his own road put up, has been paid for, and everybody who watches him ride past knows the difference, and so does he.

He did not believe he had lost to a better man. He had examined it on the first night, because a man who refuses to is a fool, and he had rejected it on the evidence.

He had lost to bad information.

Renner had said the town was destitute. The men who came out of Kaldo's garrison had said the wall was nothing, forty-odd men, half of them sulking and the rest useless, under a prince who did not know which end of a ledger to hold.

Sethe had taken none of it on trust. He had checked it the careful way, which is to ask several people separately and see whether they agree, and they had all agreed.

Every one of them had been reading from the same page, and the page was a year and a half old.

That was the whole of the error, and it was not a failure of nerve or of judgment. It was a failure of intelligence, and intelligence can be bought. A man who loses to bad information has not been beaten. He has been shown where his information was bad.

What the page had missed, he had spent half an hour in a tower room establishing. The boy had steel. Four men in black plate that turned a blade like a wall, and crossbows that threw every bolt off one pattern. Somebody had sold him that. An exiled prince still has a name, and there are men with deep purses who owe it.

And the boy had been lucky with the ground. A strip of wet bottomland by the river that narrowed where nobody expected it to, and Renner riding into it at the front in his own colours and coming off his horse at three hundred yards to something nobody saw. Luck does not happen twice in the same place. Steel can be priced, and matched, and bought out from under a man.

The mages he could not account for. Three of them had stopped in the middle of working, the way a lamp stops, with nobody near them, and when he asked about it in the tower he had been left to sit in the silence until it went cold. He assumed it had been bought, like the steel, and he put it on the list of things to find out.

On the fourth reading he stopped reading the lines and read the page.

It was set out the way a provincial house sets out a delivery of lime. Ruled columns, the quantity, the rate, where the rate came from, the working, the sum. Standing crop destroyed, nine hundred and fifty bushels, at the market rate on a stated day in a stated town. The wages of every man employed on the earthworks, by name and by the day. The treatment and keep of the wounded, and the wages each of them would not earn while he mended. Cartage of eleven days of stores, in cart-days. And sixth of eleven, the value of the standing orders Wayford had cancelled without notice in the four weeks before the march.

Near the foot was a line he read twice before he understood that it was about him. Board and lodging, one prisoner of rank, thirty-four days, at the rate of the keep's own table.

He had been charged for his dinners.

There was no ransom on it anywhere. A ransom says a man was worth the taking. This said he had come onto somebody's land and broken things, and was expected to pay for them, in the same columns as a merchant who had short-delivered a load of tin.

He was not worth taking. He was worth billing.

What kept him at the table was what the page left out.

There was no line for Kaldo's dead. He went down the columns a third time looking for one, because a house that bills a man for his dinners does not forget to bill him for a widow. Two hundred men had come down that road and the boy had not needed to bury one of his own. The plate accounted for most of that and the ground for the rest, and Sethe liked neither explanation any better for its being sufficient.

And there was no line for the granary.

He had seen the smoke from it on the afternoon he was taken, and known the building, because the Kaldo men had drawn it for him on a map in the winter. A full granary is worth more than the crop and the wages together, and a clerk who charged for dinners would not have missed it. It was not on the page because there had been nothing in it. Three lines above, in the same hand, was the cartage for moving the barony's stores.

The boy had moved his grain before anybody came, and then sent Wayford the bill for moving it.

When, the page did not say. Sethe could guess. The earthworks line said the town had dug for weeks, and every frightened town digs when it hears a column is coming. The sixth line said what it had heard. Four weeks before the march, Wayford had stopped buying without a word, and a barony that lives on a handful of customers notices the day one of them goes quiet.

The boy had billed him for his own warning.

It was the best piece of work anyone had done against him in twenty years. Every line was true and would stand in any court in the kingdom. It told him more about Kaldo than his couriers had in a year and a half, and the boy had sent it anyway, which meant the boy did not care what Wayford knew. The field had cost Sethe a morning. The page was going to cost him years, and it had been written to.

He admired it for about the length of a breath.

Then he folded it along its creases and put it back inside his coat, where he kept the things he meant to repay.

Wayford came up out of the evening on the fifth day as it always did, first as cookfire smoke over the junction, then as lamps, then as the smell of a town that lives on horses.

The steward met him in the yard and gave him the accounts worst first, the way Sethe liked them. The purse was short by the sum on the page. The assessment had been laid on the road by traffic, and paid in full and on time, and not one keeper had refused.

Sethe had half hoped one would. A refusal is a thing a baron can deal with.

"They heard it read," he said.

"Every line, in hall, when I laid the assessment." The steward did not apologise for it. "A man who is asked for money is owed the reason, my lord, and I had no better reason to give them than the one on the page. I gave it to the youngest clerk because his voice carries. I did not tell him he could leave anything out, and he is not a man who leaves things out on his own."

Sethe knew the clerk. A thin, exact young man with a narrow hand, whose columns always closed. He made a note, not on paper, that the young man was to be kept well away from anything that ever needed doing quietly. A clerk who does not skip is a clerk who writes down what he sees.

"And the item on the orders," Sethe said.

"Was read sixth," said the steward. "Of eleven."

He said nothing else about it, and did not need to.

In the weeks after, Sethe could feel the town doing sums.

He could not have pointed at any of it. The keepers were correct, and the guild men who took the tolls were correct. But talk in the hall ended a sentence early when he came through a door, and at the reckoning a keeper looked at the assessment line in his book, then at Sethe, then back at the line, the way a man compares a price with the goods.

He took it for grumbling. Grumbling passes, and he meant to give it a reason to, by putting back into that purse a great deal more than had gone out of it, from the place it had gone to.

It was not grumbling, and it did not pass, and he had no idea that anything had started.

The Kaldo men were the other account he settled that month.

Three were left in Wayford. Four had died on the day at the corner of a granary yard, ridden down in the open. Two had come out of the woods around Kaldo a fortnight later with their boots and a story about firing the boy's grain, which they told in the kitchens to anyone who would feed them. The third had never marched, because he was the one who knew the most, and a man does not spend his best source on a battle.

He did not tell the two what had been in that granary. He heard their story once, all the way through, and thanked them for it.

Then he settled with the three of them the way he settled anything. He paid each man what he had been promised, to the coin, and a little over, and told them there was work for three steady men at a station at the far end of his road, and that two of the household would ride out with them in the morning to show them the way. The one who had stayed behind counted his coin in the yard and put it away, and then looked up, for no reason Sethe could see, and said, "It was all true, my lord. Every word of it, the day we walked out of that gate. You were the one said a year and a half was nothing, in a place like that."

Sethe had said it. He remembered the room and the fire and the sentence, and he did not want to have heard it again, and he had. It changed nothing. The household men came back on the fourth day and the three did not, and nobody in Wayford asked after three men who had never been from Wayford. He slept better that week than he had since the field, and noticed that he did, and was not troubled by it.

What came next he built at his table in the evenings, on paper first, the way he had built everything he owned.

More mages, and not hedge-work. Men with a lineage and a tower's schooling, paid what such men cost, because Renner had paid for five grey-robes at the cheapest rate Sethe's people could find and had got exactly what that rate buys. More men, borrowed or hired or levied. Plate, once he knew who was selling it. And orders that were kept and paid and priced, so that nothing Wayford bought or stopped buying ever told anybody anything again.

All of it cost money. The purse was short. So all of it would need time.

And before any of it, information. Men he paid, sent to look, who came back and said what they had seen and on what day. He wrote that into the instruction and put a line under it. What you saw, and the day you saw it. He did not dwell on where he had learned it.

The last piece came to him at the waypoint, sideways, while he was going over the assessment lines with the factor.

The waypoint sat on the junction where the south road came in, and every packet bound for the capital crossed its long table. Crown post, merchants' letters, private hands. Bags came in off the south stages, were sorted and entered, and went on in the north bag, which was sealed at its hour.

The factor had been skimming the tolls on that table for years. Sethe had known for most of them and let it run, because a man who is already taking a little can one day be asked for a little more.

Sethe watched the sorting and thought about the boy who had written the page in his coat. A man who wrote four letters and then a bill. A man who put everything on paper, and sent it where it was going.

The boy would write to the crown. He would write the war the way he had written the bill, in full, with the columns ruled, and send it the way a careful house sends things, in the ordinary packet on the quarter.

And a report of that war, in that hand, with those figures, was the one document in the kingdom that could hang him. Three barons had marched on a prince of the blood. No form of words made that a border quarrel once somebody had written it down properly and it had arrived.

"Anything from Kaldo lately," he said.

The factor had the register open before the question was finished. Nothing under that seal since the summer quarter. Kaldo sent on the quarter, regular as rent, crown post, one packet.

The quarter was three weeks off.

The rest of it arrived all at once, and it was less a plan than a map with one road on it.

Everything that barony sent north crossed this table, because the land gave no other way through that did not add a week, and nobody adds a week to send a letter. If the crown never heard what Kaldo was, it would go on believing that a third son had been sent south to a dead town to be forgotten. And while it believed that, nothing would happen to Kaldo that Sethe did not allow. No crown men. No inquiry. He would have as long as he needed.

Men could be bought away from him. Mages could be outbid. Money could run. The junction could not go anywhere. It was the one thing he owned that would still be exactly where it was in ten years, the only real advantage he had ever had, and for a year and a half he had used it to count carts.

He did not give an order. He had a conversation.

"Who rides the last south stage on quarter day?"

The factor gave him a name.

"He is slow that day," Sethe said. "He comes in after the north bag is sealed." He put a coin on the table, and then a second, which was more than a slow afternoon was worth and was meant to be. "Anything that comes in after the seal is held for the next bag."

"It always is, my lord."

"Anything under Kaldo's seal and addressed to the crown is held until somebody asks for it."

The factor looked at the coins and did not pick them up yet. "And if somebody asks?"

"Then it was held, and here it is, and the stage rider will be spoken to about his habits." Sethe looked along the table at the other bags. "Only the crown's. Leave everything else alone. Merchants count their letters. The crown cannot miss what it never knew was sent."

The factor picked up the coins.

The Kaldo packet came up the south road on quarter day in the stage rider's bag, an hour and a half after the north bag had been sealed. It was thicker than the last one. The factor entered it as held, in the ordinary column, in the ordinary hand.

Sethe did not go down to look at it. A packet that has been held is an accident of the road. A packet that has been in a baron's hands is something with a name. He did not open it, and he did not ask what was in it. He had a fair idea.

It was not in the next bag, or the one after. Within a month nobody could have said where in the building it was, and nobody asked, because nobody had known it was coming.

Roads are bad and couriers are men. Seals break. Packets fall behind presses and turn up at midsummer, and every clerk in the kingdom keeps a column for it. Nobody notices a letter that simply does not arrive. There is only a silence where an answer would have been, and a man in the south who has had silence from the capital for years and has a perfectly good explanation for it.

It was the most important document anyone wrote in the south that year. It went astray in the ordinary way, and it cost two coins and a conversation.

Sethe did not think of it as a crime. He thought of it as a column.

Milford he gave less thought.

Paxton had sat at the back of the road with his hundred, watched the front of the line come apart, and taken all hundred of them home before anybody could stop him.

Sethe knew what that was. They had put Paxton at the back because Milford had brought drovers and herdsmen off open pasture, and a weak man at the back of a losing line does the one thing weak men can be relied on to do, which is leave early. Paxton had broken and run and got clean away with it. It also meant Milford still had its hundred, and still owed the coalition a fight.

A man who runs once can be managed. He stands where he is put the second time, provided somebody has written the odds down for him first.

Sethe was wrong about Paxton, and it would be a long time before he found out how wrong.

The hundred went home and talked, which was the other thing drovers can be relied on to do.

They had stood at the back of the road with a clear view of all of it, and not one of them had been touched, so what they carried home to Milford's pastures was not a defeat. It was a story, and they told it in the order they had found it hard to believe: forty-odd men in iron who did not go down, a town that had not shut its gate, wounded Grayrock men carried into Kaldo's church and laid beside Kaldo's own. The Grayrock men who walked home that autumn told the last part themselves. By the first snow it had gone out of the junction market and down every road that met there, in alehouses Sethe never drank in, and there it waited, the way stories wait in winter. Nobody moves a household in the snow.

He took the account out of his coat that night and read it for the fifth time, and folded it along its creases, and put it back.

He had not lost. He had learned. Next time would be different.
