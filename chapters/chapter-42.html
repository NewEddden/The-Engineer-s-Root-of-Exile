Chapter 42 - Dew

For nine days the line was the best thing in Kaldo.

The gate crew used it for everything. A cart on the road, which was not on the board, became a rider, which was. A heron landing on the moat became, once, a boat, and Ward had the man who sent it stand in the gate tower for an hour in the sun looking at the heron so that he would know one in future. A fisherman's skiff went past the South Gate on the moat and the keep heard about it before the fisherman had tied up. The wall-crew man who had read his name in Doran's class sent all well every hour on the hour through his whole watch, unasked, until Ward told him that all well was what the keep assumed when it heard nothing, and that the needle was not for saying good morning.

Children came up the high street to watch the poles, as though the wire might do something if they waited long enough. A carter tied his horse to one and was spoken to by Tess. The man who had asked whether the wire was for hanging people asked again, and was told again, and seemed a little disappointed.

At the top of the hill Emeric kept the needle in the room where the line came in, on the table under the window, and found reasons to walk past it. He did not need the reasons. He walked past it anyway, the way a man walks past a new door he has hung, to see it hang.

On the tenth morning it did not answer.



Rylan found it. He had taken to sending all well to the gate at first light himself, before the petition line formed, because he liked to watch it come back, and on the tenth morning he pressed the key and waited, and the needle in front of him lay still, and stayed still.

He pressed again. Nothing.

He came to find Emeric at Ward's drill in the yard, which he had never done before, and Emeric went up the stair two at a time with the dust of the yard still on him and pressed the key himself, and his own needle kicked under his hand the way it should, which meant the current was leaving the room. Nothing came back.

He sent Rylan down the hill at a run. Rylan came back at a walk, an hour later, to say that the gate tower had seen nothing at all all morning, that the gate crew had been sending all well since first light and wondering why the keep was so quiet, and that while he was standing there the needle at the gate had jumped and swung, perfectly, as clean as it had on the first day.

Emeric was at the table by then with the key under his hand. He had been pressing it every few minutes for an hour, and at some point in the last few minutes the answer had begun to come back.

By noon the line was working as though nothing had happened. It worked all afternoon and all evening. Ward sent the bell from the gate at dusk, as a test, and the keep's needle went three to the right, fast, before Emeric had finished turning his head.

The next morning it did not answer.



He went at it as he went at anything he had built that would not do what it was built to do, which was by suspecting himself first.

That was not quite true. On the second morning, before he had suspected himself of anything, he asked Ward whether the gate crew were sending at first light, or whether they were asleep at the key.

"They are sending," Ward said. "I stood behind them."

Ward did not say why he had gone down to the gate in the dark. Emeric worked that out on his own, and was ashamed of having asked.

The jars were his, so he started with the jars. He carried the six from the keep down to the dyehouse in a handbarrow in the afternoon, with Tess, and tested them one by one against Abloh's compass, and they were all as good as the day they were set up: the copper bright and pink under the blue, the zinc eaten a little, the brown pots sweating. He carried them back up. The next morning the line failed again.

The needles were his, so he took the keep's needle out of its box and looked at every turn of the silk-covered wire for a break, and found none, and put it back. The next morning the line failed again.

The earth plates were his. He had Rylan and Declan pull the keep's plate out of the canal ring on a rope, and it came up furred with weed and a little rust and otherwise sound, and he had them scour it and drop it back in a different place, a little further out, where the water was deeper. The next morning the line failed again.

He began to dread the mornings. He would wake before it was light, and lie there knowing he would get up and climb the stair and press the key and watch the needle not move, and he would do all three, and then go down to Ward's drill and be put on the ground by a man who had the decency not to remark on how easily it was done that week.

Ferra, when he told her about it at her table, asked him whether it failed every morning.

"Yes."

"Every single one. Since the tenth."

"Yes."

"And comes back every day."

"By noon. Sometimes earlier."

"Then it's not broken," Ferra said. "Broken things stay broken." She went back to her page.

He went up the hill and stared at his own jars again anyway, because they were his, and he understood them, and he did not understand mornings.



Tess did not ask anybody's permission. She simply went and stood in the high street one morning before it was light, by the first pole below the keep, with her bench book and a stub of chalk, and waited.

Emeric only found out because he came down the stair from the line room that morning, having pressed the key and watched it fail, and met her coming up the gate yard with her shoes soaked through and her book held inside her shirt to keep it dry.

"It's wet," she said, before he could ask what she was doing there.

"What is?"

"Everything." She took the book out and opened it. She had been down the whole line in the half-dark, from the keep to the gate, and up again, and every pole she had been to she had put her hand on. "The poles. The pegs on top. The grass. The wire's wet. It runs off the wire in drops and goes down the pole." She showed him a column in the book, in her careful hand, a line for every pole she had touched and a mark beside each. Wet, wet, wet, wet. "The sun gets to the high ones first, by the keep. They dry first. The low ones by the gate stay wet longest. The street's in shadow till late."

He looked at the column, thirty-one lines long, every one of them the same.

"Nothing touches the wire," Tess said. She said it as she had said it to the woman with the shirt. "The wet touches it. All the way down."

He saw it then, the whole of it.

The current did not care that it was meant to go to the gate. It went where it could. Every night, while the town slept, the air cooled and the dew came out of it onto everything in the street, and onto thirty-one larch poles and thirty-one wooden pegs, and a wet peg on a wet pole was a road to the ground. A poor road. But thirty-one poor roads, one after another all the way down the hill, and every one of them carrying off a little of the current into the earth before it got as far as the gate, until by the bottom of the high street there was nothing left in the wire to move a needle. Then the sun came up over the east wall and dried the poles one at a time, the high ones first, and the roads closed, and by noon the current went where it had been sent.

He had counted on damp poles when he built the line, a little lost at each one. He had not counted on every pole in the street soaked at once, every night, from the top of the hill to the bottom, and for a week he had been looking at his jars.

"Yes," he said. "That's it."

Tess closed the book. She did not look pleased with herself. She looked as she did when a sum came out late.

"So it wants to not touch the pole," she said.

"It wants to sit on something that doesn't drink."



The potter heard what was wanted and put both hands flat on his wheel and looked at the prince.

"In May you wanted my pots soft," he said. "So they'd drink. Now you want them so they won't."

"Yes."

"Glazed."

"Glazed all over. Fired hard."

The potter looked up at the sky, appealing to somebody who had known him longer, and then asked what shape.

Emeric drew it on the potter's own slate: a knob of clay about the size of a child's fist, with a waist round its middle for the wire to lie in, and hollow underneath like a bell, so it could be pushed down over the top of a peg and the inside of the bell stayed dry. The rain and the dew would run down the glaze on the outside, over the swell of the knob, and drip off the lower edge, and never find a way across the dry inside of the bell to the peg. A wet glaze was still a poor road. A glaze with nothing running across it was hardly any road at all.

The potter looked at the drawing of what Emeric called an insulator and said it looked like a mushroom, and it did, and he made thirty-five of them, because he said some would crack in the kiln and some would get dropped and the prince would be back for more if he only made thirty-one. He was right about both. Three cracked in the firing. Tess dropped one on the cobbles of the high street on the first day and stood over the pieces with an expression Emeric had not seen on her before.

Abloh waxed them. He would not let anyone else do it, because he said a glaze would hold off the water and a wax would hold off what the glaze missed, and there was a way to put wax on clay so that it stayed on clay, and nobody else in Kaldo knew it. He sat in the soap yard with the knobs warming in a tray by the kettles and rubbed each one with a cake of beeswax until it shone, one after another, all afternoon, and complained the whole time that he had been a sailmaker once.

The gate crew put them up. A man on a ladder at every pole, with Tess at the bottom of the ladder watching. The peg came off the top of the pole and a new peg went in, shorter, and the insulator went down over it, and the wire was lifted off the old staple and laid in the waist of the knob and tied there with a turn of soft iron wire, so that nothing touched the line from one end of the high street to the other except clay and wax and air.

It took two days. They finished at the gate tower on a hot still evening, with the swifts going round the tower and half the street watching, and Ward sent all well up the hill, and the keep sent it back.

That proved nothing. It had always worked in the evening.



Emeric did not sleep much that night.

He was up before the light, and in the line room before Rylan, and he stood at the window and watched the town come grey out of the dark below him: the roofs, the market, the high street with its row of poles going away down the hill, each one with its small pale knob on top like a cap. Down by the gate the street was still black. The air was cool and very still. When he put his hand on the stone of the windowsill it came away wet.

Dew on everything.

Rylan came up the stair yawning and stopped in the doorway when he saw who was already there.

"Go on," Emeric said.

Rylan sat down at the table and put his hand on the key and pressed the left end, slow, once, and let it up. All well.

The keep's needle kicked under his hand and lay still.

They waited.

A cart went over the cobbles somewhere below. A cock crowed in the lower ward and another answered it. Rylan, who was not good at waiting, began to drum his fingers on the table and stopped when Emeric looked at him.

The needle went left, slow, and came back.

Rylan let his breath out in a long whistle.

The needle moved again before either of them could say anything, and it was the open hand, the fingers spread. Say again.

Rylan laughed out loud. "They don't believe it."

"Tess doesn't," Emeric said. "She'll be at the bottom of the street with her hand on a wet pole. She wants it twice."

He leaned over Rylan's shoulder and pressed the key himself, left, slow, once.

All well.

It went down the hill through thirty-one wet poles in the grey light, and came back, and the needle swung, and went back, and lay still.
