Chapter 42 - Harder, Not Softer

The second barge came down on the eighteenth of October and the third on the twenty-second, and by the end of the month Bram had stopped talking about grade at all, which was how you knew the grade was fine.

What Emeric noticed, walking the eastern arm on the twenty-fourth, was the quiet. A Duvrenne load had been an event. It had arrived after eleven days and a coastal insurer and two payments against two loads and a fortnight of Saban writing letters, and every man on the landing had turned out for it because it was the thing the furnace was going to live on until spring. A barge down out of the hills was four men and a rope and an hour, and then it was ore, and nobody came to watch.

"That's what it's meant to look like," Ferra said, when he mentioned it. "A thing that has stopped being a crisis is a thing nobody attends. Do not go looking for the ceremony. You will only find it again when it breaks."

Bryda's letter came down with the third barge and it was one page and the hand was small and even and there was not a wasted line on it.

Grayrock's factors at the Wayford junction had closed her credit on the sixteenth of October. Not all of them. Four of the seven, which was the four that mattered, and they had done it inside nine days of the contract being known, and every one of them had given a different reason and none of the reasons was the reason. Salt, lamp oil, cordage, cloth, iron tools she did not make on the hill, and the two apothecary lines she bought twice a year. Cash in advance, all of it, from a treasury she had already described to him in February as a room with a door on it.

The last paragraph said that she was not asking him for anything and was writing because a five-year counterparty who found out in March would think she had hidden it, and that she would rather he thought her poor than dishonest.

"Well," Saban said, when it had gone round the table. "There it is."

"Say it properly."

"Everything that hill sells goes down a river for nothing. Everything that hill buys comes up a road, and the road goes through Wayford, because there is no other road, because the ground says so." Saban put two fingers on the table and did not move them. "She has changed who she sells to. She cannot change who she buys through. Sethe can't touch the ore because the ore never passes him, so he has gone and stood on the only piece of her that does, and it took him nine days."

"We supply her."

"With what, up what?" Saban said it without heat. "The water is one way. It has always been one way. It is why they were poor before your grandfather was born. You can put anything you like on a cart out of Kaldo and send it up the river road and it will get there, and it will cost you about what it costs to send it and about a third of it again in the winter, and you will be doing that every month forever, and the first time the road is out for a fortnight in February she will be sitting on a hill with no salt and I will be standing here explaining why."

Ferra had already been writing for some time.

"Price it," Emeric said.

She priced it. It was not catastrophic and it was not small, and the shape of it was worse than the size of it, because it was a permanent monthly bleed against a five-year contract whose whole virtue had been that the carriage cost nothing.

"It takes about a fifth off the advantage," Ferra said. "In a normal year. In a bad February it takes more and I cannot tell you how much because I would be making it up."

"And if we do nothing?"

"Then she buys at cash prices from four men who know she has no choice, and it comes off her side of the sum, and it comes back to us in the third year when she asks for the re-read, and she will be right to." Ferra ruled the line. "You did not buy ore. You bought a hill, and a hill has a stomach."

Paxton came on the twenty-seventh with eleven head of cattle he did not need to deliver personally, which was the second time he had used cattle as a reason to be somewhere and the second time nobody had mentioned it.

He was sixty-eight and had ridden a day, and he sat down by the fire in the long room before he took his coat off and said, "Your factor is a woman."

"She is."

"Good," Paxton said. "My grandmother ran Milford for nine years and they wrote it down as my grandfather's stewardship. Nobody has ever asked me where the roads came from." He got the coat off. "That's not what I've come about."

"I didn't think it was."

"Then I'll say it badly the first time and better the second, the way I do." He put his hands out to the fire. "You've been walking about since August thinking you took the hill and that's the war finished. You took the poorest man off the board. That's all you did. Renner had two hundred and fifty men off a sorting floor and forty in plate and a treasury he'd been eating for twenty years, and he was the loudest one and he's dead and his hill is yours, and every single person who is left was watching that."

"And?"

"And they're not frightened. That's the thing you've got wrong and I've come a day to say it." Paxton looked at him. "A man who watches the weakest of his fellows get eaten does not think, I shall be next, so I had better make peace. He thinks, that was the weak one, and I am not him, and now there is one fewer mouth in the arrangement and rather more at stake. It goes harder, not softer. It has already gone harder. You have had a letter about it this week from a woman on a hill and I have had two from a man at a junction."

The room was quiet for a moment.

"Say the second one," Emeric said.

"Sethe writes to me as a man writes to an ally," Paxton said. "He has done since September. He writes about the state of the roads and the price of wool and whether I have had trouble with any of my eastern tenants, and in the last one he asked me a question about grain carts on the river road that a man does not ask unless he is counting them."

"He thinks Milford is still coalition."

"He thinks I stood on a plain in August and did not advance because my nerve went, and that I have been ashamed of it ever since and would like a way back." Paxton said it with no expression whatsoever. "It is not a mad thing for him to think. It is what most men in this kingdom think. It is what I would think."

Emeric had the answer ready before Paxton had finished, which he recognised afterward as the whole of the problem.

"Then correct it," he said. "Publicly. You and I put our names on one page, alliance in plain words, terms visible, and it goes to the capital and to all three baronies in the same week. It ends the guessing. It makes any move against Milford a move against Kaldo, and after August nobody wants that. And it is true, which is the part I care about, because I have been letting a true thing stay unsaid for two months because the unsaidness was useful, and I have been uncomfortable about it for two months."

Paxton let him get all the way to the end of it.

"No," he said.

"Then tell me why, because I'd rather be told than guess."

"Because you've just offered to take the only thing I have and set fire to it in the town square to make yourself feel honest." Paxton was not angry. That was worse. "Sit down. I'll do it in order, like your merchant."

Emeric sat down.

"One. The day that page exists, Milford is not a barony that quietly does business with Kaldo. It is Kaldo's western flank, and it has one hundred and fifty men and no wall and eleven miles of open pasture on the wrong side of a border. You have a hill and a foundry and a granary with stone lids on it. I have cows."

"Two. What am I worth to you at this table? Not my beef. I am worth a man at a junction writing me letters he should not write, and the day he stops writing them you have lost the only window into Wayford that anybody in this kingdom has, and it will not come back, because a man who has been made a fool of once does not do it twice."

"Three, and this is the one you will not like." Paxton turned his hands over at the fire. "You want it public because you are uncomfortable. That is a good instinct in a young man and it is not a reason. You are not proposing to end a lie. You are proposing to end my usefulness so that you can stop feeling badly about a lie that I am telling, that I chose to tell, that costs you nothing and costs me the price of a letter a month."

"You said you'd not lie for me."

"And I don't. I have never written that man a word about you that is untrue. I write him about the roads and the wool and my tenants, and every word of it is accurate, and he reads what he wants underneath it, and I have never once put a hand out to help him do it." Paxton shrugged, and for a second he looked exactly his age. "I will not lie for you. I will not correct a man who is lying to himself. Those are two different things and I have had forty years to get comfortable with the gap between them and you have had about two."

Emeric sat with it for a while.

"I was wrong," he said. "Not about the discomfort. About what I was going to do with it. I was going to spend your position to buy my own comfort and I was going to call it honesty, and I'd have done it in a room full of people and been pleased with myself, and Ferra would have priced it in a week and told me what it cost."

"Two days," Ferra said, from the end of the table.

"Two days."

"That's the right answer and you got to it fast," Paxton said, "and I'd have thought better of you if it had taken you longer, because a man who changes his mind quickly can change it back the same way. Sleep on it and tell me on Thursday you still mean it."

"I'll tell you on Thursday."

"Good." Paxton put his hands on his knees. "Now. My wife has been better since the summer and has taken up an opinion about your doctor, and I have been sent, and I am going to sit here and be a nuisance about it for an hour before I let you feed me."

He did not raise the grain, and Emeric raised it instead, at the door, on the second morning, because the price was two harvests old and grain was worth a good deal more this month than it had been worth in June of the year before last and both of them knew the number.

"I signed a page," Paxton said.

"You signed it before the fire and I'm telling you what it's worth now."

"I know what it's worth now. I knew what it was worth in November, when you came over my border with your steward and a boy carrying paper, looking like a man who had rehearsed." He got his foot into the stirrup and had a small amount of difficulty with it and refused the hand. "You'll not improve a signed page in my favour to make yourself easy. That's twice in three days and I've only the one lecture."

The other thing happened quietly and Emeric did not think about it properly for eight months.

The contracts house in the capital, the one that had answered him in four lines a year ago and in two pages in September, had asked politely about grain. Ferra had written back on the fourteenth with a price and a quantity and the terms of carriage, and had written again on the second of November because the first had not been answered, and the second was not answered either.

"They've cooled," Saban said. "They do. A man in a room in the capital writes to eleven baronies in a good month and does business with two, and the other nine get a warm letter and then nothing, and it means somebody down the corridor priced you and did not like the price. It's not a thing. If it's a thing it'll be a thing in the spring."

"You're sure."

"I'm not sure, I'm experienced, which is worse and cheaper." Saban was already reaching for the next sheet. "Put it down and don't chew it. You've a hill with no salt on it and that's a real problem with a real number on it."

So he put it down.

Four of Grayrock's seven junction factors closed her credit inside nine days of the contract being known. He cannot touch the ore because the ore never goes past him, so he has stood on the road, and the road is the only thing she has that goes past him, and I did not think about the road once in the whole of September because the river had made me stupid about carriage. The water going one way is the oldest fact about that country and it is in Saban's February note in Saban's handwriting and I read it and understood it and did not use it.

She wrote to tell me she was poor rather than have me find out in March and think she was hiding it. I have been three days working out why that sentence sat badly with me and it is because it is the first thing she has ever done that was not arithmetic, and it was still arithmetic, and I would like to stop being surprised by that.

A fifth off the advantage in a normal year to cart her supply up. More in a bad February. Ferra will not guess at the February number and is right not to. We are doing it anyway and it starts in November, and I want it written here that we are doing it because a supplier you let starve stops being a supplier, and not because it is decent, so that nobody including me gets to feel warm about it later.

Paxton rode a day to tell me that taking the hill makes the rest of them harder and not softer, and I argued with him for about four minutes and then stopped because he was right. Then I offered to burn his position down in front of witnesses to settle my own stomach and called it honesty. Second time this year I have got the appointment right and the reception wrong. Ferra says two days and she is being kind, and I have written this down so that the next time I want to do a true thing publicly I have to read this page first.

The capital house has not answered two letters. Saban says it is nothing and that he is not sure, he is experienced. I have written the two dates at the bottom of this page in case it is something, and I expect to look at them in the spring and feel foolish.
