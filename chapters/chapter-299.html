Chapter 299 - Two-Thirds

The king's hearing sat in the capital on the fourteenth of April, from the ninth bell until a little after four in the afternoon, and Kaldo heard it that night between ten and one, in figures, at the top of the keep.

None of them went north. The king had written in November that they were not to come, and had named them, the son and the captain and the old man, and Ward had seen to it since that nobody argued. There was the other reason too, which they had said once at the table in March and not again. Every one of the six had a door now, and so did Armand, and so did Tess; and Emeric could feel a door across a crowded room as plainly as a lit window across a street. He had no reason to think he was the only man alive who could. He had stood to the waist in the sea off South Sentinel with his hands on a written stone and counted the people in the world who knew there was such a thing as delving, and the high Church had been the first he counted. The council sat with the lords spiritual on its right hand, as it always had. Nobody from this keep was going to sit in a gallery over those benches with a door open in his chest to find out whether any man on them could feel it.

So Keller went in for the king alone, as he had said he would, and his notes came out.

He wrote them as the hall sat, in his small square hand, and his young clerk carried them across the city at noon and again at the end of the sitting to a house on the drapers' street: three storeys and a shop, with a back room over the yard where Emeric had once slept behind a grated window the night before a hearing of his own. Saban's man kept the house. He put Keller's pages into Ferra's figures at his kitchen table, which took him until dark, and when it was dark he took them up to his attic, where there was a set.

Tess and Ivo had built it in the autumn, smaller than the line room's, a row of valves on a board the size of a tea tray, and it had gone north on the express a week before the Long Table under a bolt of grey worsted in a draper's crate. Its wire ran over the roofs from one of the house's chimneys to the next and had washing pegged along it, because Saban had insisted on the washing. It ate a crate of Kaldo's cells a fortnight, and the cells went north marked buttons.

By day nothing it said reached Kaldo. It went up and did not come down. After dark the sky gave it back, and Tess had found the hour for herself over the winter, sitting up night after night with her hand on the dial and the hiss in the box. Emeric had told her why, once, at the bench. She had found the hour, which he had not. By April it was chalked on the dial's rim, a quarter past nine, and under it, in the capitals she kept for things nobody was to touch: CAPITAL. NIGHT.



The line room was full.

The shutters stood open on a soft night, with the river loud below from the thaw and the town lit ward by ward out to the walls. Down in the inner ward the great dome held the old glass room's violet in its ribs the way two hands hold a lamp; the glaziers were back with the spring, up on the crown again by day.

Ward had the bench under the window, with a folded paper on his knee. Saban had the stool at the end, his hands on his hat. Bram filled the door. Imani sat on the floor against the wall with her knees up and her shoes beside her, where she could see everybody's face and nobody had to look at hers. Armand stood at the window, because he could stand now for as long as he liked and had not got over it, in a robe that showed a hand's width of stocking at the ankle. Tess was at the set in her chair with her fingers on the dial, and Ferra at the operator's shoulder with a pad of her own and a pencil behind her ear. The loudspeaker was turned up so that the room could hear the hiss, which was all there was to hear.

Osric had carried Tess up the keep stair at eight with Declan on the bottom end of the chair. He was sitting on the stair outside the door now, because there was no room for him inside, and he had not asked for any.

Emeric sat on the sill beside Armand with his back to the town and his sense out on the castle from habit. It was no use to him. The capital was seven hundred miles off.

At twenty to ten the hiss changed.

A man's voice came up through it, small and pressed flat by the paper cone, patient, the voice of somebody who had spent his life reading out lengths of cloth to customers who meant to argue. "Two. Nine. Nine. Four." A pause. "Two. Nine. Nine. Four."

"That's begin," said Ferra.

Saban's rule was that nothing went on the air in words, and every group went twice. The operator wrote the figures in fours as the voice gave them, and Ferra read them off over his shoulder and wrote under them in her own hand without stopping, the way she read down a column of costs and wrote its total at the foot. The key was in her head and nowhere else. When she had a sentence, she read it out.

"Ninth bell," she said. "The hall full."

Keller had begun where a magistrate begins, with who was in the room. The lords spiritual on the right of the dais, nineteen, and the cardinal at their head, who said the prayer. The lords temporal on the left, forty-five, every seat taken, which had not happened at a council in Keller's memory; His Grace of Aldane on the first bench, down from his own country through the April mud with thirty men. His Majesty in his own chair, with the council's two men at the foot of the dais steps. His Highness the Lieutenant standing behind the chair. The Princess Maeve in the gallery, in grey. The crown's magistrate at the crown's table, with the duchy's three crates under it.

And one place empty, which Keller named because a magistrate names what is missing too: the step at the king's right hand where the Marshal of the crown's host stood at every council, and had stood at every council for twenty years. General Varek was on the strait. He had been on it since the first week of March, when Kesrath put forty sail in its roads and lit fires on every hill along its shore, and the council, on the Lieutenant's motion, had sent the crown's general to the crown's border with half the crown's host behind him, which was exactly where any council in the kingdom would have sent him. Kesrath had not crossed. Kesrath had not gone home either.

"Forty sail," said Ward, "in March. That's not a fleet. That's a bill somebody paid."

"Somebody did," said Saban. "I'd like to know whose purse." He wrote it on his cuff, which he did with things he meant to keep.

"Varek won't leave it," said Ward. "Not with forty sail across the water. Not for a council. Not for anything that's not an order." He looked at the loudspeaker. "And there's nobody left to give him one but the man standing behind that chair."

"Sixty-four," said Ward, to his paper.

"Sixty-four," said Ferra, and went on.

The close had sent a canon of the cathedral to put the question, a man Keller did not trouble to name. He read it once, in the old form: whether the crown, having taken the practice of the duchy of Kaldo upon itself, had kept the oath sworn at its crowning to keep the realm in the Light. He read the heads of the twenty-six articles. He argued from the schedule and nothing else; he had Kaldo's books, Keller wrote, and needed nothing else.

"He's sent his points next," said Ferra. "He says he wrote them out last night, so they'd be on paper whatever happened to them in the hall."

There were five, and she read them flat, the way she read a contract back to the man who had signed it.

The lords spiritual had no jurisdiction over the king's person and never had. The council had found against a king twice since the old law was written, the last time three hundred years ago, and both times for an act any child in the hall could have named; nobody had named one today. To keep the realm in the Light had never been construed by any court, because it could not be: it was a prayer, and the council did not try prayers. And the crown was not a communicant. The crown was an office. An office could no more be a heretic than a seal could.

"He said that to their faces," said Bram, from the door.

"He's Keller," said Imani.

The fourth was the Seers' Table. Keller had written to Ferra in February for the class's own notebooks, every one since the first, and she had sent them up in a box of their own with Sela's minutes on top; and he had read the council what the children had seen. The small round things in Maren's bread that made it rise, drawn in a dock-quarter boy's pencil with the date beside them. Pell's dishes, a hundred pairs, and the clear ring round the green where the broth would not go bad. The blister off the cow in Hester's byre. That, Keller had told the council, was what the duchy taught its children to see that was hidden from other men, and most of it had been hidden in the bread. Then he read them the minute in Sela's hand for the week the class was named, which recorded that His Grace had told the children it was the worst name he had ever heard.

"The hall laughed," Ferra read. "The temporal side. I wrote down who." She did not look up. "He's put a list."

Emeric put his hand over his eyes.

"It is the worst name," said Armand, to the window. "I said so at the time."

"You said it was apt," said Ferra.

"I said it was apt and the worst. The two aren't exclusive."

The fifth was the word blessed. Keller had asked for the needle's roll as well, Ottoline's sorted sheets for the whole of the pox winter, every arm in four baronies by name and parish; and he had laid the Grayrock priest's letter on the crown's table with the roll beside it, open at that house in the pit rows. Visited by the pair in January. Refused. The reason written down in the vaccinator's hand, word for word, as the mother gave it. The duchy had not told that woman her prince was blessed, Keller said. It had sent the needle to her door and written down that she said no. What the duke's people paid him was a bared arm, and the duchy kept the receipts.

And under the five, as if it belonged to none of them, a line on its own, which Ferra read in the same voice and then read again because Imani asked her to: that the crown's table knew of no law, no doctrine and no council of this realm that had ever held it a sin that children did not die.

Nobody in the line room said anything.

"After each one," Ferra read on, "the canon stood up and said, it has been asked. And sat down again."

"Ah," said Armand.

They looked at him.

"It's the tower's. From the examinations." He had his hands behind his back, the way he had stood at the end of a lecture for fifty years, except that the back was straight now and did not hurt him. "When the examiners have put a question, and the student has answered it, and there's no fault in the answer, the eldest says it has been asked, and the student sits down." He looked out at the town. "And fails, as a rule. It means the question was never the point."



The afternoon came through after eleven.

The close had brought one man into the hall who was neither a lord nor a clerk, to prove a hand and not a deed: a mason of Kaldo named Gideon Marl, to say before the council that the letter in the eighteenth article had come to him in the Chancellor's own hand.

Ferra stopped writing for the length of one group, and then went on.

He said it was his before the canon had finished asking. Then he stood a moment longer, as if there were more, with his big hands closed at his sides; and the canon thanked him, and he sat down. Keller wrote that he looked at nobody on the benches while he did it, and nobody on the benches at him.



The king answered at three.

Keller had written at the head of it that he gave it as near as he could take it down, and that it was short.

His Majesty had stood, as the old law let a king stand to answer for his oath. He had said that he swore at his crowning to keep this realm in the Light. That he had read every page the council had before it. That every page was true, and he would put his seal to each of them again tomorrow, in this hall, if the council would bring him the wax. That he had nothing to add to them. Then he had sat down.

Emeric was looking at the loudspeaker's wooden box as if his father were in it.

Twice, now. A king did not stand in council, and he had stood twice, once in November to give himself into their keeping and once today to answer them; and both times, Emeric thought, he had known to the copper what it would cost.

Keller had put one line under it. His Majesty had looked at the south door a good deal through the afternoon. Keller did not know why, and had not asked.

Ward turned the paper over on his knee, and turned it back.



There had been a recess before the count, and Keller had used it.

"He's sent a page before the voices," said Ferra. "He says he's going to lose, and he'd like us to have why before the count and not after, when it'd read like an excuse."

"That's Keller," said Saban quietly. It was the first thing he had said all night.

It was not the law, Keller wrote. He had won the law. But of the forty-five on the temporal benches he could count thirty who had sent a son up the tower stair to the floor at some time in their lives, and eleven who had buried one. The council had heard that morning, from the tower's own books, that last season thirteen of the king's thirty-four out of Kaldo's base had stood up from the chamber, where one in ten had always stood. A man who had buried a son could hear only one of two things in that. Either his son had died for nothing, because there had been another road and nobody had told him; or Kaldo was a sorcerer. Keller would not ask any man to choose the first aloud, in front of his peers. He did not think many would.

Ward did not move. Emeric felt his note go very steady, and stay that way.

At the window Armand said to the glass, "I knew some of those boys." He did not say anything else, and nobody asked him to.



The count came through at a quarter to one.

The clerk had called the voices by name, the right hand first and then the left. Keller had not sent the names. He had sent the right hand's figure, nineteen against, as he had counted it, and the left as a total, with any voice that had moved from his count named after it, and two more he thought the duchy would want.

Ward unfolded his paper.

Emeric had seen it most weeks since November, on the wine store's table: the council's sixty-four in Ward's hand down a sheet of the duchy's grey in two columns, with Saban's marks against some of the names, and at the foot a figure out of Keller's last letter, which had come south on the express on the tenth. Forty-two. Two parts in three of sixty-four wanted forty-three. Ferra had done the sum in November and drawn a box round it, and every person in the room could have said the answer without looking.

The voice from the capital read a group, and the hiss came up over it like water over a weir, and the group was gone.

Tess's fingers moved on the dial by a hair. The hiss thinned. "Again," she said, to nobody within seven hundred miles who could hear her; and the voice came back, a little louder and perfectly patient, and gave the group a second time, as Saban's rule said it must.

The operator wrote. Ferra wrote under him.

"Forty-three," said Ferra. "Twenty-one."

She did not look up. The next group was already coming, because Saban's man was not in the room and did not know, and she went on with it.

"Two parts in three," she read, "and not one voice over."

Ward looked at his paper. "Forty-two," he said.

"The forty-third," Ferra read, "was Lord Grier's."

Ward found the name. Emeric watched him find it, a third of the way down the right-hand column, where it had sat since November without anybody once thinking to move it. Ward took the pencil from behind his ear and drew a line from the name across the sheet into the other column, and put the pencil back.

"He has given his voice with the crown in every matter since he took his seat," Ferra read. "He stood to give it, which he need not have done. He was sober. I make no remark on it. K."

At the end of the room Saban took his hands off his hat.

He did not say anything, and Emeric did not need him to. He had heard that name once before, at the long table in the delve the summer before last, in the flat voice Saban kept for a price read off a board, with a page of somebody else's book turning under it. And Lord Grier's. All of it.

Ferra was still reading. His Grace of Aldane had given his voice against, and had not looked at the dais while he gave it. At Aldane's name Ward did nothing at all, which Emeric had learned long ago was the most Ward ever did. And a lord of the north, of the oldest blood on the temporal benches, whose daughter the crown's council had offered the Duke of Kaldo in the autumn of the year before last, had given his voice against.

Ferra read the name, and the rest, in the same voice.

On the floor against the wall Imani said, very low, "Contraindicated."

Ferra's pencil did not stop. But Emeric saw the corner of her mouth go, once, and come back; and Bram in the door, who did not know what the word meant in that room, looked from one of them to the other and decided against asking.



The finding was the last page but one.

The council found that the oath sworn at the crowning, to keep the realm in the Light, had not been kept. It committed His Majesty to the keeping proper to a soul in question, which was a phrase out of the old law, Keller noted, and meant the chapter, and did not say so; in his own rooms, until a place should be appointed. It recorded that while its king could not act the realm by its custom was governed by its Lieutenant, and that it had one. It named no act. It did not name the chamber, or the close, or any lord spiritual, or the cardinal. It was the council's in every line, and the lords spiritual had laid the question and given their voices and done nothing else that any paper would ever show.

"The Church's name is on nothing," Ferra read. "I have read it twice."

"It's well drawn," said Saban.

"It was drawn to be read for a long time," said Ferra.

His Highness the Lieutenant had given no voice, Keller wrote. He had stood behind the chair all day with his hand on its back. When the count was given he had not sat down in it. He kept his hand where it was and said, loudly enough for the gallery: I'll keep it for him. Until he's home.

There was one more line of Keller's, a short one at the very foot of the sheet, the last thing the operator took down before the voice in the paper cone said two, nine, nine, four, end. The Lieutenant's first act, the same evening, under his own seal: the half of the crown's host still in the capital, in the Marshal's absence on the strait, to the command of His Grace of Aldane, his uncle, until the Marshal's return.

Nobody at the table said anything for a moment.

"Until the Marshal's return," said Ferra, "from a border he can't leave, until a fleet goes home that somebody is paying to stay." She wrote the date in her book and drew a box round it, which she did for nothing else. "So Aldane has the crown's army in the capital, and the king is in a room in the capital."

Emeric did not say anything. He was looking at the box again.

"He means it," said Imani.

"He means one of the things it means," said Ferra.

Emeric thought of a careful hand in a margin, copied out in red, and a single small question mark beside a figure the hand did not understand. He did not know which of the things Corbin had meant. He was not sure Corbin did.

The Princess Maeve had folded her paper very small during the count, Keller wrote, and taken it away with her. The cardinal had said the prayer over the finding, as he had said it at the ninth bell, and between the two had said nothing at all. He had looked at the gallery a good deal. Keller did not know whom he had expected to see in it.

Emeric felt Imani turn her head on the floor and look up at him. He did not look back. He looked at the box with the paper cone in it and thought about a gallery over the right-hand benches, and the door in his own chest, and seven hundred miles; and was glad of every one of them, and did not like being glad.

The last page was short. His Majesty had asked, going out between the council's two men, whether this would reach Kaldo tonight. Keller had said he believed it would, by means he had not asked about. His Majesty had said: then tell my son the order stands.

"I have told you," Ferra read. "K."

The voice gave a last group, twice, and nothing after it. The hiss came back up and filled the box.

"That's end," said Ferra.



Nobody moved for a while. The operator took the earpiece off and laid it on the board and sat with his hands in his lap, which was a thing the line room had taught him.

Tess turned the gain down until the hiss was a breath, and then off.

"Not a group lost," she said. "All night."

Ferra looked at the pad, the whole of it, page after page of figures with her plain words under them, every one there. "No," she said. "Not one."

They went down the way people go down from a room after a burying, in ones and twos, without anybody saying it was time. Ward folded his paper into his coat and went without a word. Armand went down the stair three steps at a time, because he could, and could be heard doing it all the way to the bottom.

Tess had a book on her knees under her rug. When Bram came across for her chair she held it up to him.

"Bram."

It was the Dust House log: fat, in oilcloth, with the island's salt dried white along its corners, and a slip of red paper standing up out of it near the back.

"The cube," she said. "The warm one. It's all there, what I did and what I didn't. The page with the red." She did not let go of it yet. "If it matters while I'm out, run it."

"Run it how?"

"However it says. I've written how." She let go. "You read my hand better than anybody."

He put the book inside his coat, flat against his chest, and buttoned the coat over it. Then he put his hand on the back of her neck, once, the way he did, and took it away.

"The island boat goes on the seventeenth," he said.

"I know. I'll be under." She looked up at him. "You'll be at the collar first. Then you'll go."

"Hm," said Bram.

Osric came in off the stair and took the chair's front, and Bram took the back without being asked, and they carried her down between them, the smith and the knight, a step at a time, with Tess sitting very upright in the middle saying left, left, your left, Osric, at every turn of the well.

When Emeric looked back from the door, Ferra was still at the table, and Imani had come up off the floor to sit beside her with her head against Ferra's shoulder. Ferra had her own book open. She wrote the date, and under it, 43 to 21, in figures, and under that nothing.

"It's half past one," said Imani. "Come to bed."

"In a minute," said Ferra, and drew a box round it.



Imani had said yes on the second of April.

She had said it in her surgery, with Armand's sheet open on the table between them, all of it: the six weeks under in her hand and Bettany's, the dark lines and the shed and the jar, and after them February's checks, Ferra's and Emeric's, every page. She had read it through once more that morning while Tess sat in her chair across the table with her hands in her lap and said nothing for an hour and twenty minutes, which Tess said afterward was the longest she had gone without speaking since the forge loft. Then Imani had shut the sheet and said yes; and then, because a yes was no use without a day, the sixteenth. The Hod would be home from the Band on the fifteenth with every canister on the dock full, and Bram's new boards would be dry.

On the afternoon of the sixteenth Emeric was in Imani's surgery reading Tess's card, because she had asked him to.

"Tell me what I've missed," she said, and went on writing at her table.

It was Armand's card, near enough, in the same plain upright hand. The first hours were the stone's. Every hour after them, arms above the cuffs and both sides of the neck, with pulse and breath and heat; lines that came and went were the work; a line that stayed an hour by the watch on the box's shelf meant the mending had stopped and she came out, and coming out meant the room bled down through the box over a day. Under that, copied whole: a line that stays dark is not always a line that has stopped; look before you bleed the room. And one line new, at the bottom, underlined. Nothing that thins the blood. Nothing at all. Ask me first. I.

"You've not missed anything," he said.

"I missed something last time." She did not look up. "Read it again."

He was reading it again when he felt the boy on the stair: a small quick anxious note that came up the hospital stair two at a time and stopped on the landing outside the surgery a long while, the way a note stops when its owner is deciding something. Then it knocked.

It was the boy who swept the Motion Shop after school. His name was Tibb; he was eleven, and had swept round Tess's chair since he was eight, and he had grit from the shop floor in the turn-ups of his trousers and his cap in both hands.

"Please, missus," he said to Imani. "I want to ask you for one of the grey ones. Like Miss Tess has. On her wrist, under the strap. She takes it off to wash at the trough, and it's grey, like an egg." He looked at his cap. "She told the shop she's going to sleep in the glass garden a while, like the old master did. And the old master was bad, and he's not now. So I thought it must be the grey one, because what else would." He took a breath. "And I've got my chest."

He coughed, as if to show her, and went red because he had.

"I'm off the shop every winter with it. Miss Tess says it's not my fault." He looked at the cap. "It's still off."

Imani looked at him for a while. Then she came round the table and said, "Shirt up," and he pulled it to his chin, and she put her ear to his back and then the flat of her hand, and told him to cough, and listened, and told him to do it again. Emeric watched her face go away from the room into one ear and five fingertips, as it did over a chest, and come back.

She pulled his shirt down.

"There's a whistle at the bottom of the left side," she said. "That's the wet in your mother's back room, and you'll have it until you're out of that room, and I'm writing to the Works Office about the room tonight. That's your chest."

"And the grey one?"

She sat on the edge of her table, so that her face was level with his.

"No," she said. "And I'll tell you why, because you came up my stair on your own to ask, and that wants a proper answer." She held her hand flat at the height of his head, and then lifted it a hand higher. "You'll be about there in four years. Your bones aren't done. You're still being drawn, all of you, every night while you sleep. What Miss Tess is going to have, it can't be done to a drawing that isn't finished. It would wait. It would put you to sleep under that tree for months, and wait, and you'd wake up eleven, with the same chest, and the summer gone, and the shop swept by somebody else."

Tibb thought about it with his lips pressed together, the way Sela's school taught.

"How old's finished?"

"Eighteen. Some are sooner and some later, and I'll not guess which you are, so it's eighteen for everybody."

"That's seven years."

"It is."

He picked his cap up off her table. "Wren said you'd say no. I said I'd ask anyway."

"Good," said Imani. "Always ask." She stood up. "And when somebody says no to you, you don't go and do it anyway in the middle of the night while they're asleep."

Tibb looked at her with no idea in the world what she meant, and said he wouldn't, missus, and went; and Emeric felt his note go down the stair a good deal slower than it had come up, and out into the lane, and stop, and then go off toward the works at a run.

"That last bit was for Armand," said Emeric.

"It was for everybody." Imani took her coat off its hook. "Come on."

She took him down to the foot of the dispensary stair, where the hospital's book stood on its slope by the door: not hers, the hospital's, where the ward's rules went once they had stopped being only hers. The boiled water. The laundry. The hour for the doors. Thirteen years of them, in her hand and Bettany's. She turned to the next clean line and wrote standing up.

16 April, yr 13. The garden. Nobody under eighteen, whatever they ask for and whoever asks for them. Their bones aren't finished, and it can't work on a thing that isn't finished. It sleeps them and changes nothing. I.

She blotted it.

"There. Now it's not mine. Now it's the hospital's, and when I'm gone it's still there." She shut the book. "Ferra taught me that. She doesn't know she did."



The inner ward had gone green while nobody was looking. There was grass along the paths where there had been mud since the footings, and it was still full afternoon, and the old pear by the gate was in blossom from its top to its lowest fork, the way it had been the April it snowed.

Armand was on the bench under it. He was early everywhere now. He sat the way a man sits who has been told by a surgeon to rest and has decided to do it as hard as he can, legs out, ankles crossed, face turned up into the blossom; and Ferra stood beside the bench with her stone book under her arm, reading figures off the back of an envelope.

Emeric felt the squirrel before he saw it.

It was a very small note. Beasts carried less than people did, pound for pound, so much less that the wall sensor had never once rung for a dog, and this was not a dog: a pinprick, quick and warm and busy, up in the pear over Armand's head. He looked. It was halfway along a branch, red-brown, with its tail up over its back and tufts on its ears, holding a cluster of blossom in both hands and eating it from the stalk end, the way a man eats a pear.

"That's next September," said Ferra, without looking up from the envelope.

"What is?"

"What it's eating. That tree gave the kitchen four bushels last year." She turned the envelope over. "It's had a peck of them since Tuesday."

The squirrel dropped the stalk on Armand. Armand did not move.

Emeric watched it finish, and run up the branch to the next cluster, and stop there with one forefoot raised to look at nothing, and start again. Kaldo's air was the thickest in the four baronies. This was a creature that had been born in it and slept in it and eaten out of it all its life; and its note was a squirrel's note, as far as he could tell. He had never thought to feel for a squirrel anywhere else. He had nothing to hold it against.

"Bettany's rats," he said to Imani. "The dense shelf. Are they any different?"

Imani had sat down on the end of the bench, since Armand had not moved his legs for her. She thought before she answered, which meant the answer was not a simple one.

"Heavier. A tenth, maybe. That's the feed." She squinted up at the branch. "And they've learned the feed door. Bettany put a peg through the hasp in the winter because the latch wouldn't hold, and they worked the peg out. So she wired it. They're working on the wire."

"And the plain shelf?"

"Sits and waits to be fed, like gentlemen." She shrugged. "It could be nothing. The dense shelf's fed better and handled more. Bettany likes them best, and she'd tell you she doesn't."

"How many litters since she started them?" said Ferra.

"Ask Bettany. A good many. A rat's a grandmother before she's a year old, and dead at two, mostly. Three if she's lucky." Imani shrugged again. "Bettany writes every one of them in a book when it goes. She cries over the book, and she'd tell you she doesn't."

Ferra wrote something small on the envelope. "Then the town should be clever in about four hundred years."

"Parts of it," said Imani.

Emeric did not laugh. He was thinking of a tooth on a sailcloth between two beer rings, bleeding its mana out into this air as if this air were nothing; and of whatever had grown it, which had not lived here, and had not been a rat.

"Has anybody ever put an animal through the rite?" he said.

Armand opened his eyes.

"The tower did," he said, to the blossom. "Long before me. A hound first, the ledgers said, and then horses, because a cavalry horse that never tired would have bought the tower a duchy. Every one of them died on the floor, and most of them quicker than the boys did." He took a petal off his sleeve and looked at it. "The cathedral had a sermon out of it inside the month. They still read it to the tower's first-years, to show them what the floor is for."

He put on a voice a little higher and rounder than his own.

"The beasts of the field were not made in the likeness of the Light, and the Light does not choose them. They are lower things, and cannot bear its touch. Here again we see how great the Light is, that it will not stoop; and here again we see how its choosing lifts the worthy out of the dust they came from." The voice went away, and his own came back. "There are four pages more. I forgot them on purpose."

"How much did they give the hound?"

Armand looked at him properly for the first time.

"A boy's measure," he said. "What else would they have given it?"

At the end of the bench Imani had gone still, the way she went still over a card with a wrong figure on it.

A boy's measure, into a body that carried a fraction of a boy's mana to the pound. They had poured a man's cup into a thimble and watched it run over, and preached on the thimble for a hundred years.

"Mm," said Emeric.

He held his hand out to Imani, palm up.

"What?"

"The bread in your pocket."

"That's for two in the morning." But she gave it him, the heel of a loaf in a twist of paper, and watched him with no expression at all while he broke off a corner and went and crouched at the foot of the pear and held it up on his flat hand and made the noise that people make at squirrels.

The squirrel stopped eating. It looked down at him. It looked at the bread. It looked at him again for rather a long time, with the blossom still in its hands; and then it turned its head away, quite slowly, the way a woman at a dance turns away from a man she has decided not to see, and went on with September.

Armand laughed. It was a young man's laugh and seemed to surprise him.

"Do the noise again," said Imani.

He did the noise again. The squirrel ate.

"It has a whole tree," said Ferra. "You have a crust."

"Pull it," said Armand.

"No."

"Why not? You pull everything else."

"It'd come out of that tree through the air screaming and take my thumb off." He put the crust down on the grass between two roots and stood up and stepped back, three paces, and stood still. "And I want it calm."

"Then you'll want to be quicker than a squirrel," said Armand, and shut his eyes again.

Emeric looked up at it. It had seen the bread, and weighed the bread, and weighed him, and found the tree better; it was all there in the way it held the blossom. He could have gone up the trunk after it. He could have put it in his legs, Rylan's way, and been at the roots before it knew, if it ever came down to them. And then he would have had a squirrel in his fist with its heart going faster than he could count and its teeth in his thumb, and nothing in it calm enough to ask.

He left the crust between the roots and came back to the bench.

"Not today," he said.

"You're letting a squirrel win," said Armand.

"I'm letting it get used to me."

"What do you want with it?" said Imani. "Honestly."

"I want to ask it something."

"It's a squirrel."

"I know."

Imani gave him the look she gave a patient who had told her the pain was nothing. Then she looked up into the pear. "Well, don't name it."

"I've named it," said Ferra.

"What?"

"September."

The squirrel finished its cluster. It came down the trunk head first in little stops, flat to the bark, its tail going; stopped at the fork; came on; stopped at the roots with its nose working at the crust from a foot off. It looked at the four of them on the bench. Then it hopped once, and had the crust up in both hands, and went back up the pear with it in three runs, and sat on the branch over Armand's head and ate Imani's two in the morning without any hurry at all, looking at Emeric the whole time.

"There," said Armand, who had not opened his eyes. "Now it's used to you."

Ferra wrote on the back of the envelope, under the bushels: 16 April. Offered September a crust. September took the crust.

She looked at it, and drew a line under it, and put the envelope in the stone book.



Tess drove herself in at twenty past seven, in her own chair, without a mask, while the last of the light went off the great dome's glass.

They were all in the pass-box's outer room, which had not been built for it. Bram had the wheel. He had opened it at seven, past his own chalk, wide, with both hands, and stood with his palm on it while the pipe began to sing in the iron under the floor. Ferra had the feed gauge book open at a clean page with Tess's name at the top, and her stone book under her arm. Ward had the cuffs out of the iron press and his book down off its nail. Imani's card was pinned up under ONE AT A TIME. Saban stood at the back with his hat. Armand stood in the outer door because there was no more room inside it. And Osric stood by the box with a book under his arm, and did not look at anybody.

It was the electricity book. Emeric knew it by its board covers gone soft at the corners: the book he had written for her years ago, the one she had learned the dynamo from and kept on the shelf over her bed. Nobody had told Osric to bring it. Nobody had told him which.

Tess looked at it under his arm. Then she looked at him, and said nothing at all, which from Tess was a great deal.

"You can sit with her," said Imani to Osric, "if you're useful."

"What's useful?"

"Lift her, first. Then read. Loud."

"Can she hear?"

"I don't know. Nobody knows. Read loud anyway."

Tess had turned her chair to her sister. "Make him go home at midnight," she said. "He won't for you. He'll go for Imani, so make Imani."

"I'll make Imani," said Ferra.

"I can hear you," said Imani.

"Good," said Tess.

She unbuckled the leather band at her wrist, the one she had worn every day and every night for nearly two years, and took the stone out of it, a long small grey egg, and held it in her fist; and handed the empty band to Ferra without looking, the way she handed a tool back across a bench. Then she drove into the box, and Imani shut the outer door behind her and opened the inner.

Through the glass they watched her go down the path under the tree at a walking pace, the motor whining faintly in its drum, the tree's violet on her hair. Bram's new boards lay north of the trunk, beside the place where Armand had lain all winter, with the head to the roots: new oak, a straw mattress, a folded blanket and a jug. She drew the chair up alongside them, square, as she drew up to a bench, and turned the motor off.

Osric went in next, masked. He talked the whole way down the path; they could hear him through the box and could not make out a word, only the sound of a man talking because the alternative was not talking. He put one arm under her knees and the other behind her back and lifted her out of the chair, as he had lifted her out of her own at Wayford the day it was wanted for its stones, and laid her on Bram's straw as carefully as a man setting down a full glass. She said something to him. He said something back. Then he came out, and pulled the mask off, and stood with it in his hand, red to the ears.

"What did she say?" said Ferra.

"She said I was enjoying it." Osric looked at the mask. "I said I was being useful."

Ward went in with the cuffs.

He did the ankles first, rolling her trousers to the knee and putting the bands on over her stockings, as he had done them for Emeric; and Tess lay propped on her elbows on the boards and watched him at it.

"They don't cast," she said, loud, for the glass. "My legs. They don't do anything. I can't feel them."

"You don't know what they'll do," said Ward. "Nor do I. So they're cuffed."

He did the wrists. When he had turned the key in the last one he did not leave it in the lock. He put it back on its cord, and the cord inside his coat, and buttoned the coat over it.

"Armand left his in," said Tess.

"Armand had stolen his."

In the outer door Armand inclined his head, slightly, to nobody.

Ward came out and wrote in his book, in his own square hand this time: 16 April. Stone cuffs, two sets, out. Tess. W. He hung it back on its nail.

Imani went in last, masked, with her watch.

She knelt on the roots at the head of the boards; and Tess lay on the straw with her cuffed hands on the blanket and the stone in her right fist, and turned her left hand over and looked at her thumb. The old burn ran along it from the nail to the joint, pale and shiny, the one she had had since she was eleven, before the plague year and Bram's house. She looked at it the way she looked at a part before she put it in its drawer: once, all of it, to know where it was.

Then she looked at the glass.

"Somebody stay," she said.

"Everybody's staying," said Imani. "Look at them."

Tess looked. Bram at the glass with his hand flat on it. Ferra beside him with the empty band. Ward. Saban, come forward from the back. Armand in the door. Osric with the book. Emeric.

"That's too many," said Tess. "Make Bram sit down. He's been standing since four."

She drank from Bram's jug, because Imani held it to her mouth. Then she put the stone in her mouth and swallowed it dry, with the face she made at a bad weld, and lay back on the straw.

Emeric felt it go.

Her note went down the way a lamp goes down when somebody turns the wick: not out, only down, and steady. And her door, the one he had brought her through by the hand in her room over the works the summer before last, opened in her and went on opening, wider than he had ever felt it stand, and the room began to go into her from every side.

Imani counted with two fingers at her throat, and counted again.

"Seventy," she said at the inner door, coming out on the fourth minute. "In step. She's gone under." She pulled the mask down. "She said something, going. I didn't catch it."

"What did it sound like?" said Bram.

Imani thought about it. "Like she'd found the fault."

Bram sat down on the crate by the collar.

Ferra opened her stone book on the box's shelf at the last page, under Inventory, sundry, below the old man's pencil and her own ruled line beneath it, and wrote: 16 April. Tess's, from her band. Swallowed beside the tree, twenty to eight. F. She ruled nothing under it, and put the empty band inside the cover.

"I'll take tomorrow night," said Armand, from the door. "I owe that box six weeks."

"You owe it a great deal more than six weeks," said Imani. "You can start tomorrow."



The first line came up at ten to nine.

Imani saw it from the stool in the box's door before she had gone in for the hour: a thread of silver inside Tess's left wrist, above the cuff, fine and bright as a scratch on new tin, branching once. It dulled while they watched, and sank, and was gone; and a little higher up the arm another came, and went.

"That's the work," said Imani; and went in at nine, and came out, and wrote it. Came and went.

Osric read from eight until past one.

He sat on the paving with his back against the box's iron and the electricity book on his knees, and on Imani's hours, when she had the inner door open a hand's width, he read to the gap. He read the way Tess had taught him at her drafting board at night years ago, with nobody to know: a finger under the line and his lips a word ahead of his voice, slow at the long words and steady at the rest, and loud, as he had been told.

Emeric heard his own sentences come back to him across the years in a garrison knight's voice. He had forgotten most of them. He had forgotten how many commas he used to use, and how sure he had been.

"A current must go round," Osric read, "or it does not go at all. There is no such thing as half a circuit. If it will not go, look for the place where the round is broken. It is nearly always a joint."

"It is nearly always a joint," said Ferra, on the crate, without looking up from the feed gauge book.

"He's very sure of himself," said Imani from the stool, "for a young man."

"I was right."

"You were sure," said Imani. "Read on, Osric."

At midnight Imani told Bram to go home, and Bram said hm, and sat a while longer with his hands hanging between his knees, and then got up in two goes and went. Emeric let his sense follow him out across the ward. He did not go home. He went to the forge, and the forge's lamp went up in its window and stayed up; and nobody in the outer room said anything about it.

Ward slept on the camp bed behind the screen post in his coat and boots, and woke at every hour as Imani went in, and went back to sleep as she came out, without seeming to know he had done either.

Some time after one Osric stopped in the middle of the dynamo. His head had gone back against the iron and his mouth had come open, and his finger was still under the line where it had stopped. Ferra got up and took the book off his knees without waking him, and marked his place with a feed slip, and wrote something on the slip. Imani threw her spare blanket over him from the stool. He did not stir.



After that it was the three of them.

They took the hours in turn. The card said Imani's hours were nine to six, and Imani had written the card, and Imani gave up the stool at two without a word when Ferra held her hand out for the watch. She had taught them both the day before at her surgery table, two fingers and the watch, the arm turned to the lamp by its cuff, the neck one side and then the other, what a line looked like that was working and what one looked like that was not; and made them practise on each other until Ferra said she would know his pulse in the dark, and Imani said that was the idea. Ferra went in on the two o'clock hour and did every part of it in order, exactly as shown, as if somebody might audit her afterward; and came out, and wrote.

Imani leaned across and looked at the sheet.

"Bigger."

"It fits the column."

"Bettany reads it at six. Bettany hasn't got your eyes. Bigger."

Ferra wrote the line again underneath, very large and very slowly, so that every letter stood up on its own like a fence post; and Imani laughed into Emeric's shoulder without any sound, and stayed there.

That was how they did it in the end, without anybody saying that was how they would. Whoever had the hour had the stool. Whoever did not slept against whoever did. At two Imani slept on the paving with her back to the iron and her head on Emeric's shoulder, and her hand found his wrist in her sleep and lay on it with two fingers over the pulse, which she would deny in the morning. At three Emeric had the stool, and went in without a mask, and looked, and came out and wrote came and went on her sheet in his own hand for the first time; and when he sat down Ferra was asleep against the side of the box with her feet across Imani's knees. At four Imani had it, and he and Ferra were on the paving, and Ferra was not asleep.

"Forty-two and two-thirds," she said.

He waited.

"Two parts in three of sixty-four. It's forty-two and two-thirds. I did it in November and boxed it, and I've done it again every hour since the fifteenth, as if it'll come out different if I do it often enough." She had her eyes shut. "They needed two-thirds of a man."

"They got a whole one," said Imani, from the stool.

"They got all of Grier." Ferra opened her eyes and looked up at the glass where the violet came through. "And the king's in the chapter's keeping on a paper that doesn't say chapter, and his son's on a paving stone at four in the morning watching my sister breathe, which is the right thing for him to be doing tonight. I want that written down somewhere."

"I'm not watching her."

"You've had your sense on her since twenty to eight." She shut her eyes again. "I can tell."

He did not answer, because she was right, and because the only other thing he could have said was about a gallery and seven hundred miles, and he did not want to say it on this paving.

Imani turned on the stool.

"Are you going to?" she said.

She did not say to what. She did not need to.

"He said the order stands."

"That's not what I asked."

He did not say anything. After a while Ferra's hand came across in the half dark and found his on the paving and held it, and did not ask him anything either. Imani watched the two of them from the stool a while, and then looked at the watch on the shelf, and put her mask up, and went in.

When she came out she sat and wrote. Then she reached down from the stool without looking and put her hand on his head, and left it there.

"Came and went," she said.



Bettany came at six with her two coats over her arm, and read the night's sheet from the top, standing, every hour of it, with her lips moving. At the two o'clock line she held the sheet nearer the lamp.

"Who wrote this one?"

"I did," said Ferra.

Bettany looked at it a while longer, and at the line under it, the one with letters like fence posts.

"It's very small, Chancellor," she said. "The first one." And she wrote her own six o'clock under the lot of them, large.

Ferra got up off the paving in two goes, like her father, and read the night's fall off the gauge into the feed gauge book under Tess's name, and said it aloud, because she always did: seventeen, since twenty to eight. Imani said that was less than either of the others, and Ferra said she could see that, it was a number. They went out together into a grey morning with the glaziers' ladders already going up against the great dome's ribs; and Emeric stopped in the outer room's door and looked back.

Osric had woken. He was sitting up against the iron under Imani's blanket, rubbing his face, and he had found the book on the crate beside him and opened it at the feed slip. Emeric could read the slip from the door, in Ferra's small hand. Page sixty. You stopped at the brushes. F.

Osric looked at it for a long time. Then he found the line with his finger, and cleared his throat, and went on reading to the gap in the box's door, from the place where he had stopped.
