Chapter 196 - One to a Mark

He had to know, and there was only one way to know, and he had done it once already and had not liked it then.

Harrow Bridge had put it in him. Not the fighting. The hour at the long table in the counting house with Saban's word mages in the air, when the sum had come out all at once: hundreds of cheap stones, bought in the capital all winter and carted north under canvas, for nothing anybody could name, unless you meant to break them. A man who wanted a town dark did not need an army. He needed a sack of flawed lamp stones and the stomach to stand near them when they went. Aldane had had the stomach. The stream ground had gone dead, and every stone on it.

The quarry had answered half of that. A fire crystal let go in a drilled face, and the lamp stone on the ledge had gone out between one breath and the next, and the light on the mast had shivered and burned on, white, because the wheel at the sawmill did not care what the medium was doing and neither did the copper. Current went through a dead field as if the field were not there. He had built half a city's plans on that night.

And now he was building the other half on a box full of gravity stones.

So on a hot afternoon in the first week of summer, with the draw bench in the shed pulling copper and the knock of the pair coming through the plank wall as steady as a clock, he went round into the yard behind the shed with a steel and a double-jack, and a block of sandstone off the foundry's scrap heap, and a chip of crystal the size of his thumbnail wrapped in a rag in his pocket.

It was a bad stone. He had chosen it for that. A pump stone with a fault through it like a hair in glass, off the bottom of a tray at the etching floor where Vesk kept the ones that would never be worth the cutting. It was the kind of stone Aldane had bought by the hundred. If the box could stand one of these a yard off its side, it could stand whatever a man could carry to its door in a sack.

He drilled the block where it stood, against the shed's back wall, a yard from where the box stood on its base plate on the other side of the planks. Bram came out and watched him do it and did not offer to take the jack, because he knew better. Ward stood at the yard gate with two of his men and let nobody through it. Armand had come down on his stick and sat now on the shady side of the yard on his stool, with his stick across his knees and his hat over his eyes, looking, to anybody on the foundry side of the gate, like an old man asleep in the sun.

Tobe was inside, at the lock, with the day's flask in the chamber and his hand nowhere near the key. He had been told to do nothing, whatever he felt, whatever the knocking did, and Tobe did what he was told to the letter and always had.

On the shed's back door, on a nail, Bram had hung a lamp stone, lit. Its light was nothing in the afternoon. It did not need to be anything. It needed to go out.

Emeric put the chip in the hole and packed the mouth with wet clay off Bram's boot, and put his palm flat on the stone above it.

"Slowly," Armand said from under his hat. "Then all at once."

"I remember."

"You remember the words. Do it the way I said them."

He did. He gave the chip what it wanted, and then a little more than it wanted, and then more, feeling it drink and swell and strain against the fault in it, the way a man feels a rope go taut in his hands before he feels it fray. It was a small stone and a weak one. It did not take long. He held it on the edge, where Armand had taught him in the quarry to hold it, for the space of two breaths, so that when it went it would go all at once and not in a dribble. Then he pushed.

It went with a sound like a knuckle cracking under the block, and a spit of clay out of the hole. Then the medium went out of the yard like the heat going out of a room when a door is opened on a winter night, all over his skin at once, and he had his hand on a dead stone in dead ground and could feel nothing, nothing at all, as though he had gone deaf in a sense he had never had a word for.

The lamp stone on the door went grey.

Bram said, "There," quietly, and lifted his left arm a little way from his side and let it fall again. The band on his wrist had gone heavy. It went heavy the way it had gone heavy in the quarry, like a ring of lead, and he stood with his arm hanging and did not rub it.

And through the plank wall, the knock.

It did not falter. It did not slow. Knock, and knock, the ring going round under the weights inside the iron, one down while the other came up. On the shed roof above them, where the shaft came up through the box's lid and on through the roof boards to its pulley, the balls of the governor went round and round in the sun, level, flung out at the distance they had held all morning, and the belt ran, and inside the shed the draw bench hissed and the wire came off the reel.

Nobody said anything. Emeric took his hand off the block and found that he had been holding his breath and let it go.

Armand got up off his stool. It took him a while, and nobody helped him, because he had made it plain years ago what he thought of being helped up. He came across the yard with his stick, slowly, into the dead ground, and in through the back door past the grey lamp stone, and Emeric went after him.

The box stood in the shed as it always stood, black and riveted, sweating a little in the heat, knocking. Tobe was at the lock with his hands at his sides, as he had been told. Armand went up to the box and put his hand on it, flat, on the side panel above the lock, the way he had put his hand on the quarry mast in the dark, and on a thousand stones before that, and held it there.

He held it a long time. Emeric watched his face, and it did what it had done at the quarry, which was nothing.

"Nothing," Armand said at last.

Emeric put his own hand beside the old man's. Through the iron, very faint, so faint he would not have been sure of it if he had not been standing in a field of absolute nothing that made the faintest thing loud, he felt it: the box full. Packed. The way he had felt the first flasks through their iron the morning Ned Tallis carried them up from the lake in a sack, like a banked fire felt through the door of a stove. Outside the iron there was nothing at all. Inside it, a yard away, the band.

"It's there," he said. "It's all there."

"I know it's there," said Armand. "The shaft's turning. I can't feel it and I can hear it." He took his hand away and looked at it. "It isn't dead. It's shut. It's your first cell, lying in the iron shell Bram made for it with nothing touching it but iron and the cap screwed down. I'd have wagered you could break a stone on that cell's lid and it would never know. Now I needn't wager." He looked at the box. "This is that cell, the size of a shed."

Tobe put the day's second flask through the lock at the hour it was due, while the shed stood in dead ground and the lamp stone hung grey on the door. Outer door, bar, inner door, key. The knocking picked up a little, as it always did, and the balls swung out a little and eased it. Nothing about it was different. The lock opened on nothing but the box.

The yard filled back in over the rest of the afternoon and into the evening, slowly, from the edges, the way Armand's notebook said a dead place did. At dusk the lamp stone on the door came up, faint, and by full dark it was burning as it always burned. Bram's band went light on his wrist. He did not say anything about it. He went back in to the bench.

That night Emeric wrote it in his own book, under the quarry, in one line: a dead field outside the iron does nothing to what is inside it. And under that, because a thing proved once is only proved once, and he had spent two lives learning what that meant: Once. In daylight. A small stone.

Wes came up from the lake the week after.

He had been the boy in the Hod's hold on her first night, with Garrick and a lantern, calling up through the hatch in a voice that went high when the cells began to glow. That had been years ago, and he was not a boy now, though everybody on the lower water still called him one, because that was how the lower water was. He had gone to hull two with Garrick in the spring before the Hod went into the sea, to learn her racks before she flew, and he had been in her hold ever since, two nights in seven through the dark of the year. He had filled more racks and carried more flasks than any man in Kaldo but Ned Tallis. He could tell a full flask from an empty one by the heft of it in the dark, though he had no gift at all, or had not had one; Armand's readings said he had some now, a little, like everybody who had gone up long enough. He had been told so at the long-room table with the rest. He had not asked what it meant.

Saban had asked Ned for a man who could learn the lock and not be frightened of it, and Ned had sent Wes, because, Ned said, Wes was not frightened of anything that did not deserve it, and was careful of everything that did.

Tobe taught him. Tobe taught the way he did everything, with as few words as the thing would bear. The outer door. The bar. The inner door. The key, a half turn, steady, and hold it there until the lid is off, and you'll feel it go through the handle, a sort of shiver; then back, and the inner door, and the bar, and the outer. The empty comes out and goes in the rack by the door, lid up, so anybody can see it's empty. The book on the shelf; you write the hour.

"When?" Wes said.

"When she wants it."

"How do I know when she wants it?"

Tobe thought about it. He had known for two months, and he had not once had to say how, because nobody had asked him.

"You'll hear it," he said. "She goes slow. Heavy. The knock goes long, like a man walking uphill. The balls come in." He nodded at the roof. "Then she wants it."

Wes listened to the knock for a long time, with his head on one side. Then he nodded, because it was a thing he understood. It was the Hod. Every man who had ever flown her had it in him like the ground under his feet: when she goes slow and heavy under you, when the deck starts to go, you open a flask, and if one flask isn't enough you open the next one, and you don't stop to think about it, because the men who stopped to think about it were at the bottom of the sea. Ned had opened all four, one after another, on the night the rod cracked, and Tobe had been there with his hands on the rail. Everybody on the lower water knew it. On hull two there was a stone at the helm that went cold under the pilot's hand when the lift bank began to run on what it held, and the drill for a cold stone was a flask by the lift bank, and if the stone stayed cold, another.

That was what Wes heard, when Tobe said slow and heavy. He heard the deck going out from under him.

Emeric was in the shed for most of that week. He had been in the shed most nights since the pair first turned. He told himself it was for the dies, which was true: the bench was drawing copper finer than the plate he had cut two autumns ago had been made for, and he was cutting a second plate in the corner by the lamp, a hole at a time, with a steel and a glass and a great deal of patience. It was also because he liked the knocking. He did not tell anybody that.

He watched Wes learn. He watched him on the first night and the second, with Tobe at his elbow, and on the third with Tobe across the shed at the bench, and saw that he was good: slow and steady at the bar, never hurrying the key. He heard Tobe tell him when, and how, and heard the words slow and heavy, and thought nothing of them.

He would think of them afterward for a long time.

Bram had put the pair on the bench day and night by then. It did not tire. The rolls' wheel drew iron by night and rail by day and could not be asked for more, and the big house that the pair was the small brother of would want more copper wire than a summer of days held. So the pair drew through the night as well, with one of Ward's men at the shed door and a pourer at the lock and Jorrin or one of his lads at the reel.

On Wes's fourth night Tobe went across to the foundry's cookhouse at the second hour after dark, because he had not eaten since noon, and left Wes at the lock.

Tobe had put a flask in at dusk. It was in the book, in Tobe's square hand, with the hour. The box was full.

Emeric was at the bench in the corner with the die plate in a vice and his eye to the glass. He heard the bench snag before he saw it. Everybody in the shed heard it: a sound like a cough in the reel, and the lad at the reel saying something short and bad, and the rod in the die plate jamming where a seam in the casting had caught at the hole, so that the chain came up tight as a bar and the drum stopped turning and the whole weight of it came back down the belt onto the shaft.

The shaft slowed.

The balls came in. The rod came down. And inside the box, on the third brass lever, the governor leaned the only way it had been built to lean when the shaft went slow: heavier. Both drops heavy. The knocking went long and deep, like a man walking uphill with a load, and slowed, and slowed.

Emeric lifted his head from the glass. He knew what it was. He knew it before the lad at the reel had finished swearing: the bench had snagged, the box was full, and the governor was doing what it was for. In a moment the lad would get the rod free, or Jorrin would throw the belt off, and the shaft would pick up and the balls go out and the knocking ease. There was nothing wrong. He began to say so.

Wes was already at the lock. He had a flask out of the rack. He was not running. He had never run in his life in the Hod's hold or in hull two's; a man who runs in a hold falls over something. He put the flask in the lock, lid to the socket, and shut the outer door and threw the bar and opened the inner door, in the order Tobe had taught him, steady, all of it in the time it took Emeric to put the steel down and stand up.

"Wes," Emeric said.

Wes turned the key.

For a breath there was nothing. The knock went long, once more, very slow and very heavy. Then the box cracked.

It was not loud. It was a crack like a stick breaking under a boot, and every man in the shed who had ever been on the lower water heard the rod go in the Hod's hold, and froze. Then the soft panel went.

It went the way Emeric had drawn it to go on the day he drew it: all its soft rivets at once, with a noise like a handful of gravel thrown on a roof, and the panel out of the box's side and down into the sand pit, flat, in a spray of sand. Out of the hole where it had been came heat, a gust of it like an oven door opened, and with the heat something else that was not air and not heat. It came out across the shed in one rush and went past them and through them. Emeric felt it on his face without it being wind. He felt it go into him through the skin, thick, sweet, the band on the ground for the space of a breath, as though somebody had opened a door onto the top of the sky. The lamp by the lock flared white, far whiter than a lamp stone had any business being, so that every man in the shed threw a hard black shadow up the walls. Wes stood in it with his hand still on the key, lit like a man in a doorway at noon.

Then it thinned, and spread, and was gone into the shed and out of the doors and up through the roof boards, and the lamp by the lock was a lamp again.

The shaft had stopped.

The belt hung slack. The balls hung down on their arms against the shaft. In the box there was no knock at all. The silence where it had been was the loudest thing Emeric had heard since the quarry.

Nobody was hurt. He made sure of that first, going round them one by one with the lamp: Wes at the lock, white, with his hand still on the key's handle as if it had frozen there; the lad at the reel, who had sat down where he stood; Jorrin in the doorway with a bar in his hand that he had been going to throw the belt off with. Ward's man had his sword half out and did not seem to know it. The sand had taken the panel. The firebrick had taken the heat. The box stood on its base plate with a square black hole in its side and smoke coming out of it, a little, from somewhere inside.

He held the lamp to the hole and looked in. He could see the near sleeve and the foot of the near weight, standing on the wave, and the firebrick behind it cracked in a star and blackened. The stone in the top of that weight had been pushed past what it could hold. It had been told heavy, and heavy was pressing, and pressing was spending, and it had been drinking through its neck as fast as the neck would let it to make up what it spent; and then the box round it had gone from full to twice full in a breath, and it had drunk, and gone on drinking, because there was nothing in its stone to tell it to stop. A stone pushed past what it can hold does not stop. It fails. It lets go of everything it has and everything it is, all at once. Armand had written that in the middle of a paragraph in a notebook, a long time ago, where it would not draw attention to itself.

And the failure does not only destroy the stone. It pushes the medium out of the ground round it.

Emeric put his hand in through the hole, past the hot brick, toward the weight, and felt nothing. Not the thinness of a shed at night. Nothing. The inside of the box was a hole in the world the shape of a box.

Bram came in at a run, which Bram did not do, with his coat over his nightshirt. The foundry's night man had gone for him at the crack. He stood in the doorway and looked at the panel in the sand, and at the hole, and at the smoke, and at the prince with his arm in the hole up to the elbow. Then he looked at Wes, still at the lock, and at the rack, where there were two empties standing lid up by the door, and not one.

He did not ask. He looked at Emeric, and Emeric told him.

Bram listened. Then he went to the bench and found a plate the size of the hole, near enough, and a brace and a box of bolts, and began to bolt it over the hole through the old rivet holes, by lamplight, with Jorrin holding the plate. He did not say why and nobody asked him. A box with a hole in it was not a box, and whatever was going to be done in the morning, Bram meant it to be done to a box.

Wes took his hand off the key. It took him two tries. He stood by the lock with his hands hanging and looked at the floor, and did not say anything, and did not go.

In the morning the box was quiet. Bram's plate was on it, bolted tight, and the bolts had been done up again at dawn by Bram, who had not been home. The shaft stood still on the roof. The balls hung.

Armand came down in his chair with his stick and sat on his stool by the box, and Emeric told him all of it, standing, while the old man listened with his hands on the head of his stick and his eyes on the iron.

"One stone," Armand said when he had finished. "The other?"

"I don't know. It's in there. It wasn't the one that went."

"Then give it something."

Emeric put a flask through the lock himself. Outer door, bar, inner door. He turned the key and felt the lid go and the flask give up what it held, the shiver through the handle, into the box.

The far drop stirred. He heard it: one knock, low, like somebody turning over in his sleep. The shaft on the roof moved a hand's breadth and the balls lifted off it, a little, as if they had been woken.

Then nothing.

He waited with his hand on the key. He waited for the count of a hundred, and then another hundred. The shaft did not move again. He put his other hand flat on the box's side, and through the iron, where last week in dead ground he had felt the whole band packed in, he felt a hole. The flask had gone into it the way a cup of water goes into a dry field. It had been there for one knock, and then it was gone, and the field was as dry as before.

He took his hand off the iron and told Armand, and Armand was quiet for a long time.

"It fills back in," Armand said at last. "From the edges. Slowly. That's what a dead place does. It's in my book."

"I've read your book."

"Then you know where the edges are." Armand turned his stick a quarter turn on the floor, and looked at the box, and it was the only time Emeric had ever seen him look at a thing he had helped build as though it frightened him. "There aren't any. You made it so nothing could get in. That's the whole of why it works. Nothing outside can touch what's in it. A dead field outside doesn't know it's there." He put his hand out and laid it on the iron. "And nothing outside can come in and heal it, either. It'll stay like that. Not a day. Not a season. It'll stay like that till somebody fills it from the inside."

"With what?"

"With what you've got," said Armand. "And more than you'd like."

It took two days, and it took more than he liked.

The ruined stone had to come out, and to come out it had to be got at, so the box was opened on the second day: Bram's plate off, and the weight lifted out of its sleeve through the hole on a chain and carried across the yard to the forge on a barrow. When the side came off Emeric stood at the hole and felt the shed's thin air lean into the box the way warm air leans into a cold cellar when the door is opened. It did nothing he could measure. The shed had very little to give and the hole wanted a great deal, and he could have stood there with the side off until the autumn and the hole would still have been there.

The stone in the top of the weight was not a stone any more. It was a grey crust in the cup, crazed right through like a pot that has been put in the fire wet, and it came out in pieces on the end of Bram's pick. The forge took the weight on its plate that night and seated a new one in the cup, with its neck drawn to the same bore as before, and it came out in the morning grey and set and asleep.

The new stone came from the kept portion, from the same locked box in Ferra's strongroom the two blanks had come out of in the spring. A four-ounce blank, raised at Grayrock, weighed on Ilsa's beam and written in Lusk's book and never sold.

Ferra entered it at the Crown's figure. She did it at the long-room table, with the gravity page open, and when Emeric looked at the line she did not wait to be asked. A stone the barony kept was a stone the barony did not sell, and a stone it did not sell was one it could have sold, to one buyer, at one price, which was the Crown's; and the Crown's last price was in her book with a ring round it, eleven Gold and four Silver the pound. So the blank was worth a quarter of that, whatever anybody at the table thought it was worth. Two Gold, eight Silver, five Copper. She wrote it, and she did not write anything beside it.

The weight went back into its sleeve on the third morning. The panel went back on, not Bram's plate but the panel itself, dug out of the sand and hammered flat and put on with new soft rivets, fewer and further apart, exactly as drawn. The box was shut.

Then Emeric put two flasks through the lock, one after another.

The first did what the flask on the first morning had done. One knock. The shaft a hand's breadth, and the balls lifting, and then nothing, and the hole in his hand through the iron as deep as before, or nearly. He did not wait for the count of a hundred this time. He put the second in.

One knock. Another. The shaft went a quarter round and stopped, and went again, and the knocks came closer and found their pace, one weight down while the other came up, and the ring went round under them, and the balls went out on their arms and held level over the roof. Through the iron, under his hand, the box was full.

Ferra did the count standing at the lock, because she did not trust a count she had not stood beside. In the Flasks column, on the line for the night of the vent and the two days after, she wrote four, and underneath it, in the gravity page's hand, one stone. The flask Tobe had put in at dusk she did not count against the night. It had been owed.

Wes had not gone back to the lake. Nobody had told him to go and nobody had told him to stay, and so he had come to the shed each morning at the hour Tobe had told him, and stood by the lock, and had not touched it, and had carried what he was asked to carry and held what he was asked to hold, and gone home at night. Tobe had done the pouring. Wes had watched.

On the third evening, when the pair was knocking again, Emeric found him outside the shed's back door, sitting on the sandstone block with the drilled hole in it, looking at his hands.

"I'll go back down in the morning," Wes said, before Emeric could say anything. "Ned'll have me. Or he won't, and I'll go on the lighters. I know what it cost. I heard her say it." He did not look up. "Four and a stone."

"Why did you open it?"

Wes looked up then.

It was the only question that mattered, and Emeric had nearly not asked it. He had had it all worked out, lying awake the first night with the smell of the hot brick still in his nose: the bench had snagged, the box was full, the lad had opened a second flask on a full box. It was all true. Every word of it was in the book. He had lain there and seen it laid out like a column of figures, complete, and that was the thing he had learned to be most afraid of in himself. A complete column.

"She went slow," said Wes. "And heavy. The knock went long. The balls came in." He said it carefully, like a lesson. "That's when she wants it. That's what I was told."

"And one didn't seem enough."

"One didn't seem anything. Tobe put one in at dusk." Wes looked at his hands. "I didn't think about that. She was going, and she was going like the deck goes, and you don't stand and think when the deck goes. You open one. And if it's still going you open the next. That's the whole of it. That's what you do." He shut his hands. "That's what you do up there."

"I know," said Emeric.

He sat down on the other end of the block, which was not built for two, and looked at the yard where the dead ground had been a fortnight before.

"You did what you were taught," he said. "Right. Exactly. In order. You were taught it on a ship, where it keeps you alive, and nobody told you it was the wrong thing to do to a box, because nobody wrote down that it was. I didn't write it down." He looked at his own hands. "I knew. I knew the Hod's rule. I've known it since the night I told Ned to carry four flasks at the helm. I knew a box isn't a ship and a slow knock isn't always a thin box. I thought about it the week Tobe started. And I didn't say it, because it hadn't happened, and there was always something else to say."

Wes did not say anything.

"Go down to the lake if you want to," Emeric said. "Ned would be glad of you. But I'd rather you stayed. You're the only man in Kaldo who knows exactly what it looks like from the lock when it's about to go. Nobody else has seen it. I'd rather that was in the shed than on a lighter."

Wes sat for a long time. Then he nodded, once, the way Ned Tallis nodded at a thing that settled something.

Emeric said it again at the long-room table that night, after supper, to all of them, in fewer words. The bench had snagged. The lad had done what the Hod had taught him. The rule for the box had never been written, because the man who should have written it had not. He said it flat and in order, and then he said the other thing, which he had not meant to say until he heard himself start.

"I've done this before," he said. "I had a thing in front of me that was all true, and I moved on it before I'd asked the one question that wasn't on the paper. A man paid for it in a yard in front of forty men." He did not say the name. Nobody at the table needed it said. "This time it was four flasks and a stone. That's cheap. It won't always be."

Nobody said anything. Ferra had her pen in her hand and did not write. Ward, in his chair by the door, looked at him for a moment and then at the fire, and that was all Ward said about it, then or later.

Then Bram asked what the rule was going to be, and they talked about that.

A rule needed something to read. That was the first thing Bram said and the last, and it was the whole of it. Tobe had known by the hour. Wes had known by the knock. The knock had lied, because the knock meant two things and a man at the lock could not tell which. A pourer who had to guess would guess, and the next one would guess wrong.

So Nevin cut a stone.

He cut it in the etching floor, on a sliver of force crystal no longer than a finger joint, from a line Emeric drew for him on a card, and he cut it clean, as he cut everything, without being able to read a word of it. It was the oldest rule in the barony, the one Emeric had found sitting on the frozen mud of his own courtyard in his first winter because he was annoyed: a stone cannot feel the world, but a stone working against the world works harder or easier as the world goes, and how hard it works is the one thing it always knows. Nevin's sliver was told to push, always, as hard as it could, against a hairspring. In thin air it could not push much. In the band it could push a great deal.

Bram built the rest. The sliver in a brass cage on the box's inner wall, above the lock. Its hairspring on a spindle, and the spindle out through the box's side on a packed collar no thicker than a pen, and on the outside, above the lock at a pourer's eye height, a brass dial the size of a saucer with a needle on it. It was the float gauge Bram had put on the second tower's tank that spring turned inside out, so that Weekes's men at the hand pump could see the water from the ground: a needle on a dial, climbing as the thing behind it filled. Any man who had stood a shift at that pump would know what it was without being told.

Emeric scribed the mark on the dial himself, on the fourth night, after watching the needle for most of two days. When the box was fresh from a flask, the needle stood high, near the top of the dial. It fell as the drops spent, slowly, through a day and a night. It did not move when the bench snagged. It did not care about the knock at all. When it came down to a certain place the knock began to go long on its own, with nothing on the belt, and there he cut a line across the brass with the steel.

He wrote the rule on a card and Bram screwed the card to the box under the dial, and Ferra copied it into the front of a new book, a plain one, bound in board, from her own shelf.

One flask to a mark. Not before the needle reaches it. Never two. Write it down.

Under that she ruled four columns: Hour. Mark. Flask. Name. The hour it was poured. Where the needle stood when it was poured. Which flask, by the number Ned Tallis's people painted on each lid at the lake. And the name of the man who turned the key. She put the book on the shelf by the lock where Tobe's had been, and took Tobe's away, and Tobe did not mind.

The needle came down to the mark a little before midnight. Emeric was there, and Bram, and Tobe, and Wes. Nobody had asked Wes to come. He had come.

Tobe looked at Emeric, and Emeric looked at Wes.

Wes went to the dial and looked at it for a long time, at the needle standing on the scribed line and not a hair below it. Then he took a flask out of the rack and read the number on its lid, and put it in the lock, lid to the socket, and shut the outer door, and threw the bar, and opened the inner door, and turned the key a half turn and held it there until he felt the shiver in the handle. Then back, and the inner door, and the bar, and the outer, and the empty out and into the rack lid up. The needle climbed. The knock picked up and the balls swung out and eased it.

He took the book down off the shelf and the pen out of the pot, and stood at the shelf, because there was nowhere to sit, and wrote the first line in it in a careful hand that had not written much.

Midnight, less a quarter. On the mark. Flask 41. Wes.

Over the next month he became the most careful man at the lock. He never poured a hair above the mark. He never poured without writing it first, and would not let anybody else, and when Tobe once poured and wrote after, Wes looked at him across the shed and said nothing at all, and Tobe did not do it again. When other men came to learn the lock, it was Wes who taught them, standing beside them with his hands behind his back and the book open on the shelf, and the first thing he showed them was not the door or the bar or the key.

It was the needle, and the line across the brass.
