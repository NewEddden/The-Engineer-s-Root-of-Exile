Chapter 196 - The Debt

Ward was at his door at a quarter past five on the third morning home, with a lamp in one hand and a sheet of paper in the other.

Emeric knew the paper before he knew the lamp. It was Emberfell's paper, cream and heavy, with the dome pressed into its corner, and it had come off the ship in Ward's coat on the first morning and gone straight into Ward's room without a word said about it; and he had hoped, in the way a man hopes about a tooth, that it might stay there.

"Up," said Ward.

"It's dark."

"It is January. It will be dark until seven." Ward held the paper up to the lamp, so that the light came through it and showed the writing backward, in a square hand with a long tail on every letter. "Her Grace has written it out. Two years. I am to see it done." He lowered it. "Your legs are a clerk's, Highness. I have it in writing now."

Emeric lay and looked at the ceiling.

"She wrote it down," he said.

"She wrote it down on the quay, while you were saying goodbye to the knights. She gave it to me, not to you." Ward did not smile. Ward almost never smiled; but there was a stillness about him in the lamplight that in another man would have been a grin. "She said you would lose it."

He got up.

It was not, when he read it on the stair by Ward's lamp, very much. That was the worst of it. Rowena had not written out a knight's training, or a duel's, or anything with a blade in it at all. She had written out legs. The keep stair, top to bottom and back, at a run, twelve times. The wall walk from the South Gate to the river tower in a crouch, the knees bent to a line she had drawn in the margin with a little sketch of a man that was clearly meant to be him, with his backside too high. Standing on one foot on the edge of a step with his eyes shut, to the count of a hundred, each side. Then the yard: the turn, the one she had beaten him with twice, a hundred times on the left and a hundred on the right, slow, at a walk, until the feet did it without being asked. At the foot of the sheet, under her mark, she had written: Every day. Sick or well. Ward to count. If he complains, double it.

"Does it say anything about complaining?" said Emeric.

"Yes," said Ward.

He did the stair twelve times. By the ninth his thighs had gone hot and stupid and the stair had become a thing that went on forever in the dark; by the eleventh the kitchen boy coming up with the coal had stopped on the landing with the scuttle in both arms to watch, and Ward had sent him on with one look. On the wall walk in the crouch his knees burned and the river went by black and smoking below him in the cold and the first carts were coming across the east bridge with their lanterns swinging. On the step with his eyes shut he fell off twice on the left side and not at all on the right, and Ward counted both, and wrote them on the back of Rowena's sheet with a stub of pencil, and said nothing.

The turn, in the yard, by the light of the arc lamp over the gate, was the worst, because it was the one thing on the sheet he had thought he could already do.

"Again," said Ward, at the fortieth.

"What was wrong with it?"

"Your weight went to your toes. Her Grace saw it at the fourth pass. I have seen it at every one." Ward folded his arms. "Again."

At a hundred and sixty on the left he understood, very suddenly, in the arch of his own foot, what she had meant; and did it once right, and felt it, and lost it at a hundred and sixty-one.

"Better," said Ward. "Once." He put the sheet in his coat. "Tomorrow at a quarter past five."



He wrote his own at breakfast, at the end of the long room table, on the back of a spoiled sheet from the census, while his eggs went cold.

Hers stayed. Hers was right; he had felt it be right, once, in the arch of his foot at a hundred and sixty. But hers said what the feet should do, and nothing about what to build under them. A knight's yard grew legs the way a hedge grew, by being there every morning for years, and she had given him the years. The other world had a whole shelf on the subject. The trial had put that shelf in him with the rest of the medicine, and he went down it now the way he went down a column: the muscle, which came quickly if it was loaded and fed; and the cords that tied it to the bone, which came slowly whatever you did, and which were what had clicked under Imani's thumbs in the harbor house kitchen.

So he wrote it out the way the shelf did, by the month. Weight, and a little more of it every week, and never so much at once that the cords fell behind the muscle; every fourth week light, to let them catch up. Slow on the way down, always, because it was the lowering that built the cords. A barbell, which Bram did not yet know he was going to make: an iron bar as long as a man and a half, with round iron plates that slid on at either end and a collar to hold them. Squats under it. Split squats, with the back foot up on a bench. Heel drops off the edge of the keep stair, on one foot, counting four on the way down. Ward kneeling on his ankles while he lowered himself face first toward the floor with his hands behind his back, which the shelf called a Nordic curl and which he suspected Ward would enjoy. No jumping until April. Then a box, up and down; and only after that the rest, one thing at a time, in the order the shelf would have put them in if the shelf had known about gravity. Six eggs a day, and milk, and whatever the river had in it. Eight hours' sleep.

At the bottom he wrote the one line the shelf had never needed. Inward. At night. Slowly.

A knight's body mended faster than a commoner's, on its own, wherever the mana in it happened to go. His could be told where to go. He had closed a cut on the heel of his own hand that way in the autumn, behind a shut door, to see whether it could be done: the mana sent in thin, at the pace the flesh would take it and not a breath faster, because faster was what the tower did to a man in a stone room, and the man got up silver. It had taken a night and left no line. He had never tried it on anything bigger than a hand. He wrote under it, small: twice as fast? And under that, because the sum was not a hard one: one year.

Ward read it upside down across the table, over his porridge, which he ate every morning as though it had been ordered.

"That is more," said Ward.

"It's different."

"It is more." Ward reached across and turned the sheet over, as if there might be something kinder on the back. There was a figure for the lower ward's hearths. He turned it back. "Her Grace said that if you complained, I was to double it."

"I'm not complaining."

"No," said Ward. "You have doubled it yourself." He pushed it back across the table. "I will count both."

Maren, coming down the room with the pan to see who wanted the scrapings, read the line about eggs on her way past.

"Six," she said.

"Every morning."

"It's January, Highness. Hens don't lay for anybody in January, and they'll not start for a prince." She looked at the sheet, and at him, and at his plate. "There's herring. There's the hard cheese from Milford that nobody eats. There's milk, if you'll drink it."

"All of it."

She went off down the room with the pan, and he heard her tell the kitchen boy on the stair, not quietly, that the prince was eating like a carter now and to put the herring on.



Imani wanted his blood at nine.

She had sent a boy up the keep stair to say so, in those words, and the boy had said them in the long room in front of Hale and two clerks and Saban, and had not understood why Saban laughed. Emeric went down to the ward with his sleeve already rolled.

She had the whole crew on a list. It was on a slate at the end of her desk in the ward's front room, in her own hard upright hand, with a line for every one of the twelve who had gone up: Maren, Saban, Ferra, Fletcher, Imani, Orrin, Vesk, Cade, Tobe, Ward. Vantic. Emeric. Beside the first ten, in order, she had written a number and a word: when each had bled, and how much. Beside the last two she had written nothing at all, and drawn a line under the nothing, twice.

"Sit," she said.

He sat on the stool by the window. She took his arm and turned it over, the inside up, in the grey light, and looked at the veins in the crook of his elbow the way Bram looked at a casting, for the flaw that would let it down; and then she put a cord round his upper arm and tied it, and took a needle out of its case. It was off the needle maker in the works: hollow, steel, as fine as a heavy pin; and the glass barrel behind it was Tess's, from the lamp shop, with a plunger of turned bone and a waxed leather seal that Imani had made herself and did not like.

"You're going to bleed now," she said, "since you wouldn't up there."

"That isn't fair."

"It's medicine. It isn't meant to be fair." She put the needle in. It went in clean, a small cold sting; and his blood came up into the glass dark and slow, and she watched it come with her lips pressed together. "Every one of them gave me a cup this week. Maren gave me two, because I asked for one and she said a healer who only wants one cup of a big woman's blood doesn't know her business." She drew the plunger back a little further. "I've had them all under the glass. Bram's lens, Tess's lamp. I've counted what I can count. I've stood their cups in the cold room overnight and looked at how they settled." She took the needle out and pressed a pad to the place with her thumb. "Nothing. Everyone's blood looks like everyone's blood. Ward's looks like Maren's. Mine looks like Saban's." She did not take her thumb away. "And yet ten of us bled and two of you didn't, and it went in an order, and the order wasn't size, or age, or who was sitting where. I had Fletcher draw me where everybody was. It wasn't that."

"It's how much they hold," said Emeric. "Mana. Maren holds the least of anybody aboard. Saban next. Then Ferra."

"I know that. I'm not stupid." She looked at the slate. "Ward holds a great deal and he bled last. You and Vantic hold more than anybody, and you didn't bleed at all. It isn't hard to see the line." She took her thumb off the pad and looked under it, and put it back. "What I don't know is what it means. If the ones who hold the most sicken last, then somebody who holds enough doesn't sicken at all. And somebody who holds nothing at all, a baby, a man off the street who's never touched a stone in his life..." She stopped. "I want to know how long it takes. Up there. To hurt a body that holds nothing. And I want to know what it does to the ones who don't bleed, that they can't feel." She looked at him. "Including you."

"I felt it," he said. "Like a weight. On the skin."

"A weight isn't a symptom. It's a description." She wrote something on the slate beside his name, at last: a word he could not read from the stool, and then, after it, a question mark. "You'll come down here every month. For a year. And give me a cup. And Vantic will, though she doesn't know it yet."

"She'll say no."

"She'll say no, and then she'll come, because she wants to know too, and she can't ask anybody else." Imani untied the cord.

"Boots," she said.

He had known she would. He took them off, there on the stool, and she knelt on the boards with his right foot in her lap and did what she had done in the harbor house kitchen, the thumbs and the turning and the bending, and then the left; and made him stand in his stockings and bend his knees while she watched from the floor. The swelling had gone down on the ship. The ankle still clicked.

"Well?"

"Still hers," said Imani. "Two years."

He gave her the sheet.

She read it on her knees, all of it, and then the back, where the lower ward's hearths were, in case they bore on anything. She read it the way she read a petition, looking for the term that would cost the most; and she found it, because it was where he would have looked himself, and put her finger on it.

"Twice," she said.

"Twice as fast. Near enough."

"Who counted?"

He did not answer.

"Nobody counted," said Imani. "You wrote down a number you'd like." She tipped the sheet to the window. "And this. Inward."

He told her: the mana going in at night, thin, to the places he had worked that day and no others; and why no faster. He did not want silver in his knees.

She heard him to the end without moving. Then she got up off the floor and took his chart out of the drawer, the one she had begun at Emberfell, and wrote under the last line in the hard upright hand.

"Then it's a guess for a month," she said, writing. "After that it's a number. You'll come down on the first of every month with your cup, as I said, and you'll bring your legs. A tape from Abloh's round the thigh and the calf, both sides. Her count on one foot with your eyes shut. And a hop, on one leg, as far as you can go and land it and hold it; then the other." She looked up. "When the bad one goes nine-tenths as far as the good one, and comes down quiet, and doesn't swell after, I'll write it off. Not before."

"And the fast thing?"

"Is in the chart. Nothing off the ground till April. Nothing off a height till midsummer. And whatever it is you do fast, not till I've had the tape on you twelve times." She wrote one more word, on his sheet this time, and turned it round so that he could read it. In the margin, beside his own line about sleep, she had written: Nine.

"I'd put eight."

"I know. I read it." She gave him back the sheet and put the chart in the drawer. "Sit down."

He sat, in his stockings.

Then she took his wrist.

Not the arm she had bled. The other one; she reached across him for it, on the stool, and turned it over, and put two fingers on the inside under the band, where she always put them, and looked at the clock on the ward's wall over his shoulder, and counted.

He sat and let her. It was the third morning home and he had slept, at last; and his pulse, he knew, was perfectly ordinary. He could feel it under her fingers, slow and even. She had counted it at Emberfell and on the ship and at the foot of the keep stair on Lantern Night, and in the dome cabin three nights ago in the grey before dawn, when it had been a hare's.

The clock's hand went round. It went round again.

"What is it?" he said.

"Sixty-two."

"You had it at the first round."

"I had it at the first round," said Imani. She did not let go. She did not look up from the clock. "I'm checking it."

The clock went round a third time. Out in the ward, somebody coughed, and a nurse's feet went past the door, and the stove ticked.

"It's sixty-two," said Imani, and let go, and wrote it on the slate, and did not explain herself, and did not have to. "Go away. I've a ward."



Saban had the coaster's manifest the night before the coaster.

He brought it up to the long room after supper with the lamps lit and laid it on the table in front of Ferra, folded twice, on a rough grey paper that had been nowhere near a printer, in a clerk's crabbed hand that had been in a great hurry.

"My man on the Emberfell quay," said Saban. He sat down and put his feet toward the fire. "He copied it off the master's book while they were loading, and gave it to the master to carry, sealed, with a Silver on it for the boy who'd row it ashore at the river mouth while she waited for the tide. The boy gave it to the post rider." He looked at the fire contentedly. "So it came up the road while she lay at the bar. I'll have it from him every time now, a day ahead of the hold. It's cheap and it's ours."

Ferra unfolded it and read it. She read it once, the way she read everything, as if the paper owed her money.

"Sulphur," she said. "Forty tons of it, in sacks. Five of the sacks are marked short." She turned the sheet over. "And a list. From her clerk." She read the list and did not say anything, and then she turned it round on the table, so that it faced Emeric.

It was the terms. He had signed them on Rowena's long table in her standing room, under her seal and Saban's and his own, with the grey knight watching and the clerk of nineteen writing them fair: printing, a school, standards and drains, all in the Sovereign's name, all to be paid in sulphur and a furnace throat and a lane of ships. He had known what they were when he signed them. He had not known, until he saw them written out again in a frightened boy's hand on a list of what the coaster would want back on her next run, that the first thing Emberfell wanted was paper.

Ten thousand sheets. For the school's first books, and the harbor dues, and a printed copy of every law of the standing table, which Rowena had decided, apparently between the quay and the coaster's sailing, should be pinned to the door of every chapel on the coast. Ten thousand sheets by the summer. And the same again by the winter. And type.

"We can't," said Ferra.

She said it the way she said a column did not add. Not a complaint; a figure. She had already taken the mill's last quarter out of her book and laid it beside the clerk's list, and she put her finger on it.

"The mill made nineteen thousand sheets last year," she said. "Every sheet it could. The school took six thousand. My office took four. Lantern Night took three. The plain press took the rest, and sold every one, and Saban has orders from Grayrock he's been putting off since the autumn." She took her finger away. "If I give Emberfell ten thousand by the summer, the school has no copybooks and I have no forms and the press stops."

"Then the mill makes more."

"The mill can't make more. The mill runs on rags." She looked at him. "You know it does. You built it. A sheet of paper is a shirt somebody wore out. The rag men go round the four baronies with a cart and buy every scrap of linen anybody'll part with, and there isn't any more, because there aren't any more shirts. People don't wear them out faster because we'd like them to." She closed the book. "It's the rags. Not the vats, not the men. We could build three more mills tomorrow and they'd stand empty by the summer."

Saban, by the fire, nodded slowly, as if a sum he had been doing by himself for a year had come out at last where he expected.

Emeric sat and looked at the list.

He had known about the rags. He had known about them since the first winter, when he had stood in Kaldo's one paper loft with Tess and turned over a sheet as soft and grey and ragged as an old dishcloth and thought: linen. All of it. Every sheet anybody in this world has ever written on, beaten out of somebody's worn-out shirt. And he had known, in the way he knew a great many things he had not yet had any use for, how the world he came from had got out of that box: not by finding more shirts, but by finding something else to beat. Straw. Wood. Anything with a long fibre in it, cooked until the stuff that glued the fibres together let go of them. Cooked in what was the whole of it, and he had not had what it needed.

He had it now. It was coming up the river in forty tons of sacks, five of them short.

"Sulphur," he said.

Ferra waited.

"Some of it goes to the acid works. That's the easy part. Acid on scrap iron gives you green vitriol by the barrel, and green vitriol and oak galls give you the ink the northern clerks use, the black that bites into the page and never comes off. Rowena's clerks will want it for her laws." He tapped the list. "That's the ink. The paper's the hard part."

"Go on."

"You burn it," said Emeric. "And it makes a smoke that stinks, and you run the smoke up through a tower full of broken limestone with water trickling down over it. What comes out of the bottom is a liquor; sulphite liquor, the books called it. You chop straw into it, the straw from every field in four baronies that we burn off or plough in every autumn, and cook it under pressure for a day, and the straw falls apart into fibre. Clean, long fibre. Pale." He looked at her. "Paper. Without a single shirt in it."

"How much straw?"

"More than we'll ever use."

Ferra looked at him for a breath. She opened the book again, to a new page, and wrote at the top of it, in her small hand: Straw. And under it, a line, and under that a question.

"What does it cost," she said, "per sheet?"

"I don't know yet."

"Then I don't know if we can." She wrote it down: unknown. "Find out." She did not look up. "And don't tell me the sulphur's free. It isn't. It's a school and a printing house and the drains of a city of thirty thousand, and we've signed for all of it." She turned the page back to the mill's quarter and put her finger on the nineteen thousand again. "Every sack of it's owed."

She had been like this for three days. Not cold, exactly. Ferra was never cold; she was exact, which people mistook for it. She had talked to him at the long table about wool and the mill and the coaster as she always talked to him, and corrected him twice, and not once looked at him longer than the business needed. She was, he understood, still angry; and she was being angry the way she did everything, which was correctly, to the figure, and not one Copper more than was owed.

He did not say anything about it. She had said she would tell him.



The coaster came up on the morning tide, low in the water, and the forty tons came off her onto the quay in a yellow dust that got into everybody's eyes and made the whole of the dock quarter smell for a week like the terraces at Emberfell, of bad eggs and struck matches. The gulls hated it. Maren came down to the slip to look at it, and smelled it, and said in a voice that carried to the far bank that she had sailed eight days to get away from that smell and been sent the whole mountain after her, and went home.

Bram had a cask open in the works yard.

He had borrowed the still crew's copper for it. Not asked; borrowed, at night, with two of his own men and a handcart, the old pot still that the crew had set aside when Tess built them the column, and had set it up in a corner of the yard behind a wall of sandbags with a long copper worm running off it through a trough of cold water; and Mott, who ran the still crew, had come round the corner of the works at seven in the morning to find it gone, and followed the handcart's tracks through the snow, and found it, and stood in front of it with his arms folded and his face working, while Bram fed it Duvrenne oil from a ladle.

"It's a spirit still," said Mott.

"It's a still," said Bram. "It doesn't know what it's stilling."

"That's my copper. We made the first Kaldo white in that copper."

"You'll have it back."

"Smelling of that?"

Bram considered this with his head on one side. "No," he said, honestly. "Probably not."

Emeric came into the yard while they were still standing there. He had meant to stop it; it was a fire, near oil, in a yard full of men, and nobody had worked out yet how much of the lightest part would come off before anybody was ready for it. But Bram had put the still behind the sandbags and the worm a long way out, and had a bucket of sand and a wet sack beside the fire stone that heated it, and Tess was at the end of the worm in her chair with her data book on her knees and a row of little glass jars in a wooden rack, changing the jar under the spout every time the stuff coming out of it changed colour; and the colours were coming out in order, one after another, exactly as he had told Bram on the slip they would. A clear one that smelled sharp and went off the back of the hand before you could put it to your nose. A pale one, like weak tea. A thicker one, the colour of honey. A brown one.

And at the bottom of the pot, when Bram let it cool and tipped it, black. Thick, slow and black, that came out of the copper in a single rope and would not let go of the ladle.

"Pitch," said Bram.

He rubbed some between his finger and thumb and pulled them apart, and it came up between them in a string, and stuck. He looked at it the way he had looked at the oil on the slip.

"It sticks to anything," he said. "Brass. Glass." He tried it on the side of the copper, and on Tess's jar rack, and on his own boot, and then on the iron of the still's leg, and it stuck to all of it. "That'll seal a joint you couldn't get a hair into."

"Don't put it on the still crew's copper," said Mott.

"I've put it on already," said Bram.

Emeric stood and looked at Tess's rack of jars, five of them, the colours of a spoiled sunset. Tess looked back at him over her data book.

"Seven casks," she said. "That's what this is out of one. Half a cask." She tapped the honey-coloured jar. "That's your grease. For the lathe. And the axles." She tapped the clear one. "And I don't know what that is, except it'll burn your eyebrows off, because it nearly burned Bram's."

"It's what goes in an engine," said Emeric. "One day. Not yet."

"Then I'll keep it in a jar," said Tess, "and nowhere near Bram."

Bram had gone back to the pitch. He was working a lump of it in his palm the way a boy works a lump of wax, warming it, and looking at it.

"There's something else," he said, without looking up. "The brooch."

Emeric waited.

"I had it out of its box yesterday. To clean it. The silver had gone dark at sea." Bram turned the pitch over in his hand. "The stone. Your mother's. The chipped one." He frowned at the pitch. "It looks different."

"Different how?"

"I don't know. Different. The chip." Bram shrugged, a smith's shrug, grudging, that said he did not like saying a thing he could not measure. "It's a stone. Stones don't change. I've looked at it every day since November, setting it, and it's different, and I can't tell you how." He put the pitch down on the still's lid. "Probably the light. It was a long voyage."

"Probably," said Emeric.

He meant to go and look at it that afternoon. Fennick found him first.



Fennick was in the long room when he came up, in his burned coat, with his three-fingered hand on the table and his laugh already going, because Ferra had just told him that his powder for the spring was cut by a third.

"By a third," said Fennick, delighted. He looked at Emeric as he came in, as if Emeric had arrived in time for the best part. "She's cut me by a third, Highness. For the spring. With the Wayford cuttings to blow from March and the quarry face to bring down and a culvert under the north road nobody's going to dig by hand." He spread his hands. "A third."

"There isn't any more," said Ferra. She did not look up from the book. "I didn't cut it. The capital did."

She turned the book so that Emeric could see. It was the powder account: Ward's book, copied into hers, every pound that had come into Fennick's powder house since the day it was a chalk line in a field, by weight, with the date and the price. It came by the pound. Never more than twenty at a time, in little kegs, up the capital road in a carrier's cart, from a chandler in the capital who bought it from the royal fireworks makers under their licence, and sold it on at three times what they charged him, to quarries and mines that had a paper from the crown saying they could.

"The chandler's written," said Ferra. "The makers have told him they'll sell him less this year. They want it for the king's birthday at midsummer and they're short themselves." She looked up at last. "They're short because the saltpetre's short. It comes from somewhere in the north, off the walls of old cellars, and there isn't much, and the fireworks get it first because the fireworks are the crown's." She closed the book. "It's on the licence. A third less for every quarry and mine in Randillia. It isn't only us."

"It's only me who's got a culvert," said Fennick.

Emeric stood at the end of the table and looked at the book under Ferra's hand.

He had been watching that account for a year without seeing it. Every pound of it went somewhere. Fennick blew rock with it for the Wayford line and the quarry. The acid works took a share, because the lead chambers needed saltpetre with their brimstone, a little, every week. Tess had taken two pounds in the autumn for the cartridges under her chair. And all of it came up the same road from the same chandler under somebody else's licence, at somebody else's price, in kegs of twenty pounds, in a quantity that the crown's fireworks makers decided, for reasons of their own, in a city twelve days away.

And there was nothing to be done about it this spring. That was the thing he made himself look at, standing there. If he wrote to the chandler for two hundredweight instead of two, the chandler would tell the makers, and the makers would tell somebody at court, and somebody at court would want to know why the prince of Kaldo, who had four baronies and an iron road and a works that never slept, needed enough saltpetre to blow the roof off the capital. It would be all over the palace in a week. He knew exactly what his brother would make of it.

"Then we make our own," he said.

Fennick stopped laughing.

"You can't make saltpetre," said Fennick. "It grows. On walls. In old cellars and stables and under dovecotes. You scrape it." He said it with the authority of a man who had lost two fingers to the stuff. "Nobody makes it."

"You can grow it," said Emeric. "Faster than walls do."

He told them. It was not hard to tell; it was hard to do, and slow, and it stank, which Fennick found very funny and Ferra did not. You built a shed, open at the sides and roofed against the rain. Under it, a bed of earth and old mortar and dung and wood ash, mixed and heaped in long ridges a man's height. You watered the ridges with the worst liquid in the barony, which was what came out of the backs of the houses and the tannery pits and the stables, and you turned them with a fork every few weeks; and the stuff in the dung that rotted, rotted down into the stuff that made saltpetre, slowly, in the dark at the heart of the heap. And after a year, or two, you dug the heaps out and soaked them in water, and the water came away with the salt in it; and you boiled the water down with more ash, and what crystallised out of it at the end, white and needled, was what came up the road in Fennick's kegs.

"A year," said Fennick.

"Or more. I don't know this ground."

"And the culvert's in March."

"The culvert gets dug by hand," said Ferra, without looking up. "I'll pay the men."

Fennick looked at Emeric, and at her, and laughed again, but differently, a man's laugh at a joke that has turned out to be his own.

"By the tannery," Emeric said. "And the middens behind the dock quarter, downwind. And one at Grayrock, by the mine stables. Small, to begin." He looked at Ferra. "I want them started this month. For the blasting."

Ferra wrote it down. Saltpetre beds: tannery, middens, Grayrock. For the blasting. She underlined nothing.

Nobody asked him what else it was for.

Ward was at the window. He had been there the whole time, with his back to the glass and his arms folded, and he did not say anything at all; but when Emeric looked up from the book, Ward was looking at him, steadily, the way he looked at a man across a yard before a bout. Then Ward looked back out at the river, and the moment was gone, and Fennick was asking about the dung.



He went up to his room late, with his thighs aching from Rowena's stair and a page of figures for the straw tower in his coat, and took the coat box down off the shelf over his table, where it had been since the ship.

The brooch was in it, on its square of black velvet, where Bram had put it back. Bram's silver star, eight points, darkened by the sea and cleaned again until it shone; and in the middle of it, held in its four claws, his mother's stone.

The chipped one. The spare that nobody had wanted for a bead, because the old break in its face ran further into it than it looked, and a bead had to be whole; grey and dull and the size of a pigeon's egg, in the silver collar Bram had raised round it by hand, with the flake gone from one side and the edges of the break worn smooth, as if somebody had dropped it on a hard floor a long time ago. He had worn it through a duel and up the inlet and over the sea.

He took it to the lamp and turned it.

The break looked the same. The same small smooth hollow where the flake had gone. He turned it to the light the other way, and the other way again. A stone. Grey. And at the very edge of his sense, where he kept it open, thin, all day, the same thing he had always felt in it since the day it came out of its box in the chancery: not a note. The shape where a note had been. Like a room somebody has just walked out of.

And yet, turning it, there was a line he did not remember. In the hollow of the old break, as fine as a hair, so fine he could see it only when the lamp was behind his hand: a crack, or the shadow of one, running into the stone from the worn edge.

Or the shadow of the collar. Or the light. Or nothing.

He held it there until his arm ached. Then he put the brooch back on its velvet, in its box, on the shelf, and went to bed.

At a quarter past five Ward knocked.
