Chapter 360 - Lights in the Lee

South Sentinel came up out of the sea low and green at the near end and grey where the works were, with the old grove standing along the inner shore like a row of brooms somebody had left out.

From the air the Cradle was what it had been built to look like: a long shed on a stone pier, tarred sailcloth over sawn timber, and nothing else. The yard's three punts were pulled up on the shingle above the tide line, which they never were. Past the bay mouth the reef showed as a broken line of white where the swell came in on it, and past the white, in the lee of the south headland, the water was smooth and dark and had nothing on it at all.

Emeric looked anyway. He had his sense open before Ward had the hull down to the works pier, and he went out with it over the bay, past the cold stones of the Cradle's rim, past the reef, onto the smooth water, and down.

They were there.

Not on the water. Under it, a long way out, moving: notes, close together, dense and bright the way a full stone is bright, more of them than he could pull apart at that distance. They went along the outside of the reef and turned and came back. He held still on the deck with his hand on the rail and let them go past him twice before he could count them. Eleven. Then twelve, because two had been swimming so close together that they rang as one.

And they were leaking. He knew that feeling now better than he wanted to. It was the jar on his bench with the iron sleeve off, a dense close thing going thin at the edges into air that couldn't hold it. Every one of them was a little less than it had been when he first touched it, and he had only been touching them a minute.

"Twelve," he said.

"Twelve what?" said Saban, beside him.

"I don't know yet. Twelve of them. And something behind them, further out, bigger." He let it go. "Much bigger."

Saban looked at the smooth water past the reef for some time. He didn't write anything down. He never did.



Fletcher was at the foot of the gangway with his operator behind him, a thin young man with ink on his fingers and the last sheet off the pad folded in his shirt pocket, because he'd been told to keep it and didn't know what else to do with it.

"Majesty." Fletcher didn't waste the rest of the greeting. "My man on the end lamp saw them first, twenty to four. Lights, under. Gold mostly. One or two green. They came in to the reef and stopped, and they've been going up and down the outside of it since, like dogs at a gate that won't open." He looked at Ferra, because Ferra was the one who would want the next part. "Nobody's gone out. I had the punts pulled up. I've had nobody on the water since half past three."

"Why half past three?" said Ferra. "Your man saw them at twenty to four."

"Because that's when she came out of the tank," said Fletcher. "Ten minutes before he saw anything. She went up the ladder and out along the slab and stood at the end of it, and she's stood there since. Won't come in. Won't eat. I took her a stool." He considered the stool, and what had happened to it. "She looked at it."

Ferra wrote that down: the time, and under it, came out first.

"And the seals," Fletcher said. "You'll want the seals."

The bay had kept its seals since the first summer, twenty or so, grey and fat and rude, who hauled out on the flat rocks along the inner shore and barked at the punts. At noon, Fletcher said, every one of them had gone into the water at once, which he'd never seen them do, and swum out through the gap in the reef, and they were lying out there now in the swell off the gap with their heads up, all facing in.

Emeric could see them when he looked for them: small round dark heads on the water, very still, in a loose line either side of the gap, like posts along a road.

He thought of the thing she had shown him on the day of the memory read and wouldn't put into his hands: a name called into a dark sea, and small fish turning on it, and turning, and passing it on.

"The seals are on our side, then," said Imani, coming down the gangway with her bag.

"I wouldn't say that," said Fletcher. "I'd say the seals know something I don't." He turned to Zale, who was coming down behind her one step at a time on his stick, with a face that dared anybody to help him. "You're to go to her. She asked for you. Twice, and once in a way I understood."

"Walk," said Imani.

"I'm walking."

"Walk slower."



She was at the end of the slab, where Fletcher had said, with her feet in the water.

The Cradle's deck was long and wet, and the shed roof over the near third of it made it dark, so that coming out from under it onto the open stone was like coming out of a barn. She had her hood back. She was facing the lee. Her skin in the low sun was the grey-white it went when she was at ease, the inside-of-a-shell sheen on it, and down the backs of her arms it had gone in slate-coloured patches, the way it did when she was frightened and holding still about it. She had a cloth from the galley tied round her waist over the dark slick thing she wore, and Emeric didn't know what that meant, and didn't ask.

She turned when she heard them. She looked at Emeric first. She always did; Imani had stopped remarking on it about a year ago. Then she looked at Zale, and at his stick, and said something short.

"She says I'm slow," said Zale.

"You are slow," said Imani.

Zale asked her a question. Emeric caught one word of it, the word from the memory read that meant something like home, or where you belong, and caught nothing at all of her answer, which was long and low with the click at the back of it.

"They're hers," said Zale. "Her people. She knows the lights. She knew them before Fletcher's man did; she heard them in the tank." He listened again. "They won't come up in the light. Nobody comes up in the light, here. She says this is the land the stories are about, and they're more frightened than she was, because she was too hurt to be frightened, and they're not."

"Do they know we're here?" said Ferra.

Zale didn't need to ask that one. "They know the hull's here. Everything in the bay heard you come down."

The guest said one more thing and turned back to the water.

"She says dark," said Zale. "They'll come at dark."



Ward put them on the beach below the slab, at the top of the sand, where it went soft and grey and then into the dune grass.

"Far enough that nobody can say we came at them," he said, to Rylan, walking the line. "Near enough that nobody can say we weren't there."

"That's a fine distance."

"It's thirty paces. I measured it with my feet."

The knights came down off the pier in plate with their helms under their arms, Rylan's and Declan's and Osric's, and stood where Ward put them, and then, because it was going to be a while and nobody had told them not to, stopped standing. Arthur and Mira and Bren went to the south end of the line on their own, where the sand ran out against the rocks under the Cradle's footing, which was where Emeric would have put them if he had been asked, and nobody asked. The four sentries walked down the hull's ramp one after another with the ramp groaning under each of them and stood at the back, seven feet of dust plate and shield, their slot helms dark, looking at nothing.

The cook came along the pier at six with a pot of fish stew on a pole between two of Fletcher's men, because nobody had eaten and she didn't hold with that, sworn or not. She put it down in the dune grass and stood over it with the ladle until everybody had a bowl, the king included. She gave the sentries a look as if she'd have liked to feed them too.

They ate looking at the sea.

"I've got sand in my greave," said Osric.

"You're sitting in sand," said Declan.

"I'm aware. I'm saying I've got it in my greave. It's a report."

Imani put Zale on an upturned crate from the yard and told him that if he got off it before she said, she would sit on him, and Zale said he'd like to see that, and she said he wouldn't, and he stayed on the crate. Ferra sat on the sand with the ledger open on her knees and wrote while she ate, which she wasn't supposed to do and always did. Mira had her bowl balanced on her knee and was talking low to Arthur about the reef gap, how wide it was, how many could come through it abreast; she still turned her head a little further than other people did to see on one side, and Emeric had long since stopped noticing that, and noticed it now.

Saban walked the length of the beach once, by himself, from the rocks to the far end of the grove, and came back with his face shut.

He sat down beside Emeric in the grass and didn't take a bowl.

"They'll have seen the shed," he said. "From the water."

"They've been under the water."

"You don't know what they can see from under the water. Nor do I. That's my point." He looked at the long dark roof of the Cradle against the sky. "Two years. Every man on this island on double pay, every one of them sworn, the word guest and nothing else. Nobody outside the sworn has set foot on it. The Church doesn't know it's here. And there are twelve of something sitting off that reef who've been looking at it all afternoon."

"I know."

"I know you know." He said it the way Orrel had said it on the station steps that morning, a thousand years ago. "I'm telling you anyway, so it's said before and not after."

The sun went down behind the grove. The island's shadow came out from the foot of the trees and went down the beach over them, and over the slab and the Cradle and the bay, slowly, and the water in the bay went from blue to slate. The guest at the end of the slab was a thin dark shape against the last light on the sea.

When the shadow reached the reef, they came.



Out past the white, the lights stopped going up and down. They gathered at the gap. Emeric felt them do it before he saw it, twelve dense notes pulling together into a line, and then he saw it with his eyes too: gold, under the water, a long way down and coming up, and one green, and the seals on either side of the gap rolling out of the way as they came through.

Up and down the beach men set their bowls down in the grass.

The lights came in across the bay under the surface, fast and then slower, and slower, and in the last of the deep water, where the sand began to shelve, they stopped.

Nothing happened for a while. Then the first of them stood up.

He came up out of the bay on legs, waist-deep, and the water poured off him, and he stood there swaying as if the sea had pushed him. His lamp was at his throat on a cord, a stone the size of an egg with lines cut in it, giving a low steady gold light that lit his face from underneath and the sea around him in a ring. Then the others came up behind him, one and two at a time, and stood, and Emeric saw at once that standing was costing them.

They came in through the shallows the way she had come up the beach in November, on legs that weren't used to being asked: short steps, feet set down too hard, knees going. One of them went forward onto his hands in the wash and stayed there with the small waves breaking over his arms, and the one behind him got him under the shoulder and hauled him up without a word. They were all of them like her. Longer than most men, lean, swimmer-shouldered, the same grey-white skin with the sheen on it, the same close dark-green stuff from chest to hip that was neither cloth nor skin. Gills opened along their necks in the wash and closed in the air. Every one of them carried a long shaft with two points on it.

And they were tired. Emeric could feel how tired. Their notes were still brighter than anything on the coast of Ostrellin but a full stone, and every one of them was going down as he listened, steadily, the way hers had gone down in the cell under the deck the first week, morning by morning, before anybody knew why.

The one in front had something in his free hand, held up in front of him like a lantern. A flat ribbed shell, wet, catching the gold from his lamp. Emeric didn't need to see the lines cut in it. He could hear them: a small settled note in the grooves, faint and steady, like a glass that had been struck in May and had not stopped ringing.

"That's her shell," said Zale, very quietly, from his crate.

The one in front got to where the water was at his knees, and looked up the beach, and saw them.

Emeric tried to see it as they would. A beach in the cursed land at dusk. A long line of men in plate at the top of it, sitting up out of the grass, steel catching the last light. And behind them four things taller than any man, made of dust and plate, with dark slots for faces, that didn't move at all.

The points came up.

All twelve, at once, the way soldiers' points come up, and two of them stepped in front of the one with the shell and set their feet in the wash. Their skin went dark. It went from the throat down, slate, then black, in the time it took to breathe, until in the dusk they were nearly gone except for the lamps at their throats and the pale ends of the points. The one in front said something, loud and short, and it was the first sound any of them had made, and it carried up the beach like a dog's bark across a yard at night.

Somewhere on the line a sword came an inch out of its sheath. One of Declan's men got a knee under him.

"Stand still," said Ward.

He didn't say it loudly. He was at the middle of the line in his plate with his helm on the sand beside him, and he looked down the beach at the twelve black shapes in the water and the points, and then he did a thing Emeric had never seen him do anywhere in his life. He unbuckled his sword belt and laid it across the sand in front of him, sword and all, and sat down. Properly down. Cross-legged, like a man at a fire, with his hands on his knees.

"Sit down," he said.

"Ward," said Rylan.

"Sit down, I said. All of you. Swords off. On the sand where they can see them."

It took a while. A beach's worth of men in plate sitting down make a noise like a cart of pans going over, and Osric said something under his breath about where the sand was going to go, and Declan told him to shut up. Rylan sat last of the knights, slowly, with his face saying he would have words with Ward later. Arthur and Mira and Bren were down before anybody, because Ward had told them, and that was the whole of the Crestless's opinion on the matter.

"And them?" said Rylan, low, with his chin at the sentries.

"Leave them," said Ward. "They're furniture."

Emeric sat down too, on the sand next to Ferra, and Ferra closed the ledger over her pencil and held it on her knees. Imani sat on his other side with her bag. Saban was the last on the beach to sit, and he did it the way he did everything, carefully, as though the sand might be a trick, and as soon as he was down his eyes went along the line in the water, one face and the next and the next, and his lips moved very slightly.

The points didn't come down.

One of the two in front was shaking. Not with fear, or not only; Emeric could feel his note, and it was going down faster than the others, and his arms were shaking with holding up the shaft the way a man's arms shake at the end of a day carrying stone. The water went in and out round their knees. Nobody moved. On the end of the slab above the beach, the guest hadn't moved either.

Zale got up off his crate.

"Zale," said Imani.

He got up on his stick, and took two steps down the sand, and called out across the water to them, in their own tongue, three or four words, carefully, the way he'd learned them in Fletcher's cabin in the mornings, from a distance.

The effect was immediate and wrong. The one with the shell flinched as if Zale had thrown something. The others pressed in round him. One of them made a sound that wasn't a word at all, a sharp hiss with the click at the back of it, and the points came forward a hand's breadth, toward the man on the stick.

Zale stood very still. "Wrong word," he said, without turning round. "I think I just told them I'm hungry."

"Sit down," said Ward, "and stop helping."

Zale sat down where he was, in the sand, with his bad legs out in front of him and the stick across them.

And the guest came down off the slab.

She came down the stone steps at the end of the Cradle's footing that the yard used to get to the beach, and along the bottom of the line, past Arthur and Mira and Bren, past the sitting knights, past Ward, past Emeric close enough that he could have touched her, and she didn't look at any of them. She walked down the sand on her own two legs, which had stopped being lent some time in the winter. Her hood was down already. The cloth round her waist came loose at the water's edge and she let it go, and walked on into the sea, into the ring of their lamps, until the water was at her knees and the nearest point was an arm's length from her chest.

She said something.

Emeric didn't understand a word of it. He didn't need to. He had heard her voice every way it went for a year and a half, hurt and fed and angry and laughing with the click under it on Fletcher's deck, and he had never heard it go like this, low and level and entirely without asking.

The one with the shell lifted his head.

His lamp came up with it, and lit her, and he looked at her face in its gold light a long time. His skin went pale. It went pale from the throat up, black to slate to grey, the way a cloud goes off the moon, and then the others' did too, one after another down the line. The points came down. They came down into the water, all of them, and the shafts went under, and the one with the shell went down after his on one knee in the sea, and the eleven behind him went down after him, one by one, in the wash, with the small waves breaking against their chests.

He said a word. Then a longer one, three or four sounds together, with his head bent.

Behind Emeric, Zale let his breath out through his nose.

"That's a title," he said quietly. "I've had it from her once before. About her mother." He waited, and the captain, if that was what he was, said it again, and Zale nodded slowly, like a man told the price of something he'd suspected was dear. "It's the queen's daughter. As near as I can give it."

Ferra's pencil had stopped on the closed cover of the ledger.

"Of course she is," said Imani, to nobody.

Saban said nothing at all, which was worse.

Then the captain said one more thing. It was softer, and it wasn't a title. It was short, two sounds and a little fall at the end, and Emeric knew it before he knew he knew it, the way you know your own name called across a crowded street. He had been shown it once and not given it: a sound held up in the dark and kept back. A name called into the sea, and small fish turning on it.

Out past the reef, every seal in the line turned its head.

"Ilenne," the captain said.

She stood in the sea with it said aloud for the first time since the November before last. Emeric watched her do it. She'd carried it behind her teeth through the cell and the knife and the tank and Hollow Night, and she had not said it to Imani, who had had her hands inside her, or to Zale, who had learned her words in the mornings, or to him. And now a tired man on his knees in the wash had put it into the bay, and there was no taking it back.

She turned round.

She looked up the beach, past the sitting knights and the sentries, past Emeric, to Ferra, and to the book on Ferra's knees. Then she said it again, herself, slowly, the way you say a word to somebody who is going to have to spell it.

"Ilenne."

Ferra opened the ledger. She put the pencil down in the fold, and took out her pen, and wrote.



The man who had gone down in the wash didn't get up.

The others had come up off their knees when the captain did, but he was the one who had been shaking, and he stayed on his hands in the shallow water with his head hanging, and the waves broke over his wrists, and he was breathing in long ragged pulls through his mouth while the gills on his neck opened and closed on nothing.

Imani got up with her bag.

"Imani," said Ward.

"He's going to drown in a foot of water," said Imani. "Sit down, Ward." She went down the sand without hurrying, the way she went down the ward at night, with her lamp in her other hand, and Ilenne said one word to the captain without looking round, and the two nearest guards stepped aside to let a woman of the cursed land through.

She knelt in the wash beside him and didn't touch him at first. She held the lamp where he could see her hands, and showed him them, both, empty, and then put two fingers on the side of his throat below the gills and kept them there, and Emeric felt her sight open, careful and narrow, the way she opened it for a child. After a while she said something to Ilenne, in Kaldo words, slowly, and Ilenne said it again to the guard in theirs, and the guard got one knee under him and then his feet, and stood, with Imani's hand under his elbow.

His shaft was lying in the water where he'd dropped it. Imani picked it up for him, because it was there, and held it a moment in her lamp before she gave it back.

When she came back up the beach she sat down beside Emeric again and put her bag between her feet, and wiped her hands on her trousers, and said, low enough that it went no further than him and Ferra:

"Bone."

"All of them?"

"I looked at six. They're bone. White, carved, lashed on. Not a grain of stone in any of it." She looked at the twelve in the water. "Whoever put that thing in her back, it wasn't one of these."

Emeric reached for the points with his sense, to be sure, and they were nothing: bone and wood and sea water and the faint living leftover of whatever animal the bone had been. Nothing in them drank. He remembered the shard in Bram's two-lock box under the mill, and the feeling of reaching into it and finding no bottom to its thirst.

He didn't say anything. Ferra wrote one line, small.

Down in the water the captain was talking to Ilenne. He talked quickly and low, and she answered him, and twice he looked up the beach at them while she did it, at the line, at the sentries, at the long roof of the Cradle over the slab, and Emeric could see Saban, at the edge of his eye, watching him look.

Then the captain said something to Zale. To Zale, directly, across the water, a short question, and Zale, still sitting in the sand where Ward had put him, answered it carefully in four words and pointed with his stick.

At Emeric.

"He wants to know who holds the land," said Zale. "I told him. I think I told him. He's looking at you, at any rate, so it went somewhere."

Emeric got up.

He went down the sand to where it was wet and stopped there, at the edge, with the small waves coming up over the toes of his boots and going back. He didn't go into the water. The captain came forward through the wash on his bad tired legs until they were three or four paces apart, close enough that Emeric could see the lines cut in the stone at his throat: round, and in, and crossing each other at angles that looked chosen.

The lamp's gold came up into Emeric's face.

The captain stopped.

He looked at Emeric's eyes. He looked at them the way Ilenne had looked at them in Fletcher's iron cabin with her back against the tank, after the read, and his skin went dark again from the throat, all at once, and he took a step back in the water without seeming to know he had done it.

"Suvarre," he said.

He didn't say it to Emeric. He half turned and said it to Ilenne, and it was a question. She answered him with one word, short.

The captain looked at Emeric again. Longer, this time. Then he lowered his head, not as far as he had for her, and not as if he'd decided to.

Behind Emeric, up the beach, Zale said something in their tongue. Emeric didn't need it translated. He had heard Zale ask it in the cabin, more than once, in the long mornings: what does it mean, the word for his eyes.

Ilenne turned her face away, toward the open water, and kept it there.

"No," said Zale.

Ferra wrote the word down again in the ledger as near as she could spell it, the way she had in the register a year and a half ago, and ringed it, as she had then.



The ask took a long time, because Zale had to have most of it twice.

The captain did the talking. Ilenne stood beside him in the water and helped, when Zale lost a word, with her hands: a flat hand going down, a hand cupped round nothing, two fingers walking. Zale sat in the sand with his eyes half shut and his lips moving, listening the way he listened to the lead line, and gave it to them in pieces, and corrected the pieces.

"Her house," he said. "Her mother's house. That's the queen. They had her shell. The thing that took it from me took it home, the whole way, and they read the lines on it, and that was how they knew she was alive, and where." He listened. "There was a lot of weeping, I think. He's going quickly over that."

"Go on," said Ferra.

"Her mother asks," said Zale, and stopped, and had it again. "Asks. It's a polite word; it's not tells. Her mother asks that we bring her home. Ourselves. Not to the edge of the thick water. He's very clear about the edge; he's said it three times. Not to the edge. To the city. All the way to the door." He opened his eyes. "In the boat that goes under. She's told him about the Challenger. I heard her word for it."

"What's her word for it?" said Imani.

"Loud," said Zale.

Ferra wrote. Emeric looked at the twelve standing in the wash with their lamps at their throats and their points down, and thought about the plan in the ledger, underlined once on the mess table in April: the wall's edge, the lock, and let her swim home. Nobody had planned past the lock. It had been somebody else's sea.

"Ask him why they can't take her," said Ferra. "Now. Tonight. They've come all this way."

Zale asked. The captain answered, and it went on a while, and Zale's face got slower and graver as it went, and when he gave it to them he gave it plainly.

"They came fast," he said. "There's a word she uses for spending, for money, the way you'd say a man spent his wages; she uses it for what's in her. They've spent nearly all they brought, getting here. They didn't stop. He's got enough left in the twelve of them to get the twelve of them home, if they go tonight and don't stop either." He nodded at Ilenne. "She hasn't. She's not full. He can feel it on her, the same way you can. Out in the thin, a long way short of the wall, she'd lose the tail, and they'd be carrying a woman on legs under the water for a thousand miles, and they can't. They'd lose her. They'd lose themselves trying."

"She's at five and a little," said Imani. "Of six. I'd have said the end of the summer." She looked at the water. "He's right. She'd be on legs before the wall."

Emeric could feel it too, without trying: the twelve notes going down, slowly, steadily, as they stood there, and hers beside them, lower than theirs and holding.

The captain said something else. Short. Then he said it again, the same words, looking up the beach.

"And her mother wants to see us," said Zale. "With her own eyes. He said that twice, so I'll say it twice. The queen wants to see the people from the cursed land with her own eyes." He paused. "That's the word, the cursed land. That's us. I'm not dressing it up."

"Of course she does," said Saban.

He got up.

He got up off the sand, which he had sat on under protest, and stood with his hands at his sides, and when he spoke it wasn't in the reasonable voice at all. Emeric had heard that voice from Saban perhaps three times in all the years he had known him.

"Majesty."

"Saban."

"Twelve." Saban didn't look at the water. He looked at Emeric. "I've got twelve faces. I'll have them on paper before I sleep, and they'll be good likenesses, and it won't matter, because before the summer's out they'll be standing in front of a queen describing this beach. The pier. The shed and what the shed's the shape of. The knights in their plate, and the four of those." He tipped his head at the sentries without turning. "Two years. Every man on this island paid double and sworn. The one piece of ground in the world nobody but us has ever seen. And it's been seen tonight by a power from past the wall, the first we've ever met, and they're going home with it in the next hour, and there is not one thing I can do about it." He took a breath. "And somebody tried to kill a queen's daughter with a fork, and it wasn't these, and we don't know who it was. And neither do they, or they'd have said. And you're about to say yes and sail into the middle of it in a boat that sprays."

"It doesn't spray now," said Ward, from the sand. "Two plugs and a gauge."

"Ward," said Saban.

Ward shut his mouth.

"I know," said Emeric.

"Knowing isn't counting." Saban's hands had closed. He opened them, deliberately, one finger at a time, which Emeric had never seen him have to do. "You'll be choosing a side in a war you can't see the edges of. I want it written that I said so. Before. Not after."

"It's written," said Ferra.

She turned the ledger round on her knees and held it up for him in Imani's lamp. Saban read it. Saban: the island seen, by twelve, foreign. A side chosen, blind. Against. He read it twice. It didn't make him look any less angry. He nodded once, and stayed standing.

Emeric looked at Ferra.

"Don't look at me like that," said Ferra. "You're the king."

"You've got the veto."

"I've got a veto. I haven't got a navy." She looked down the sand at the twelve in the water, and at Ilenne standing among them with the water round her knees, and her mouth went thin. "The wall's edge was the plan when nobody had asked us. Now we've been asked." She shut the book on her finger. "I'm not using it. Write that down somewhere, because I won't."

"The end of the summer," said Imani. "Not before. Tell him I said so."

"The boat takes twelve," said Ward.

"Then twelve go," said Emeric. "Whoever they are." He turned back to the water. "Zale. Tell him yes. When she's full. Tell him the end of the summer, and tell him who said so."

Zale told him. It took four tries, and on the third Ilenne put her hand flat on the water between them, palm down, the way she had put it on the slate in Fletcher's cabin to say yes, and the captain looked at her hand and then at Emeric and then, for a long time, at Imani.

He asked something.

"He wants to know if you're the one who took the stone out of her," said Zale.

"Yes," said Imani.

The captain bent his head to her. Not the half nod he'd given Emeric: all the way, slowly, the way he had to Ilenne in the wash, with his hand on the lamp at his throat.

Imani looked at him, and then at the sand. "Tell him she did most of it," she said. "Tell him she held still."



They went back under before the last of the light was off the sea.

The captain gave the shell back first. He held it out to Ilenne in both hands, the way she had held it out to Zale on the night before his run, and she took it and held it against her chest, and he said something to her that Zale didn't translate and nobody asked him to.

Then the twelve turned round and walked out into the bay.

It was worse going out than coming in. The legs were no better and the sand shelved soft under them, and the swell came in against their chests and pushed. The one Imani had got up went down again a few yards out, and his neighbour couldn't hold him, and they both went under in a wash of foam and came up spitting.

Ward got up.

He left his sword on the sand where he'd put it, and walked down the beach in his plate, and on into the sea, steadily, without looking back to see who was watching, until the water was at his waist and he had the fallen man by the arm. The man flinched. He was a soldier, black from his throat to his hip in the water, in the cursed land, with a man in steel holding him up, and his free hand went for the shaft he didn't have any more. Then it stopped. Ward stood there with the swell slopping against his breastplate and held him, patiently, the way he held a boy on the ropes, until the man had his feet. Then he walked him out a little further, to where it was deep enough, and let go.

The man looked at him. Ward nodded, the way he nodded across the yard when somebody's bout was finished, and came back in.

At chest depth they went under, one and then another. Emeric felt each of them change as they went, deep in the note, a hard pulling-in like a man getting a load up onto his shoulder that he shouldn't be lifting, and every one of them was lower afterward than before. Then the lamps were under the water, gold and one green, going down and out across the bay, faster now, toward the gap in the reef. The seals rolled off the line on either side and went after them. Out past the white the lights went along the reef once, all together, the way they had all afternoon, and turned, and went away into the lee, smaller, and further down.

Ilenne stood in the shallows until the last of them had gone past the reef. Then she came up out of the water on her own legs with the shell held against her, and went along the bottom of the beach and up the steps onto the slab, and stood at the end of it, where she had stood all afternoon.

Zale went after her, slowly, on his stick. He stopped a little way along the slab from her and leaned on the stick and didn't say anything. He wasn't looking at the lights.



The beach emptied by degrees.

Rylan got the knights up, which sounded like the pans going back into the cart, and walked them along to the works pier, and Osric went at the back with one greave off, pouring sand out of it in a long thin stream and telling Declan precisely how much. The sentries walked up the ramp into the hull one after another and stood in their frames. Saban went to the mess hut by himself with a lamp and a pad of Fletcher's paper, to put twelve faces down before he lost any of them. He didn't say goodnight to anybody. Ward went with Fletcher to look at the punts, which didn't need looking at. On the end of the pier Fletcher's operator sat at his key in a little cone of lamplight with his tongue between his teeth, sending what Ferra had given him to send, to Tess on the pad in the Motion Shop: Guests came at dusk. Twelve. Gone home tonight. Her house asks the whole way. Asked, answered yes. All well. F.

Ferra and Imani and Emeric stayed where they were, on the sand at the top of the beach, in the dune grass, with the cook's empty pot behind them and the bay going black.

Imani had her chin on her knees. She was looking along the slab to where Zale was standing a little way off from Ilenne, leaning on his stick.

"Sailors," said Imani.

"Don't," said Ferra.

"I said one word."

"You said it in a voice."

"I didn't say anything," said Emeric.

"You were going to," they said together, and Ferra put her shoulder against his hard enough to tip him over into Imani, and Imani pushed him back upright without looking, as if he were a shelf that kept leaning.

"We've just told a queen we'll bring her daughter home," said Ferra, after a while. "In a boat her daughter thinks is loud. To a city nobody's ever seen. Past a wall that kills ships. And I've got to put it in the accounts."

"Which column?" said Imani.

Ferra thought about it honestly. "I'll have to rule a new one."

"Her mother's a queen," said Emeric.

"So are we," said Imani. "I keep forgetting. I keep thinking I'll get a better chair."

Ferra opened the ledger on her knee, and Imani lifted the lamp and held it over the page without being asked. On the page from the deck that afternoon, under Out to South Sentinel. Lights in the lee. and the small pencilled Guests?, Ferra wrote in ink: 24 June. Twelve, out of the lee at dusk, her mother's guard. Asked the whole way. Yes. Gone home the same night. Then a line on its own, in the middle of the page, with space round it: Ilenne. She looked at it for a while. Then she took the pencil out of the fold and ruled one neat line through the question mark, and blew on the ink, and waited for it to dry.

Out past the reef the last of the lights went down. After that the water in the lee was only water, black and moving, with the yard's one lamp laid across it in a long broken stripe from the end of the slab, where she was still standing with her feet in the sea. Then, because it was dark and it was his job, Fletcher's man came down the pier and lit the second lamp.



At the Well that night the fourth sense stone flickered again, and the night hand wrote it under the old line from the October before last, in his ordinary hand: 21.40. Sense stone 4, one flicker. Cause not found. Others steady.
