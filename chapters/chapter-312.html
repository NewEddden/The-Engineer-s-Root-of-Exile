Chapter 312 - My Man in the Capital

Saban came home on the twenty-ninth of August with a head cold, a new hat, and eleven slips of paper sewn into the band of it, and he went straight up the hill from the station to the castle without stopping at his own door, which was how Ferra knew, before he had said a word to anybody, that some of it was bad.

He had been gone four days. Since the escheat nothing with Kaldo's name on it went up the north road without being opened twice, and the laundry bags that had carried his capital man's lines south all summer had begun to come down the line with their seams picked out and sewn up again by somebody who sewed worse than a laundress. So he had sent word north a fortnight ago by a road that did not use bags, and on the night the proclamation came, when the table rose, he had gone himself. Not to the capital: Saban had not set foot in the capital since the spring and did not mean to while the Lieutenant's chancery kept his name on a list. He had taken the night train to the railhead and a hired horse beyond it, to an inn on the north road which he would not name even to Ferra, where his man in the capital came out once a quarter to be paid and to drink something better than the capital's beer, and had come out early this time because he was asked; and Saban had sat in its back room for two nights with a jug and a fire he did not need, and people had come to the back door.

"It's priced," he said in the wine store that evening, and laid the slips out in a row under the lamp, face down, like a hand of cards he had not decided how to play. "All of it. You'll want to know what a thing cost before you know what it's worth." He blew his nose. "Ferra will, anyway."

"I will," said Ferra, and opened her book at a clean page and wrote the date at the top of it.

They were all there. Armand at the head, in the chair that had always been his, with his knees up and a cup in his long new hands; Bram with both elbows on the board, which made the board creak; Imani at Emeric's left with a pencil she was not using; Ward at the foot by the door, where he could see the stair. And on the bench against the wall under the wine racks, not at the table at all, the king.

Harrold came down to the table when he was asked, which was not every time it met, and tonight Emeric had asked him. He would not sit at it. Ward had offered him the head, and Armand had got up out of it with a great deal of ceremony, and Harrold had looked at the two of them and at the chair and gone and sat on the bench by the wall with his back to the racks. He had sat at the head of tables all his life, he said, and would like for once to hear what got said at one. Nobody had moved him. He sat there now in a plain brown coat of Abloh's that did not quite meet across his shoulders, with his hands on his knees, and listened, and almost never spoke; and Emeric, whose sense was out over the room from habit as it was out over every room, felt his father's note at the edge of it all evening: a knight's hard brightness kept well down, under a quiet that gave nothing away to anybody, and had been built for that.



"The towers first," said Saban, and turned over the first slip.

"Venn's," said Ward.

"Venn's are where they were. Two to a tower, set on end, same as June. They've had a ball up the river at a kite most mornings since, and they've got better at it; my man says they've stopped cheering when they hit." Saban put his finger on the slip. "This is what's new. Under every pair of Venn's, on the floor below the guns, there's a chest. Iron-banded, about the size of a child's cradle. Carried up the tower stair at night by eight men in the crown's coats, set down, and a man put to sleep beside it on a straw tick. A man in grey."

Emeric knew what was in the chest before Saban said it. He sat and let Saban say it anyway, because Saban had paid for it.

"What's in them?" said Bram.

"My man asked a gun crew, at the third jug. They said stones. He asked what sort. They laughed and wouldn't say, and then at the fourth jug one of them said it after all, to show he knew." Saban looked at Emeric. "The sort that's no use to anybody till it's broken."

He had drawn it once on a slate, at the end of a long winter evening, with Ward watching him from the door: the size of a stone against the reach of the dead air it left when it went, the cellar and the yard and the north road and the quarry, and the curve through them that was not a straight line. A stone twice the size did more than twice. He did not need the slate now. He put the capital's towers on the map of it in his head, the old gate towers and the four on the palace wall and the two either side of the cathedral porch, and set a chest the size of a cradle in each, and drew the rings; and watched the rings run into one another across the roofs until there was nothing inside the walls that was not inside one of them.

"The whole city," he said. "Wall to wall. The river. A good way out over the fields on the north side."

"Dead," said Imani.

"Everything in it that wants a stone. The lamps. The bands. The suits are plate and leather; the stealth suits are wool. Arthur's baton is a stick." And he himself would stand in the middle of it and not feel a man, as he had stood on the stream ground, and that he did not say, because he had said it once already, the winter of the war, and Ward had not forgotten it. "He's made his own capital a stream ground."

"His guns are stones," said Bram. "Venn's. They're gravity. They'll go dead with the rest."

"My man asked that as well. Fourth jug." Saban almost smiled. "The crew said the chests are for the day the guns don't do it."

Ward had not moved since the slip went over.

"Aldane broke his stones to blind us," he said. "And we weren't using our eyes." He looked at the slip as though it had been written to him and not to Saban. "Somebody's told the prince so. He'll have powder."

"He'll have powder," said Saban. "I'm coming to it." He turned the slip back over. "Nine silver, and four jugs at the tap under the north gate for my man and three gun crews, and my man's head the morning after, which he's put on my account."

Ferra wrote nine silver. She did not write the head.



"The men in grey," said Saban, and turned the second.

"There's more of them. Fourteen came south for Aldane. My man counts forty-one in the city now: one with every chest, two on every gate, four in the palace. Off the coast, same as before. Same trade. Break a stone for money and don't stay for the rest." He tapped the slip. "Only this time the crown's issued them something besides their wages. Lightning stones. Filled, and keyed, with a release cut in the face the way Ward's flash stone is cut. Except they don't flash." He looked at Imani, and then away from her. "My man saw one used on a post in the palace yard, for practice, at forty paces. The post's not there."

"How many?" said Imani.

"He saw one box opened. Twenty in it, in straw, like eggs. He didn't see how many boxes."

Imani said nothing for a while. She had been on the ward in the small hours of the third of July with Rylan's arm on a board in front of her and the smell of it in the room, and Emeric had watched her face then, and he watched it now, and it was doing the same thing: going very calm, and very far off, like somebody counting jars on a high shelf.

"Then I want twice the burn dressing I've got," she said. "And the oiled silk. And Bettany back on nights from the first of the month, and twenty more beds made up by October whether anybody's in them or not." She looked at Ferra. "Write it in my column, not yours, or you'll argue."

"I'd not argue."

"You'd price it."

Ferra wrote it in Imani's column.

"A grey mage," said Armand from the head of the table, "is a man who can break a stone and was never taught to do one other thing with it. The tower turned out a dozen a year, in my day, the ones the assessors couldn't place. We called them breakers, and we did not ask them to dinner." He turned his cup in his long fingers. "Put a loaded stone in a breaker's hand and he can do once, badly, at forty paces, what was done to Rylan's arm at the ferry."

"Once is plenty if it's at you," said Imani.

"I did not say it was not plenty." Armand drank. "I said it was once."

"Two silver," said Saban. "A porter's nephew in the palace yard. He was pleased to be asked anything."



The third slip was the shortest, and Saban did not read it. He put it down and kept his hand on it.

"The garrison's out of its barracks. All of it, since the middle of the month. The barracks by the river are empty and locked, and the Lieutenant's put the men out to lodge in the wards. Four to a house in the lower town. Six in the big houses on the hill, with a sergeant in every street and an officer in the vintners' hall." He lifted his hand. "The householders were told it's an honour to the city. They've each been given a paper for the men's board, to be paid after."

"After what?" said Ward.

"It doesn't say."

Ferra had stopped writing. "So there's nothing in the city a man could hit," she said, "that hasn't got a family round it."

"That's what it's for," said Emeric.

He had said, at the long room's table four days ago, with Ward against him and Ferra's war column already headed and dated before anybody spoke, that a short war was one where the other side could see who you had chosen not to kill. And here was his brother, seven hundred miles off, taking the choosing away. A soldier asleep on every kitchen floor in the lower town with somebody's children on the stair above him. An empty barracks by the river that nobody would ever trouble to hit, because there was nothing in it but beds.

"The lower town won't bear that long," said Harrold.

They all looked round. He had not moved on the bench.

"They bore the plague year. They bore the war. They did not bear the bread tax, in my father's time, past the first frost." He looked at his own hands. "A man on your floor eating your bread on a paper is a bread tax. Give it till the frost."

"My man says they're bearing it the way you'd bear a wet week, Majesty," said Saban. "Not well. Not loudly either."

"Not yet," said Harrold, and went back to his hands.

"That one cost nothing," said Saban. "The whole city knows it. I paid a silver for the count of the houses, because Ferra would ask."

"I'd have asked," said Ferra.



The fourth was not a slip. It was a sheet, folded in four, and when Saban opened it out on the board it was his man's pencil on the back of a smith's own price list: nails by the hundred, hinges by the pair, a cart tyre shrunk on at so much the wheel.

"There's a proclamation on the cross in every market square in the city," said Saban, "under the Lieutenant's seal. That the ban on fire-tubes is lifted for the crown's use, in the present emergency. And under the proclamation, in every square, there's a smith with a forge on a cart and a queue." He turned the sheet so that it lay square in front of Bram. "That's what they're making. My man stood behind the queue and drew it."

A tube of iron about as long as a man's arm. A stock like a crossbow's, cut square. A touch-hole on the top of the breech with a little pan beside it, and a hook for a match. Saban's man had drawn it carefully and badly, the way a man draws who has never drawn anything but has been told to get every line, and he had got every line.

Bram turned the sheet towards him with one finger and looked at it for a long time, breathing through his nose.

"He's rolled it," he said.

"Rolled it?" said Saban.

"Strip of iron. Wrapped round a bar, hot, and hammered on the seam till it held." Bram put his thumb down on a line along the barrel that Emeric had taken for a slip of the pencil. "Your man's drawn the seam. He's got a good eye; he didn't know what it was and he drew it anyway. It'll go there." He took his thumb away. "Fourth shot. Fifth, if he's lucky, and he'll not be. And when it goes it goes into the hand that's holding it."

Emeric looked at the drawing.

It was a bad gun. It was a bad copy of a bad copy of the levy's rifles, seen across a frozen meadow by men who had been running away, and there was not a line on it that was his; and it was his anyway. He had known, the year he drew the rifle at Bram's bench, that it was the first thing he had ever made that would go on being what it was after it left his hands, in anybody's hands. He had known it from the outside, like a thing out of a book. He looked at the seam that Saban's man had drawn so carefully on the back of a list of hinges, and thought of the smith in the square who did not know about the seam, and the boy of seventeen in a crown coat a size too big who would be handed the tube in a yard and told it was an honour; and found he had put his hand flat on the table beside the drawing, and was pressing down, as if that would keep it where it was.

He took his hand away. Imani had seen. She did not say anything.

"Powder," said Ward.

"Middens," said Saban. "Same as you did. The Lieutenant's men are paying the night-soil carts by the barrow, and there's beds dug behind the tanners' yards by the river that you can smell from the bridge. They'll be a year coming, my man thinks. Meanwhile they're buying every pound the royal fireworks makers have, which isn't many, and the fireworks makers are very rich this summer and very frightened of it."

"And the Church?" Armand had put his cup down. "The Church has preached against the tube for a hundred years. I have heard it done from a pulpit, at length, by a man who could not have told you which end the ball came out of."

"The Church has said nothing," said Saban. "Not from a pulpit, not in a square, not on a door. My man thinks that's the strangest thing in the city this summer." He folded the sheet in four again. "A silver to the smith, to let my man look. The smith thought he was buying. My man said he'd think about it."



"Last of the city," said Saban. "The gates."

He did not turn this one over at all. He knew it.

"Every gate captain changed since the escheat, and half the men under them. Not for better men. For married ones. Every man on every gate now has a wife and children inside the walls, in a house whose street is written down, and the list's kept in a press in the Lieutenant's own office with his own key on it." He let them hear it. "You can buy a man on a gate. Any trader can. I've done it, at more gates than I'll tell Ferra about. You can't buy him back his children's street."

"So he doesn't trust them," said Ward.

"He trusts their wives."

"Who has the north gate?" said Harrold, from the wall.

Saban told him a name. Harrold shook his head slightly; he did not know it.

"The Peverells had the north gate," he said, "from my grandfather's day."

"Old Peverell's gone to his daughter's in the country, Majesty, since July. His son had the gate after him for a fortnight. His son's not married."

"No." Harrold looked at the far wall for a while. "The old man used to let me out of it at night when I was sixteen. With two horses. He never once wrote it in his book."

Nobody asked him where he had been going with two horses. At the head of the table Armand had gone very still with his cup halfway to his mouth, and then put it down without drinking; and Emeric, who had heard an old man tell him once, years ago, about a boy of sixteen who waited at the foot of the tower stair with two horses every free day for four years and never came up, looked at the board and did not look at either of them.

"Six silver," said Saban, after a decent while. "A clerk in the Lieutenant's office who copies the list out fair once a week, because the Lieutenant likes a thing copied fair. The best six I spent this month."



He turned the next slip face down again without reading it, and left his hand on it.

"And one I paid for twice," he said.

Ward's note, at the foot of the table, went flat and hard.

"One of my men in the capital's been selling to the Lieutenant's people as well as to me. Since about Midsummer." Saban said it the way he would have said that a cask had come in short. "I'd a feeling in July. Little things. A question asked of somebody I'd sent, a day after I sent him, by a man who shouldn't have known to ask it. So in July I told this one a thing I told nobody else, and nobody at this table either, which I'll beg your pardon for now." He looked round the board. "That Kaldo's people, if they ever came, would come up the river by night, under the old bridge, in boats."

"And?" said Ferra.

"And four days later there was a chain across the river under the old bridge. Two chains, and a boom of whole trees between them, and a guard boat at each end with a lantern." Saban folded his hands on the slip. "The Lieutenant paid for that one, not me."

"That's the..." said Imani.

"Don't," said Saban.

Bram made a noise in his chest that was nearly a laugh. Ward did not.

"Which man?" he said.

"I'll not say at the table." Saban met his eye mildly. "It's not that I don't trust the table, Captain. It's a habit, and I'd like to keep it."

"What will you do with him?" said Emeric.

"Keep him. Pay him a little more, so he thinks I don't know." Saban turned his hat on his knee. "He's the best road into the Lieutenant's office I've got, now I know which way it runs. He'll hear what I want heard, and he'll carry it up the stair, and they'll pay him for it, and they'll buy more chain." He looked at Ferra. "Nobody's hurt by it. Not him either, if he's careful."

"Which column do I put him in?" said Ferra.

"Freight."

Ferra wrote it. From the bench by the wall came a small sound, short and dry, that in another man would have been a laugh; and Emeric felt his father's note lift with it, and settle again, like a dog that has heard its name and decided not to get up.



"That's the city," said Saban. "There's two more. One's the cheapest thing I bought all week and I nearly didn't bring it."

He took up the seventh slip and read it, this time, holding it out at arm's length to the lamp, because he would not wear spectacles in company.

"There was a carter at the inn the second night. A big man, with a split felloe on his near hind wheel, waiting on the wheelwright, and nothing to do but drink and talk, and nobody to talk to but me. He hauls the long road north, five weeks each way. He hauls for the brothers' mother house at Ostrel, and has done, nine years: bronze, in pigs and in sheet, from the founders on the south coast, and pays the march its toll at Pryor's Gate going up and again coming down, empty, which he says is the only road in the world where they charge a man for his empty wagon. The oak comes down from the north forests in other men's carts, in long baulks; he's queued behind them at the gate every time he's been." Saban put the slip down. "A walled yard behind the mother house. He says the yard takes it in by the cartload. He says nothing comes out of the gate. Not a cart. Not a board. Not a nail. Nine years."

"Nothing comes out?" said Emeric.

"Not a board."

"So I priced it," said Saban comfortably. "It's a roof. They've been building the great church at Ostrel since before I was born; every brother you ever met will tell you so whether you asked or not; it's to be the biggest in the world. The biggest roof in the world wants a deal of oak, and leading, and bells. Nine years of carts at the rate he says comes out at about what the House would lend a duke in a bad year." He shrugged. "A very rich roof. A jug of beer, that one. He was glad of the company."

Emeric was not at the table.

He was on a black riverbank at the crossing, with the ferry turning loose in the current behind him and Rylan's breath going wrong on the boards, and above the trees past the crossing a small white hull lifting out of the dark, unhurried, its stones working away in it as the K Discovery's worked in her, and nobody at its casting seat. And then at the long table, three nights later, with a pencil, drawing it from memory as well as he could for Armand, who had looked at the drawing for a long time and said that there was no such thing.

A roof did not need a wall round it.

"Find out how many carts," he said. "In a year. Since he started. And whether it's more this year than last."

Saban looked at him for a breath. Then he took his pencil out of his coat and wrote it on the back of the carter's slip, small, and did not ask why.

At the foot of the table Ward was looking at Emeric. Ward had felt that hull go over too. He did not say anything, and after a while he looked back at the door.

Ferra had already written roof. She did not cross it out. Under it she wrote: E. asked how many a year, and whether more this year. And beside roof, very small, she made the little hooked mark that Emeric had seen her put perhaps a dozen times in thirteen years against a figure she had written down and did not yet believe.



"And the council," said Saban.

He took up the eighth slip, and did not hold it out to the lamp. He held it in both hands on the table in front of him.

"Which cost me nothing at all, because my man in the minute room would bring me that for love. He's Emberfell born." He cleared his throat, which the cold did not help. "You'll know the council sat again on the nineteenth, behind closed doors, to certify the escheat and put the seal to it; no set in that room, no gallery. Before the vote the reader got up with a letter. From Emberfell, under the sovereign's seal, to the lords of the council. A queen's letter is read into the record whole; that's the custom; it's older than the council and they've never found a way round it. So he read it." Saban looked down at the slip. "My man wrote down what he heard that night, from memory, and put it beside the minute clerk's half-sheet in the morning to be sure. This is what was read."

He read it in his own voice, which was a trader's, and flat; and Emeric heard the other voice under it anyway, the court one, and the scar pulling at the mouth.

"My lords. I am told you mean to take Kaldo from the king's son by escheat, for the crime of fetching his father off a ferry. I have read your charge. I have read it twice, which is once more than it deserves."

Bram grunted.

"I was the king's second wife and I am sovereign in Emberfell, and I write to you as neither. I write as the friend of his third. Amara was my friend. Her sons are my friend's sons, which makes them mine to speak for, since she cannot, and since nobody in that room appears about to." Saban paused. "I will tell you what I know of the elder of them, and you may write it in your record beside your charge and let the next council read both."

He put the slip down.

"And there," he said, "the Lieutenant said, Enough."

Nobody spoke.

"He didn't raise his voice, my man says. He never does. The reader stopped. The Lieutenant held out his hand, and the reader walked the letter down the length of the table to him, and he folded it and put it inside his coat. And the minute says: the remainder not read, by the Lieutenant's order." Saban turned the slip over, though there was nothing on the back. "There was a second sheet. My man saw the reader turn to it before he was stopped. Then they voted, and you know how."

"She never sat down to write a thing in her life," said Harrold, from the wall. "She'll have written that standing at a window."

Nobody answered him.

Emeric sat with it. Rowena in the gate yard last autumn in her blue half-cloak, looking up at the cage round the keep and saying, Good gods, Emmy, what are you doing to it. Rowena, years ago, telling Ferra that the two notes he hummed over a sum he hadn't told anybody about were his mother's; Ferra had told him, and he had never asked Rowena how she knew. He had not known she called his mother her friend. He had not known she called her anything.

"She'll not tell us the rest," said Ward. "She wrote it to them."

"I'd like to know what's on the second sheet," said Imani.

"So would my man." Saban sat back. "The Lieutenant read it, after. At the head of the council stair, by himself, under the lamp, a good while. My man was on the landing below with the minute book and couldn't get up past him to the clerks' room, so he stood and waited, and watched him read." He blew his nose again. "Then the Lieutenant went down the stair past him two steps at a time, like a man going down to hit somebody. My man says there was nobody at the bottom."



"That's the lot," said Saban.

He squared the slips into a stack, eight of them, and put the stack in front of Ferra for her book. The ninth, which Emeric had watched him take out of his hat band with the others at the start of the evening and keep under the flat of his left hand through the guns and the gates and the carter and the queen, he put back into the band without a word; and put his hat on; and said good night to the table, and to the king, and went up the stair.

Emeric gave him the length of the yard. Then he went up after him.

Saban was waiting at the top of the wine store steps, under the yard lamp, in the warm dark. Somebody in the kitchen across the yard was scouring a pan and singing at it, badly, the same line over and over.

"This one's not the table's," said Saban. He took off his hat, and took the slip out of the band, folded very small, and held it out. "It's my woman in the palace laundry. It came out on the last night with my man in the capital, sewn into his coat lining by her own hand; she'd not trust it to a bag. Not this one." He did not let go of it at once. "It's about your mother."

Emeric took it.

"I've read it," said Saban. "I'd sooner I hadn't been the first, but somebody had to be, and I'd sooner it was me than a man with a needle on the night express." He nodded across the yard at the keep. High in its west face one window was lit, not by the castle's light but by something smaller and yellower that moved a little. "Take it up to him before you open it. It's his as much as yours. I'd not want to be the man who read it to him."

"Saban."

"It's a laundress's word, at night, at third hand. It's not proved." Saban put his hat back on. "But she's been right about everything she's ever sent me, and she sat on this six weeks because she was frightened. She's right to be. She asks that nobody comes to her." He looked at the slip in Emeric's hand. "Nobody will."

He went off across the yard towards the gate, and down the hill. Emeric stood under the lamp with the slip in his fist and felt Saban's note go all the way down into the town, steady, past the market and the river street, to his own door, and in; and then he went up the keep stair.



His father was awake. He had known he would be; he had felt the note from the yard, upright and still at the table under the window, not reading.

He did not have to knock. The door opened while he was still two steps below it.

"You walk like a man trying not to wake anybody," said Harrold. "You always did. Since you were seven." He stood back to let him in. "It wakes everybody."

The room was the room Ferra had given him in July, and it looked, eight weeks on, as if a careful man had been put into it and told he might stay. The bed made square. The coat Abloh had made him on its peg. The castle's light in its glass shade on the desk, switched off; Harrold had asked Ferra in his first week what it cost to run, been told to the copper, and gone back to the candles he had read by for forty years, and Ferra had let him, and charged the candles to the house. One of them was burning now in a saucer on the table under the open window. Beside it, in a stack as tall as a child, were Kaldo's year books from the first, Ferra's fair copies; and the top one, year five, lay open on the others with a spill of paper in it to keep his place.

Out of the window the town went down the hill in its lamps to the river, and beyond the roofs the dome sat lit from inside on its rise like a lantern somebody had set down and forgotten.

"Saban kept one back," said Harrold.

"You saw."

"He'd had it under his hand since the guns." Harrold shut the door. "I've watched men hold back a paper across a table since before you were born, Emeric. Lately, mostly your brother."

Emeric held the slip out.

"He said it's yours as much as mine," he said. "He said not to open it on the stair."

Harrold looked at the slip in his son's hand and did not take it straight away. Then he did. He sat down at the table, in the candle's light, and unfolded it, and smoothed it flat with the side of his hand; and then he turned it round on the table, the way Ferra turned her book, so that it lay between them and they could both read it.

It was in Saban's hand, small and fast, on the back of a list of the palace's linen: sheets by the dozen, napkins by the score, the chapel's altar cloths by name. Saban had written down what the woman had said to him, in her order and not his, and had not tidied it.

In the second week of July, the week after the ferry, the cardinal had come down to the royal crypt in the forenoon with two lay brothers and asked for the dead queen.

Not for the first queen. For the dead one. For her rest, he had said: that she be given into the Church's keeping, as the king had been given into it until the river, and kept with every honour until things were settled.

The crypt keeper could not open the inner grille without the key, and since the Lieutenant had taken the chair the crypt's key, like the treasury's, had been kept by the first queen's household. So the keeper had sent his boy running, and the first queen had come down the crypt stair herself.

Saban's woman had been in the crypt chapel behind the screen with the altar linen, which went up to be washed on the second Monday of the month; and when she heard who it was on the stair she had not dared come out.

The first queen said no. She said it again, louder, and turned and said to the keeper that he had heard her say it, and the keeper said he had. Then she said something more, low, close to the cardinal, that Saban's woman did not catch. And the cardinal went back up the stair with his brothers behind him, and did not look at the keeper as he passed.

That night, a little after one, the first queen's own steward came down to the laundry and asked for the heavy canvas off the drying racks, all of it, and the packing straw. Eight men of the first queen's household, her own and not the palace's. A dray in the chapel yard with its lamps hooded. The first queen herself in the yard in a dark cloak, holding a lamp, and nobody else with a light. Saban's woman, sent down with the canvas, had stood by the yard wall and seen them bring something up the crypt stair: long, and wrapped, and so heavy that the eight of them stopped twice on the stair to set it down.

In the morning the inner grille had a new lock. And the keeper brought the pall up to the laundry himself, the one that had lain over the dead queen's place since before Saban's woman came to the palace, which went up to be washed at Midsummer and Midwinter and came straight back down; and told her to wash it and fold it and keep it in the press, because there was nothing for it to go back over.

Nobody had said a word since. Not the keeper, not the steward, not the first queen's women. Nobody she knew knew where.

Under it Saban had written, smaller: She is sure. S.

Harrold read it twice. Emeric watched him read the second time; his father's eyes went back to the top of the slip and down it line by line, at the same pace as the first time, not skipping, as if he were walking a road twice to see whether it was the same road.

Then he sat back.

Emeric felt it happen in him. The knight's brightness in his father's note, which he kept down all day under the king's quiet, came in from the edges, hard, to the middle, like a hand snatched in against the chest off a hot iron; and stayed there.

"It took ten men to carry her down," said Harrold.

His voice was at the old pitch, the one from his father's office the night before the south road: low and level and very clear, at a volume that carried exactly to the edge of the table and died there. A whisper is not a secret. Harrold talked at that pitch in Kaldo all the time, from habit; he had used it on purpose once before, after the table four days ago, to say a thing about the cathedral he wanted his son to have and nobody else. There was nobody on the stair tonight. There was nobody in the keep who did not already know everything his father could have told him. He used it anyway, and this time Emeric did not think he knew he was doing it.

"The crystal," Harrold said. "I had it brought in at night as well. There are not many ways to get a thing that size down that stair. Ten men, with ropes. They stopped three times." He looked at the slip. "Sabina did it with eight."

"You went down," said Emeric.

"Once a year."

He did not have to ask which day.

"I told the keeper I was going down to pray," said Harrold. "I don't pray. He knew it. He'd open the grille for me and go back up and sit on the stair till I came out, and he never once asked what I did down there." He was quiet. "I didn't do anything. I stood."

"I went down once," said Emeric. "I was twelve. He wouldn't open the grille."

"No. I'd told him not to."

Emeric waited. His father did not go on for a long time; the candle had leaned over and righted itself in the draught from the window before he did.

"People say they look asleep," said Harrold. "In those. The makers say it. They sell them on it." He put a finger on the edge of the slip and moved it square to the table's edge, as Ward squared a letter on a desk. "She doesn't. I wasn't going to have you see that at twelve."

Emeric had seen it. In the overlay, a month gone, through his father's eyes, for as long as the memory had held it, which had not been long; and his father had let him, and had not looked away from it himself, and they had not spoken of it since. He did not speak of it now.

"Why would she?" he said instead. "Sabina. She hated her."

"Yes."

"Then why?"

Harrold took so long that Emeric thought he would not answer.

"Because he asked," he said.

He picked the slip up, and read the middle of it again, the stair and the keeper and the something more that the laundress had not caught, and put it down.

"I wrote to you about her," he said. "In June. That she'd refused the close something once, a long time ago, and never told me what."

"I remember."

"That's twice."

And then he sat and worked at it, and Emeric watched him do it. He had watched it once before, at Ferra's table, a month ago: his father sitting very still with his hands on his knees and his eyes on nothing, taking a thing he had looked at every day of his reign and turning it over until he found the side of it he had never seen. A son of twenty-two at the palace door, on the night of the farewell. A left hand raised in blessing. The right hand closed. It had taken him a long time then. It did not take as long now.

"I think I know what it was," said Harrold. "The first time."

He did not say what. Emeric did not ask him.

"It doesn't matter what it was," said his father, after a while. "It was the same thing." He looked out of the window, at the dome on its rise. "She's watched him at my elbow since she was sixteen."

That was all he said about it, and it was enough. Emeric could do the rest of the sum himself. Whatever Augustine had wanted with the queen's body, he had wanted it, as he had wanted everything, for what it would do to the king. And there was one woman in the capital who had spent thirty years refusing to be the Church's road to her own husband, out of nothing nicer than pride; and when the cardinal came down the crypt stair asking for the other road, the one she had always hated, she had not given him that either.

"What does he want with her?" said Harrold.

His voice had not changed, but something under it had.

"He has what was in her head. In his hand. You told me so; you heard it." He did not look at the grey bead in Emeric's band. He looked carefully at the candle instead. "What does he want with the rest of her?"

"I don't know," said Emeric.

It was nearly true.

He thought of Armand in the dome at dawn, at the foot of the tree, on the morning after the stair, waking with hands that were not old any more and looking at them. He thought of a grey bead that had answered a string at the ferry, out of a closed fist, as a thing answers that is awake and can hear. He thought of a crystal that kept a body exactly as it went in, no better and no worse, for as long as anybody liked. Three things. He had never in his life set them side by side, and he did not do it now. He came up to the edge of it, as a man feels for a step in the dark with his foot and finds the step is there; and he took his foot back. Not tonight. Not in this room, to this man, with that candle and that slip between them. Not until he knew something, and he did not know anything; he had a feeling and a laundress.

"Saban could find her," he said. "In a month. Less."

"Yes."

"Then..."

"And if Saban can find her, so can he."

Harrold turned the slip face down on the table.

"Count them," he said. "Eight men. A steward. A dray driver, if he isn't one of the eight. A woman in a cloak with a lamp. That's who knows where your mother is tonight, and that's the whole of it, and not one of them is a man the cardinal has ever been able to buy, or Sabina wouldn't have let him near a rope." He laid his hand flat on the back of the slip. "Every man you send to ask is one more man who knows somebody is asking. And your brother pays people to notice who is asking. You heard Saban. One of his own sells to the Lieutenant, and he's a careful man, and he didn't know it till July."

Emeric said nothing.

"She's safer lost," said Harrold. "Lost to us is lost to him."

It was the right answer. Emeric could see that it was the right answer the way he could see that a beam was the right size, from across a room, without having to work it. He sat with it anyway, because it was his mother, somewhere in the capital tonight in a dray's worth of laundry canvas, in a place he did not know, kept by a woman who had never forgiven her; and for once in his life he did not want the right answer. He wanted to send somebody.

"Nobody goes looking," he said.

"Nobody."

"I'll tell Saban."

"Saban's told you," said his father. "He brought it to you on a stair and not to the table."

He picked the slip up by one corner and held the corner to the candle.

Emeric did not stop him. The linen list caught first, the sheets and the napkins and the altar cloths, and then Saban's small fast hand on the other side of it, and Harrold turned it in his fingers so that it burned evenly and laid the last of it in the saucer, and they both watched it curl and go grey.

"Write nothing down," said Emeric. "You told me that. The night you sent me here."

"I did." Harrold blew the ash flat with one breath. "And by every account I have read you have written everything down since, every day, for thirteen years, and built a city out of it." He nodded at the stack of Ferra's books by his elbow without looking at them. "I'm in year five. There's a page on the drains I've read three times. I still don't understand it, and I can't put it down."

Emeric laughed. It surprised him. It came out of him short and rough, not much of a laugh, and his father's mouth moved at the corner, and that was all either of them made of it.

"Your brother," said Harrold.

"In the morning."

"He's in the stables by five."

"You know that?"

"I've been in this house eight weeks," said Harrold. "I can count."

Emeric looked at him.

"Tell him yourself," said his father. "Not from me. He'll take it better from you, and he's had enough of things from me that came round by somebody else." He was quiet. "Go down to the stables and tell him about his mother, and then let him show you the horses, and look at them as if you knew anything, and say they're good."

"I don't know anything about horses."

"Neither did I," said Harrold, "at his age. I had to be taught by a man who kept a gate."

The candle had burned down a good inch. Out of the window the dome's light had gone down to its night glow, and below the keep somebody was walking the wall with a lantern, and the singer in the kitchen had stopped.

"I'd like to thank her," said Harrold. "Sabina."

"Can you?"

"No." His father considered it, honestly, the way he had considered the drains. "She'd take it as an insult. And she'd be right to."

Emeric did not ask why. He thought he knew, and he thought that if he made his father say it the knowing would be worth less.

"Go to bed," said Harrold.

"I'm going to sit here."

His father looked at him for a while across the saucer of ash. Then he reached out and moved the candle from his own side of the table into the middle of it, so that it lit them both the same; and picked up year five, and found the spill of paper, and opened it at the drains.

Emeric sat. He let his sense go out, from habit, over the castle and down the hill. Ferra in the counting room, still up, a quick bright note bent over a column. Imani on the ward among her sleepers, moving bed to bed. Ward in the wine store, alone at the table where they had left him, not moving at all. And somewhere below them all his brother, asleep, whom he would go and find at five.

He did not go down to any of them. He sat at his father's table under the window, and his father read about the drains, and turned the page, and every so often made a small noise at something on it; and the candle burned, and neither of them looked at the saucer.



On the first of September Emeric rode out along the old road by the Whispering Woods, the one they had come in on, with Rylan beside him at a walk.

It was Rylan's first morning out of the yard on anything but his own feet. Imani's list said nothing about horses, which he had read aloud to her at breakfast to make sure; and she had said a walk, and the mounting block, and that if he leaned out of the saddle to open so much as a gate she would hear about it before he got home. Waylon had chosen them the two quietest of the castle's four and stood in the stable arch watching his brother get up into the saddle with an expression of great politeness.

They went out round the ring road and up onto the old road, and for an hour neither of them said much. Rylan flexed the arm on the reins without seeming to know he did it. Past the last farms the trees came in on the right, dark and close-set, and the fields opened on the left, stubble now, most of them cut. A mile into the wood Emeric turned his horse without saying why, and Rylan turned his, and they came back the way the carriage had come.

Kaldo announced itself two miles out.

He was waiting for the smell, he found, the way a foot waits in the dark for a stair that has been taken up. It did not come. Stubble, horse, the wood, a bonfire of hedge trimmings somewhere off across the fields. What came instead was a light, low over the trees ahead and to the right, small and very bright, where the sun had found the top floors of the tall new tower, the one with the crane still on its crown, and was coming back off the glass.

The road came out of the trees through the farmland, and crested, and the whole thing arrived at once.

The dome first, because the eye went to it now before anything: up on the rise in the middle, iron ribs and glass, higher than the keep's two square towers beside it, with the tree a dark shape inside and the six round towers standing about it under their blue cones. Then the glass towers, standing up out of the roofs along the civic ring and the avenues. Red tile packed between the avenues, and the avenues lined with trees. The wall all the way round, whole, with its bastions pushed out at the turns; outside it the ring road, black, and the viaduct beside it. While he looked a white train came round on the viaduct from behind the west wall, five cars, and ran along under the near wall and out of sight behind a bastion, and no sound of it reached the crest at all.

East, the river, wide and brown as it had ever been. The Port Town along the river road; the port's blue cranes over the quays, a coaster at the deep berth, and beyond them the works' stacks, their smoke leaning off east toward the sea. South-west, between the wall and the woods, the garden suburb in its young trees. From the north the aqueduct came striding in over the fields from the Mere, arch after arch with no gap in it anywhere, and a train going along the top of it toward the town, small as a thing on a shelf. Above the Mere the falls dam stood grey in the notch of the hills. The west hill's terraces and its ruin were as they had always been. And up the river past the port, in the gorge, a raw place of rock and timber and cranes where the river dam was going in.

He read it three times again, and got three new answers.

Defensively, a better wall than the one he had first seen from here. Restored, every stone, with lamps along its walk and bastions so that a man on any point could see the wall's foot both ways; built to make men come at it across open ground, and Ward would see they did it badly. And every stone of it, old and new, laid by people who had never once had to think about the sky. A white hull had gone up off the crossing in July without a light or a sound. The tower kept ships in its yard. He had a ship of his own that the capital's tower had written down in a margin as a falling star. From up there a wall was something to look down at.

Economically, nothing left to read. Every berth taken that he could see, trains on all three lines, the works' smoke going up in the middle of a working day; and the whole of it, every rent and due and office, forfeit to the crown by a sheet with a leaning seal. The cranes had not noticed.

Sanitarily, nothing at all. He went looking for it the old way and the town gave him nothing to read. Ottoline's cards had a hundred and one thousand people in the four baronies at midsummer, a good many of them down there, and he could not smell one of them from here.

Rylan considered the town a while longer, with a hand to his ribs. "Doesn't smell of anything," he said. "I keep thinking I've lost my nose."

Then the fourth, at the river, the one he had looked at too long. Force. Still going past the town every second of every day, as it had since before anybody here was born, and still, for everything he had built, doing very little but carry the Grayrock barges; up in the gorge the cofferdams held a piece of it aside while men poured stone, and it would be two years yet before it drove so much as a lamp.

Two years.

He looked at it slightly too long, and this time did not make himself stop.

"Home?" said Rylan.

"Home."

"Good. If I'm late to dinner Imani'll put it on the list."



Saban's next slips came down on the ninth of September in a laundry bag that somebody had sewn up again properly, which Saban took as a good sign and Ward as a bad one.

There was nothing from the carter: he was on the road north with his mended wheel, and Saban's man would find him on his way back. There was nothing from Emberfell. There was a new count of the men in grey, forty-four, and a line from the tap under the north gate that the Venn's crews had stopped going out to the river at dawn. They could hit a kite now, the Lieutenant had told them, and he would rather the city did not watch them do it every morning.

And at the bottom, on the back of a list of the palace's candles, three lines in Saban's hand from the woman in the laundry.

The first queen to chapel at seven every morning, as for thirty years. The crypt keeper given a new coat, good wool, by the first queen's steward. Nothing else.

Harrold read it over Emeric's shoulder in the study, standing, with his cup of tea in his hand going cold, and then read it again.

"A new coat," he said.

Emeric waited.

"She always did pay her people properly," said his father, and went to the window with his tea, and stood there drinking it, looking north.
