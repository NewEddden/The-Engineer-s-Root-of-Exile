Chapter 380 - Forty Tonnes, Times Six

Ferra's sheet said forty tonnes a month, and six months, and under it, in her small hard figures, 230 before fishing and the greenhouse decks, call it 240, and under that, in a different pencil, Maren to check.

Maren had it pinned to the board over the range at five in the morning on the twenty-sixth of January, a Monday, with the kitchen still dark except for the range and the one lamp, and the chairs still up on the tables in the front room, and a cup of tea going cold at her elbow. She'd been looking at it since four.

It was a lot of food. She'd known it would be. She'd fed the castle since the first hungry winter, and the knights' mess, and the yard's dinner hour, and every Long Table since the first, and she'd fed eight hundred people at a sitting before now, at the crowning, on trestles in the square. But she'd fed them once. She'd never fed eight hundred people every day for half a year from what could be put in a hold in January, on ships, with no market at the end of the lane.

Two hundred and forty tonnes. A kilo and a half a head a day, Ferra had written, and Maren had crossed out the kilo and the half and written more for the knights, less for the old man, and then, after a while, crossed out the old man, because Armand ate more than any of them now and pretended he didn't.

She took the sheet down and sat at the end of the long table with it, and her tea, and the green ledger.

She didn't open the ledger. She put her hand on it.

The flakes had been an accident, and she'd never tell anybody that, and Bram knew, and would.

It had been the second Tuesday in December. She'd gone up on the Hod with the rest of them at six in the morning for her hour at the Band's foot with the scrubber shut and Imani's clock on her, the way she'd gone up every Tuesday since September; and she'd left a big pan of wheat on the back of the range to cook through for the morning's frumenty, the way she always did; and she'd bled at forty-nine minutes, and come down with her nose stuffed with Imani's gauze and her temper in bits, and gone straight to bed in the afternoon for the first time in her life, and forgotten the wheat.

By the next morning it had gone tacky and stiff, like wet sand. She'd been going to throw it to the hens. But Bram had made her a pair of steel rollers for the pastry in the autumn, a beautiful thing on a cast frame with a crank, and she'd been in a mood to break something, and she'd put a handful of the spoilt wheat through it out of pure spite, to see it squashed.

It hadn't squashed into a sheet. It had come off the rollers in flakes. Each grain on its own, pressed thin as a fingernail, curled a little at the edge.

She'd stood there with the crank in her hand and looked at them.

Then she'd put them on a tray in the bread oven, low, because she was a cook and that's what you did with anything thin and wet that you didn't want to go off, and taken them out when they'd gone gold and crisp and smelled of toast. And then she'd tasted one, and stood in her kitchen in her apron with the light coming up grey over Gate Avenue, and eaten the whole tray with her fingers, standing up.

The king had a sheet in his pile of sheets that said toast it. Oats, he'd meant, and barley, a dry porridge for the galleys that you didn't have to cook. She'd done that in October, and it was good, and it kept. But that was his. The flakes were hers. Tess had looked at the tray on the Thursday and looked at Emeric and said, "He's got the face," and Emeric had said, "What face?" and Tess had said, "The one where somebody's done a thing you were going to tell them about," and Maren had pretended not to hear either of them.

She'd put them in milk, the next week, because a dry flake was a biscuit and nobody wanted to eat biscuits for six months. Cold milk from the cold store, in a bowl. They went soft in about a minute, and before that they were the best thing she'd had in her mouth since Zale's coffee. Wren had eaten four bowls at one sitting and gone to the Motion Shop sick and come back the next day and asked for a fifth.

And then Bram had built the gun.

That had been his idea and Fennick Crake's, and she'd never forgive either of them. It was a short black iron bottle with a lid that bolted down and a long lever, on a frame over a fire, and you put rice in it, or wheat, and bolted the lid, and heated it till the gauge said it was mad, and then Fennick knocked the lever with a mallet and the lid came off all at once into a sack the size of a tent and the whole of the yard went deaf. And in the sack there was rice, puffed up to six times its size, white and light as foam.

The first time they'd fired it in her yard behind the eating house, two of the girls had screamed and the coffee house under the South Gate had sent a boy up to ask if the Church was back.

Fennick had fired it eleven times since. He'd said each time was to check the gauge. Bram said the gauge had been checked on the second. Fennick said you couldn't check a gauge too often. Maren said that if he fired it in her yard one more time without telling her first she'd put him in it, and he'd looked, for a moment, as if he were considering whether he'd fit.

So now there were three of them. The toasted oats, which kept for ever. The flakes, which kept if they were dry and the cask was sealed and nobody left the bung out. And Fennick's rice, which kept, and floated in milk like snow on a pond, and didn't fill a knight for more than an hour but made the knight very happy for the hour.

She'd put a sugar glaze on a batch of the flakes once, just to see, and her girls had fought over them, and she'd taken the tray off them and said that no man going past the end of the world was eating sweets for his breakfast, and the girls had looked at each other in a way she didn't like.

They'd be doing it the week she wasn't looking. She knew that.

She wrote it out for the hold on the Monday morning, in her square hand, with the tea gone cold.

Toasted oats and barley. Forty casks. Dry. Bung in.

Flakes, wheat. Sixty casks, lined. Dry or they go to paste. Bung in or I'll know.

Puffed rice. Twenty casks. Light. Stow it on top of something heavy or it'll float off in the first sea.

With milk. The cold store's milk, or the tins if Tess gets the tins. Not with beer. I've seen the knights.

Then she looked at it, and crossed out I'll know, and wrote over it, smaller, someone will know, and sat with her pencil over it a while longer, and didn't change it back.

Ilenne came into the kitchen at nine with her hood up and sea water in a bucket, and sat down at the end of the long table where the light from the window was, and took out a stick of charcoal and a pad of Tess's cartridge paper, and began to draw fish.

She'd been doing it for a week. Imani had asked her to. Past the wall, past the thick water, there would be fish in the nets, and some of them would be like the bay's fish and some of them wouldn't be, and Imani's shade cards could say how dense a thing was but not whether it would put a man to sleep or turn his water green. So Ilenne was drawing every fish she'd ever eaten at home, and every one she'd been told not to, and writing beside each one in her careful new Kaldo letters what it was for.

Maren put a cup of the strong tea in front of her without asking. Ilenne drank tea now. She'd decided in the autumn that Kaldo's tea was the one thing in Kaldo nobody had explained badly.

"That one," said Maren, and pointed.

It was long and flat, with a fin all the way down its back like a comb, and Ilenne had drawn it lying on its side with its mouth a little open.

"Good," said Ilenne. "Very good. White. You cook it with leaves." She wrote GOOD beside it, in capitals, and then, after thinking, LEAVES.

"What leaves?"

Ilenne thought. "Sea leaves. The long ones. You do not have them." She looked at Maren. "Your cook on the island. She knows. She has a leaf like it. She put it with my fish. The second time." She drew a long ribbon of weed beside the fish. "She is a good cook."

"Is she," said Maren.

Ilenne looked up at her. There was something in her colour, at the throat, a faint shift Maren had learned to see over the autumn and never learned to read.

"Not like you," said Ilenne. "Like the island. She knows the fish. You know..." She waved the charcoal at the kitchen, the range, the hanging pans, the rows of jars, the long table. "Everything else."

"That's a very nice thing to say."

"It is true." Ilenne went back to her drawing. The next one was round and spiny and she drew it with a heavy black line round it and wrote NO. "This one, no. This one is pretty and you eat it and you sleep two days and maybe you wake up." She drew a little face with its eyes shut beside it. "The guards eat it for a joke. To show they are guards."

"Do they wake up?"

"Mostly," said Ilenne, and drew another.

By eleven there were thirty-one fish on the paper, and nineteen of them said GOOD, and four said NO, and the rest said things like ONLY THE TAIL and NOT IN THE WARM MONTHS and, beside one long gleaming creature with a face like a disappointed bishop, MY MOTHER LIKES IT. I DO NOT.

"Which one's that one?" said Maren.

"That one is for the queen," said Ilenne. "If she asks you to eat it, you must eat it. Then you are a guest." She considered the drawing. "Then you can be sick after. Outside."

"You," said Maren. "Not me."

Ilenne put the charcoal down.

"You come," she said. It wasn't a question, quite, but it was going up at the end the way her questions did when she wasn't sure of the words.

Maren took the tea cup away, though it wasn't empty, and took it to the sink, and washed it.

"I've the stores to get in," she said, with her back to the table. "Two hundred and forty tonnes. That's a lot of fish, love. You'd better draw some more."

When she turned round Ilenne was drawing again. She didn't look up. But her colour had gone very slightly pale at the throat, the pale the guards had gone when she'd come into the pier hall on South Sentinel in her own skin; and Maren didn't know what it meant, and found she could guess.

The stores went into the yard's long shed on the Tuesday and the Wednesday, from the railway and the cold store and the counting house's warehouses and Milford's carts, and Maren stood at the door of the shed with Ferra's list on a board and a pencil behind her ear and checked every one of them in.

Flour in barrels, lined, by the hundred. Salt beef and salt pork in brine, from Hester's herds, by the cask. Peas and beans dried hard as shot. Oats. Barley. The Milford potatoes in straw, which would keep for four months if nobody let them sweat. Onions in nets. Cabbage as kraut in crocks, which was Emeric's, and which the knights had complained about for three years and now ate without being told, because Imani had stood up in the mess in the autumn and said she'd not lost a man to the gums since the first crocks went out and she didn't propose to start now. Jars of everything Maren and her girls had put up since September, fruit and pickles and the plum jam and the tomato that Zale's seed had made, sealed with wax and packed in sawdust. Cheese in wax. Sugar from Milford's beet. Tea and coffee in lined chests. Zale's spices in little tins. The soy sauce, eight casks of it, which Ilenne had insisted on, because the merfolk liked it and she wanted to see her mother's face.

And the mana food, in its own corner of the shed, with Imani's mark on every sack: Kaldo's induced crops, the wheat and the beans from the dome's beds and the walled district at Milford, that carried the dense mana in them the way a well carried water. Counted on the dose cards. Eaten a little every day by everyone aboard, so that by the time they reached the thick water their bodies had been told about it.

Imani came on the Wednesday afternoon with the medical stores, which had their own shed, and their own list, and a lock Saban had fitted, and stood with Maren in the doorway while the crates went in.

"Two of everything," said Imani. "Me and Bettany. If one hospital deck goes down, the other one's whole." She ticked the crates off on her own list. "Dressings. Splints. Plaster. The blood in the cold boxes, which go on the K Endeavor and nowhere else. The vaccine kit. The drain cuffs." She stopped at a crate stencilled WASH. "And these."

She opened it. It was full of small brass pumps, each the size of a lamp, with a glass bottle screwed underneath and a long thin nozzle on top like a heron's beak.

"What are they?" said Maren.

Imani took one out and worked the handle twice with her thumb and turned the nozzle on her own wrist, and a fine cold mist came out of it, so fine it hung in the air for a moment like breath.

"Boiled salt water," she said. "With the antiseptic in it. A hand pump, so it works with every stone on the ship dead. For a wound, a burn, a cut you can't get under a tap." She put it in Maren's hand. "Tess's sprayers for the fields were where it started. The ones Bren cleaned with a wire. I took the nozzle and made it finer." She looked at the crate. "One in every galley. You'll want to know how it works."

Maren looked at the thing in her hand. Then she pulled her left sleeve up, where there was a scald on the inside of her forearm from the stockpot on Monday, angry and red, that she'd been putting butter on, which Imani had told her nineteen times not to do.

She worked the handle. The mist came out cold, and stung, and then didn't.

"Hm," she said.

"Butter," said Imani, looking at the scald.

"Never."

"I can smell it from here."

Maren put the sprayer back in the crate and pulled her sleeve down and wrote Wash. One a galley. And show them on her list, and didn't say anything else, and neither did Imani. They stood in the doorway of the shed together and watched the crates go in. After a while Imani said, without looking at her, "Tuesday," and Maren said, "I know," and that was all either of them said about it.

The beer was the last thing, and the king did it with her himself, at night, in the inn's old brewhouse behind the square, where the first beer Kaldo had ever brewed on purpose had been brewed sixteen years before by a man who'd never done it in his life and a boy who'd watched him.

He came at eight on the Wednesday with a basket. He'd brought a thermometer, a long glass one in a wooden case, and a book with nothing in it yet, ruled into columns, and three earthenware jars with cloth over the tops that had the class's yeast in them, the one the class had grown out of Maren's own barm years ago and kept going ever since in the Polytechnic's cold room like a pet.

"You'll want it clean," he said.

"I know how to keep a copper clean."

"I know you do. I'm saying it so it's in the book." He wrote it in the book. Clean. Scald everything. Maren knows.

They brewed. It took most of the night. He was a better brewer than he'd been sixteen years ago, and worse than he thought he was, and she let him think it, because she'd been letting men in her kitchen think things for thirty years. He measured everything. He wrote everything down. The heat of the mash every quarter hour, on the thermometer, in the book. The time the wort went into the copper. The hops, by weight. The time it came off. The heat when the yeast went in. "So the next one's the same," he said. "And the one after that. So it's not luck."

"It's never been luck."

"It's been you. That's not the same thing. You can't put you in a cask."

She looked at him across the copper. He was looking at the thermometer.

"No," she said. "You can't."

He had a list of his own, which he didn't show her, but which she read upside down over his shoulder while he was skimming, because she'd been reading men's lists upside down for thirty years too. Beer, two kinds, light and dark. Cider, the Milford apples. A perry, which was new, the hard little pears off the orchard belt that nobody had ever found a use for. Mead, with Hester's honey, which was new as well and which he'd underlined. The still's whisky, the good one, from the oldest cask. Ginger beer and Fletcher's soda water and the tonic for the ones who wouldn't drink. Fruit syrups. Teas. And at the bottom, tasting set, labelled, small, and ask Ilenne, and ask, and then a word she couldn't read because his hand was over it.

"Who's it all for?" she said.

"Whoever we meet."

"You don't know who you'll meet."

"No. That's why there's so much of it." He put the skimmer down. "If you take one drink, you're saying you know what people like. If you take a dozen, you're asking." He looked at the copper. "Ilenne says her mother'll want the soy sauce and won't touch anything else. Armand says the old races brewed and we'll find out what with. Ferra says we'll get it wrong and should bring a lot so we can get it wrong in a lot of ways."

"Ferra's right."

"Ferra's always right. It's very tiring."

The gift chests were Ferra's too, with Saban's notes in the margins. Maren saw them on the Thursday in the counting house's back room, twelve of them in plain oiled oak with brass corners, open on the floor with Ferra on her knees among them. Glass, the good glass, Orrin's, in straw. Soap in paper. Lamps. A gramophone with six records in a box. Bolts of the Kaldo cloth, the allowed kind. Iron and steel tools, the plain ones, chisels and saws and a surveyor's level in a case. Seed, in jars, labelled. Salt. A microscope in a walnut box, which Maren didn't understand and which Ferra handled as if it were a baby.

"Who's that for?"

"Somebody who's kept yeast in a jar for a thousand years and will want to see what's in it," said Ferra, and closed the box very gently.

And the collection kit, which wasn't for giving but for bringing back: a crate of empty jars with screw lids and waxed labels, a press for leaves, seed papers, a ledger with columns for where a thing had come from and what ground it grew in and in what season and what the people who grew it did with it, and a note on top in Hollis's bad hand that said NOT ONE PAIR. AT LEAST TWO. FROM DIFFERENT PLACES. OR IT'S IN-BRED BY THE SECOND YEAR AND YOU'VE WASTED THE BOAT.

Hollis was sending one of his seed hands to go with it. A lad called Tobin Marsh, nineteen, who'd never been further than Wayford and had to be told twice that the ships flew.

She caught Ferra hiding the cask on the Friday.

It was half past five in the morning and still black outside, and Maren had come down to the long shed early to see the last of the casks stowed for hull four's stores before the yard's men came on, because she didn't trust the yard's men with a bung. And there in the lamplight at the far end of the shed, between the flour and the kraut, was the king's purser, the chancellor of the realm, the woman who held the kingdom's books in her head, on her knees beside one of Maren's own beer casks with a pot of paint and a brush, painting out the brewhouse mark and painting in, very neatly, the word VINEGAR.

Ferra looked up. She didn't stop painting.

"Good morning," she said.

"Good morning," said Maren.

Ferra finished the R. She put the brush in the pot. She sat back on her heels and looked at the cask.

"For the night something goes wrong," she said. "There'll be one. Out there. The night we lose a relay, or a man, or something comes up out of the water that nobody's got a card for. And everybody'll be at the bottom of the hold with their faces, and somebody'll have to go and find something." She tapped the cask. "Nobody steals vinegar."

"I'd steal vinegar."

"You'd know what was in it." Ferra got up and wiped her hands on a rag. "You'll not tell him."

Maren looked at the cask for a long time. Then she took the pencil from behind her ear and her own list off its board, and went down the list to Beer, hull four, twelve casks, and crossed out the twelve and wrote eleven, and then, at the bottom of the page, under the potatoes, in her square hand, Vinegar, one, hull four, under the flour. Not for cooking.

"There," she said. "Now it's on the list. Now it's not stolen. Now it's stores."

Ferra read it over her shoulder, and something in her face went very slightly soft, the way it hardly ever did for anyone but the two people it always did for.

"Thank you," she said.

"I'll not be there to see you drink it," said Maren. "Somebody ought to know where it is."

Ferra didn't answer. She didn't need to. She'd seen Imani's dots too.

Tuesday came. The twenty-seventh had been the last one in January, and she'd gone up with the rest of them at six on the Hod, and sat at the foot with the scrubber shut and Imani's clock on her and her hands folded in her lap, and looked at the cloud deck going off below them like a field of snow under the morning, and thought about potatoes.

She bled at fifty-one minutes.

It was the best she'd ever done. Imani wrote it down with a dot. It was still a dot. The line on Imani's card, the one that let you sail, was no bleeding at all, the scrubber shut, the whole watch, and nobody had ever been able to argue with it, least of all the people who bled; and Maren had known since the second Tuesday in October that she wasn't going to get to it by April, and probably not by any April, and that knowing a thing and having it said were different, and that Imani was going to have to say it, and that Imani would hate saying it more than Maren would hate hearing it.

So she didn't make Imani say it yet. She sat on the bench at the Band's foot with the gauze in her nose and her head back, and said to nobody, "That's the coffee," and Imani, writing, said, "You didn't have any coffee," and Maren said, "I had it yesterday," and Imani let her have that.

That night, after closing, with the chairs up and the range banked, she took the green ledger down and sat at the end of the long table with it, and her pencil, and opened it.

The marks were on almost every page now. She'd been making them since October. A small cross in the margin beside a dish: this one keeps. A circle: this one'll do at sea if you've the hens. A line: don't. Nineteen years of her own hand and some of her mother's pasted in the front, and every page of it gone over now with a pencil, as if she were getting it ready for somebody.

She was getting it ready for somebody. She'd known that since October too.

She turned to the back, where the empty pages were, and wrote at the top of the first one, Stores, K. Discovery and the rest, and under it, in order, everything she'd checked into the long shed that week. The flour, the beef, the peas, the kraut. The flakes, in their sixty casks. Fennick's rice. The soy sauce. The jars. The tea. The wash sprayers, one to a galley, and show them. The eleven casks of beer and the one of vinegar under the flour, not for cooking. The tasting set. The yeast.

And under that, after a while, in a smaller hand, things that weren't on anybody's list.

The king won't eat when he's working. Put it in front of him and stand there.

Ferra drinks her tea cold because she forgets it. Make it strong so it's still something cold.

Imani eats standing up. Make her sit.

The old man says he doesn't want seconds. He wants seconds.

Zale will eat anything. Don't let him.

Ilenne likes the long flat fish with the comb on its back, with leaves. Ask the island woman about the leaves. She'll know. She knows the fish.

She looked at that line for a long time. Then she put the pencil down, and closed the ledger, and sat at the end of her own long table in the dark with her hand on the cover, the way she'd sat on Monday morning before any of it, and stayed there till the range had gone out.
