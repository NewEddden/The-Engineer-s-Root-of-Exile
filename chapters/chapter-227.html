Chapter 227 - The Intake

They came into Ward's yard at six on the morning of the fifteenth of June, sixty-one of them, in whatever they had that would stand a day of falling down, and stood about by the well in twos and threes as young people stand in a place where they have been told to be and not yet told why.

Emeric watched them from the keep stair, where he could see the whole yard and nobody in it would think to look up. Forty of them were Commencement's: the oldest of the school's fourth and fifth years, fifteen and sixteen, who had chosen Ward's department at Commencement last September in front of their families, and been kept at their books through the war winter, and given a paper in May with the day and the hour on it. They had washed. Several of them had clearly been told by their mothers to stand up straight and were doing it with great concentration. The other twenty-one were the levy men from the winter, who had stood on the stream ground facing Aldane's banners and come home in February and had not, it turned out, wanted to go back to the plough: older, eighteen and twenty and one of them nearer thirty, brown and quiet and broader than the school's children, standing a little apart from them by the gate with their thumbs in their belts. They had not washed, or not specially. They had stood in a line before.

Arthur stood with the levy men. He had been Ward's since the first Commencement, the first child in Kaldo to say knighthood out loud, and he had stood in the levy line on the stream ground all winter with the rest of them; and Ward had put him in this class as well, because the class was a new thing and Ward wanted one in it who knew the yard. He was taller than he had been in December, or stood taller; he had a new coat with all its buttons, and he was talking to a square red-haired man beside him in a low voice and not looking at anybody else. Kit Bastow, the red-haired man was, who had stood on the cart under the oak at Cressing Cross with a rope round his neck beside Arthur, and whose brother Lew had stood on the other side of him. Lew was not there. Lew had gone back to the plough, and said so, and Kit had come anyway.

Somebody came up the stair behind him with two cups.

He knew who before he turned. He had known her step a long time. Ferra sat down on the step above him with her book on her knees and gave him one of the cups without a word, and looked down at the yard over his shoulder.

"You came down at the first bell last night," she said.

"It was the second."

"It was a quarter before the second. I counted." She drank her tea. "The night watch was still on the near gate. You were lucky."

"Was I seen?"

"No. But you were lucky, and I'll not have a rule that only holds when you're lucky." She did not look at him. Her ear, the one nearest him, was pink. "A quarter before is before."

"I'll wait the quarter."

"You'll wait the quarter," said Ferra, and leaned forward, and put her mouth to the back of his neck, under his collar, very briefly, where nobody in the yard could have seen it if they had looked up, which none of them did; and sat back, and opened her book, and was the Chancellor of Kaldo again before he had finished turning round. "Ottoline wants to know whether to pin the sheet up at noon or keep it in the counting room. Ward says pin it. I said I'd ask you."

"Pin it."

"That's what I told her," said Ferra, and wrote it down.

No rite. That was said first, by Ward, standing on the well's step with his hands behind his back, before anything else, as it had been said to every class Ward had ever taken.

"Nobody in this yard will ever lie on a stone floor," said Ward, "with a drain in it, and be filled until they live or die. Not here. Not ever." He looked at them. "If that is what you came for, the gate is behind you."

Nobody moved. Some of the school's children looked puzzled, because they had never in their lives been told there was such a floor; some of the levy men did not look puzzled at all.

"Good," said Ward. "Ottoline."



Ottoline Fesk had a trestle by the armoury door, under an awning, with a box of blank cards and her punch and a pot of ink and three pencils sharpened to needles, and she sat behind it as she sat behind the tabulator in the counting room, very upright, in a grey coat buttoned to the neck, with an expression of a woman who had been asked to count sixty-one children and intended to count them correctly whether they liked it or not.

"Name," she said to the first of them. "Age. Father's trade. Mother's. Street. Number." She wrote. "Your number's on your door. If it isn't, it will be by Friday, and you'll tell me."

There were four things to be done, and Ward had chalked them on a board by the trestle, and the four were done to every one of them, one after another, all morning, at four stations round the yard, with a card going from station to station in each one's hand and Ottoline punching the figure at each.

The run, which was twice round the yard's measured track, timed by Declan with the works' chronometer in his hand and his face showing nothing at all, so that nobody could tell from it whether they had done well.

The lift, which was a bar with iron plates on it that Bram had made for the purpose in the winter, and which went up a plate at a time until it did not.

The standing jump, from a chalk line, feet together, onto a sanded board marked in inches, which Osric ran, and could not run without talking, so that every one of the sixty-one was told something about the jump that they had not asked to know.

And the rail.

The rail was the one they did not know. It was a short length of iron, sleeved, on two posts, with a stone set in its one end under a brass cap and a dial on the cap with a needle; and you put both hands flat on it, and pushed, not with your arms but with whatever was inside you; and the needle moved; and Rylan counted, aloud, slowly, until it stopped moving, because you had nothing more to push with. Then you took your hands off, and sat down on the bench beside it, and most of them sat down rather suddenly.

Most of the town could do it a little. Nine years of stones in every kitchen and lane and every worker's hand had seen to that; Emeric had seen to that, on purpose, and said so to nobody outside the long table. A farmhand who had never touched a stone could push into a rail for a count of thirty or forty before he gave out. A man from the still crew, who had carried charged fire stones in his bare hands every working day for six years, could push for a hundred and more. A knight could push for minutes, and Rylan, who had his own hands on the rail before the first of them and pushed until the needle hung at the top of its dial and stayed there, did not stop until Ward said his name.

"Showing off," said Osric, from the jump.

"Showing them where the top is," said Rylan, and took his hands off. He was not even breathing hard. "Next."



Three of them were golden from the first hour.

Emeric had expected Arthur, and Arthur was. He went round the track as if it were downhill both ways, and took the bar to a plate more than any of the school's boys, and jumped a foot further than anybody before Osric could finish telling him how; and at the rail he put his hands on it and pushed for a count of a hundred and forty, and took them off, and did not sit down, and looked round for whatever was next as he had looked round in the falling lesson nine years ago, before anybody else in the line was up. He had been on the plant floor and the still crew since the oak; he had carried the stones. It showed.

He had not expected the other two. He should have.

The boy came to Ottoline's trestle a little after seven, tall and rawboned and sunburnt, with river mud on his boots because he had come the river way, and gave his name as Bren Tallis, sixteen, his uncle's lighter, the quay lane, number four. Ottoline wrote it. And Ward, at the well, who had been watching the trestle all morning without seeming to, turned his head.

"Tallis," he said. "The lighterman's nephew."

The boy went red to the hair. "Yes, sir."

"You asked me a question. In this yard."

"Yes, sir." The boy did not look away, though he plainly wanted to. "You said yes."

"I did," said Ward, and turned his head back to the well, and that was all; and the boy stood at the trestle a breath longer with his card in his hand and his face burning, and then went to the track and ran it as if something were behind him, which it was.

The girl came at half past eight. She was a carter's daughter from the west side, square and fair and freckled, with her hair in one thick plait to the waist and her sleeves already rolled; Mira Holt, sixteen, her father's carts, the third lane off Foulard Street, number nine, and she said the number with some pride, because it had only gone up on the door in April. And Ward did not turn his head at all. He said her name, at the well, to nobody, as she said it at the trestle, a beat behind her, as if checking it against something.

"Mira."

She heard him. Emeric saw her hear him and saw her decide not to look round, with her chin up, exactly as she had stood once in a line of children with her chin up for the laugh.

She ran the track two breaths behind Arthur and a breath ahead of Bren, and Bren saw it and did not like it. She took the bar to within a plate of the best of the levy men, who did not like that either. She jumped badly, because she had never jumped from a chalk line in her life and nobody had ever thought to teach a carter's daughter, and Osric told her four things about it, and she listened to all four and jumped again, and the second time she jumped further than Bren. And at the rail she put her two square hands on the iron and pushed, and Rylan counted, and she went on pushing; and at a hundred Rylan looked up from the dial and at her face, which was set and white with a line of sweat along the hair, and went on counting, and at a hundred and thirty the needle stopped, and she took her hands off and sat down hard on the bench and put her head between her knees.

"She's carried her father's harness stones since she could walk," said Rylan, to nobody, writing the figure on her card. "Every carter on the west side has. Six to a team." He looked at the card. "Nobody ever told her to stop."

By noon the rest of them knew. Nobody said anything; nobody had to. They had watched the three of them go round the four stations, and they had seen Rylan's face at the rail, and they could add.



Ottoline pinned the sheet up at noon.

She had asked whether she should, and Ward had said yes, and Emeric, who had been asked nothing, had agreed with Ward in silence; because they were Kaldo's children and every one of them could read, and a figure that was kept from a man who could read it was a figure he would imagine worse. So at noon, on the board by the armoury door, there were sixty-one names in Ottoline's small hard capitals, and four figures after each, and the four figures were in order from the best to the worst, column by column, as the tabulator had sorted them in the counting room in a quarter of an hour, with a clatter that Ferra said she could hear through two floors.

They came and read it. All of them. In a crowd, and then one at a time, as the crowd thinned.

Most of them were ordinary. That was the first thing the sheet said and the plainest. Most of them sat in the middle of every column, a few seconds this way or that on the track and a plate more or less on the bar and a count of forty or sixty at the rail, as any town sits in the middle of everything; and they read their names in the middle and looked relieved, or disappointed, or nothing, and went to get their bread.

Some of them were poor. Four or five were at the bottom of two columns, and one was at the bottom of all four.

He was a thin boy, the son of a man at the tan yard in the lower ward, with arms like a lath and a big quiet head that looked too heavy for his neck; Noll Finch, fifteen, the youngest of the Commencement's forty and the smallest. He had run the track last, by a long way, and come in with his face grey. He had lifted the bar with no plates on it and not with the first. He had jumped a little less than his own height. And at the rail he had put his hands on the iron and pushed for a count of nine, and the needle had barely moved, and Rylan had said "Nine," in exactly the voice he had said "A hundred and forty," and written it down.

Noll Finch came to the sheet last of all, when the yard was nearly empty and everybody else was at the bread, and stood in front of it with his hands in his pockets, and read it.

Emeric watched him from the stair. He watched the boy find his own name, which was not hard, since it was at the bottom; and read the four figures after it; and then go back to the top of the column and read down it, slowly, every name and every figure, all sixty-one, as a boy reads a list he is hoping will turn out to have been wrongly added; and arrive at the bottom again, at his own name, where it had been the first time.

He read it again. All of it.

Then he went and got his bread, and sat with it at the end of the bench by the rail, by himself, and did not eat it.

A Commencement boy, broad and loud and in the middle of every column, went by with three others and said something to the others, not to Noll, about the bottom of the sheet, and the others laughed; and Mira Holt, who was sitting on the well's step with her bread and her plait over her shoulder, said, without getting up or looking round, "You were forty-first on the track. I'd not laugh at anybody from forty-first," and the boy went red and the others laughed at him instead, which was worse.

Noll Finch did not look up. He did not seem to have heard. He sat at the end of the bench with his bread in his two hands and looked at the rail.

Imani came and sat beside him.

She had been at the yard all morning with her bag, under the awning by the trestle, because sixty-one people falling down a great deal on cobbles was a thing she would not leave to Osric; she had strapped two ankles and a wrist and told a levy man with a cough to go home and come back when he could breathe, and he had argued, and gone. Now she sat down on the end of the bench by the rail, beside Noll Finch, not too close, with her bag at her feet and her own bread in her hand, and ate some of it, and looked at him sideways.

"Hold your hand out," she said.

He looked at her, startled. Then, because she was the healer and the healer was not a person anybody in Kaldo argued with, he held out his hand.

She took it by the wrist and turned it over and looked at it: the nails, the palm, the wrist bones, which stood out like knuckles. She put two fingers on the inside of the wrist and counted, with her eyes on the yard. Then she let go of it and looked at his face, at the skin under his eyes and at the corners of his mouth, quite openly, as she looked at a child brought to the dispensary.

"What did you have for breakfast?"

The boy said nothing.

"Yesterday, then. Supper."

"Bread," said Noll Finch, after a while. "There's six of us. My father's on the half shift at the tan yard since the winter. It's not..." He stopped. "There's bread."

Imani nodded as if he had told her the weather. She broke what was left of her own bread in two and put the larger half in his hand on top of the bread he was not eating, and stood up, and picked up her bag.

"Eat both," she said. "Now. While I watch."

He ate both. She watched.

Then she went up the yard to Ward at the well, and said something to him that Emeric could not hear, short, with one hand making a shape in the air like a bowl; and Ward listened, and looked across the yard at the boy on the bench, and nodded once. And that evening, when the class went in to supper, there was a line at the bottom of the plan's week in Ward's square hand that had not been there in the morning, which said that on yard days the class ate at the keep, at noon, from Maren's kitchen, at the Holder's charge, every one of them, and that nobody would be asked why.

"He's not weak," Imani said to Emeric afterwards, on the stair, when he asked. "He's hungry. They look the same on a sheet." She went on up. "Ask me again in a year."



Armand came down into the yard in the early afternoon, for the rail.

He had not been asked. He came anyway, as he came to everything in Kaldo that had to do with a stone and a figure, slowly, down the keep's outer stair with his stick in one hand and the rail in the other, a step at a time, and across the yard to the bench by the rail station, where Rylan stood up for him; and he sat down there with his stick between his knees and watched the last of the levy men put their hands on the iron, and said nothing.

The year's readings were in his box already. He had taken them in the last week of April, as he always did, three readings on three days, every person in the four baronies who would let him and the matched men who never touched a stone, five years of them now, in his small columns. He had told nobody what this year's said. He had not been asked; it was understood at the table that the sheets were his, and that he would say what they said when he had decided what they said, and not before.

Emeric came down off the stair and sat on the bench beside him.

"Interesting?" he said.

Armand watched a levy man push for a count of seventy and sit down.

"Ninety years ago," he said, "when I was an apprentice in the tower, they told me a commoner could not push into a stone at all. That the faculty was shut in them, as it was shut in knights, and nothing could open it. I wrote it down. I taught it, for fifty years." He watched the next man put his hands on the rail. "That girl pushed for a hundred and thirty. Her father's harness stones."

"Ninety years ago?"

"Seventy-odd. I exaggerate when I'm out of breath." Armand's mouth moved. "Which I am. Those stairs were built by a man who hated old men."

He was out of breath. Emeric could hear it, a small catch at the top of each one, as there was at the top of the stairs most days now and had been, if he was honest, for a year; and he saw Armand's right hand go inside his coat, to the front, high, over the breastbone, and stay there, flat, as a man holds a hand over a pocket with something valuable in it. It was a thing Armand did. Emeric had seen him do it on the keep stair in the first year, and in the second, and at the long table more times than he could count. And he had felt, every time he let his sense out near the old man, the thing the hand was over: the place in the middle of that great slow bright note where it stuttered, and caught, and went on. He had felt it the first night Armand taught him to go out at all, in the old man's own room, and drawn back from it; and Armand had said, from a long way off, don't look at the middle, everybody looks at the middle. He had not looked since. He had not asked. Six years, and he had never once asked.

Armand saw him see it. He took the hand out of his coat at once, quite naturally, and pointed with it at the rail, where a broad levy man of about thirty was pushing with his whole face screwed up and the needle hardly moving.

"That one's trying with his arms," he said. "They all try with their arms, at first. As if it were a plough. Tell him."

Rylan told him. The man stopped trying with his arms, and the needle moved.

Emeric sat on the bench beside his old teacher in the June sun and watched the rest of the levy men push, and did not ask, because Armand had taken the hand away so naturally, and because he had never once asked Armand anything about himself and did not know how to begin; and after a while Armand's breathing settled, and he began to argue with Osric about the jump.



At four Ward taught them to fall.

He did it as he had done it nine years ago in the same yard on the same cobbles, to a line of children that had included all three of the ones now standing at the front of this one; and he did it, Emeric saw, with very nearly the same words, in the same order, as if he had written them down then and been reading them ever since. Go down without a hand out. Roll the shoulder under. Be up before the other man has his feet back.

"Arthur," said Ward.

Arthur went down. He did it without thinking, as a man sits down in his own chair: the shoulder under, the roll, the feet, up, in one movement, faster than the eye wanted to follow, and the school's children made a small sound. Kit Bastow said something to the levy men about showing off. Arthur went red again.

"Again," said Ward. "Slowly."

Arthur went down slowly, so that they could see it, which was harder, and which he did worse, and which was why Ward had asked.

Then they fell. All sixty-one of them, in four lines across the yard, for an hour, on cobbles, in the June heat, with Ward and Rylan and Osric and Declan going up and down the lines putting their hands on shoulders and backs and the backs of heads, and Imani under the awning with her strapping; and they fell in heaps, nearly all of them, as Arthur had in that first lesson, and got up, and fell again.

Bren Tallis fell well. He was a river boy and had been falling into boats all his life, and by the fifth fall he was up before anybody in his line, and looked along the line to see who had seen it, and saw that Mira Holt in the next line had been up before him, and stopped looking.

Mira fell badly and got up angry. She fell badly four times and got up angrier each time, and on the fifth she did something with her shoulder that she had seen Bren do, and rolled, and came up on her feet, and stood there for a breath with her plait over her face and an expression of the deepest astonishment; and then she pushed the plait back and looked at Bren, and Bren looked at the cobbles.

Noll Finch fell like a dropped coat, every time, all hour. He did not once come up first. He did not once roll the shoulder under. But he did not once stay down, either; Emeric watched him, from the stair, every fall, and every time the boy went down in a heap of elbows on the cobbles he was moving again before the heap had finished arriving, getting his knees under him, getting up. Slowly. Badly. Up.

At the end of the hour Ward walked down the fourth line, where Noll was, and stopped in front of him.

The boy stood very still, with his elbows bleeding, waiting.

"You do not stay down," said Ward.

"No, sir."

"Good," said Ward, and walked on, and did not look back; and Noll Finch stood in the fourth line with his elbows bleeding and his mouth open, as if he had been handed something and did not know where to put it.



Rylan spoke to them at the end of the day.

Not Ward, which surprised them, and not Emeric, which surprised Emeric a little, until he understood that it had been decided between Ward and Rylan in the winter without him and was right. Ward stood at the well with his hands behind his back. Rylan stood on the well's step beside him, a plain man of middle height in his shirtsleeves with the pale raised lines of the tracery going up the insides of both his forearms to the elbow, ring over ring, where every one of the sixty-one could see them, and one more line up the side of his neck into his hair; and he looked down at the yard full of them, sitting on the cobbles in the long evening light with their cards in their hands, and did not say anything for a while.

"I was picked off a list," he said.

He said it in the voice he used for the price of fish.

"By a captain who'd never asked me anything. Eleven of us left out of a company, and he put four names forward, and mine was one, and nobody tested me and nobody asked me. I was proud for a week." He looked at his forearms. "You know what I got. Some of you have seen the rest of it. I'll not tell it today." He looked up. "What I'll tell you is that nobody picked you. You picked this. And nobody in this yard is going to be put out of it because of a figure on that board."

Several of them looked at the board. Noll Finch, at the back, by the rail, did not.

"Some of you are near the top of that sheet," said Rylan. "Good. You'll find out what the top costs. Most of you are in the middle, which is where most of everybody is, and that's no shame; the middle's where a company stands. And some of you are at the bottom." He did not look at Noll. He did not look at anybody. "There's a book. Ward and I made it this winter. Ward wrote it and I told him what to put. It's a plan. Day by day, week by week, for two years. It's written so it'll take the most ordinary body in this yard and make it fit to stand in a line next to the best one. Not as good. Fit to stand next to." He let it sit. "It works if you do it. All of it. Every day it says. It doesn't work if you do half."

He stepped down off the well.

"That's it," he said. "There's supper. The Chancellor's paying, so eat a lot."



The supper was in the long room, because nowhere else would hold them, and it was too loud from the first breath.

Maren had sent up three hams and a mountain of bread and the summer's first peas in butter, and a cask of the small beer the works drank, and the long table had been pushed against the wall and the trestles from the school brought up and set in three rows, and sixty-one young people who had fallen over a great deal and been counted all day sat down at them and became, within a quarter of an hour, a single enormous noise. The school's children sat with the school's children and the levy men with the levy men, for the first half hour; and then Kit Bastow, who had a voice like a dropped bucket, stood up on a bench with his beer and began to sing a song about a miller's wife that the levy had sung on the stream ground all winter, and the levy men joined in, and then some of the school's boys, who should not have known the words and did, and Osric, who could not sing at all and knew it and sang louder than anybody to make up for it, and the whole long room shook with it, and Maren came to the door and looked at it with her hands on her hips and went away again.

Emeric sat at the end by the door, out of the way, with Ferra beside him with her book shut on the table, which was as near as Ferra came to joining in. She had come up from the counting room for the bread. She stayed for the song, and her mouth moved at the second verse, and he looked at her, and she said, "I know the words. Everybody knows the words. It doesn't mean anything," and turned a page in the shut book with great dignity.

A Commencement girl, two places down from Arthur, leaned along the bench in the middle of the third verse and asked him about the oak.

Emeric saw it from the end of the room. He saw Arthur go red, all at once, from the collar up, as he had not gone red at the run or the bar or the rail or at anything else all day; and saw the girl's friends lean in to listen, and two of the school's boys stop eating; and saw Arthur look at his plate.

"I stood on a cart," he said.

"In a rope," said the girl. "My brother said. In a rope, all night, in the frost, and you never cried out."

"I didn't have the breath to cry out." Arthur moved a pea round his plate with his knife. "It was a very tight rope." He looked at Kit Bastow, singing on his bench. "Ask him. He was on the cart too. He talked the whole night. He told the man with the whip what he thought of his mother."

"Did he?"

"He told him in some detail," said Arthur, and the girl laughed, and her friends laughed, and the boys went back to eating, and Arthur's colour went down a little; and he went on moving the pea round his plate, and did not look up for a while, and Emeric, at the end of the room, found that he did not want to look at him either, and looked at Ferra's shut book instead.



Ward read them the week at nine, when the hams were gone.

He did not stand on anything. He stood at the end of the long room by the cold hearth with a sheet of paper in his hand that he did not look at, and waited until the room was quiet, which took less time than anybody would have expected, and read it to them flat, in order, as he read a roll.

"Yard," he said. "Monday, Wednesday, Friday. Six to noon. The plan's work. Falling. The bar. The track. The sword, when I say. Plant floor and the stone crews, Tuesday and Thursday. You carry what the stone handlers carry. You handle what they handle. When there is a Band crew, there will be places on it, and they will go first to those who were promised them." He did not explain that. "Books, Saturday morning. Reading, figures, the plan's book. Sunday is yours." He turned the paper over, still without looking at it. "What is expected. You are in the yard at six. You do what the plan says on the day it says it. You tell the truth in the book. If you are hurt you say so to the healer, not to your friend." He looked at them. "What sends you home. Drink on a yard day. A blow at a man who is down. A lie in the book. Going anywhere I have said nobody goes alone, alone."

Nobody said anything.

"That is the week," said Ward, and folded the paper, and went out.

They sat in the long room in a kind of silence after him, the sixty-one of them, with the empty ham bones and the beer; and then Kit Bastow said, quite loudly, "Well, that's me sent home by Wednesday," and the whole room laughed, the levy men loudest, and the noise came back up, and it was a supper again.



Emeric went down into the yard at ten, by the outer stair, on his way to the shed, and found that he was not the last one in it.

There was a lamp by the armoury door. Somebody had left it burning on its hook over Ottoline's board, where the sheet still hung; and under it, on the cobbles, in the circle of its light, with his back to the yard, Noll Finch was standing with his hands in his pockets, reading the sheet.

Emeric stopped at the foot of the stair, in the dark, where the boy would not see him.

He did not go over. He knew what he would say if he did, and it was true, and it would be no use at all: that a sheet taken on the first day of a two-year plan says where a body starts and nothing else; that nine at the rail was nine more than the boy's grandfather could have pushed; that the plan was Rylan's and Ward's and worked. It would come from the duke, at ten at night, to a tanner's son alone in a yard. The boy would hear the duke and not the words.

He was still deciding when Rylan came out of the long room door, with a hambone in one hand and his coat over his shoulder, on his way home to Nessa; and saw the lamp, and the boy, and stopped.

Rylan looked at the boy for a while. Then he came across the cobbles, not quickly, eating the last of the ham off the bone, and stood beside Noll in front of the sheet, and read it with him. Both of them, side by side, in the lamplight, with their hands in their pockets, saying nothing.

"You know where I'd have been on this?" said Rylan, after a long time. "At your age?"

The boy shook his head.

"Nowhere. They'd not have let me in the yard." Rylan threw the hambone over the wall into the moat. "I was a river boy. Couldn't read my own name. I'd not have known which line was mine." He looked at the sheet. "You read it four times. I watched you. Every name."

The boy said nothing.

"That's a thing, you know," said Rylan. "Reading it four times. Most of them read their own line and went for the ham." He put his hand on the boy's shoulder, briefly, and took it away again. "The plan's got a day for the track and a day for the bar and a day for the rail, and it hasn't got a day for reading every line on a sheet four times, because I didn't think of it. You'll be no use to me at the rail for a year. But I'll want you at the book on Saturdays." He picked up his coat. "Go home. It's late. Your father'll think you've joined the army."

The boy went. He went across the yard toward the gate, slowly at first, and then not slowly; and at the gate he looked back, once, at the lamp and the sheet and Rylan under it, and then he was gone into the lane.

Rylan stood under the lamp a while longer. Then he reached up and turned it out, and the sheet went dark, and he went home.

Emeric stood at the foot of the stair in the dark for a while after he had gone, with his hand on the cold stone of the rail, and found that he was grinning like a fool, and that there was nobody in the yard to see it; and went on down to the shed.
