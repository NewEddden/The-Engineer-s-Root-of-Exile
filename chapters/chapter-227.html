Chapter 227 - The Far Panel

It went at the second hour after midnight on the night of the summer quarter day, and it went on the far side, where nothing had ever touched it.

He was asleep. He had been asleep for perhaps an hour, which was more than most nights that summer, and what woke him was not the sound, though there was a sound. It was the sense. It was the band going out of the dome all at once, a whole round room of it, fifty feet across and twenty-five high, the way a held breath goes out of a man who has been hit; and he was on his feet in the dark with his hand on the wall before he knew what he had felt or where.

Then the sound came up to him through his open window from the round room below. Glass. Not a crash. A long falling slither, and a single flat crack at the bottom of it, like a slate going off a roof onto a flagstone.

He did not stop for a lamp. He went down the gallery in his shirt with his hand on the rail and the sense out ahead of him, and it told him before he got to the pass-box what his eyes told him when he did. The dome was open. On the west side, low, a hand above the beds, where the glass came down to the steel ring, there was a hole in it the size of a door, a triangle of night sky where a triangle of Orrin's glass had been, and the band was pouring out of it. Out and up, into the dark over the palace, into the open air over the city, a great slow invisible fountain of it, thinning as it went, spreading, the whole of the dome's charge going out through one small hole into the night like smoke up a chimney.

There was nothing he could do about it. It was gone before he reached the gauge. He stood at the pass-box door and watched the stone behind the gauge's window go down the brass, past Ferra's red line, past half, past a quarter, to the bottom, and stop, and he felt the round room become an ordinary room: thin, ground, cold, with the night air coming in through a hole in the glass and moving Imani's willow leaves.

And in the middle of the room, in the dark, the seedling was going out.

He could see it with his eyes. The screens were drawn; there was no light in the round room but a little starlight through the hole. And the seedling had been glowing, as it glowed every night now, faintly, along every vein, and it was going dark. Not all at once. The light went out of the tips of the leaves first, and then out of the smaller veins, and then out of the pale middle vein of each leaf, and down the stem, like a lamp being turned down slowly by a hand. And as it went, the leaves moved.

They were folding. Every leaf on the plant, the good ones and the three burned ones at the top, was folding itself up along its middle vein, slowly, the two halves of each leaf coming together like hands closing, and drooping on its stalk against the stem. The way a plant in his old world had folded its leaves at dusk, or when a finger touched it. He had seen that in a glasshouse once, as a boy, in another life. He had never thought he would stand in the dark in a shirt and watch a thing do it because it was dying.

He did not wake anybody for the first quarter hour. There was nobody to wake who could do anything that he could not.

He did the first thing himself. He went to the rack on the gallery where the cartridges stood in their sleeves, six of them, full, filled in the band two nights ago, and took one, and went to the collar, and then stopped with the cartridge in his hand, because there was a hole in the dome the size of a door and a cartridge put in now would go straight out of it.

So he went into the dome.

It was only air. There was nothing in there any more to walk into; it was an ordinary room with a hole in its roof. He went through the pass-box with both doors open, which nobody had ever done, and across the beds to the west side, and looked at the hole.

It was not the cracked pane. That was the first thing he saw, and it went through him like cold water. The cracked pane, the one above the collar with its fine bright line across it from the first fill, was on the east side, and it was still there, whole, holding its crack. The panel that had gone was on the west. The far side. Opposite the collar. As far from the collar as it was possible to be in that dome, where no fist of charge had ever hit and no cartridge had ever been opened and Bram's cup had never been needed.

The triangle lay on the floor among the comfrey in one piece, cracked across once where it had hit the flags. He picked it up. It was one of Orrin's, ground true on its edges, and it was whole, except for one corner. At one corner, where it had sat in the steel node, in the node's socket, the glass had gone. Not broken off. Crumbled. A little fan of fine cracks ran in from that corner across the pane like the veins of a leaf, and at the corner itself, where the glass had sat against the iron, there was a small chip, old, its edges dull and weathered, that had been there since the day it was set. A flaw from the grinding, or from the setting. A bruise, where a man had tapped it into its seat a little too hard in the spring.

He stood among Imani's beds in the dark with the pane in his hands and understood it.

Nobody had thrown anything at it. Nothing had ever had to. It had only sat there, in its socket, at the edge of the dome, with the band leaning on it from inside, day and night, never stopping, never letting go, for six weeks. And every day the sun came up over the city and fell on it, and the glass warmed and the iron node round it warmed faster, and grew, a hair, and pressed on it. And every night the sun went and the iron cooled and shrank, a hair, and let go. Every day. Every night. And the bruise at its corner, that would have lasted a hundred years in a window, had been worked at by that, a hair at a time, for forty days and forty nights, with the band leaning on it the whole time, until tonight it had had enough.

The baffle had done nothing. The baffle had never been the problem. He had called it solved on the second fill with the whole table round him saying solved, and it had been solved, and it had been the wrong thing to solve. The fist at the collar was a thing that happened once, loudly, where everybody could see it. This was the other thing. The steady push. The thing that never stopped, and made no sound, and found whatever there was to find.

He put the pane down on the flags very carefully, as if it could still be hurt, and went to wake Bram.

Tessaly Rook woke at the same moment, two streets away, in her room at the back of the lower school, and did not know why.

She lay in the dark with her eyes open. The room was quiet. The school was quiet; the six boarders from the far baronies asleep in the front room, and the girl who warmed stones with her hands asleep in the end bed with her arm over her face as she always was. There was nothing to hear. The window was open onto the yard and the night was warm and still.

She had felt something.

It was gone now. It had been gone before she was awake enough to know it was there, the way a sound that wakes you is always already over. But she lay there and put her hand, without thinking about it, flat on the wall beside her bed, the way she put her hand on a wall she was raising to feel whether it would hold, and there was nothing in the wall. There was nothing in the room. And yet a moment ago there had been, not in the room, somewhere; somewhere near; a great slow push, like the air before a storm, like the moment in the tower's assessment hall when the eighth stone had been put into her hand at nineteen and she had felt it and been unable to hold it. Something very full, very near, had opened.

She got up and went to the window.

The yard. The wall of the yard. Over the wall, the roofs of the second ring, and over the roofs, very near, two streets off, the palace on its rise, black against the stars, and on the palace the drum, and on the drum the dome, a dark low bowl on the top of it with the canvas drawn inside, as it was every night. She looked at it for a long time. There was nothing to see. There was never anything to see; the prince's physician had a winter garden up there, the whole city knew it, the yard men had told everybody. Feverfew. Willow.

There was a light on the palace's east range that had not been there when she went to bed. And then another, on the gallery that ran round the drum. Somebody was up, at the second hour, in the palace, with lamps.

She stood at the window until her feet were cold. Then she went back to bed and lay down and did not sleep.

In the morning she crossed the bridge to the tower for the day's teaching, as she did every morning, and went up the keep stair to Armand's hall, and he was at his bench under the windows with a sheet of readings, and looked up and said good morning, and asked after the children.

She told him the children were well.

She did not tell him about the night. She had meant to. She had meant to all the way across the flat and over the bridge; she had had the words ready. And then she was standing in his hall, in front of his bench, with the light on his white hair and his readings in front of him, and she thought of the Drop House's stack going to a thread of smoke in the autumn before last, with the lights on over the dock, and of how she had not told him that either, and of why. She had not told him because she did not know what it was, and a thing she did not know was not a thing she could stand in front of Armand with, who knew everything, and say. And she had not told him because some part of her had wanted, for once, to keep a thing that was hers until she did know.

That had been the first thing. This was the second. She noticed that she was counting them, and did not like it, and went to her class.

Emeric saw the window from the gallery, a minute after he came back from the dome with the pane.

He had come up the gallery to go to Bram's lodging, and he stopped at the gallery's west end, where it looked across the court to the palace's west range. The guest rooms were in the west range. There were four of them, on the first floor, with windows onto the court, and on a quarter day they held whoever had come for it and stayed the night: tonight, for the first time, the House's factor and her clerk.

One window was lit. The second from the end.

It had not been lit a minute ago. He had come along the gallery toward the pass-box in the dark three minutes before, when the glass fell, and every window in the west range had been dark; he had not thought about it but he would have seen a light. It was lit now. Not the clerk's; the clerk had the end room. Hers. A lamp stone, turned up, and a shadow crossing it once.

Nobody could have told her. There was nobody awake in the palace but himself. He had not rung a bell or called out or woken a servant. The only sound had been the glass, and the glass had fallen inside the round room, inside the drum, inside canvas, fifty yards from her window through stone. A woman asleep in the west range would not have heard it.

A woman asleep in the west range who could feel a stone would have felt the rest of it.

He stood at the end of the gallery with the night air on his face and looked at her window for as long as it took to know that. He thought of a lamp stone on a table in a small room at the end of the long room, in the rain, a year ago, guttering; and two fingers laid on its brass cup without looking; and the light coming up full and even and staying there, while she finished her sentence.

He had not asked her then. He had not used her name since that day, and he had not asked, and he did not ask now. He did not go across the court and knock. He did not send anybody. He stood and looked at the window, and after a minute the light in it went down again, turned low, and then out, as if whoever was behind it had decided that there was nothing to be done and nothing to be said and had gone back to bed.

Asking would have been the end of it. A woman of House Ducatis who was a mage, and who nobody knew was a mage, lodged in the palace of Kaldo on a quarter day, in a year when the Circle was counting every mage that went south and the Church was reading the Circle's counts. It was one more line in a file somewhere, if anybody ever wrote it down. He was not going to be the one who wrote it down.

He went to wake Bram.

Bram came in his nightshirt and his boots, and looked at the hole, and at the pane on the floor, and at the corner of it, for a long time, by lamp.

"Iron grows in the sun," he said. "Glass less." He turned the pane over. "Every day it's pushed on her corner and every night it's let go. And the whole time the thing inside's been leaning on her." He put his thumb on the old bruise. "She was set hard. Somebody tapped her in. I'd have tapped her in myself." He looked at the dome, at the other three hundred triangles in their sockets, catching his lamp. "They were all set the same way."

"I know."

"Then it's any of them. Any night." Bram put the pane down. "And it's all of it at once, when it goes. Not a crack. The whole pane out of the socket and the whole of it gone out of the hole."

"I know."

Bram looked at the seedling in the middle of the room, folded, dark, drooping on its stem.

"Is it dead?"

"Not yet."

They closed the hole with a sheet of iron. Bram cut it in the fitting shop at the dock, four miles off, and sent a boy on a horse, and it came back in an hour, and Emeric and Bram bolted it into the empty socket from inside with their hands, a black triangle among the clear ones, with pitch round its edges. Then they went out through the pass-box and shut both doors, and Emeric put a cartridge in the collar.

And another. And a third. He watched the gauge come up the brass to Ferra's red line, and stop there, and not a grain over.

Then he sat on the gallery floor with his back against the drum's stone and his sense in the dome, and waited.

It took until the first grey in the east. It took so long he thought, twice, that it would not come. He sat and felt the band stand in the round room, even and still at the line, round a plant that did not drink it. Folded. Dark. A shape in the medium like a stone again. He did not move. He had sent Hobb running across the flat and over the bridge to the island at the third hour with a note to Imani, four lines, in his own hand: A pane went at the second hour, the far side. The whole dome went out through it into the open air over the palace. It is closed and filled again. The same hour, as promised. E. He had not had an answer and did not expect one before morning. He sat and waited for the plant.

At the first grey, the lowest leaf moved.

He felt it before he saw it. The thread. Thin, very thin, the medium going in through the stem again, the way it had gone in on the twelfth morning after the planting, hesitant, like a man who has been very ill putting his hand out for a cup. And then he saw it, when the light came up enough to see: the lowest leaf on the stem, the first of the long narrow ones above the two grey hands, unfolding. Slowly. Its two halves coming apart along the middle vein, the way a hand opens after a long time clenched. And then the next.

He did not get up. He sat on the gallery floor with his back to the stone and watched his mother's tree open its leaves one at a time in the dawn, and did not sleep, and did not try to.

The wired glass was Bram's, and it was ready by the evening, and it was a stopgap, and he said so.

"It's not the answer," he said, at the long room, with a pane of it in front of him on the table. "It's what we do until there is one."

It looked like any of Orrin's triangles, clear and ground, except that there was a net in it. A fine square mesh of iron wire, a finger's width to a square, lying inside the glass, right through the middle of its thickness, from edge to edge. Orrin had rolled it in that afternoon, with Bram standing over him: the glass poured out on the casting table in a sheet, still soft, and the mesh laid on it, and the roller going over it and pressing the mesh down into the glass until the glass closed over it, and then cut and ground like any other triangle while it was still warm enough to cut.

"It'll still crack," Bram said. "Glass cracks. That's what glass does. But when it cracks, it can't fall. The iron holds it. It'll sit in its socket in pieces with a net through it, holding, and the thing inside will get out through the cracks a little, slowly, not all at once. And somebody will see it in the morning, a pane full of cracks with a net in it, and change it." He put his hand flat on the pane. "Iron was the first thing that ever held the medium. In the prince's first flask, in the iron room. It holds this. The dome can't go all at once any more. Not through one of these."

"All of them?" said Ferra.

"Every one. Three hundred and some. Orrin says a fortnight if he does nothing else." Bram looked at the pane. "And they're heavier, and the net shows, and you'll see it from the street in the daytime, a dome with a net on it. And it's not strong. It's no stronger than what's there now. It just can't fall out." He took his hand off it. "It's a stopgap."

Emeric looked at the pane on the table, the clear glass with the fine iron net lying in it.

"Do it," he said. "All of them. And we'll find the answer."

Ferra wrote it down. Then she wrote, under it, in the same hand, The far pane: an old chip at the node, worked by sun and night. The baffle did nothing. The baffle was never the problem. And she did not ask whether he wanted that line in the book, and he did not ask her to take it out.

Imani came in at the end, from the island, with his note in her hand. She had read it at dawn, when Hobb reached her. She put it on the table in front of him, open, so that his own four lines lay face up between them, and looked at him for a while, and then sat down in her chair without saying anything.

It was, he understood, the most she had ever said to him in thanks.
