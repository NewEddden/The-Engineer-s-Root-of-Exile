Chapter 148 - What Cannot Be Given Back

Eleven had come up out of the cellar under the Staple. Seven of them were alive at the first frost.

Imani had the four in her register in the order they went, which was the only order she trusted. The first had died in the Staple on the second night after the hanging, in the room off the yard where she had put them all, with the shutters open because the smell of the cellar was still in the house and she would not have them breathe it. The second had died on the fourth morning, a little before she had meant to move them. She had moved the rest that night, in the ship, on stretchers lashed along the hold, because five days of a cart on the Wayford road would have killed more of them than it carried, and she had sat on a crate between the stretchers the whole way with her hand on one wrist or another and Osric at the levers, and had not looked out once. The third and fourth had died in Kaldo in the first ten days, a day apart, of the same nothing.

She had not been able to write a cause for any of them. She had written spent, which was the word she had used in the cellar and which meant nothing, and she had crossed it out each time and written not known, which at least was true.

Then it had stopped. There had been seven for eleven days, and then fourteen, and then a month, and she had begun, carefully, the way she let a man put weight on a bad leg, to count on seven.

They had the end room of the long ward, the one with the south windows, because it was the warmest and because it had its own door. Seven beds, a stove, a table where she kept their sheets. Nobody else was put in there, and the ward staff went in two at a time and not alone, which was her rule and which nobody had asked her to explain.

Hew was in the bed by the door, because he liked to see who came. Colley beside him. Jem across, with his face to the wall most of the day. Perrin at the window, because he was nineteen and wanted the light, and because he was the only one of them who ever stood at it. Wat in the corner, with his hand on the bed rail. Hild, who had told Imani no in the cellar and no again on the steps of the counting house, in the bed next to Wat's. Senna at the far end, by the stove, who did not know what year it was and was not always sure where she was, and who asked, most mornings, whether the lamp would be put out soon, and was told each time, gently, that there was no lamp, and that it was morning.

They were not old. Imani made herself say that, in her head, every time she came in, because her eyes told her otherwise and her eyes had been wrong about bodies before. The oldest of the seven was thirty-four by the book. Perrin was nineteen. And they lay in their beds with white hair and thin skin gone loose over the bones of the hand, and the slow careful movements of the very old, and Senna's gums had drawn back from her teeth the way they did at eighty, and Hew had lost four teeth in the Staple and two more in the ward, and when Perrin stood at the window in the mornings he held the sill with both hands.

She knew what an old body looked like. She had sat with enough of them. What she had never seen was an old body that had been young a year ago, and it was not the same. The years had not been lived in these. They had been taken out, all at once or near it, and the body had been left to make what sense of it that it could.

Armand came every morning at the second bell.

He came up the long street on his stick, slowly, in the grey robe he had worn in the capital and never stopped wearing, and he came up the ward stair more slowly, and he came into the end room and sat down on the stool by the first bed and put his stick against the wall, and he did not speak until he had got his breath. Imani had offered to have the stool carried downstairs and the seven brought to him. He had looked at her as though she had proposed he be carried up the stair on a board, and she had not offered again.

He had his instruments in a flat case: the glass, the three standards in their felt, the little brass rule he used for things she did not ask about. He measured each of them first, every morning, before anything, the old way first, with his fingers on the inside of the wrist and his eyes shut, and then with the glass, and wrote it down in his own small hand in his own book. Then he took a stone out of the crate.

The crate stood by the stove now, not in the store. Imani had moved it the day Armand began, because the stones had to be to hand and she was not going to have a boy running to the store for each one. They sat in their straw, fifty-three of them, the ordinary cut, a little bigger than a lamp stone. She could not feel anything from them. Armand said they were full. Emeric had said, standing in her store with his face gone flat, that it was theirs, and she had said it back to him, and she had not thought until afterward what exactly she had agreed to.

It was theirs. It had been taken out of these seven, and the four in the ground, and the nineteen before them, in the chairs in the cellar with the cradle stones under their hands, and it was in the straw by her stove, humming to men who could hear it. And Armand was putting it back.

He did it the same way every time. He put the stone in the patient's hand and closed their fingers round it, and put his own hand over theirs, round the outside, so that the stone was held between them, and he shut his eyes again. And then nothing happened that Imani could see, for the length of a long hour.

"It is not difficult," he had told her the first morning, when she asked what he was doing. "That is the difficulty. The stone would give it all at once, if it were let. It wants to. Everything wants to go from full to empty. So I stand in the way. I let a very little through, and a very little more, and I do not let it go faster however much it pushes, and I do not let my attention off it for a moment, because the moment I do, it goes at the rate it wants, and that rate is the one they have a name for in the capital." He had looked at the hand under his. "It is the same total. I only keep the door narrow."

"And if it went at the rate it wants?"

"Then it would be the rite," Armand said. "And I have seen that done."

He did an hour with each of them who would have him. That was six hours, and he did it every day, sitting on a stool with his eyes shut and an old hand over a young one that looked older than his own. Hild would not have him. She would not touch a stone. She would not have one in the room if she could help it, and the first morning, when he took one out of the straw, she had begun to scream, a thin high sound with no breath behind it, and had not stopped until Imani had put herself between the bed and the crate and said her name over and over. After that Armand went to Hild's bed last, and only to measure, and never with a stone in his hand, and she let him hold her wrist and watched the door the whole time.

Senna could not say yes. Imani had asked her, every morning for the first week, carefully, the same question in the same words, and Senna had smiled at her and asked whether the lamp would be put out soon. Imani did not count that as yes. So Senna had no stone either. Armand measured her and went on.

That left five.

It stopped the decline.

Imani did not believe it the first fortnight, because it was the kind of thing she had learned not to believe in the first fortnight. Men got better for a fortnight all the time. Men got better because somebody was sitting with them every day, or because the food was better, or because the stove was lit, and then they stopped getting better and went on as before.

So she measured. She did not have Armand's glass and would not have known what it told her if she had. She had what she had always had. She weighed them, every third day, on the old grain beam that the factor had left in the store downstairs when he went bankrupt, which Bram had cleaned and re-hung and put a board across, because a man lying on a board on a grain beam could be weighed as honestly as a sack. She counted the pulse against the ward's sand glass. She had them walk, the ones who could, from the bed to the door and back, and counted how many times they stopped. She looked at their gums and their nails and the colour of the inside of the eyelid. She wrote it all on each one's sheet, in a column, every third day.

For the first ten days in Kaldo, every column went down. That was when the third and fourth had died.

Then it did not go down. It did not go up either. Colley weighed what he had weighed. Hew stopped losing teeth. Perrin walked to the door and back and stopped twice, and the next time twice, and the time after that twice. Jem turned over one morning and faced the room, and asked what day it was, not the way Senna asked but the way a man asks who wants to know. Imani told him, and he said, "Right," and lay there with his face to the room after that.

It did not give anything back. That was the other thing the sheets said, and she read it every third day and did not flinch from it. The hair was white and stayed white. The skin stayed loose. Hew's teeth did not come back and Senna's gums did not come down and Perrin held the sill with both hands. Whatever had been taken out of them had been taken out of the years they had left, and it was gone, and nothing Armand held in his hand under theirs was going to put a single one of those years back.

Perrin asked her. He asked on a morning in the fourth week, at the window, with his back to the room, and she knew he had been waiting until the others were asleep or pretending to be.

"Will I look like me again?"

She did not answer at once. She did not know what he had looked like. She had met him in a cellar.

"No," she said.

He did not turn round.

"The stone is stopping it getting worse," she said. "That's all it's doing. It's not undoing it. I don't know anything that undoes it. I don't think anybody does."

"Will I get old? After? Like other people?" He was holding the sill. "Or is this it. Is this what I look like when I'm forty."

"I don't know," Imani said. "I'll tell you when I do."

"You always say that."

"Because it's always true," she said. "Would you rather I said something else?"

He thought about it, at the window, for long enough that she thought he was not going to answer.

"No," he said. "The other lot said something else."

Emeric came on a morning at the end of the fourth week, while Armand was with Colley.

She heard him on the stair, because he did not walk like anybody else in Kaldo. He walked like a man who had been taught to walk in a corridor where somebody might be watching, and had never unlearned it, and it made no sound on the treads, and the absence of the sound was what she heard. He came to the door of the end room and stopped in it.

He did not come in at once. He stood in the door and looked down the room, bed by bed, the seven of them, the way she had seen him look at a page when he was reading it for the thing that was wrong with it. His hands were in his coat. His face did the thing it did now, since Wayford, which was nothing at all.

Then he came in, and went to Perrin at the window, and spoke to him for a little, too low for her to hear, and Perrin said something back and very nearly smiled. He went to Hew by the door and stood by his bed and let Hew tell him who had come up the stair that morning and who had gone down, because Hew liked to tell people that. He did not go near Hild. He did not go near Senna. He stood by Colley's bed for a while and watched Armand's hand over Colley's, and Armand did not open his eyes, and Emeric did not ask him to.

Then he came to her table, where she was writing up the sheets, and asked her two questions. He asked whether she had enough staff for the end room at night, and she said she had. He asked whether there was anything she wanted from the fund, and she said there was not, and he said that if there was she was to ask Ferra and not wait for him, and she said she knew that.

And then he went. He had been in the room, by the ward's glass, a little less than a quarter of an hour.

She watched him go down the stair. He did not look at the crate by the stove, coming in or going out. He had not looked at it once. She would have noticed if he had, because she had been watching for it.

Armand opened his eyes when the stair had gone quiet, and took his hand off Colley's, and looked at her across the room.

"He will come less," Armand said. "Not more."

"I know."

"It is not that he does not care."

"I know what it is," Imani said, and bent to her sheets again, and after a while Armand shut his eyes and put his hand back.

She did know. Or she knew a part of it. She knew a man had come to her in the second week of autumn and told her he had felt something in his teeth. She had written it at the back of her register, in his words, and not written under it what she thought, and she did not write it now either. But she thought it. She thought that whatever Ward had felt in his teeth in that cellar had come out of the man who had just stood in her door with his hands in his coat, and that he knew it had, and that he could not stand in a room with the seven of them for longer than a quarter of an hour because of what he had found out about himself in the room they had been kept in.

She did not know that. She was guessing. She wrote nothing.

It was Senna who told her. Not in words. Senna did not have the words to tell anybody anything.

Imani was writing up the sixth week's columns on a wet evening with the stove going and the lamp on the table, and she came to Senna's sheet, and stopped.

Senna's column had stopped going down.

It had stopped when the others had. Imani went back up it with her finger, every third day, the weight and the pulse and the walk that Senna did on Imani's arm, and the gums, and it had gone down for ten days and then stopped, on the same day near enough as Colley's and Hew's and Perrin's. And Senna had never had a stone. Not once. Armand had measured her every morning and gone on to the next bed.

Imani went down the room to Hild's sheet. Hild, who screamed at the sight of a stone. Hild's column had stopped too. A day later than the others. Stopped.

She sat back.

She thought about it the way she had been taught to think, by her mother, who had not had a single book to teach from, which was that when a thing happened to two people who had nothing in common, the cause was the thing they did have in common, and it was usually something so ordinary nobody had thought to look at it. Her mother had found a well that way, once, in the plague year before the one that killed her. Four houses in four streets, and nothing in common but that the women in all four fetched from the same pump on their way back from the same market.

Senna and Hild had a room in common, and a stove, and a crate by the stove, and the ward's food, and the ward's staff, and the ward's water.

The ward's water.

Imani had boiled every drop that went into the ward since the first year of the wells, when Emeric had stood in the side aisle of the church among her beds and told her what was in the water. It was the first rule of the ward and it had never been broken. The copper stood in the store downstairs, and was filled from the tank every morning and boiled, and drawn off to cool into the covered cans, and the cans went up to the ward. And the copper stood in the store beside the place where she had put the crate of stones, the day Emeric carried it up, at the back, behind the lime. For a week before Armand began. And then the crate had come upstairs, to the end room, and stood by the stove, and the end room's cans stood on the stove's shelf to keep warm for the night, a foot from the straw.

She got up and went downstairs with the lamp and looked at the copper. She looked at the place behind the lime where the crate had stood. It was an arm's length from the copper's side. She went back up and looked at the cans on the stove shelf. A foot.

She did not sleep much that night.

Armand came at the second bell, and she was waiting for him at the top of the stair with a can of the night's water in one hand and a can drawn fresh from the tank in the other, unboiled, and before he had his breath back she held them both out.

"Tell me if there's any difference between these," she said.

He looked at her. Then he looked at the cans. He did not ask what she meant, which was the first thing she noticed. He took out the glass, and one of his standards, and he held the glass over the first can for a long time, and over the second for a long time, and then over the first again.

"Yes," he said.

"The first has it in."

"A very little. Yes."

"From the stones."

"From being boiled beside them," Armand said. He said it slowly, the way he said a thing he had known for some time and not expected to be asked about. "Water that is heated near a charged stone takes some up. Not much. It gives it back as it cools, but not all of it, not quickly, and what it keeps, it keeps for a day or so." He looked at the can. "I had not thought about the copper."

"You knew that. About water."

"I knew it of another liquid," Armand said, and said nothing else about it, and she saw that he was not going to, and let it go, and wrote in her head that there was a thing Armand knew about liquids that he would not tell her, and that Emeric almost certainly knew it too.

"Senna's never had a stone," she said. "Hild's never had a stone. They've stopped. Same as the others. Near enough the same day." She put the cans down on the top step. "They drink the water. That's all they have that the others have. They drink it every day, a cup when they wake and a cup at night and whatever they want between, and it's been sitting a foot from the crate since the day you started, and in the store beside the crate for a week before that."

Armand leaned on his stick and looked at her for a long moment.

"Then half of what I have been doing for six weeks," he said, "the water has been doing for me."

"More than half, maybe. I don't know. I can't tell them apart now. They've all had both, except those two."

"No," said Armand. "You cannot. And nor can I." He was quiet for a while. "Which is a thing I would have lectured a student for, once, for a week."

Imani was not listening. She was thinking about the rest of the ward.

The copper did not boil only for the end room. It boiled for all of it. Every can that had gone up that stair for seven weeks had stood an arm's length from a crate of full stones. The ferryman with the broken leg on the other side of the partition had drunk it. The Tarrow girl with the fever had drunk it, for nine days, and gone home. The ward staff drank it, because it was there and it was clean. She drank it. She had drunk it every day, from the can on her own table, while she wrote up the sheets, and not known.

"Is it doing anything to them?" she said. "The others. The ones who weren't in the cellar."

Armand thought about that honestly, which she was grateful for.

"I would not expect it to do much," he said. "For a body that is not short, a little more of what it already has in it. I have measured nobody else in this ward, so I cannot tell you. I can measure the staff tomorrow. I can measure you." He paused. "I would not expect harm. That is not the same as knowing."

"No," said Imani. "It isn't."

She went into the end room, and sat down at her table, and opened her register at the day's page, and wrote, under the date, in the same hand she used for everything:

The ward's boiled water has carried charge from the Staple stones since the crate came in. Found by the columns of the two who had no stone. Every patient in the ward and every one of the staff has drunk it for seven weeks. None of them was asked. I did not know.

She looked at it. Then she wrote, under it:

This is my fault and I have written it here so that it is on the page.

And then she got up and went down the room to Senna, who was awake, and asked her, the way she asked every morning, whether she knew what year it was.

Wat died in the eighth week, in the night, between the third bell and the fourth.

Nothing had changed. That was what Imani went back over, afterward, at her table, with his sheet in front of her. His column had stopped going down when the others had, in the second week, and it had not moved since. His weight was his weight. His pulse was slow and even. He walked to the door and back with his hand on the rails of the other beds and stopped once, every time. He took Armand's hour every morning without a word, and the water, and ate what he was given, and slept, and in the day he lay with his hand on the rail of his bed, the way he had stood with his hand on the back of the bench in the square. He had not said one word to anyone since the cellar. Not to Imani, not to Armand, not to the others. Perrin had tried. Wat had looked at him, not unkindly, and turned his face to the ceiling.

The night staff had looked in at the third bell and he had been asleep, breathing. They had looked in at the fourth and he had not been.

She found nothing. She went over him in the grey light before Armand came, carefully, all of him, the way she would have for any man who had died in her ward without telling her why. No wound. No fever. Nothing in the chest that she could hear. Nothing in the belly that she could feel. Nothing on him at all but what the cellar had put there, which had been on him the day she met him and had not got worse. His face was quiet. His hand was on the rail.

She wrote not known on his sheet, and under it she did not cross anything out, because there was nothing to cross out.

Armand came, and stood by the bed a long time, and then sat on the stool and took Wat's wrist the old way, with his eyes shut, as though he were measuring him.

"There is nothing there," he said, after a while. "Nothing at all. Not low. Nothing." He opened his eyes. "Which is what there always is, after. I do not know why I looked."

"His columns didn't move," Imani said. "Not for six weeks. He didn't get worse. He just stopped."

"Yes," said Armand. "The others will too. All of them. Sooner than they should." He did not say it cruelly. He said it the way he had told Emeric, years ago, in a workroom, what the rite did. "I can stop them spending. I cannot give them back what was spent. And a body that has been left with this little of its own to live on does not have far to go when the ordinary things start asking for it. A winter. A cold. Nothing at all." He looked at Wat's hand. "He had nothing at all."

Imani said nothing. She had known it, the way she had known the columns would not go up. She had not said it to herself in words, and now somebody had, and it was worse.

Doran came in the afternoon, for the book.

He came up the stair slowly, which was new. He was a man past sixty now and had begun to walk like it, even to Imani's eye, which was used to the old, and he stood in the door of the end room and made the sign over the bed in the corner, and then came to her table with his book under his arm, the great black parish book that had been in the church since before Imani was born, and sat down, and opened it at the page, and uncapped his ink.

He wrote it the way he always wrote it. She had watched him do it a hundred times. The name first. Then the day. Then the place: the ward, he wrote, and then, because Kaldo was particular about its book since the prince had plotted every line in it on a map, the long street. Then the cause, and she gave him not known, and he wrote that without a pause, because he had written it before.

"Parish?"

"Wayford. Lord Hollin's tenant. On the Milford side."

He wrote it.

"Age?"

"Thirty-one," Imani said.

Doran's pen stopped.

It stopped a hair above the page, with the ink on the nib, and stayed there. He did not look up at her. He looked, instead, past her, down the room, at the bed in the corner, at what lay on it with the sheet drawn up to the chin and the white hair on the pillow and the face that had fallen in on itself around the gums. He looked at it for a long time. He had buried a great many people. He had buried the old, and he knew what old looked like, better than anybody in the barony, because he had seen more of them at the end than anybody but Imani. And what lay in the corner looked older than any man he had ever put in the ground.

"Thirty-one," he said.

"By the Staple's own book," said Imani. "His parish has him too. I can send to Wayford for the page if you want it."

Doran was quiet.

"I don't want it," he said. Then, carefully, as though he had to find the words one at a time on a floor that was not level: "I have written a great many ages in this book that I did not believe. Women say they are younger. Men say they are older, when there is land in it. I write what I am told. That is what the book is. It is not what I think. It is what I am told, by the people who know." He looked down at the page. "I have never been told a thing I believed less, and had less reason to doubt."

He wrote it. Thirty-one. In the same hand as the rest, the same ink, no mark beside it.

He blotted it, and closed the book, and sat with his hand flat on the cover for a moment.

"There will be six more," he said. It was not quite a question.

"Yes."

"Then I will write six more." He stood up, and tucked the book under his arm, and made the sign again toward the corner. At the door he stopped. "Imani. The ones who did this. They're hanged."

"Five of them."

"Five of them," Doran said, and nodded, and went down the stair, slowly, with the book.

They buried Wat in the churchyard on the lake side the next morning, in the row where Kaldo put the people who had no family in Kaldo, and it rained. Armand came, on his stick. Perrin came, which Imani had not expected and had not tried to stop, on Declan's arm, in a borrowed coat, and stood at the edge of the grave with both hands on Declan's forearm the way he held the sill, and did not say anything, because there was nothing Wat would have wanted him to say. Emeric did not come. Ward came, and stood at the back, and afterward walked down with Imani to the gate without speaking, and at the gate looked at her once and went away up the hill to the keep.

On the way back up the long street, Armand told her about the study.

He told her the way he told her anything, at his own pace, with his breath going on the slope, stopping every so often to lean on his stick and look at the lake as though he had come out to look at the lake.

"I took the readings in the first days of autumn," he said. "The fifth year's. You were still in Wayford. I took them myself, the way I have taken them every autumn, the whole barony by crew. And last week I sat down with all five years at once, which I had not let myself do before, because I had said I would not look at the answer until the time was up. And I looked."

Imani waited.

"He is right," Armand said. "Emeric. About the rate. The men on the etching floor have moved. Every one who has worked there more than two years. Not far, most of them. But in the same direction, all of them, and further every year, and nobody else in the barony has moved at all. The foundry men who handle finished stock have moved. The crew of the ship have moved further than any of them." He looked at the lake. "Declan has moved further than I would have believed. And not one of them has a mark on him. I have looked. I have looked at every one of them with my own eyes, stripped to the waist, the way I was taught to look at boys in the capital. Clean. All of them. It was the same total, or it is going that way, and it came at a rate the body could take, and so there is no scar." He was quiet a moment. "The tracery is an injury. It was always an injury. I have the proof of it in a box with my own lock on it, and it is sound, and I checked it three times because I did not want it to be."

"You can't publish it."

"No." He did not ask how she knew. "I told him that yesterday. I think he knew it before I said it. It needs years, and it needs a great deal of stone, and a thing that needs years and a great deal of stone will be done by the men who have both, and it will not reach one boy in one chamber in the capital. And the Church." He moved his hand, a small movement, as though brushing something off a table. "But that is not the reason. That is not why I will not."

Imani waited again. She had learned that about him. If you waited, he came to it.

"Five men in Wayford bought the rite," Armand said, "and worked out for themselves that it could be run the other way. Out of a man, into a stone. They did not understand a word of what they were doing. They had a merchant's book and a lime pit. And they got it working, and it paid." He started up the street again, slowly. "What is in my box is the other half. How fast. How slowly. How much a body can give, and at what rate, before it stops. It is exactly what they needed and did not have. If I publish it, I am not publishing a finding that could have saved the boys in the chamber. I am publishing a manual for the next cellar."

They walked a little way in silence.

"I assessed boys for forty years," he said. "My hand on the wrist, once, and a number, and the number was what they were. I told them what they had been born with, and they believed me, because I was the grey robe and I had been told it too. And I stood in a gallery twice and thought it was solemn." He stopped again, and leaned on the stick, and did not look at the lake this time. He looked at his own hand on the stick's head. "I could not stop it then. I did not know. Now I know, and I have the proof of it in a box, and I still cannot stop it, and I have seven people in your end room who show me every morning what the knowing is worth in the wrong hands. That is what I have got for being right." He took a breath. "Six. I have six."

The hand on the stick's head was shaking.

Imani had seen it before. She had seen it for months, if she was honest, in the mornings when he came up the stair, a fine fast shiver in the fingers that stopped when he gripped something and started again when he let go. She had not written it down. It was not her business unless he made it her business, and he was an old man, and old men's hands shook. But it was worse today than she had seen it. Six hours a day of holding a door narrow with his eyes shut, for eight weeks.

She did not say anything about it. She took his arm, which he permitted, on the slope, and they went up the rest of the long street together, slowly, to the ward.

That night she sat at her table in the end room with the lamp and the register and the six of them asleep, or pretending, and the crate by the stove, and the cans on the stove's shelf a foot from the straw.

She had been thinking about it all day, under everything else. Under the rain and the grave and Doran's pen stopping and Armand's hand on his stick. She had been thinking about Senna, who could not say yes, and Hild, who screamed at the sight of a stone, and how both of them had been given the thing they refused, or could not agree to, in a cup, every morning, by Imani, without knowing. And how it had worked. As well as Armand's hour. Maybe better. Without anybody holding a door narrow. Without anybody's hand shaking at the end of the day.

A little. Every day. In a thing a person did anyway.

That was the way. She had known it since the morning with the cans, and she had not wanted to know it, because of what she had written under it in her register. But it was the way. Not a rite. Not an hour with an old man's hand over yours and your eyes on the crate. Not a thing done to you, that you had to sit still for and be brave about. A cup of water, boiled beside a stone. The body taking what it needed, at the rate it could, out of a thing it was going to drink in any case.

It was the gentlest thing she had ever seen work.

And it was also, she thought, exactly what she had done to the ferryman and the Tarrow girl and her own staff and herself, without asking.

Both of those were true. She had learned a long time ago that the true things did not arrange themselves into one answer for her convenience.

She turned the register to a clean page at the back, after Ward's line, after her own line about the water, and dipped her pen.

She had never seen a dose of this written down. She did not think anybody had. There were doses for willow bark and for poppy and for the foxglove that her mother had been so careful with, written in her mother's hand in the old book downstairs, worked out a generation at a time by women who had watched what happened. There had never been a dose for this, because nobody had known it was a thing that could be dosed. Men had it or they did not. The rite put it in at a rate that killed. The Staple took it out at a rate that killed. And in between there had never been anything at all, no number, no line, no cup.

She wrote the date. Then she wrote, slowly, in her plainest hand:

Water, boiled within an arm's length of one full stone of the common cut. Drawn and let cool. One cup on waking, one cup at dark. Every day. Not stronger. Not in place of food.

She looked at it. Then, because it was her register and she wrote what she knew and what she did not know in the same ink, she wrote under it:

Safe for the six of the Staple over eight weeks, as far as I can see. Safe for the ward and the staff over seven weeks, as far as I can see, and they were not asked. I have not seen it given to a child on purpose. I do not know what it does over a year. I do not know what it does over ten.

And then, under that, after a long time:

It does not give anything back.

She blotted it. She sat with the register open under the lamp and read it over, the first dose of its kind that anybody had ever put on a page, in her own hand, in her own book, with its three lines of not knowing under it like a fence.

Down the room, Senna stirred, and said, into the dark, in her small clear voice, "Is the lamp going out soon?"

"Soon," said Imani. "Go to sleep. It's night."
