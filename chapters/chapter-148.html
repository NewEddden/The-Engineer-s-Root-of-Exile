Chapter 148 - A Man's Height

She had not looked like a ship since October.

Fletcher had opened her up the week after the steamboat came home. He took her flat deck off plank by plank and stacked it in the shed in numbered piles, and cut away the inside of her down to the frames, and then built her again from the inside out, the way Emeric had drawn her on the long table in the summer: a hull inside a hull. The inner one was a rounded shell of laminated oak, thin layers glued and pinned crosswise, curved everywhere, without one flat wall in it, because a curve carried a load the way a barrel's hoops did and a flat wall bent. That was the part that would be sealed. The rooms were in it, and the stair, and both helms. The outer hull went round it with a hand's width of air between the two, and in that gap ran everything Fletcher was not supposed to ask about: the ducts, the speaking tubes, the corded dials, and the row of glass-lined cases under the deck line that he had held a lamp over in April and called ballast.

All winter the shed under the north wall had rung with it. Mallets and adzes. The glue pot that never cooled. Bram's boys riveting the light iron lattice that the outer planking hung on. In March Haddon's black tar came off her, all of it, scraped and burned and scraped again, and the new planking went on in its place, oak, oiled until it was the colour of a chestnut, with brass straps along every seam. In April the dome went up over her stern on brass ribs, and the copper panels went onto the ribs, darkened on purpose with a wash of Bram's until they were a deep red-brown and would never go green. On top of the dome, where a mast would have stood if she had needed a mast, sat a little house of glass under a bronze canopy: the top cockpit. Ivo had made every pane in it, and every pane in the bulb of glass at her bow below the cutwater, small ones, thick, set in a grid of bronze like the leads of a church window. Small, he had explained to Fletcher, at length, because a small pane took cold and weight better than a big one, and when one cracked you lost one.

Fletcher had listened to all of it. He had built the grids.

The irises had come down the capital road from Wayford in a crate of straw, from Ottoline Fesk's shop under the clock, four a side, each a ring of thin brass leaves that slid over one another on a cam and closed like the pupil of an eye. Ottoline had sent a letter with them. It was three pages long and was about tolerances. Fletcher had read it twice and fitted them, and the first time he turned the ring on the forward port iris and watched it open he had said, to nobody, that he had been building boats for forty years and had never once had a boat look back at him.

By the first week of May she stood in the shed on her three legs, under the lamps, a hundred feet of brown oak and brass and dark copper, with the glass bulb of her bow cockpit catching the light and the glass house on her dome catching more of it, and the long tilting arm of her propeller folded up behind her stern like a heron's leg. Fletcher walked round her every morning before he did anything else. He did not say anything. He did not have to.



Somebody had to look at her feet.

The legs and the foils had been in and out of the river all winter on her trials, and they would be in and out of it more, and every time they went in, something could foul them, or crack, or work loose, and the only way to look was to haul a hundred feet of ship out of the water, which needed a dry dock, which Kaldo did not have. So Kaldo got a diver instead.

The diving dress took Abloh three weeks and Bram two. Abloh made the suit: canvas, two layers, with a layer of the coast's rubber gum between them, cut to cover a man from his neck to his feet with gloves sewn on and the seams taped inside and out. Bram made the helmet: copper, spun on the lathe in two halves and brazed, as round as a bell jar, with three small windows of Ivo's thickest glass in brass rims, front and both sides, and a collar of brass at its base that bolted down onto a brass corselet on the man's shoulders, with the suit's neck clamped between them. A hose came off the back of the helmet, Abloh's canvas again, wound with wire so it could not kink or crush. At the top end of the hose, on the slip, was a pump: two brass cylinders on a frame, with a long handle across them like a fire engine's, that two men worked up and down to send air down the hose into the helmet. A valve on the helmet let the used air out in bubbles.

A standard diving dress, Emeric called it, because that was its name, and Abloh asked what it was standard to, and Emeric said it would be, in time.

What the dress did not have was lead. On Earth a diver went down in boots soled with lead, and a weight on his chest and another on his back, forty or fifty pounds of it, so that he would sink at all; and to come up, he had to be hauled. This one had two gravity stones in a flat iron case between the shoulders, under the corselet, the same stones as Tess's chair, worked the same way, by a lever on the right hip with the same notches. Heavy took him down. Lighten brought him up. And because that was the rule, beside the slip on the morning of the trial there was also a coil of good rope with a bowline already tied in it, and a crate of lead weights with straps, and a dock-quarter man called Penn who had dived for dropped anchors all his life with a stone in his arms and a breath in his chest, and who had been told that the stones were the first way and the lead was the second, and that he was going to learn the second until he could do it asleep.

Rylan went down first.

He had asked. He had come out of Emeric's water book in the winter swimming better than anybody in the garrison, and he had asked in his way, which was to arrive at the slip the morning the dress was finished and start taking his boots off. Emeric did not argue with him. Rylan had come up out of the hold of Garrin's boat beside him, more than a year ago now; and since then there had been a certain kind of thing that Rylan asked to do first, and Emeric let him.

They dressed him on the slip. It took four of them. The suit went on like a sack, and the corselet over it, and then Bram lifted the helmet down over his head, and Rylan's face looked out of the front window, round, solemn, enormously magnified, like a fish's, and Tess, in her chair at the top of the slip, laughed so hard she had to put her drafting board down.

Rylan rapped on the glass with one canvas finger. Then he walked down the slip into the river.

He walked until the water was at his chest, and stopped, and they saw his gloved hand go to the lever on his hip. Heavy, first notch. He went down. Not fast; steadily, the way a man goes down a stair, the water closing over the copper dome of the helmet, and then there was only the hose going down into the brown water from the pump, and the two men at the pump handle working it up and down, and a slow rising string of bubbles breaking on the surface a little further out each time, where he was walking along the river bottom toward her bow.

Emeric stood at the edge of the slip and opened his sense.

He could not see Rylan. Nobody could. But he could feel him: a warm steady note down there in the cold of the river, a knight's note, dense, and the two small sharp notes of the gravity stones in their case on his back, and the long dull spread of the ship's three legs going down into the water ahead of him. He watched the warm note reach the forward leg and stop, and move round it, slowly, and go down it to the foil, and stay there a while. Then on to the after pair. He felt it go round the port quarter leg twice. Then a third time.

The bubbles stopped moving.

The men at the pump kept pumping. Bram, at the hose, had his hand on it. Nobody said anything for a quarter of a minute. Then the bubbles came back toward the slip, and the note came with them, and at the foot of the slip the copper dome came up out of the water, streaming, and then the rest of him, walking up out of the river on Lighten with the weed hanging off his shoulders and his gloved hand held up in front of the window.

There was something on the glove. Shining. A smear.

They got the helmet off him. He stood on the slip with water running out of the neck of the suit, grinning, and held the glove out to Emeric.

"Port quarter," said Rylan. "The ram. Where it goes into the leg. It's weeping." He turned the glove so the smear caught the light. "Your soap stuff. The glycerine."

Fletcher, who had come down the slip behind them, took the glove and smelled it, and looked at the ship's port quarter leg going down into the water, and said a word Emeric had heard only from Declan. Then he went to get his tools.

It was a seal. The leather packing round the ram had dried and shrunk over the winter in the shed and let the glycerine out, a drop at a time, too slow to see from the deck. Up in the cold, Emeric thought, watching Fletcher go, the ram might have held, or it might not, and a leg that would not come up was a leg that would be hanging out of her when she came down on water at speed. He did not say so. Rylan knew. He was grinning at the river.

Penn went down that afternoon in the same dress, with the stones' lever wired off at the case and the lead strapped on him and the rope tied round his chest. He came up on the rope, hauled by four men, coughing, swearing, and delighted. He went down again the next day and the day after. By the end of the week he could go down on lead and come up on lead and do his work on the bottom between without anybody holding the rope but one boy, and Rylan had gone back to being a knight, and was sorry about it.



Maren was sworn on the ninth, in the long room, with Ward listening by the door and Ferra writing it down.

She had been going where Emeric went since the steamboat's first run to Grayrock. She had cooked on the boat, and at the lodging at court, and over a brazier in the snow at the cursed shaft, and she had never once asked what anything was for. She stood at the end of the long table now in her apron with her arms folded, and heard all of it: what the ship in the shed was, and what was under her deck line, and what she would do, and what nobody outside that room was ever to know she could do. Ward watched her face the whole time. Ferra wrote.

Maren listened to the end. Then she unfolded her arms.

"Does an oven work up there?"

Emeric had been ready for a good many questions. He had not been ready for that one. Ferra's pen stopped.

"It's a fire stone," he said. "Under a plate. It works anywhere."

"Bread rises different, high up." Maren looked at him with deep suspicion. "My mother said so. In the hill country. She said bread came out flat at the top of the pass and her mother never believed her."

She was right. He had not thought of it. Water boiled cooler up high, and dough rose faster and fell flatter. He had the page for it somewhere in his head, in a cookery book from the other life with a section in the back for mountain kitchens, and he went and got it, that night, in the delve, and wrote it out for her in the morning with the rest: a sheaf for the galley. A bread that took the height. A stew that kept three days. A pudding steamed on the back of the range. Porridge. Twenty things, scaled for the whole crew, with notes in the margin for the heights.

She read it at the kitchen table with her lips moving, and then took it down to the shed and stood in the galley with it, which was a space the size of a cart under the dome, with a fire-stone range set in iron, a small oven, a copper sink with a pump, and every pot and pan held down by a bar or a peg or a strap, because Fletcher had asked what happened to a pot when a ship tipped and nobody had liked the answer. She stood in it and looked round it with her arms folded and the sheaf in her hand, and said it would do.

On the last day of the fitting she burned the onions.

The range was hot before she had finished cutting the second one. She had been cooking over coal and wood for twenty years, and in all that time a range had taken a quarter of an hour to come up, and this one came up while she was reaching for the knife. The first onion went black in the pan in the time it took her to turn round. She said a word, loudly, that carried all the way down the shed to where Emeric stood with Fletcher at the bow. It was the same word Fletcher had said at the leg. Emeric had heard it, before that week, only from Declan.

She threw the pan out of the galley hatch into the river and started again. She did not burn anything after that, then or ever.

The bread came out right. The first meal ever eaten at her saloon table was eaten that evening, in the shed, with the lamps lit and her doors shut, by Maren's bread and Maren's three-day stew on its first day: Emeric and Ward and Bram and Tess, and Fletcher at the end of the table, who ate two bowls of it without speaking and then sat looking along the saloon's curved oak wall, at the brass of it, at the little round ports above the table, and at the lamp swinging very slightly from its hook, though the ship was standing on her legs in a shed and could not swing at all. It was the river, Bram said. Coming in under the shed doors.

Fletcher said nothing. He was watching the lamp.



She went into the water on the night of the fourteenth, and up out of it a little after midnight.

No moon. A light wind off the land. They took her down to the reach below the town under oars in the dark, past the north wall and under the east bridge and out between the marsh and the fields, as they had taken her for every trial since the spring before; and anybody on the wall who looked down saw a long low dark shape going by with men at its sweeps, and thought nothing of it, because by now the whole town knew that the prince's black hull went out at night, and the whole town knew that nothing ever came of it.

At the bottom of the reach they shipped the sweeps and let her drift.

There were six of them aboard. Fletcher in the bow cockpit, the glass bulb under the cutwater, with Bram beside him: the water helm, the legs and the forward foil and the reverse stones, because if anything went wrong it would go wrong at the water first. Tess at the stone-keeper's station amidships, inside the seal, lashed to her ringbolts, with the matrix dials in front of her on their cords and her data book open on her drafting board and a pencil behind her ear. Maren in the galley, because she had said she would not be left on a slip while her own range went up into the sky without her. And at the top of the dome, up the sealed stair, in the glass house under the bronze canopy, with the dark river all round below the panes and the stars all round above them: Emeric, at the levers. Ward behind him with a hand on the back of his seat.

The top cockpit was where she would be flown low. That had been settled on the long table. From up there a man could see all of her at once, bow to stern, and all round her, and down past her sides at the water, and back past the propeller at where she had been, and low down that was what mattered: what was near, and what she was about to hit. Higher up, where nothing was near and the cold came through even double glass, the bow helm was deeper in the seal and nearer Tess's dials. But tonight she was going up a man's height, over water, and Emeric wanted to see everything.

He put his hands on the levers.

There were twelve of them on the matrix, in a row, brass, each with its notched quadrant and its little engraved plate in his own hand: bow port, bow starboard, waist, quarter, stern, and the long one in the middle that moved all of them together. Every notch on every lever woke one more stone in its group. Nothing aboard her decided anything on its own. Nothing would ever be automatic. A lever moved a stone, and a hand moved the lever, and that was all.

He drew the long lever back one notch.

Nothing happened that anybody could see. But he felt it. He had his sense open all the way round, wide, the way he held it now without thinking, and through the oak and the brass and the lattice under him he felt the cases along her deck line wake, one in every group, the notes of the stones coming up all round her like a row of candles being lit in a long room, and her weight began to come off the water.

She did not rise. Not yet. She sat lower in the water, and then not as low; the river along her sides changed its sound, a long low sigh, as though she had let out a breath. Second notch. The sigh went on. Third.

And one of the notes was wrong.

He felt it before anything else could. Starboard quarter, the third case from the stern: its stone was pulling, but not as the others pulled. Higher. Thinner. It was taking more than its share and giving it badly, the way a man rowing out of time with the others gives his oar more and the boat less. It would show on the dial in a moment. It had not shown yet.

"Starboard quarter," he said into the speaking tube to the stone-keeper's station. "Third from aft. It's hot."

A pause. Then Tess's voice came up the tube, small and tinny and annoyed: "It's not. It's reading the same as the rest." Another pause. "Oh. Now it is." Her pencil, scratching. "How did you know?"

"Take it out of the group."

She did. There was a switch on her board for every case, a plain brass key in a row of keys, that took a stone off its lever without Emeric having to touch anything up here, and she turned it, and he felt the thin wrong note go quiet, and the ship's weight shift a hair to starboard and settle as the rest of the group took it up. She had asked for those keys in the winter. He had not wanted them. She had said that a stone-keeper who could only watch a dial and shout was a stone-keeper with nothing to do, and that if he was going to make her sit in a box in the middle of a ship he could at least give her a way to stop it killing them, and he had made the keys.

Fourth notch.

She lifted.

It was nothing like the foils. On the foils she had come up all at once, with a roar, standing up on her blades and running. This was slow, and silent, and level. The water let go of her. He felt it let go all along her bottom at once, a long soft sucking release, and then the sound of the river along her sides was gone, and there was only the drip of it running off her, pattering down into the black water below, a long hiss of falling drops all round her like rain on a pond, and then not even that.

A hand's width. A foot. Her shadow, from the stars, on the black water under her, getting wider.

He held the lever on the fourth notch and did not touch the others.

Her legs came out of the river. He felt them go: the three long bronze legs and their flat feet hanging down out of her bottom, clear of the water now, dripping, the weed on the port quarter foil trailing. A man's height. He stopped her there.

She hung.

A hundred feet of oak and brass and copper, standing on nothing, a man's height above the river at the bottom of the reach, in the dark, with the marsh on one side of her and the empty fields on the other, and nothing in the world under her but air. She did not tip. Her lift was in the ring of cases up under her deck line, above her weight, so she hung from it like a bucket from its handle and did not need anybody to balance her. She did not drift. The wind off the land was light and he held her against it on the stern channels, a breath, hardly anything. She did not make a sound. There was no boiler and no fire and no engine. There was nothing to hear but the last drops running off her legs into the river, and Ward breathing behind him, and somewhere below him in the hull, very faintly, through the oak, Maren saying something in the galley that he could not make out, and then, quite clearly, a pot lid rattling in its strap.

He held her there for as long as it took to count a hundred, slowly, out loud, because Tess had asked for the count. Then he let the long lever go forward, one notch at a time, and she came down.

She came down onto the water as softly as she had left it. Her legs went in first, and the river took her feet, and then her bottom, with a long low kiss of water along her length, and she sat on the reach again, rocking, very slightly, as though she had never been anywhere.

Ward took his hand off the back of the seat.

Up the speaking tube from the stone-keeper's station came the scratch of Tess's pencil. Then, from the galley, small and far off, Maren's voice, to nobody: "Well, the soup's still in the pot."



Fletcher came up the sealed stair from the bow afterward, while Bram was opening the hatches and the men were getting the sweeps out. He came up slowly, because he was not young and the stair was steep and he had built it, and he stood in the door of the glass house with his hand on the frame and looked at the levers. Then at Emeric.

He did not say anything for a while. He looked at the twelve brass levers in their row. He looked out through the panes at the dark river, and at the shape of his ship below him, lying on it, long and quiet.

"She has a name?" he said.

"The K Discovery."

Fletcher turned it over. "What's the K?"

"Kaldo."

"Aye." Fletcher nodded slowly. He looked at the levers again, and then out at the legs he had been told were for beaching, standing down in the black water under her quarters with the weed still on the port foil. "I was told," he said, "the legs were for beaching."

"They are."

"Aye. They are." Fletcher was quiet. "And the frames were heavy because you liked them heavy. And the slots were for nothing. And the cases were ballast." He took his hand off the door frame. "What else have I been told?"

Emeric looked at him.

He had been ready for this one since the spring before. He had thought about what he would say, on a good many nights, and he had decided long ago that when Fletcher asked he would not lie to him again, and he had wondered whether Fletcher would ever ask, because Fletcher was a man who could wait.

"Most of it," he said. "Not all of it. Most."

"Will you tell me the rest?"

"Some of it. When I can. Some of it never." Emeric met his eyes. "Some of it isn't mine to tell. Some of it, if I told you, somebody would come for you to make you tell them. I'd rather they didn't."

Fletcher thought about that, standing in the door of the glass house he had built, in the dark, with his ship under him. He was an old man who had built boats for forty years and had sat in the reeds for six with a hull nobody would buy, and had been told by a prince with tar on his hands that somebody had come.

"That's fair," said Fletcher. "That's a fair answer." He turned to go down the stair, and stopped, with one foot on the first step, and looked back. "She went up level," he said. "Didn't she. Dead level. Not a hair of list on her."

"Not a hair."

"Aye," said Fletcher, to himself, and went down.



They brought her back up the river under oars and had her in the shed with the doors shut a little before dawn.

The two of them were on the slip when he came down her ladder. He had not known they would be there. Nobody had told them when she would be back. They had been told she was going out, and that was all, and they had come down to the shed at some hour of the night he did not ask about, Ferra with her book under her arm and Imani with a shawl over her head and her basket, and sat on the slip on an upturned crate side by side, by the look of them, for some while, in the cold, not talking much.

Ferra stood up as he came down the last rungs.

"How high?"

"A man's height."

"That's not a number." She had the book open already, and her pen. "A man's height is anything from five feet to Ward. How high?"

"Six feet. Six feet two. At the keel." He thought. "Eight at the bow, if you count the cutwater. She sat a hair stern-down until Tess took a stone out."

Ferra wrote it. She wrote it with the date, and the hour, and then, after a second's thought, the words a hair stern-down, and blotted it, and shut the book.

Imani had come up on his other side without his noticing. She took his wrist, the way she had taken it a dozen times in the ward, and put her two fingers on the inside of it, and looked at the sky over the north wall where it was going grey, and counted. He let her. He stood on the slip at dawn with the ship behind him in the dark of the shed, and Ferra's book shut under her arm on one side, and Imani's fingers on his pulse on the other, and waited to be told.

"Too fast," said Imani.

"I was quite calm."

"You've told me that before." She let go of his wrist. "You were quite calm the night the boat came home, and the night of the shaft, and the morning they brought you up off the river." She settled her shawl. "Your heart's never once agreed with you. I don't know why you keep saying it."

Ferra, on his other side, made a very small sound through her nose that he would not have heard if he had not been standing next to her. It was a laugh. She did not look at either of them. She looked at the shed, and the long shape of the ship in the dark of it, and after a while she said, to nobody, that she supposed she would have to put a ship in the books now, and that she had no idea what to call the line.
