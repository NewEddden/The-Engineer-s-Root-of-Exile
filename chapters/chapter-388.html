Chapter 388 - Loaded

They loaded the K Discovery on the Wednesday and woke the relays on the Thursday, five miles up, with the ship hung off the Well's lee like a boat tied up to a pier.

The loading took all of Wednesday and most of Bram's temper. There were fifteen of them. Eleven Band relays, BR-02 to BR-12, each in its sealed drum the size of a rain barrel, grey-plated and heated, with the antenna boom folded down its flank like a bird's wing and the whip coiled under it in a felt bag; and four ground relays, squatter and heavier, for the places on the chain that weren't in the sky, which were the South Sentinel depot, two rocks nobody had named yet in the far west, and the Rim at the top of Ilenne's mother's city, if Ilenne's mother said yes. They came down to the coal quay from the Works on flatcars under tarpaulins, and Tess walked beside every flatcar the length of the quay on her stick, with Ambrose on the other side, and if the driver went over a sleeper too hard she said his name, once, and he didn't do it again.

The K Discovery took them into her hold in a cradle of steel racks that Cade's men had built in January and Bram had made them take out and build again in February because the first lot rang. Each drum had its own rack, its own clamps, its own run of cable to the patch board at the hold's forward end, where the set lived. Ambrose spent the Wednesday afternoon on his back under the patch board. Tess spent it on a stool beside him reading the cable numbers aloud. At six Bram came down the hatch, looked at the fifteen drums in their racks in two long rows with the lamps on them, looked at the patch board, looked at Ambrose's boots sticking out from under it, and went back up the hatch without a word, which from Bram was a speech of thanks.

On the Thursday morning she went up.

It was a still morning, cold and clear, with no wind on the ground and the westerlies up top no worse than Orla's radiosondes had said at dawn. The K Discovery went up off the coal quay with her racks full and her necks shut, slowly, heavy with what she carried, and the city went down under her into a coin and then into a smaller coin. Sorrel had the wheel. Emeric was in the hold with his mother.

Amara had come aboard in her kit. She'd put it on in the castle at six, by herself, which took her eleven minutes now and had taken her forty in December. It was the armour he'd built her when the made body was new, the one Ferra still called the doting son's, with more stones on it than anybody needed: close dark plate over the made body's silicone, light, jointed, with the reserve canisters in the kit back between her shoulders and, beside them, the set. The set was a valve set, small and very good, Ambrose's best, with the antenna run up the back plate and over her shoulder under a seam. She heard it in her own ears. There was no speaker. Anything that came in on it, she heard the way she heard a voice in the room.

She was sitting on an upturned crate at the patch board's end when the ship came up into the Band, with her hands on her knees, listening to nothing yet. Emeric felt the Band come up round the hull, the thickening, the weight of it, the way you feel a room fill. His mother felt it too. He saw her head lift.

"Oh," she said. "That's a great deal."

"It's the Band."

"I know it's the Band. I've been up in the Band." She turned her head toward him. Her face in the made body was very like her face in the old one, which was very like his, and the mouth was exactly right, the way the mouth went when its owner was about to tease him. "I've never been up in the Band in this. It's loud. It's like standing in the sea." She lifted one hand and turned it over, looking at the plate on its back. "I can feel it in the canisters. They want to fill."

"Let them."

"I shall, thank you. I'm not new."

The ship hung off the Well's lee at twenty-six thousand feet with the lines out and the Well's long grey flank a hundred yards off the starboard side, close enough that Emeric could see Orla Garran standing at the hangar's edge with her arms folded, watching them the way she watched every hull that came alongside her station, as though it might be about to scrape her paint. Tess and Ambrose went across on the line basket with the test key and the log. The plan was simple and had been on Ferra's sheet since September: the relays would be woken one at a time, here, in the hold, with their stones charged in the Band and their deciders brought up and their sets warmed; and each one, when it was ready, would be made to beat once on Ambrose's key from the Well, out of its wandering time, so that its first heartbeat could be heard by somebody before it went anywhere. And the somebody was Amara.

That had been Emeric's rule, and Ferra had written it in, and nobody had argued. The tech was proven before it was personal. And then, once it was proven, the first thing it did was personal.

Harrold came down the hatch ladder a little after ten.

He hadn't been going to come. He'd said so at breakfast, twice. He'd said he had the Isle's escrow papers to sign, and Keller's man to see about the capital's water, and that he'd been up in the Band once in his life and once was sufficient. Then at a quarter to eight he'd been at the coal quay in his coat with his hands in his pockets, and had got aboard without saying anything, and had sat on the forward deck for two hours with his collar up looking at the sky going dark blue over him. Now he came down the ladder carefully, an old man's way of going down a ladder in a young man's body, and stood at the foot of it in the lamplight looking along the two rows of drums at his wife on her crate.

"I came to watch," he said.

"You came to see if I'd break," said Amara.

"That too."

"Sit down, then. There's another crate." She didn't turn her head. "You'll want to be quiet. I'm working."

Harrold sat down on the other crate, by the hatch, with his elbows on his knees, and was quiet.



BR-02 woke at twenty past ten.

Emeric did the stones himself, because they were his to do: the lift stones at the drum's base that would hold it in the Band without a hull under it, the decider in its glass cup in the heart of the drum, the two little fixed-job stones that kept the valves warm. He went down the first rack with his hand flat on each drum's plate and felt them come up, one after another, like lamps being lit down a street, and when he'd done the first he said into the speaking tube to the Well, "Two's up," and Tess's voice came back, small and brassy, "Two's warming. Give it four minutes," and he went and stood by his mother.

They waited the four minutes. The ship creaked. Harrold breathed.

"Key," said Tess's voice in the tube.

On the Well, a hundred yards off, Ambrose pressed it.

Emeric heard nothing. There was nothing to hear; the beat went out of BR-02's whip on its own frequency and came back off it, and none of it was sound. But his mother's head moved, very slightly, the way a person's head moves when someone speaks to them from behind; and she listened, with her made hands on her plated knees, for perhaps three seconds.

"Good," said Amara.

That was all. She said it plainly, the way you'd say a loaf was done. Ambrose wrote it down on the Well. Emeric heard Tess say it after her into the tube, "Two, good," and the pencil going.

"What does it sound like?" said Harrold from his crate.

Amara thought about it.

"Like somebody knocking on a door in a house I'm not in," she said. "Twice. Short. It's a tidy knock." She turned her head a little toward him. "The ones that aren't right won't be tidy. That's all I'm listening for."

Three woke at a quarter to eleven. Four at ten past. Both good. Five took longer, because one of its warming stones had come up sluggish in its seat and Emeric had to go back to it twice, and when the key went Amara listened for nearly ten seconds with her head on one side, and Harrold leaned forward on his crate, and then she said, "Good. Slow, but good. It's waking up," and Tess in the tube said, "Five, good, slow," and the pencil went.

Six was good. That made five.

Seven was the long one. Seven was the relay that would sit furthest out at the far end of the first leg, where nothing else would hear it but its neighbours and the convoy, and Bram had given it a bigger warming stone and Ambrose a second valve, and Tess had said at the start that it'd want ten minutes, not four, and that nobody was to stand over it. So when Emeric had done seven's stones and said, "Seven's up," into the tube, and Tess said, "Seven's warming. That's ten. Have a cup of something," and the tube went quiet, there were ten minutes in the hold with nothing in them.

His mother turned her head toward him.

She didn't say anything. She'd felt it, he thought. She'd felt it since the dome in December, the shut edge of something in him, and she'd said, When you want to, and hadn't once gone near it since. She was a delver. She'd been a delver before he was born, and a gifted one, Armand said, long before she died. She didn't need to go near it to know where it was.

His father was looking at the drums.

Ten minutes. He'd planned it for this. He'd planned it, God help him, the way he planned a test: the hold, the ten minutes, nobody on the Well who didn't know. He'd thought that if he had a plan it would come easier. It didn't. He stood by the patch board with his hands in his pockets and found that all the words he'd got ready were for a table, for the six, for Ferra's book, and that none of them was any use at all in a ship's hold five miles up in front of his mother and his father on two crates.

So he said it the way it came.



"I did a thing in December," he said. "To Corbin. I've undone it. I undid it on Saturday. I want to tell you both what it was, now that it's done, because I couldn't tell you while it wasn't, and I should have. I should have told you first." He took his hands out of his pockets. "In the first week of December, at the quarter's check, when he'd opened to me so I could look for Augustine in him, I put a piece of myself in the stone on his ankle. In the drain stone. A one-way piece. Cut loose. With three conditions on it. If he gave the Church a way into Kaldo, or gave an order to harm Kaldo's people, or if the anklet was forced. And one thing it would do if any of them came. It would have..."

He stopped, because his mother had put up her hand.

She hadn't moved otherwise. She sat on her crate with her hand raised, palm out, the way she'd held it up in the covered yard to stop Ward calling the bout before she'd finished; and her made face had gone perfectly still, and he understood that she'd known before he got to conditions. She'd known at one-way.

"It would have stopped his heart," said Amara.

"Yes."

"And burned itself, after. So there'd be nothing to read."

"Yes."

"And it cost you." She lowered her hand. "A one-way piece always costs the maker. You knew that. You'd seen the pope's." It wasn't a question. "What did it take?"

"Corbin," said Emeric. "When he was small. The palace. The few good ones." He made himself go on. "The stair outside your room. He used to sit on the bottom step. I've got the stair. I haven't got him on it."

Amara looked at him for a long time.

Across the hold, Harrold hadn't moved. He was sitting on his crate by the hatch with his elbows on his knees exactly as he'd been sitting when the tube went quiet; only his hands had come together between his knees, and the knuckles had gone white.

"I'd have talked you out of it," said Amara.

"I know."

"I'd have sat you down in the dome and talked to you until you didn't want to any more, and it would have taken me about an hour." Her voice was quite level. "That's why you didn't tell me."

"Yes."

"That's why you kept me out of the room. The six argued it, didn't they. At the table. Ferra. Imani. Armand. And not me." She tilted her head. "I felt you come back from it. The Thursday. You came into the dome with it shut in you like a door, and I asked, and you said a long week."

"I'm sorry."

"You should be." She said it simply, not unkindly, the way she'd said good to the relays. "You kept your mother out because she'd have been right. That's a thing a boy does." She looked at him a moment longer. Then something in the made face changed, very slightly, at the mouth, the way it had changed on the gravel in December when she'd said when you want to. "And then you came and told her anyway. In a ship's hold. Between two relays. Because the thing was ash and you couldn't stand carrying the lie about it on top." She let her hands rest on her knees again. "That's a thing a man does. I'll take the second one. I'll remember the first."

He couldn't say anything. He nodded.

"It's off?" she said. "All of it? The piece and the stone?"

"Dust. Saturday. In front of him. Ferra's key took the band off after."

"Did he know?"

"Not what. He knew something had been there." Emeric looked at his hands. "He watched it go. He didn't say anything."

"No," said Amara, very softly. "He wouldn't."

Harrold stood up.

He stood up off his crate all at once, the way he'd stood up in the long room on the day of the yard when somebody had said Augustine's name, and stood there by the hatch ladder in his coat in the lamplight, very tall, with his hands at his sides; and Emeric looked at his father's face and for the first time in his life saw what the court had been afraid of for forty years.

It wasn't loud. Harrold had never been loud. It was cold, and quite still, and it came off him the way cold comes off a stone floor.

"He's mine," said Harrold.

Emeric didn't move.

"He's mine too. He's my son. Before you were born he was my son. He was the first one." His father's voice didn't rise at all. "Do you know what he said to you, in the yard? I wasn't there. Ward told me, word for word, because I made him. He said his father had died too, and nobody had buried him. He said he saw me every day in the corridor." He took a breath. "I've had that in my head three years. I've spent three years trying to be alive to him." "And you put a death on his ankle and didn't tell me. You sat at my table every night in December with it on him. You let me sit beside him at the sheets."

"Yes," said Emeric.

"You let me seal it." Harrold's hand came up an inch, toward the forward end of the hold, the patch board, the long rows of drums, as if the seal were there in its box. "A fortnight ago. I sat in the dome with that seal in my hand and handed you a kingdom. And all that time my eldest was in the chair downstairs with your..."

He stopped.

He stopped quite suddenly, in the middle of it, the way a man stops at the top of a stair he's run up too fast. He stood there with his hand still half raised. Then he looked at it, as if it were somebody else's, and put it down.

Nobody said anything for a while. The ship creaked. A long way off, through the plate, the Well's engine was turning over, a low steady sound like a cat in another room.

"No," said Harrold.

He sat down again on the crate, slowly. He put his elbows back on his knees.

"No. That's not fair, and I know it isn't." He was looking at the boards between his feet. "I'm angry. I'll stay angry, I should think, for a good while, and you'll put up with it. But I'll not stand in a ship and tell you you'd no reason, because you had every reason, and I'm the one who gave you them." He rubbed his face with one big square hand. "What did I ever give you to trust him with? Or him to trust me? I let a priest walk my son through his own head for twenty years and never once looked up from my papers. I sat at my own table with what I am under my skin like a coat I couldn't take off, and never once told any of them. Not him. Not Zale. Not Waylon. Not you, till you worked it out for yourself. I let all of them grow up thinking their father was a man who'd never been anything but a king." He was quiet for a moment. "A family that keeps things from itself. That's what I made. I'd thought it might stop with me." He looked up at last, at Emeric, and his face had gone from cold to only very tired. "It didn't. You did it too. You learned it from me. I'm sorry for that."

"I'm the one who did it," said Emeric.

"I know you are. I'm angry with you. I've said." Harrold looked across the hold at his wife. "I'm sorry anyway. Both are true."

Amara didn't say anything. She looked at him across the two rows of drums, and something went between the two of them that Emeric, standing at the patch board with his hands empty, could see the shape of and not the inside, the way you can see a lamp in another house across a street.

"He's not been told," said Harrold.

"No."

"Will you tell him?"

Emeric opened his mouth.

"It's his to tell," said Amara, before he could answer. She said it to Harrold, not to him. "Not ours. Not yours. Not mine. His. He did it, so it's his to say to Corbin's face, or not at all." She turned her head to Emeric. "And not by letter. Not by relay. Not in a held message on a pad that a girl in the line room reads out. To his face."

Harrold sat with that a while. He didn't like it. Emeric could see him not like it, could see him want to say I'll tell him myself, tonight, in the dome; and then see him put that down too, carefully, beside the other thing he'd put down, and not pick it up.

"Then come home," said Harrold, "and tell him. When you're back. The first week. You come home and you tell him, to his face, and I'll be standing there." He looked at Emeric steadily. "I'll be standing in the room. I'll not say a word. But I'll be there, so that he knows his father knew and didn't hide it from him. That's the one thing I can give him that I didn't give him before. Promise me."

"I promise."

"Say it properly."

"When I'm home. The first week. To his face. With you in the room." Emeric looked at his mother. "With both of you, if you want."

"I'll want," said Amara. "Promise me too."

"I promise."

His mother looked at him for one more moment, the long level look she'd given him across the covered yard before the second bout, when she'd known what he wouldn't do and had used it. Then she nodded, once, and turned her head back to the patch board, and laid her hands flat on her knees.

"Seven's had its ten minutes," she said. "Tess'll be at the tube."

She was. "Key," said Tess's voice, small and brassy, out of the brass mouth on the bulkhead, and on the Well, a hundred yards off, Ambrose pressed it.

Amara listened.

"Good," she said.



Eight was good. Nine. Ten, which Emeric had to wake twice because his hands weren't quite steady the first time and he'd brought the decider up a hair too fast, and Amara listened to it for a long while and said, "Good. Startled. It'll settle," and it did, by the second key. Eleven. Twelve, at a quarter to two, the last of the Band relays, the one with the second big mirror, whose eye Tess had spent a week in January polishing by hand in the Motion Shop with the door shut. Good.

Then the four ground relays, one after another, squat and heavy in their racks at the after end. South Sentinel, good. The first of the two western rocks, good. The second, good. And the Rim.

The Rim was the one that would go to Ilenne's mother's city, to the dry ring at the top of the caldera where her people kept their fire, if the queen let it; the one with the long mast and the cable for down below. Emeric did its stones last and slowest. Harrold had got up from his crate an hour since and come forward along the racks and was standing now just behind his wife's shoulder with one hand on the plate of it, lightly, the way you'd put a hand on a chair somebody was sitting in. Amara didn't seem to notice. Emeric thought she noticed.

"Rim's up," he said into the tube.

"Rim's warming," said Tess. "Four."

They waited four. Nobody said anything.

"Key."

Amara listened. Her made head came up, very slightly, and stayed up, and her hands lay still on her knees, and behind her Harrold's hand didn't move on her shoulder.

"Good," said Amara.

Fifteen of fifteen. Tess said it into the tube, and then said it again, not into the tube, so that they heard it faintly across a hundred yards of open sky through the hold's plate, and then Ambrose's voice behind hers saying something that might have been thank God, and then the pencil.

Amara turned her head and looked up over her shoulder at her husband.

"Every one," she said.

"I heard."

"Every one of them was good." She put her hand up, the plated made hand, and laid it over his on her shoulder. "I said it to every one of them. Even the ones after. I wanted you to hear me say it."

Harrold didn't answer. He stood there with his hand under hers and looked down the two long rows of drums in the lamplight, fifteen of them, every one awake and beating quietly to itself on its own wandering time, with the Band thick round the hull and the Well's engine turning over like a cat in the next room; and Emeric, at the patch board, looked away, because some things you don't watch.



Then there was the lock.

It was the last thing on Ferra's sheet for the day, under the fifteen, and it was meant to be nothing. Tess had built the lock in December in nine days, with Vesk, and had tried it with her own note in February on the Well's deck and been shut out like the Church; it had been tried on every drum in the home net since. But nobody had yet gone the whole length of the voyage chain, end to end, with the relays all awake and all locked and all of them in one place where Tess could watch, and Tess had said that until somebody did, she wasn't calling it a lock. She was calling it a door she hoped was shut.

So Amara did it.

She did it the way she did most things now, with no fuss: she said, "Well, then," and laid her made hands on BR-02's plate, and went. Her body sat on the crate with its hands on the drum, quite still, empty, the way it sat on the Motion Shop's old stand at the Well when she rode the home net; and Harrold, who had seen her do it at the Well and had never once liked it, took his hand off her shoulder and stood beside the empty body with his arms folded and his jaw set, two by the body, and Emeric took the other side, because that was the rule. Neither of them looked away.

She went down the chain, relay to relay, through the eleven deciders in their racks, and the four, and out of the K Discovery's hold across a hundred yards of the Band to the Well's own drum, where Tess said into the tube, "She's here. She's in the drum. She says the eye's dirty," and then back the same way, all fifteen, every lock opening for her note and shutting behind her, and back into the made body on the crate, which lifted its head and said, "Twelve's mirror wants another polish, Tess, there's a thumbprint on it the size of a sheep," in the direction of the speaking tube. It took a little under four minutes.

"Done," said Tess, from the Well. "That's a lock, then. I'll call it a lock."



They came down at four. The city came up under them out of the haze, coin and then town and then streets, and the K Discovery set down on the coal quay with her racks full and fifteen relays awake in her hold, beating to themselves every quarter of an hour at a time that wandered, in Ferra's cipher, down beams nobody else could hear, to say that they were there.

Harrold went straight to the dome.

He didn't say where he was going. He got off the ship and walked up from the coal quay in his coat with his hands in his pockets, not fast, and Emeric, who had a great many things to do and did none of them, walked a little way behind him the whole way up the hill and didn't try to catch up.

The dome was quiet in the late light. There were two boards on the gravel on the tree's north side now. On one of them Maeve lay under a blanket, deep in her stone since Monday morning, breathing long and slow, with her short hair spread on the pillow and one hand open on the blanket's edge; Mira was on the gravel beside her with her back to the trunk and a book she wasn't reading. On the other board, which had been Zale's, and Waylon's, and since Saturday Corbin's, nobody lay. Corbin had woken on the Wednesday. He was sitting on the gravel beside his sister's board in his shirtsleeves, with his knees drawn up and his arms round them, looking at her sleeping face, the way a man sits by a fire when he's thinking about something else. His left ankle, where his trouser had ridden up, was a band of white.

Harrold stopped at the north door.

He stood there a while. Then he went across the gravel, slowly, and sat down beside his eldest son, on the ground, which Emeric had never in his life seen him do, with his long legs out in front of him and his back against the trunk of the tree, so that the two of them sat there side by side by Maeve's board, father and son, with the tree humming over them.

Corbin looked round.

"Sir?"

"Nothing," said Harrold. "I wanted to sit."

Corbin looked at him a moment longer. Then he looked back at his sister.

They sat. Neither of them said anything. Mira turned a page she hadn't read. After a while Harrold reached out without looking and put his big square hand on the back of his son's neck, the way you'd put a hand on a boy's neck at a fair to keep him by you in a crowd, and left it there; and Corbin went perfectly still under it, the way he'd gone still under Emeric's hand on Saturday on the stool; and then, slowly, he didn't.

Emeric stood at the north door until the light went, and then went down to tell Ferra the fifteen were good, and the lock was a lock.
