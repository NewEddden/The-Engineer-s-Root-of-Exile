Chapter 235 - The Drop Line

Garrick went off the boat door third, behind Benet and Joss, and he did not look down, because Ward had told him on the lake not to look down the first time, and Ward was not a man who told you things for his own amusement.

He had been in the band before. Every man on the extraction teams had; that was why they were on them. He had stood the Hod's watches with the old master in the spring, a night and a day, with the brass cups on their rods, and gone out on her rail in his oilskin every hour. He had flown over the lake at Kaldo in the Skin fifty times in the last month, round and round the island at night like a bat round a barn, until Ward said he was fit. But he had never gone off the door of a ship three miles over the sea at night with nothing under him but black and a job at the bottom of it. Nobody had, but the handful in the good suits. That was the thing he could not stop thinking, standing in the door with the cold coming in round him and the wind of her going pulling at the cape on his back. Nobody in the world had done this but a handful of men in the good suits. And now him.

"Go," said Rylan, behind him.

He went.

The cold took him first. Then the fall, a long sick drop with his stomach left somewhere up in the ship, and the black going past, and the wind roaring in the cape as it filled; and then the cape took him, the way it did on the lake, all at once, and he was not falling any more. He was flying. Level. West. With the ship's dark shape already gone above and behind him, and Benet's small pale lamp ahead of him in the dark, shut down to a pin, going away west, and the stars all round, and under him, a long way under, something grey and uneven where the island lay in the sea.

He found he was laughing, in the cold, with his mouth shut. He stopped, because it was not a thing to do, and then did it again.

Behind him, one after another, the others came off the door: Merrick, and Tamlin, and Jibari last of the six, and last of all Rylan, in the black plate of his old suit, with his cape out and his lamp dark, who went down the long slope of the air behind the six of them like a sheepdog behind a flock, and missed nothing.

They put down in the dunes a mile west of the ridge, in the dark, on the lee side of a long low dune that Garrick would not have known from any of the others in the field if Rylan had not walked up to it and put his boot on it and said: this one.

Nothing was taken the first night. That had been the prince's order and Rylan's, and Garrick had thought it a waste of a night until he was on the ground, and then he understood it. They walked the ground. All of it. The dune, and the dunes round it, and the long hollow behind them where the black pools lay stinking in the starlight, and the white flat beyond the dunes that crunched under the boots like frost, where the grey shrub grew knee high in its silver leaves as far as the dark let you see. They walked it so that when they came down tomorrow night, and every night after, their feet would know where they were before their heads did.

They dug to the hatch. It was where Rylan said it would be, a hand under the sand at the dune's lee foot: a plank door, dry, with the sand whispering off it. Jibari opened the fan's box in the dune's side and told the stones in it, and they hummed. Tamlin had the bird. It was a small yellow finch in a wire cage the size of a loaf, off Imani's ward window, the brother of the one that had gone down to the well on the night it came in, and it sat on its perch and looked at them with its head on one side as if they were the strange thing.

And Benet, who was the most careful of them and had done the cave drill on the lake with the divers for a week because he had asked to, reached into his belt for the flint.

Rylan's hand was on his wrist before it got there.

"No," Rylan said. Very quietly. He did not let go. "That's the cave's. Flame, then the bird. That's for down there, where it's bad air that won't burn. Not here." He put his other hand flat on the plank door. "Here the bad air burns. The bird goes first here, and there's no flame after it. Not tonight. Not any night. Not a pipe. Not a spark off a boot nail." He let go. "Say it."

"No flame at the well," Benet said. He was white. He took his hand away from his belt and put it on his knee and left it there. "I had the cave in my head. I did the drill fifty times."

"That's why you had it in your head." Rylan looked at the rest of them, one at a time, in the dark. "That's why I'm here. Next one who forgets goes home on the first boat door and doesn't come back."

Nobody forgot again.

The bird went down first, on its cord, into the crawl-way. It sat on its perch at the bottom in the lamp stone's glow and sang, once, a short cross little burst, and Jibari said, "That's all right, then," and went down after it on his belly.

Garrick went down last. He had heard about it, and he had seen the drawings, and none of it was the same as crawling out of the end of a sand tunnel into the round room and standing up in it. It was very quiet. The walls went round him, earth rammed so hard it rang dull under his knuckle, and over the earth the black lining, glossy where the heat stones had pooled it and dull where the trowels had gone, with the weave of the sailcloth showing through it like the grain in a board. Overhead, the rails on their two posts, and the planks, and the sagging tarred cloth, and a sound on it like somebody whispering in the next room, which was the dune moving. And at his feet, a step down from the rim where he stood, the oil: lying in the bottom of the room like a black floor, perfectly still, with his lamp lying on it in one soft blur.

He stood and looked at it. He had carried casks of it up the winch for a winter. He had never seen it lie still.

They read the drum's gauge, and the tell-tale pipe at the low side, which was dry, as it had always been dry. They checked the two floats and the overflow sump, which was dry too. They checked the pump on the drum's floor, and the beam that worked it, and the rack of cells that would run the beam, and Rylan wrote what they found in a book that went back to Ferra. Then they came up, and closed the hatch, and the wind box ran for an hour at low flow while they dressed the dune with their hands, the way you would make a bed, until Rylan walked round it twice and could not tell where they had been.

It was the dullest night's work Garrick had ever done. He had flown to it off a ship three miles up in the dark.

He thought he could do it every night for the rest of his life.

The Wain came down over the outcrops an hour after midnight, very slowly, with no lamp on her anywhere, and hung there.

Emeric was at her boat door, with the line.

They had crossed to her at dusk, the five of them, from the Discovery, in the band, on a line strung between the two hulls a hundred feet apart: clipped on, lightened, going along it hand over hand with the sea three miles under them and the sunset going out behind. It had been Zale's first real flight, if you could call a man on a rope between two ships flying, and he had done it without a word, looking straight ahead at the Wain's door the whole way, and when Fletcher took his arm at the far end and pulled him in he had said, "Right," and gone and sat down on a coil of rope in her hold with his back against her side and his eyes shut, and not moved again until they started down.

She was the one who came down. That was what she was for. And tonight was the night: the tenth on the Kesrath board, by Ferra's count, and the dark of the moon. The night the ridge was empty. The row that had worked it for nine nights would be at the anchorage under the east fort, waiting for the boat, and the next row would not be ashore till tomorrow. It was the same night, by the same calendar, that he had picked for the first crossing, the winter before last, without knowing. This time he had known. Ferra had worked it out of the board on the hut door and six months of watching from the band, and she had put a ring round it in the book.

The ridge was dark under them. Not a lantern on a bench, from one end of it to the other.

He let the sense down, a little, the way he had learned in the lake: a door open a crack. The rock came up through it, bright and strange, the pale glassy stone of the outcrops breaking up out of the sand in its splinters and ridges like the bones of something enormous, and the benches cut in the ridge's flank, stepped, empty. No warm knot on any of them. Nobody on the ridge. At the anchorage under the east fort, a long way off, forty warm knots, close together, still. Asleep.

And under the ridge, right under the Wain, the hollow.

He knew it now. He had stood on its spoil three times with Ward in the last hour of the dark, on the drilling nights, and put the sense down into it as far as it would go. The hollow under the lowest bench: a bowl in the ridge's foot, heaped with Kesrath's spoil, with the rock at the back of it going down and in. Nobody worked the lowest bench. Nobody had worked it for years; he had watched their lanterns from the band on a dozen nights and they never came down to it, not once, not even to throw their spoil; they threw it from above. There were tools lying on the lowest bench, left where men had dropped them, rusted to the colour of the rock. Whatever had happened on the lowest bench had happened a long time ago, and it had happened to men, and the men who came after would not go down there after dark. If I came up the benches again I'd go into the hole with the others.

The sacrifice ground. He did not know, yet, what had been sacrificed. He was going to find out.

"Down," he said to Ned, and Ned brought her down another fifty feet, until she hung two hundred feet over the hollow with her bulk blotting out the stars, and Emeric clipped himself to the drop line and stepped out of the door.

It was the easiest thing he did that night, and he did it first, because it was his job to go first.

He went down the line lightened, with the winch paying out above him steady and slow, and the rope running through the cam on his belt, and his hands off it; the rope held him, all of him, whatever his stones did. He touched down on the spoil at the back of the hollow in the dark and stood still and let the sense out again, round him, close. Rock. Sand. The dry, faint, mineral smell of the outcrops' stone. Nothing living bigger than a beetle for a hundred yards in any direction. He pulled one on the line: all well. And above him the winch stopped, and started again, and the line went up, and after a while it came down again with Ward on it.

Ward went to the back of the hollow without a word and began pulling rocks off the slab. They had marked it on the drilling nights: a slab of the pale stone leaning against the rock at the hollow's back, with Kesrath's spoil heaped round its foot, and behind it, where the sense had gone down and in, a slot. Ward had it clear in a quarter of an hour. It was a black gap between the slab and the rock, as high as a man and as wide as a door, going in, and the air that came out of it was cool, and still, and old.

Wes came down next, with the line's reels on his back. Then Sir Gavin, who touched down lightly and stood very straight and looked at the slot and said nothing. Then Sir Aled, heavily, with the big bag of the camp's gear slung under him on its own line. Then Ferra, fast and neat, as if she had been doing it all her life, with her book in its oilskin inside her suit; she unclipped and went straight to the slot and stood beside Ward and looked in.

Then Zale.

He came off the Wain's door well enough. Emeric watched him come, a dark shape against the stars at the end of the line, coming down slowly, turning a little as he came, as a man does on a rope. He came down fifty feet, and a hundred.

And stopped.

The winch had not stopped. Zale had. He had put his hands on the rope, both of them, and clamped them, and the cam at his belt had locked on it the way it was made to lock when a man grabbed, and he hung there halfway between the ship and the ground, a hundred feet up in the dark over the hollow, holding on.

The winch stopped. Fletcher, at the door, had felt it in the line.

Emeric let the sense up the rope. He could feel his brother at the end of it, a warm knot in the cold air, and the breath in him, fast and short and high, the breath of a man who has suddenly found that he cannot get enough of it. He could feel the hands. He did not need the sense for the hands. He could see them: two black fists on the rope, against the stars.

He did not pull anything on the line. He did not call up. There was nothing he could say from the ground that would help a man on a rope in the dark, and anything he shouted would carry.

The rope held him. That was what it was for. It held all of him, every pound, whatever his stones were doing, because Ward had had every rope on the campaign rated for a man's whole weight, because the stones were what would fail. It held him while he hung there, and it went on holding him, and nobody hurried him, and after a while, a long while, Emeric felt the breath at the end of the rope change. Slow. Deepen. The way it had at the bottom of the lake, when the door had opened.

One of the fists came off the rope. Then the other.

Zale pulled one on the line. All well.

Fletcher started the winch, and Zale came down the last hundred feet, slowly, turning a little, with his hands held out a little from his sides, the way Emeric had told him to hold them on the jetty the first night, for balance; and touched down on the spoil, and stood, and unclipped, and walked to the slot at the back of the hollow where the others were, and stopped beside his brother, and looked in.

"I remembered how to breathe," he said, very low. "Halfway down. I'd forgotten." He did not look at Emeric. "I'll remember it now."

"Everybody forgets it once," said Ward, from the other side of him.

"Did you?"

"The first night," said Ward. "I've never told anybody. I'm telling you."

Zale looked at him across the slot's black mouth. Then he nodded, and turned back to the dark.

Above them the Wain drew her line up for the last time, and climbed, slowly, her bulk leaving the stars behind it one at a time; and was a shape; and was gone.

The way in went down.

It was a passage, cut, not found: a long straight slope going down into the ridge under the benches, with a roof twice a man's height and steps cut in its floor. Steps too tall. That was the first thing, before anything else, before anybody had had time to think about anything; every step was the height of a man's knee, a little more, so that you went down them not as you went down a stair but as a child goes down a stair, one step at a time with both feet on each, a hand on the wall. The walls were smooth. Not rough-hewn; smooth, as if they had been rubbed. And in the lamp's light, going down, at the height of a tall man's eyes and a little above, in a line along the wall, there were marks cut in the stone. Signs.

Zale stopped on the fourth step and put his hand up and touched the first of them.

Nobody said anything. Ferra's lamp was on it with his. It was the tree. Small, a hand high, cut into the smooth stone of the wall in a few clean lines: the trunk, the crown, and in the crown, eight little bumps in a row, like a pod. The same tree, cut the same way, as in the margins of the book in Ferra's box, that Ansel Vey had copied off a headland in the fog on the far side of the world.

"Tomorrow," said Emeric.

Zale took his hand off the wall. "Tomorrow," he said, and went on down.

Wes laid the line. He did it the way he had done it at the lake, every night for three weeks, and he did not hurry. He tied it off at the slot's mouth round a horn of rock inside the slab, where nobody standing in the hollow could see it, and went down the passage ahead of them paying it out off his reel, and every twenty steps he stopped and tied it off again, to a peg Sir Gavin drove into a crack in the wall with a wooden mallet wrapped in rag. And beside it, running free, not tied to anything, through iron rings Sir Gavin hung on the same pegs, the second line. The light one. The talking line. The guideline was for the hand, and it was tied off at every turn so that a break in it at one place could not pull it out of a man's hand at another. The talking line was for the pulls, and it was tied to nothing, so that a pull at one end came through to the other without a knot to swallow it.

The passage went down a long way. A hundred steps. Emeric stopped counting. The air got cooler and stiller and the smell of the rock changed, and there was another smell under it that he knew and had not smelled since the lake: water, still water, a long way from the sun.

And then the steps went into it.

The passage did not end. It went on down, at the same slope, the same width, the same smooth walls; the steps went on down; but twenty steps below him the water lay across the passage from wall to wall, black and perfectly still, with the lamps lying on it, and the steps went down into it and the roof came down to meet it a little further on, and the water and the roof and the steps went on down together into the dark, as if the passage had been flooded to the ceiling by a hand that had poured it full and gone away.

The sump.

He had felt it from the ridge, twice, in the winter. He had felt how long it was: forty paces, a little more, down and along and up again, to where the passage came up out of the water on the far side into a space the sense had not been able to find the end of. He had felt how deep: at its lowest, a little under the height of two men. Twelve feet. Thirteen. Well above the line where pure breath turned to poison.

They made ready on the steps. Nobody talked much. The capes were already off; they had come off in the hollow, and Ward had them, all five, rolled, in the bag at the slot's mouth with the parachutes. The loops were checked, each diver by his partner, bottle, bag, canister, hoses, valves, in the order they had done it a hundred times on the jetty, aloud, in low voices. The gauges read, and said aloud, and written by Wes on his slate. The helmets. Zale's hands were quite steady on his collar bolts. Emeric watched them and said nothing about it.

The bird was the last thing. It went into its bell: a jar of the glassworks' clear glass as big as a man's head, with a steel lid on a rubber gasket that Bram had made and tested in the ice tub, and the finch on its perch inside with a breath of the hollow's air round it. It would be two minutes in the bell under the water. Emeric had tested it with a sparrow in the lake. The sparrow had come out cross and well.

Then he went down the steps into the water, and it came up round his boots, and his knees, cold, colder than the lake, and he felt it come up round the suit and press on it gently all over as it rose, the way the lake had, and it came up to his chest, and over the chest lamp, and up the front of his glass, and the lamps behind him went green and then dark green, and he was under.

It was easy. That was what he remembered afterwards, and what frightened him, a little, later, lying awake on the far side of it.

Forty paces of flooded passage, going down at the same slope, with the steps under his boots, and then along, level, with the roof a hand above his helmet, and then up. Clear water. Clearer than the lake by a long way; nothing lived in it to cloud it, and nothing had stirred it in years, and his chest lamp went through it and lit the smooth walls and the tall steps under him and the fine grey silt lying on every step like dust on a shelf, untouched. He went down it slowly, the way he had drilled them, never fast, on his hands and his knees down the flooded stair, with the reel of the guideline paying out off his belt behind him and the talking line in its rings coming after, and his own breath going round and round in the loop, quiet, with no bubbles, so that nothing rose to the roof and nothing fell from it.

He did not use the stones at his hips. There was nothing here to hurry for.

At the bottom of the slope, where it ran level, he stopped, and tied the line off round a knob of stone at the foot of the wall, and looked at the gauge at his wrist. Thirteen feet. He looked back up the slope. Two lamps were coming down it after him, a little apart, green in the clear water, and a third behind them, and he knew them by the way they moved on the line before the light reached their faces. Ferra first, neat. Then Sir Gavin, slow and careful, as if the water might break. Then Sir Aled, big, heavy, too heavy, sinking a little between steps and correcting on the line, with the bird's bell in its net at his belt swinging behind him. Zale would be last, with Wes behind him at the top, on the steps, with the line's end.

He reached back along the line to Sir Gavin, who was the first of the two knights, and put his hand on the line in front of the knight's hand, so that the knight's hand came up against his. Not holding it. Just there. So that the knight felt, in the black water with his lamp in front of him and the roof a hand above his head, that there was a hand ahead of his on the line, and that it was not moving.

He felt Sir Gavin's breathing in the sense, fast and high; and then, after a moment, slower.

He did the same for Sir Aled. He did not need to do it for Ferra. She had gone by him already, along the level, with her lamp ahead of her and her hand on the line, and turned, at the foot of the far slope, to see that they were coming.

It's clear, she said, in his head. It's so clear.

Nothing lives in it.

Then that's the first thing in my life I've been glad nothing lived in.

They went up the far slope together, slowly, hand over hand, on the line, with the knights behind, and Zale's lamp coming along the level after them now. The water got paler overhead. Then there was a ceiling of silver over him, flat, wrinkling a little, with his own lamp lying on it; and he put his head up through it into air.

Black. Still. Cold. Dry.

He stood on the steps with the water at his waist, and his lamp showing him the steps going on up out of the water ahead of him, the same tall steps, and the same smooth walls, and beyond the lamp's reach, nothing. Not dark; nothing. A dark that went away on every side of him further than his light could follow it, and gave back no echo of the water dripping off his suit, or gave it back so late and so faint that he could not tell where from.

He did not take his helmet off.

He went up the steps out of the water, and stood on the top one, on a floor, level, smooth, and let the sense go. All of it. For the first time since the lake.

It went out into the dark and did not come back for a long time.

Rooms. That was the first thing it told him. A great many rooms, cut in the rock, opening off each other and off long straight halls, going away from him in every direction, on more than one level; stairs going up, and down, and across; and all of them empty, and dry, and still, with the medium lying in them thicker than the air at the ridge's foot, a little, not much, slow and settled as dust. Nothing living. Not a knot of warmth anywhere, from here to the edge of what he could feel. And at the edge of what he could feel, a long way off, going down, the water again: the other water, the deep water, standing in its shaft. And under it, very far, the pale blur he had put on the trestles in the grey place, because that was what it was.

Behind him, one after another, the others came up out of the water onto the steps. He heard the water running off them. Nobody spoke.

"Flame," he said.

Sir Aled had the lamp, an old one, a real one, a wick in oil in a brass and glass case, the only flame on the island that night, because it had to be. He knelt on the top step with his helmet on and his breath going round in his loop, and put his back to the others, and lit it with a flint and steel, shielding it with his body, and held it up in the still air at arm's length, away from all of them, away from the loops, and they watched it.

It burned. A small steady yellow flame, standing straight up, not lying down, not going blue. It burned the way a lamp burns in a room.

"Bird," said Emeric.

Sir Aled put the lamp down on the step, well away, and unscrewed the bell's lid, and the finch in it shook itself and put its head out of the jar and looked at the dark, and hopped up onto the rim, and sat there. They waited. It sat there a long time. It preened under one wing. Then it opened its beak and sang, the same short cross burst it sang every morning at the ward's window, and it went off into the dark ahead of them like a small bright thread, and came back, and did not fall over.

Emeric took his helmet off.

The air was cold and very dry and smelled of stone and of nothing else at all. He breathed it. It was air. Old air, still, that had lain in the rock for longer than he could think about, but air, with breath in it, enough, and the flame had said so and the bird had said so. He breathed it again.

Then the others took theirs off, one after another, and stood on the top of the steps in the lamp stones' light, dripping, with their helmets under their arms, and breathed, and looked round at the dark, and did not say anything, for a long time, because there was nothing any of them had a word for.

They made camp under a stair.

It was the first stair they came to, off the hall at the top of the steps: a flight going up the side of the hall's wall to a gallery, with a hollow under it deep enough to stand in. Every step of it was the height of a man's knee, like the steps in the passage. Sir Aled put the cots under it, five canvas cots on iron legs that folded, and the stores along the wall behind them, the water in its casks, the food, the spare bottles, the spare canisters, three of everything for every planned dive, as the plan said. The stair went up over their heads in the dark, step by tall step, as if it had been made for a man half as tall again as any of them, going up to bed.

"Who's that for?" Sir Gavin said, looking up at it. It was the first thing he had said since the hollow. "That's not for us."

"No," said Ferra. She had her book out, and her pen, and she had written something already. She did not say what.

Emeric went back down to the water's edge alone, when the camp was made and the lamps were set, and knelt on the step above the water, and took the talking line in his hand where it came up out of the black water in its rings and went on up the wall to its last peg. He held it. It was still.

He pulled one. All well.

For a long moment there was nothing. Forty paces of flooded stair, and a hundred steps of passage, and the slot, and the hollow. And then it came back down the line into his hand, small and clear, from Wes at the top of it. One. All well.

And then, a moment after, a different one. Not a pull. A long slow draw on the line, and a long slow give back: Wes's hand, and behind Wes's hand, he knew, because it had been agreed, Ward's. The sign that meant we have you. And above that, somewhere he could not see, a lamp in a tube in the hollow's rock, pointing straight up, so that it could be seen from nowhere on the island and from nowhere on the sea, only from above: Ward's lamp to the ship, telling the ship what the line had told Ward.

He knelt on the step with the line in his hand for a long time.

It was an hour before dawn when the line went quiet.

He was awake. He had not slept. None of them had, much, except Sir Aled, who could sleep anywhere and did, on his back on his cot, with his hands folded on his chest like a knight on a tomb. Ferra was sitting on her cot with her book on her knees, writing by a lamp stone, and every so often stopping and looking up at the stair over her head. Zale was lying on his back with his eyes open, looking up at it too. Sir Gavin was sitting on the bottom step of the stair, which came to his waist, with his back to it, looking at the dark.

Emeric was at the water.

It came at the time it had been agreed it would come. Three pulls on the talking line, from Wes at the top: take in. Not take in. The other meaning they had agreed for this one hour, for the last pull of every night: we're going up. He answered it, one, all well. And then he felt the line, in his hand, go slack a little, and then, as Wes at the top tied it off round the horn of rock inside the slab and covered its end with stones, go still.

Not quiet, as a line is quiet between pulls. Still. The stillness of a line with nobody holding the other end.

He did not need the sense to know what was happening up there. He could see it. Wes, coiling nothing, because the line stayed; covering its end with stones. Ward at the slot's mouth, drawing the slab back across it, heaping the spoil round its foot the way it had been. Ward going round the hollow with a brush of grey shrub, backwards, sweeping out the marks of their boots, all of them, every print, to the edge of the rock. The lamp in its tube going out. And then the two of them walking west, a mile, to the dunes, where the boat line came down out of the black for the extraction teams, away from the ridge, and going up it, lightened, into the last of the dark, into the band, into the ship, where there would be the galley's fire and Imani and a bunk and daylight.

And the dune by the flats dressed, and the six in their Skins going up off it, one after another, off the boat door, and Rylan last.

And by dawn, nobody on the island. Nobody on the surface of it at all, from the west fort to the east, but forty Kesrathi asleep at the anchorage, waiting for a boat.

And five people under the ridge, in the dark, a hundred steps and forty paces of black water down, with a line that nobody was holding.

He let go of it. He went back up the steps to the stair. Ferra looked up from her book.

"They've gone up," he said.

"I know," she said. "You were down there with the line a long time."

Zale, on his cot, did not move. He was still looking up at the stair that went up into the dark over his head, one tall step after another.

"So we're on our own," he said.

"Till dark."

"I've been on my own under a lot of things," Zale said. "Decks. Cliffs. A whale's jaw once, in a lord's hall." He was quiet for a moment. "Never under anybody's house."

Nobody answered him. Sir Aled snored, once, on his back, like a knight on a tomb, and Sir Gavin on the stair's bottom step laughed, suddenly, surprised, into his hand, and then stopped, and looked at the dark as if it might have heard him.

Emeric lay down on his cot and put his hands behind his head. Above him the stair went up, and up, into nothing, built for feet that had never been human, in a house that had been empty longer than anybody alive could count.

He slept, in the end. He dreamed of nothing at all.
