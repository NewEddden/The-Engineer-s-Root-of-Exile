Chapter 235 - Cold and Pressure

The first try failed in about three breaths, which Bram said afterward was a record even for a first try.

They made it on the fifth of October in the stone shed at the secret dock, at the far end of the lake, where hull two's spare stones and spare rope and spare everything were kept behind a door with Ward's lock on it. Bram had built the frame on the Saturday and carried it down the lake path on the Sunday in a handcart under sacking, swearing at every root. It stood on the shed's flagged floor now under a lamp, and it did not look like much: an iron tub the size of a washing copper, lagged in felt; inside the tub, packed round with broken ice from the ice works, a cold stone, a fire stone that Emeric had told to pull instead of push, sitting in a cradle at the bottom like an egg in a nest; and coiled round the stone, down through the ice, a long spiral of Ivo's glass tube, thick-walled, as thick as a man's thumb, that came out at the bottom of the tub through a stopper into a glass trap the size of a beer mug, sitting in a felt jacket of its own.

At the top of the coil, coupled to it by Bram's brass and a short length of tube, stood one of the Hod's flasks. It had come down full from four miles on the Saturday in its quilted waistcoat, a tier of the racks in an iron bottle, and to Emeric's sense it sat on the shed floor like a lamp left burning in an empty room.

"Again," said Bram, "for the one at the back."

Tobe was the one at the back. He had come because he had carried the flask from the reeds and would not let anybody else carry it the last hundred yards, and he stood by the door with his hands behind him in the stance he had learned from Ward without knowing it.

"Air goes liquid if you get it cold enough," said Emeric. "Cold enough, and squeezed. Everything does, nearly. The Band's in the air up there, so it's in the flask with the air, squeezed, and I want it out on its own, as a liquid, in that jar. So it goes down the coil, round the cold stone, and gets colder all the way down, and at the bottom, if it's cold enough, it falls out of the air like dew out of a summer night and runs into the trap." He put his hand on the tub. "The stone's been pulling at the ice for a day. It's colder in there than the ice house."

"How cold does it want?"

"Colder than that."

"Then why are we doing it?" said Bram.

"To find out how much colder."

Bram grunted, which was agreement, and Ward, outside the open door with the lake behind him, said nothing at all.

Emeric cracked the flask's valve. In the shed, on the ground, at a hand's warmth, it could be cracked; it was the four miles and the cold that froze a valve in its seat. A hair, and the flask began to give, and the squeezed air with the Band in it went down into the top of the coil, and he felt it go: a thread of bright thickness running down through the glass into the cold, round the stone, round, down.

And at the bottom, for a breath, in the trap, something gathered.

He felt it before he saw it. A brightness at the bottom of the jar, small and dense and quite different from the thread in the coil, as a drop of water is different from a breath on a cold pane. He leaned to look. A film, pale, on the bottom of the trap, with the lamp in it.

Then it went.

It went as the jar had gone in his room nearly five years before, when he took his hand out: all at once, up. The film on the bottom of the trap warmed in the time it took him to see it and stood up out of the glass as a gas again, and the coil, which could not cool what came down it fast enough, was warming too, and the flask behind it was still giving; and the bright thread became a stream, and the stream went past the trap's stopper and through the gap where Bram's brass met the glass and out into the shed.

The shed filled.

It filled in the time it took to say so. One breath the stone shed at the secret dock was a cold room smelling of rope; the next it was the foot of the Band. Thick, warm to the sense, pressing in on everything, on the flags and the spare stones on their shelves, which woke all along the wall in a ripple, and on the four of them.

"Shut it," said Bram.

Emeric had shut it. He had shut it before Bram spoke; the valve was closed under his hand and the flask was giving nothing. It did not matter. What had come out was out, and the shed's door was a slit and its one window a slot, and the Band had nowhere to go.

Tobe put his hand to his face.

It came away red. Not a line, as Ned's had been on the Saturday before the iron; a run, down his lip and over his chin, bright in the lamp, all at once. He looked at his hand with an expression of mild surprise.

"Out," said Ward.

He had the door open behind Tobe before anybody else had moved, both leaves of it, back against the wall, and the lake air came in cold and grey; and he took Tobe by the collar, not ungently, and walked him out of the shed onto the dock and sat him down on a bollard, and stood over him. Bram came out after them. Emeric came last, because he was the one who had opened the valve, and because something in him wanted to stand in it a breath longer and feel what four miles of sky was like on the ground, in a shed, where nobody had ever felt it; and he stood in it, and felt it, and his own nose did not bleed, and his head swam, and he went out.

They sat on the dock for an hour with the doors wide, while the shed emptied into the lake air.

Tobe's nose stopped in a quarter of an hour. He held Ward's handkerchief to it and looked at the shed.

"Nobody stayed," he said, through the cloth.

"Nobody stayed," said Ward.

"That's why." Tobe took the handkerchief away and looked at it. "Up there, in the old house, before the iron, it was an hour before anybody bled. The healer had it to the minute. In there it was a breath, because it all came at once." He folded the handkerchief. "But we went out. Nobody tore. Nobody stayed in it long enough to tear."

"That is what the doors are for," said Ward.

Bram had not said anything. He was looking at the shed too, at the iron tub inside it under the lamp, and the coil, and the trap at the bottom, empty and clean as if nothing had ever been in it.

"It went," he said.

"It went."

"It was there, though." Bram scratched his jaw. "I saw it. A skin of it. Blue."

"It was there. For a breath." Emeric was looking at the coil. "And then it was warmer than it could stand and it went back. The stone can't pull fast enough. It pulls what it's told to pull, as fast as it's told, and the flask gave faster than it could pull, and the coil warmed, and the bottom of the trap warmed, and that was that."

"Then tell it to pull faster."

"I'll tell it to pull for longer first. And give it less to pull against." He stood up. "A smaller valve. A slower feed. The coil longer, so it's cold all the way down before anything reaches the bottom. And the trap in iron, not felt, in the ice, so it's cold before the first drop gets there."

Bram looked at him.

"Three days," he said.

"Three days."

Bram got up off the bollard, and went into the shed, which had thinned to ordinary air by then, and stood over the tub with his hands on his hips looking down at the coil as he looked at a bad pour, which was as a thing he meant to have right by Saturday.



The second try was on the Thursday, and it worked.

Bram had cut a valve with a needle in it, so that it could be opened by a quarter of a hair and held there, from a seat the forge had made in August for the flasks; and Ivo had drawn a coil half as long again; and the trap sat in an iron cup in the ice at the bottom of the tub, packed round with more ice, and had been sitting there since Wednesday noon. The cold stone had been pulling for two days. When Emeric put his hand on the outside of the tub's lagging it was cold through felt, and the shed's air above it had a smell to it, faint and sharp, like the air before snow.

Ward stood at the open door. Tobe stood outside it, by Ward's order, and did not argue.

Emeric opened Bram's needle valve the quarter of a hair, and waited.

It went down the coil slowly. A thread, thinner than on Monday, much thinner, going down round the stone through glass that was colder all the way than anything the Monday coil had been; and he followed it down with his sense, and felt it slow, and thicken, as a stream thickens in winter before it stops. And at the bottom, in the trap, in the iron cup, in the ice, something gathered, and did not go.

He did not look at it for a long count. He kept his sense on it and let it gather, and felt it gather, a drop and then more, the bright small heaviness at the bottom of the jar getting, very slowly, bigger. At sixty he looked.

A pool. A little one. Pale blue, the blue of a clear winter evening just before the first star, with the lamp in it, and moving slowly when the frame shivered, more slowly than water, and glowing, faintly, from inside, as it had glowed in his jar.

Bram leaned over his shoulder and looked down into the tub.

"There," said Bram.

"There."

They let it run an hour. Nobody said anything for most of it. Bram stood with his arms folded and watched the frost come up the outside of the trap's iron cup and creep over the ice; Ward stood at the door and watched the lake; and Emeric knelt by the tub with his hand flat on its lagging and his sense down at the bottom of it, at the little pool, and felt it get very slowly deeper. At the end of the hour he closed the needle valve and drew the trap up out of its cup with tongs, sealed at its neck with a glass stopper and wax, and held it to the lamp.

A few spoonfuls. No more. At the bottom of a glass trap the size of a beer mug, a little pale blue, glowing, still.

And it had cost him nothing.

That was the thing he had to stand there and let himself feel, kneeling on the cold flags with the trap in his tongs. Nearly five years ago, in his room, with his hand in a jar and a collar of leather round his wrist, he had made two ounces of this out of himself and been left so empty that he slept till noon. Armand had counted off six reasons on his fingers why nobody ever bothered, and the first was that it cost more to make than it held. It did, if you were the one it was made out of. He was not, any more. The sky was. The Hod had gone up on Saturday and come down with a flask, and the flask had cost the crew a night and the works a barrow of spent stones, and the stones had come home full as well; and here was some of it in a jar, as a liquid, made by cold and a coil and a needle valve, and he had given it nothing of himself but his hand on a tub.

"What is in it?" said Ward, from the door.

"The Band. On its own." He turned the trap in the light. "Not the air. The air went through. It's gone out of the vent at the bottom, it's still going, you can feel the cold of it. It doesn't fall at this cold. This does." He looked at the blue. "It goes first. Before the air. A little warmer than air will stand it, it comes out of the air and runs."

"Is that good?"

"It's very good," said Emeric. "It means we don't have to make the air liquid to get it. Only cold enough for this, which is easier." He put the trap into the felt box Abloh had made for it. "And it means something else later that I'll tell you about when it's later."

Ward looked at him for a breath with no expression, which in Ward meant that he had heard the word later before and had learned what it was worth.

"Tobe," said Ward, over his shoulder, "you may come in."



Bram had a second question, and he asked it at his bench in the long shop the next morning, with the trap on the bench between them in its felt box and the lid off.

"And when your stone stops?"

Emeric looked up from the drawing he was making.

"A stone's a stone," said Bram. "You told me so. In the cellar, when the forge went, and on the ship when you wrote that rule about the latch, and every time anybody's asked you what happens in a dead zone. It stops. That cold stone stops, and your coil warms, and whatever's in the trap goes up like Monday, in whatever room it's in, with whoever's in it." He put a finger on the felt box. "I'll not build a shed that only works while a stone does. Not for this. Not after Monday."

"No," said Emeric.

He had been waiting for it. It was the rule; he had written it himself at the head of the forge book, the week the forge first ran, and it held outside the cellar as well as in it: whatever the stone does first, the recipe goes into the works beside it, so that a man without magic can learn it. The stone was the first step. It had only to work once, to prove there was something in the jar worth the trouble. Then the iron.

So he drew it for Bram.

A pump first: a squeezing pump, like the fire-engine pump Bram had already made for the Hod's flasks, but driven by an engine and not a man's arms, that took air in and pressed it hard into a pipe, and the pipe through a water tank to take the heat of the pressing out of it. Then the coil. Not one coil, two, one inside the other, the squeezed air going down the inside one; and at the bottom of the inside coil, a valve, a narrow one, a hair of an opening, through which the squeezed air was let out into the wide space of the outer coil all at once. He drew an arrow at the valve.

"It gets colder there," he said. "When you let it out. A squeezed thing let loose gets cold. You've felt it. The flask valves, at four miles, freezing in their seats from what goes through them. That's this. It's why they freeze."

Bram looked at the arrow.

"Then the cold air that's come out goes back up the outer coil," said Emeric, "round the inner one, the way it came, the other way. And as it goes up, it cools the next lot coming down. So the next lot's colder when it gets to the valve, and colder again when it comes out, and it cools the next lot more, and round, and round." He drew the arrows, down the middle and up the outside. "Every time round, colder. Nobody does anything. It cools itself."

Bram looked at the drawing for a long while with his spectacles on the end of his nose. Then he took them off and rubbed his eyes.

"It cools itself," he said.

"It cools itself."

"Off nothing but a pump and a hole."

"Off a pump and a hole and a great deal of patience." Emeric looked at the drawing. It was a man's name in the other life, the whole thing, a German who had built it with a beard and a great deal of patience; Linde, the books called the cycle, and he had read it in a chemistry text in the trial as if he had built it with his own hands. "It'll take a long time the first time. Days, maybe, running, before anything falls out at the bottom. Everything has to get cold first, the pipes, the coils, all of it. After that it's quicker." He tapped the trap in its box. "The stone does it in an hour. This does it in days, the first time. But this works in a dead zone, and in a room with no mage in it, and when Tobe's running it alone at three in the morning and the stone's gone flat because somebody forgot to charge it."

Bram put his spectacles back on and pulled the drawing toward him.

"The engine's easy," he said. "There's the little one off the hoist that's been doing nothing since the derrick." He put his finger on the valve. "That's not easy. That wants a seat that'll hold squeezed air and a hair of a hole through it that doesn't wear wider every time it's opened." He looked up. "That's a forge job."

"It's one seat and one needle. Once."

"Once," agreed Bram. "And then I'll copy it." He rolled the drawing up. "And I'll want to know who's running it at three in the morning, and I'll want him to be somebody who can read a gauge and won't open a door because he's curious." He put the roll under his arm and stood up. "Tobe. If Tobe'll have it."

"Ask him."

"I'll ask him," said Bram. "He's bled for it twice now. He's a right to say no."

Tobe said yes. He said it on the dock that evening with his hands in his pockets, looking at the shed, and he said that he would want the doors to open outward and to be left open, and Bram said they would be.



The treatment works had been running since the end of September, below the town on the river bank, and on the Saturday the arms turned by themselves.

That was what everybody came down the river road to see. There were two beds, round, sixteen paces across, walled in brick to the height of a man's waist and filled to the brim with broken stone; and over each one, on a pivot at the middle, four long iron pipes stood out like the spokes of a wheel with no rim. The water from the settling tanks above them came up into the pivot under its own head and squirted out of holes along the back of each pipe, and the push of it drove the arms round, slowly, over the stone, like a sprinkler on a lawn in a garden in the other life. A thin rain fell from them onto the bed and vanished.

It smelled. Not as much as the river below the ward drain had smelled every August for as long as anyone could remember, but enough that the lower ward's boys, who had walked down to look, stood upwind and held their noses for the pleasure of it.

Emeric had the method out of a book. A library book on municipal sewage, three names on the spine, borrowed for a paper he had never written in the other life and read on a train; he had remembered, of all things, a photograph of a bed exactly like these, with a man in a hard hat standing beside it looking proud.

What the book had not told him was what furnace slag did when you broke it. He had wanted the beds filled with slag from the works, because it was free and there was a hill of it, and in August he had said so at the edge of the heap to the slag tipper, a man who had broken slag for twenty years and had been listening to him with his arms folded.

"It'll pack," the slag tipper had said. "You break it small, it packs. Wet, it sets. Like the cement." He had kicked a lump. "You want it big as your fist and nothing smaller than an egg, and you want it riddled, or your bed'll be a floor by midwinter."

So it had been riddled, every barrow of it, by boys the tipper picked himself; and the beds were not floors. Under the stone the water came out at the bottom into a channel and ran away to the river clear, or nearly clear; and Imani, who had been timing the arms with her watch open in her palm, went down the steps to the channel with a glass jar, filled it, held it up to the light and looked through it at the river.

She did not say anything. She stoppered it and wrote on the label.

"Well?" said Emeric.

"Ask me in a week." She came up the steps. "It'll be under Bram's lens tomorrow, beside a jar of the river above the town, and a jar from below the old drain, and then I'll know." She looked at the arms going round. "It looks better than it did. Looking better is how people die."

But she went up into the town that afternoon with a card in her apron pocket, to the end house of the second terrace row in the lower ward, where a plumber had fitted a fireclay pan and an iron cistern on two brackets in the little brick house at the bottom of the yard, where the privy had been until the week before; and Emeric went with her, because she had asked him to carry the jar, which she could have carried herself. She looked at the pan, and at the trap under it, and at the pipe going down through the floor to the drain Bram's men had laid and stopped with a clay plug two years ago against this day.

There were eight children in the yard. They had been there since the cistern went up.

Imani pulled the chain herself.

The water came down out of the cistern with a noise like a barrel going down a stair, and swirled, and went, and the cistern began to fill again with a hiss and a knock. All eight children cheered. The woman of the house, in the doorway with her arms folded, did not; she looked at the pan with the face of a woman who has been told a thing is clean by people who have never had to clean it.

Imani took the card out of her pocket and signed it on the cistern's iron lid, and gave it to her.

"That's your closet," she said. "Now the other half." She held up her own hands, front and back. "Every time. Soap and the tap. Or I've had Bram's men put this pipe in for nothing."

The woman looked at the card, and at Imani's hands, and then, slowly, at her own.

"Every time," she said.

"Every time. The children too. Especially the children."

The woman put the card in her apron. Then she turned to the yard and said one word to the eight children, and all eight of them went to the yard tap in a line, as if they had been waiting a year for somebody to tell them to.



The man on the ward's end table was a mason from the footings in the west ward, carried up on the Monday with his heel broken in a fall from a scaffold, and it was a bad break, and that was how it happened.

Imani had felt it with her hands for a quarter of an hour, in the afternoon, with the mason grey on the table and a nurse holding his knee and Emeric at the foot of it, and then she had straightened up and said that she could not see it. Not what was wrong; she could feel what was wrong. She could not see the shape of it, the bones of the heel, the pieces, which way each one had gone, and she would not set a heel she could not see. Emeric had looked into it, with the hollow view, at the mason's note; and the hollow view showed him the vessels in the foot bright and torn and the bones only faintly, as wood is faint to the sense, which was no use at all for a heel. So they had done what they had done seven years ago with Tess's back on the table in the old ward, the night the delve first had Imani in it.

They went into the room, the two of them, with the control stone in his hand and hers over it, and built the heel.

It was how the delve was for her. He had stopped thinking it strange. In the room, time ran slow and clean, and memory could be laid out on the floor like a drawing, and things a person had felt could be taken out of where they were kept and set down between two people and looked at from every side; and Imani had felt the heel for a quarter of an hour with her two hands, every edge of every piece of it. So she gave it to him. She did it as she did everything in the room, plainly, without fuss: she reached back for the feel of it under her fingers and held it out, and he took it, and in the middle of the room's floor between them, in the slow light, the heel began to build itself out of what her hands had found.

The big bone of the heel in three pieces. The back of it pulled up by the cord behind the ankle. A splinter turned the wrong way under the arch. He built it as she gave it, edge by edge, and it was very nearly right; and there was one place, at the inside, under the ankle bone, where her hands had found an edge and he could not tell which way it went.

"There," he said. "Under the knob. Which way?"

"Down," said Imani. "And in." And then, because it was not clear, because she was tired and the room was slow and the mason was grey on the table outside it: "I'll show you."

She reached back for it. He felt her reach: back into what her fingers had found that afternoon, for the one edge, to give it to him clearer. And he reached with her, to take it, as he had taken the rest.

And went past it.

He did not mean to. He knew afterward exactly how it had happened and could not, then or later, have said how to stop it: she had reached back for the feel of an edge under her fingers, and behind that edge, in the place where she kept what her fingers knew, there was every other edge her fingers had ever learned, all the way back; and he had reached with her, and his reach was longer than hers, and it did not stop where hers stopped.

There was a pier.

Planks, grey with salt, warm in the sun, with gaps between them where the water showed green. A smell of tar and fish and wet rope. Nets hung over a rail to dry, brown, and a man sitting on an upturned tub among them with a net across his knees and a wooden needle in his hand, mending; a big man, very dark, with grey in his short hair and his sleeves pushed up and his forearms corded from forty years of knots, and a small girl in front of him on the planks with a piece of the same net across her own knees, frowning at it.

He could not see the man's face well. The sun was behind him. He could see the hands.

"Don't look at it," said the man.

His voice was deep and easy and amused, a voice for a long afternoon.

"Shut your eyes. You can't see a knot. You can only feel it. Your eyes'll tell you it's right when it's wrong." He reached across and put his big hand over the small one on the net and moved her fingers along the cord. "There. Feel that? That's the one that's slipped. Feel it with the end of your finger. Not the flat. The end."

The girl shut her eyes.

"There," she said.

"There," said the man, and laughed, and took his hand away.

And then it was gone, all of it, the pier and the nets and the green water through the planks and the voice, as if a door had been slammed in his face; and he was in the room again, and the room was shaking.

And then he was not in the room at all. He was on the stool at the foot of the ward table with the mason grey in front of him and the nurse staring at him, and his head had split open from the back of the eyes outward, and there was blood running out of his nose onto his lip and his chin and the front of his coat, a lot of it, fast, and across the table Imani was standing with her hand over her own face and blood coming out between her fingers.

She took her hand away and looked at it. Then she looked at him.

"You were in my head," she said.

"Imani..."

"You were in my head." She did not raise her voice. It was the ward voice, flat and exact, and her hand was red to the wrist. "That wasn't the heel. That was..." She stopped. She looked at the nurse, who had gone white, and at the mason on the table, who was too far under the ether to know anything, and then down at her own red hand. "Get me a cloth."

The nurse got her a cloth. Imani held it to her face with one hand and with the other picked up a pencil off the table and drew the heel on the back of the mason's chart, quickly, in six strokes, every piece of it, the splinter under the arch and the edge under the inside of the ankle, down and in; and put the pencil down.

"That's the heel," she said, through the cloth. "I'll set it in the morning when my head's my own." She did not look at him. "Go away, Emeric."

He went.



The headache lasted until the next night. It was the worst he had had since Marrow, the year before, and it was different from that one; that one had been his own, the bill for a door forced open on purpose. This one he could feel was shared. He could not have said how. He could feel, all the next day, in the long shop and the counting room and on the keep stair, that somewhere else in Kaldo somebody else's head was splitting at exactly the same pitch as his, and he knew whose, and he could do nothing about it.

He went to Armand in the afternoon. He did not want to, and he went.

Armand heard him out by the window with his hands on his stick. He did not interrupt. When Emeric had finished, the heel, the edge, the reach, the pier, the voice, the blood, the old man sat for a while looking at the yard below.

"A read," he said at last.

"I didn't mean to read anything."

"No. That's why it cost you both." Armand turned his stick on the floor. "The room keeps what a person's lived, not only what they've decided to show you. You know that. You learned it on Marrow." He did not soften the name. "A memory given is read cleanly. She hands it to you and walks you through it and it costs neither of you more than an hour's tiredness. One taken costs one of you, or both; you paid for Marrow's, and so did he. And one taken by accident, when neither of you meant it..." He looked at Emeric. "An accident costs both. There's nobody to send the bill to, so it goes to everybody in the room."

"How do you know?"

"I don't. I'm guessing from the three I've seen, and one of them's this one." Armand looked back at the yard. "But I'd guess the same if I were a hundred. It's the shape of the thing." He was quiet. "What did you see?"

Emeric did not answer.

"Good," said Armand. "Don't tell me. It's not yours." He lifted one hand off the stick and looked at it, still from Saturday's three hours. "Tell her."



She came to the counting room after the lamps on the Wednesday, when Ferra had gone down to her father's for supper and the room was empty but for him and the tape running through his fingers, and stood in the door.

She had a bruise under one eye, faint, where the blood had pushed. He supposed he had the same. She looked at him across the room for a while without coming in.

"I set the heel," she said. "This morning. It went as I drew it. He'll walk."

"Good."

"Down and in." She came in, then, and shut the door behind her, and did not sit. She stood by Ferra's table with one hand on it. "I've not been this angry at you in years," she said. "I'd forgotten how it goes."

"I'm sorry."

"I know you're sorry. You've been sorry since the stool. I could feel you being sorry all day yesterday, at the same time as my head. It was very tiresome." Her mouth did not move. "Armand says it was an accident."

"It was."

"I know it was. I was there." She looked at the tape in his hands. "What did you see?"

He told her. Not all of it; there was not much to tell, and he did not dress any of it. A pier. Nets drying on a rail. A man on an upturned tub with a wooden needle. A small girl with her eyes shut and a piece of net across her knees. He did not describe the man. He did not say what the voice was like. He told her what the man had said, word for word, because he had it word for word, the way he had every page he had ever read; he had not been able to lose it if he tried.

"Don't look at it," he said. "Shut your eyes. You can't see a knot. You can only feel it."

Imani stood very still by Ferra's table.

"Feel it with the end of your finger," said Emeric. "Not the flat. The end."

She did not say anything for a long breath. She put out one hand, the right, and laid it flat on Ferra's table, and then turned it, so that only the ends of the fingers touched the wood; and looked at them.

"I'd forgotten his voice," she said.

It was not the ward voice. It was not any voice he had heard from her.

"I had his chair," said Imani. "And his shelf. And his needle, the wooden one, in the drawer under the shelf. I've had them thirteen years. I'd forgotten his voice. I didn't know I had." She was looking at her fingers on the wood. "I've taught forty girls to feel a pulse. I tell them, don't look at it. Shut your eyes. The end of the finger." She took her hand off the table. "I thought I'd made that up."

He did not say anything. There was nothing in him that would not have been less than what she had just said.

She stood a while longer by the table. Then she came round it, and stood in front of his chair, and looked down at him; and put her hand, the right one, on the side of his face, the ends of her fingers just at the edge of the faint bruise under his eye, the way she would have touched a mark on a patient to find out how deep it went.

"Ask next time," she said.

He looked up at her.

"Next time," said Imani. "Ask. And I'll show you. Properly. The pier and the boats and the tub he sat on, and the tar, and whatever else is in there you'd like to see." She took her hand away. "Ask, and I'll give it to you, and it won't cost either of us a nosebleed." Her mouth moved, then, very slightly, at one corner. "And you'll owe me one of yours."

"Which one?"

"I'll choose," said Imani, and went; and at the door, with her hand on the latch and her back to him, she spoke once more.

"He'd have liked you," she said, to the door. "He'd have laughed at you. But he'd have liked you."

She went out. He sat at the end of Ferra's table with the tape gone still in his fingers and the lamp hissing, and after a while he found he had put his own right hand flat on the wood, and turned it, so that only the ends of the fingers touched; and he left it there.
