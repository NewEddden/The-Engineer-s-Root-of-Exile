Chapter 130 - Declared Value

The crown had a form for it. Ferra found it in the second week of October, in one of Wexler's books on procedure that nobody had opened in twenty years, between the form for reporting a wreck on the barony's shore and the form for reporting a death by misadventure in the barony's mines.

A Return of Stone Found.

Every holder in the kingdom was bound to make one, within a season of any find of mana stone on his land above a certain weight, so that the crown could take its share. Ferra read the form out at the table in her room in the keep with the box from Grayrock open beside her. It asked ten questions. Where the stone was found. On whose land. By whom, and when. Of what kind. In what quantity, by the finder's estimate. Whether worked, or lying. Whether the finder meant to work it, and how. And at the bottom, under a ruled line, one more question that was not numbered, in smaller type: whether the finder knew of any prior claim, survey or interest in the stone.

"That one," said Ferra.

Emeric was at the window.

"Every question it asks, we answer," he said. "Truthfully. Where, whose, when, what kind. How much, as near as the survey says it. That we mean to work it, and how." He looked out at the inner ward, at the round tower with its blue roof where the water tank was. "We answer every question the crown asks. And we send the survey. A copy. In Bram's hand, so it's the barony's copy, not Renner's."

"And the last."

"The survey is a prior survey," said Emeric. "We say so. Commissioned privately by a former holder of Grayrock, now dead, and found among his papers by his widow. All true." He did not turn round. "The crown didn't ask who commissioned it. It asked whether there was a prior survey. There was."

Ferra looked at the commission in the bottom of the box, the thick roll of paper with the cracked red seal.

"That's a lawyer's answer," she said.

"It's a true one."

"It's both." She put her finger on the roll. "And this?"

He came back from the window and sat down across the table from her.

"Corbin's mother is Aldane's sister," he said. "Corbin is Aldane's nephew. Corbin sits at the king's right hand at every council in the capital and has friends in every room of the chancery, and the court Keller sits in is the crown's court, and Corbin is going to be the crown." He looked at the roll. "If I send that north today, it goes into the chancery, and somebody in the chancery reads the name on it, and an hour later Corbin's mother has read it, and an hour after that Aldane has. And then the thing in that roll is a thing the crown's court has to decide, and it'll be decided by the court Corbin's friends sit in." He paused. "And Aldane will know I've got it, and that I gave it away."

"And if you don't send it."

"Then Aldane doesn't know whether I've got it," said Emeric. "He knows the box existed. He knows Renner kept things. He nodded to me across a courtyard on the day the hill came to me, so he knows I'm the man who'll find it, if anybody does. And he doesn't know whether I have." He put his hand flat on the table beside the box. "And every day he doesn't know, he has to wonder what I'd do with it. And not do anything to Kaldo that would make me want to."

Ferra looked at him for some time.

"Each of you holds something on the other," she said.

"He holds a duchy and a seat at the king's council and a nephew who'll be king. I hold a roll of paper." Emeric looked at the box. "It's not much. But it's the only thing I've got that he can't price."

Ferra did not answer at once. Then she took the roll out of the box, and looked at it, and did not unroll it. She went to the shelf, where the left-hand box stood, the one that had held Paxton's four lines and the twin year's index and the slip with the name Saban would not say aloud, and opened it with the key on the string round her neck, and put the roll in it, at the bottom, under everything, and closed the lid and locked it.

"Until when?" she said, with her hand still on the lid.

"Until the court isn't within Corbin's reach."

Ferra looked at the box under her hand.

"That could be a long time," she said.

"It could."

She took her hand away and sat down and picked up her pen, the dark one with the gold band, and drew the Return of Stone Found toward her, and began to answer the questions, in her small upright hand, truthfully, one after another.



The return went north on the fourteenth, in the crown's bag, from the post house on the north road, with the gate clerk's slip, in the ordinary way.

The answer came on the third of November. Twenty days.

Hale brought it up the keep stair himself, as he had brought the first one in June, walking very fast with his elbows in. It was a single sheet from the chancery's mining desk, sealed with the black wafer, and it said that the crown acknowledged the barony of Kaldo's return of stone found in its holding of Grayrock, and thanked the holder for his diligence in making it within the season; that the crown would take its share of any stone raised, at one part in five, as the law provided; and that the crown would send an assessor in the spring to view the working and agree the barony's estimate of the quantity, which the crown noted was large, and would in any case wish to discuss.

That was all. A fifth. An assessor in the spring. Wish to discuss.

Ferra wrote in the column headed answered. It was the second line of ink in that column in four years. She did not say anything about it, and neither did he, and Hale, in the doorway, stood very straight and looked at the window.

"Wish to discuss," said Ferra, when Hale had gone. She read it again. "That's not the law. The law says a fifth. It doesn't say discuss."

"No."

"Somebody at the mining desk read the quantity in the survey," said Ferra, "and went pale, and went to see somebody above him." She put the letter in the right-hand box. "There'll be another letter. Before the assessor. Asking for more than a fifth."

"Probably."

"We'll give them a fifth," said Ferra, "and the law." She closed the box. "And Keller, if they want to discuss it in his hall."



Vantic wrote to the House on the Thursday after the boat came home, and on the Thursday after that, and on the Thursday after that, three letters, and none of them was a single sheet folded small.

Emeric did not see them. He knew they were long because the House's courier on the coast road, who came every Thursday for a single sheet and went away again in a quarter of an hour, stood in the gate passage on the third Thursday for most of an hour while Vantic finished, and was heard to say to Osric that he had never in four years carried anything from this factor heavier than a feather and this weighed like a brick.

She came up to Ferra's room on the twelfth of November, at noon, with a letter in her hand, sealed in the House's grey wax with a seal that was not the factor's. It was larger, and older, and had a ship on it.

She did not knock. She stood in the doorway with the letter.

Ferra looked up from her trestle. Emeric looked up from the window.

"The House," said Vantic, "has considered the factor's representations concerning the borrower's security."

Neither of them said anything.

Vantic came into the room. She laid the letter on Ferra's trestle, open. She did not step back from it this time. She stood beside the trestle with her gloved hand flat on the table beside the letter, as if she did not trust it to stay there by itself.

"I told the House what is under Grayrock's hill," she said. "I sent a copy of the survey. I told them the crown had been informed and would take a fifth. I told them that a barony with a body of gravity stone under it is not the barony they lent against two autumns ago, that the security is worth ten times what it was, or twenty, or a hundred, and that nobody can say yet which, because nobody has ever seen a body of gravity stone before and nobody knows what it will fetch when there is more than a pound of it in the market at once." She was quiet. "I told them there was no money yet. Not a copper. Only a hole in a hill with bad air in it, and a survey, and a promise."

"And the House lends against promises," said Ferra slowly.

"The House lends against what a place will be," said Vantic. "It always has. It lent against this place when it was a plague town with a debt, because I wrote to it that the town would be something else." She looked at Ferra. "You were there. You asked me why. I told you."

"You said the House lends against what a place will be," said Ferra. "You said it in the hall. In the second winter." She looked down at the letter. "I wrote it down."

"So did I." Vantic's hand did not move on the table. "I wrote it again. To the chief of the House. On paper with my name on it. That the security had changed beyond any reckoning the covenant was drawn for, and that the cap on the borrower's new works, which was set to protect the House against a borrower spending what he did not have, was now protecting the House against a borrower spending what he did. That the cap was costing the House money. Every season the borrower could not build, the House's security was worth less than it could be." She paused. "I recommended that the House lift it."

The room was very quiet.

"On paper with your name on it," said Emeric.

"On paper I could be faulted for," said Vantic. "If the stone is less than the survey says. If the bad air kills the miners and the working is closed again. If the crown takes more than a fifth. If anything at all goes wrong at that hill before the debt is paid, the House will take out my letter, and read my name on it, and ask its factor why she told it to lift the only protection it had on a bad debt in the south." She looked at the letter on the trestle. "I knew that when I wrote it."

"Then why did you write it?" Ferra said.

Vantic looked at her. Then, briefly, at Emeric, at the window. Then back at Ferra.

"Because it was true," she said. "And because I have watched this barony build for two years with one hand tied, and I wanted to see what it would do with two." She took her hand off the table. "The House agrees. The cap is lifted, from the first of December. The debt stands. The interest stands. The House's right to read every book in this barony stands, and its factor stays, until the principal is repaid." She looked at the letter. "Read it. It says so. In the chief clerk's hand, with the House's seal."

Ferra read it. It took her a long time, because she read it twice and then a third time, the way she read a thing she did not believe.

Then she got up from the trestle, and went to the shelf, and took down the barony's book, the one with the covenant's terms on its first page in Vantic's hand and Ferra's under it. She opened it on the trestle at a page Emeric knew: the cap. A ruled column, headed in Ferra's hand, the share of last year's net revenue the barony might spend on new works this year, and under it, year by year, the figure, and beside each figure what it had bought. The engine. The coke ovens. The furnace. The slip. The kiln. The glass. A column of years that had been, he thought, looking at it upside down, the hardest column in any of her books.

She took up the pen.

She drew a line across the foot of the column. Ruled, straight, the full width, with the edge of the House's letter for a rule. And under it she wrote, in her small upright hand: Lifted, the first of December, by the House, upon the factor's representation. And the date. And then she did a thing he had never seen her do in any book. She drew a second line under the first, close, double, the way a clerk closes an account that is finished and will never be opened again.

She put the pen down and looked at it.

"There," she said.

Her voice was not quite steady. Emeric saw her hear that it was not, and put her hand flat on the page beside the double line, the way Vantic had put her hand on the table.

He came across the room from the window. He did not mean to. He found that he was standing beside the trestle, beside her, looking down at the double line, and that his hand was on her shoulder.

She did not move away from it. She did not look up. She sat with her hand flat on the closed column and his on her shoulder, and he felt her let her breath out, slowly, all of it, under his palm, the breath of two years.

Vantic, on the other side of the trestle, looked at Ferra's hand on the page, and at Emeric's on Ferra's shoulder. She looked at them for exactly as long as it took to read a line. Then she picked up the House's letter from the table, and folded it along its creases, and held it out across the trestle to Ferra.

"For your box," she said.

Ferra took it, without looking up.

Vantic went to the door. At the door she stopped and looked back at the two of them, and something was in her face that Emeric could not read, and then it was gone.

"Congratulations," she said, to Ferra.

She went out. Her steps went away down the passage, even and unhurried, toward the stair and the east tower and her books.

Emeric took his hand off Ferra's shoulder.

She sat for a while longer with her hand on the page. Then she closed the book, and put the House's letter in the right-hand box, and looked at the door Vantic had gone out by.

"She wrote that letter," Ferra said, "on paper with her name on it."

"Yes."

"For us."

"For the House."

"No," said Ferra. She looked at the door a little longer. "Not only for the House." She picked up her pen. "I'll write her a line in the book. A good one. Where she can read it." She opened the book again, to a fresh page. "She reads all of them anyway."



That night, behind the shutters, he did the thing he had been working toward since the stable roof.

He had not tried the jump again. His ankle had told him not to, and so had Imani, and so, without a word, had Ward. But he had thought about it every night, lying on his bed with his foot up on the bolster: that he had seen the top of the wall and the mana had taken him there, and kept going, because he had not seen where it should stop.

So he practised stopping.

He stood in the middle of his room with the furniture pushed back and a candle on the hearth. He saw himself take one step. Only that: one step, the length of an ordinary stride, across the boards, and his foot coming down on the far side of it. And at the end of the step, his foot planted. Still. Done. He saw that as clearly as the step itself.

He let the mana go into his legs and did not tell it anything but that.

He took the step. It was a stride, no more. His foot came down on the far board exactly where he had seen it, and stopped there, and he did not go through the wall.

It had taken no time at all. He had crossed the stride faster than he had ever moved in his life, faster than Ward in the yard, so fast that the candle flame on the hearth lay flat in the wind of his passing and then stood up again, and he had stopped dead at the end of it as if he had walked into a door. His knee ached. His hip ached. The candle steadied.

He did it again. And again. One stride, and stop. Forty times, until his legs shook and his head hurt behind the eyes. And on the fortieth he did it without seeing the stop first, because he had seen it so often that it was simply there, the end of the step, the way the end of a sentence is there before a man has finished saying it.

He lay down on the floor of his room in the dark with his legs burning and the candle out, and laughed, quietly, into his arm, so that the board man upstairs would not hear.
