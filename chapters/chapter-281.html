Chapter 281 - Ornament

They took the golem up to the old quarry on the last Wednesday in November, in the dark, in four carts, under canvas, with the moon down.

It went in pieces, as it had come home in pieces: the eleven of them on their trestles in the gallery under the hill wrapped in canvas and lifted on lift stones into the carts by Bram and Ward and Emeric alone, because nobody else knew what was under the canvas, and driven up the hill track behind the gallery by Ward with no lamp, at a walk, the horses' hooves muffled in sacking. Bram had argued for a week that it could be done in the gallery. Emeric had said no for a week. A stone that woke might do something; and if it did something, it should do it on the top of a hill with nobody within a mile, and not under one, with the proving gallery's charges in their store forty yards along the working.

The old quarry was a scoop bitten out of the hill's shoulder above the gallery, a hundred years ago or more: a flat floor of broken stone the size of the market square, with a face of grey rock thirty feet high on three sides of it and the fourth side open to the night and the long fall of the hill below, and the town's lights far down and away beyond. Nobody came up here. The stone had been poor, and the quarry had been worked out and left before anybody living was born, and the floor had gone to thin grass and thistles and spoil heaps, and on the open side there was a long low bank of spoil where the old quarrymen had tipped their waste over the edge.

They put it together in the middle of the floor by the light of Bram's lamp, an oil lamp, shuttered to a slit, because Ward would not have a stone lamp near a thing that might wake. It took them two hours. The feet first, on a flat place Bram had chosen and swept; then the shins, pinned to the feet at the ankles with the old makers' own pins, which slid home in their sockets with a soft sound like a drawer closing; then the thighs at the knees, the hips, the waist. The chest last but one, lifted by all three of them on lift stones and set on the hips, and pinned. The arms. The head.

And then it stood there, in the middle of the old quarry, in the dark: a head taller than Emeric and nearly twice as broad, grey, faceless, with its arms at its sides and its hands open, as it had stood at the door in the swept city under the Isle for a thousand years; except that its left shoulder, where Bram had pinned it, was a hair low, and Bram could not leave it alone.

"That pin's not home," he said, for the third time, with his lamp up at it.

"It's home," said Ward.

"It's a hair out."

"It's been a hair out for a thousand years," said Ward. "Leave it."

Bram left it, with an expression.

Ward went off across the quarry floor to the place they had agreed, forty paces, by the north face, where he could see the whole floor and the golem in the middle of it, with the dead-zone stone in his hand. It was the size of a hen's egg, a cheap earth stone that Orrin had charged on purpose to the edge, so that one squeeze of Ward's hand round it, hard, with mana behind it, would push it over the edge and burst it; and for fifty yards round, every stone would stop. Including the golem's. That was the plan. That was the whole of the plan, if anything went wrong: Ward squeezes, and the thing stops dead, and they go home.

Bram stayed at the golem's hip with his lamp. He wanted to see the hip joint move. He had wanted to see it move since July. He had drawn it eleven times and could not draw how it moved, only how it was made, and he said that was like knowing a man's name and not his voice.

Emeric unpinned the chest's back plate, and set it on the ground, and looked at the glass port.

It was small. Smaller than Kaldo's ports, a tube of old clouded glass the length of his hand let into the back of the dry tank, with a stopper of the same grey stone as the shell. He had the canister in a padded case at his feet: one of Bram's, double-cased glass in its ring between enamelled caps, the blue of the Band's mana showing faintly through it in the dark, a pint and a half. Enough to fill the tank and wake every joint. Not enough to run it long.

He took the stopper out of the port. He put the canister's nozzle to it, and broke the inner seal, and the liquid went in, pale blue, quick, catching the slit of Bram's lamp as it ran down the old glass into the dark of the tank; and as it went in he felt it go. Out of the tank, along the rails, into the frame. Into every joint. The way water goes into dry ground.

The joints came up.

He felt them with his sense, one after another, from the chest outward, like lamps being lit down a long street: the shoulders, the elbows, the wrists; the hips, the knees, the ankles; the neck. Each joint's shaped stones taking the charge, and holding it, quietly, waiting. The rods at the elbows. The plate stacks at the shoulders. The coils at the hips. Charged, every one, for the first time in a thousand years.

The golem did not move.

"Anything?" said Bram, very quietly, at its hip.

"It's awake," said Emeric. "The body. Not the..." He looked for the word. "Not the will. The stone's still asleep. It's got its joints, and nobody's telling them anything."

He put the stopper back in the port. Then he went round to the front of it, where the chest's hatch was, the small square plate over the seat that came away on a spring catch, and opened it; and there in the dark of the chest, in its seat, was the stone. Grey, unlit, the size of a long small egg. Control stones did not glow.

He put his hand through the hatch, and laid it on the stone, and went in.



He knew where he was going. He had walked it for three months. He went in past the register with its dense strange notes, past the blocks for the joints, to the routines: stand, step, turn, brace, strike. He went to step. It was the one he knew best; he had read it forty times. A loop, round and back: left hip, right knee, left ankle, and over again, each joint told how far and how fast, the stride written out in the old makers' marks as plainly as a recipe.

He was going to make the stride shorter. That was all. One number in the loop. The length of the step. Shorter by a hand, so that if it worked, if the golem stood up and walked, Bram would see it walk with a short step, and know that it was walking the way Emeric had written it and not the way it had been written a thousand years ago; and they would know, both of them, that the old makers' hand could be written in.

He found the mark. It sat in the loop where he knew it would sit. He took hold of it, the way you take hold of a letter in your own stone to cut it again; and began to cut.

And the stone woke up.

Not the way a stone wakes when you go into it. All at once, all over, the whole library in the egg going from dark to lit in one instant, every line of it, every knot; and three things in it fired at once, together, crossing each other like three men shouting in a doorway.

One was his own cut, half made, the step's number half changed, neither old nor new.

One was the golem's old orders: a door, and a stair, and whoever came up the stair, and what to do about them; a thousand years old, and still there, and waking with everything else.

And one was the ornament.

The little knots. The flourishes. The dozen lines that appeared once each and nowhere else and did nothing, that he had looked at for two hours and called scrollwork and stepped over every night for a month. They were lit. They were the brightest things in the stone. And they had been waiting, all this time, every one of them, for exactly one thing: a hand that was not the maker's, cutting into the routines.

A guard. A lock on the door, written in the one place nobody who did not already know the language would think to look: in the lines that did not seem to do anything.

He understood it in the instant before he was thrown out. He had time to understand it, and to feel his own face go cold with it, and that was all.

The guard did what a guard does. It turned on the hand on its stone.



The hatch snapped shut on his wrist.

It had a spring. He had seen the spring and thought it was a catch. It slammed the plate shut on his forearm with the force of a sprung trap, and threw his hand off the stone and out of the hatch, and his whole arm went numb to the shoulder; and in the same instant, every joint in the golem's frame lit violet.

All of them. At once. The shoulders, the elbows, the knees, every seam where a limb met a limb, glowing violet in the dark of the quarry the way a gravity stone glows when it works, and the light of them threw the golem's shape up on the quarry's grey face behind it, huge.

And it moved.

He saw the ghosts come, as he saw them in the yard: the paths a body might take in the next breath, drawn faint across his sight. And they were wrong. They were all sharp. Every one of them. A man who has decided has one sharp ghost and the rest faint; this had a dozen, and all of them were sharp, all of them decided, going in every direction at once, because it had decided everything, all its orders at once, the door and the stair and the guard and his half-cut step, and was going to do all of them.

It swung at him.

It was a wild swing. The right arm came round from its side in a great flat arc, fast, faster than anything that size should move, and he was already out of its way, on Light Step, light in the air and heavy at the plant, sliding back across the quarry floor in one long low stride; and the arm went through the place where he had been and on, and on, because nothing in the golem had told it to stop.

And came round behind it, on the back-swing, at Bram's head.

Bram was at its hip. Bram had been watching the hip joint move, as he had wanted to for five months, with his lamp up, bent forward, his face a foot from the joint; and the back-swing came round behind the golem's body, the great grey forearm, low and level, at the height of a man's head, at his.

Emeric was there.

He did not know how. He found out afterward, from Ward, that he had crossed eleven yards of quarry floor in the time it took the arm to come round; that he had gone off his back foot on the Rail, the earth pressed up under his heel like a starting block, and gone across the floor flat and fast and light and come down heavy between the arm and Bram's head, and planted. He knew only that he was there, and that he had his own forearms up across his face, and that he had pulled earth up his forearms as he came, the way Ward had shown him in October, a skin of stone from the wrist to the elbow on each arm, pressed hard and held, and over that the shield, a skin of his own mana over the whole of him; and the golem's forearm hit him.

A shield stops damage. It does not stop momentum.

The earth on his arms took the blow and cracked across, both braces at once, like plates. The shield held. And the whole weight of the blow went through the braces and the shield and him and into the ground through his planted foot, and the ground was not enough; his heel cut a furrow in the quarry floor a yard long, and he was driven back into Bram, and Bram, behind him, went down.

Not from the golem. From him. He felt Bram go: the big man behind him knocked off his feet by Emeric's back and thrown sideways, and his lamp going out of his hand and over and over through the air in a long arc, and smashing on the stone, and the oil going up in a short bright sheet of flame and out; and Bram going down on his shoulder on the broken stone of the quarry floor with a sound Emeric would hear for a long time afterward. And then lying still.

Emeric shoved.

He did it with everything: the Plant, both feet, heavy, so that the quarry floor cracked under his boots; and his arms, with the cracked braces still on them; and the shield; and the whole of the strength in him that had been in him since April. He shoved the golem's forearm off him and the golem with it, and it went back. A step. Two. Its feet scraped on the stone. It went back off him and off Bram, and stood.

"Bram," he said.

Bram did not answer.

The golem came on again.



He tried everything he had. He found out afterward that it took about a minute. It felt like an hour.

He put heat into it, a great deal, as much as he could pour, into the chest, into the shoulder; and the stone drank it. A ton and more of stone: it soaked up the heat the way the sea soaks up a lamp's light, and went on coming, and was barely warm. He put cold into it, and the stone drank that too. He put lightning into it from his hand, the white crack of it jumping the yard between them; and it ran over the shell like water over a roof and down its legs into the ground, and the golem did not so much as stagger.

He reached for the drain. A man or a stone, by touch: he could empty either. But every stone in it was sealed inside the shell, and the shell was stone that held nothing, and there was nothing he could get his hand on that would give.

He looked into it with the hollow view, the way Imani looked into a body, and found nobody inside. He reached for a read, and there was nothing to read. It was not a man. There was nobody home. There was a lock on a door, and three orders shouting, and a body.

It swung again, and again, and again, and he went where the ghosts weren't, and there were too many ghosts.

So he hit it.

He went off the Rail, the earth pressed up under his back foot, and across the floor at it, low and flat and light in the air, lightened as he flew so that the world's pull did not drag him down into the ground before he got there; and at the instant before he hit, he let the lightness go. A stone took weight. It did not take mass. All of him arrived, everything he weighed and everything he had built in eleven years in Ward's yard and everything the pod had given him, behind one fist with a skin of shield over it, into the golem's left shoulder, into the joint that Bram had said was a hair out.

The shoulder broke.

He heard it. A crack like a tree going in a frost, and the shoulder came apart at its joint, the pin Bram had not been able to leave alone shearing in its socket; and the left arm dropped and hung, swinging, by its rails, useless.

The golem did not notice. It turned on him with its right arm, the same as before, and kept coming.

"Ward!" he shouted.



Ward squeezed.

Emeric felt it go. Forty paces off by the north face, the cheap earth stone in Ward's fist went over the edge and burst, and the mana went out of the world.

All of it. For fifty yards round. Like a wind that blew no dust; like a door slammed on a room. His shield went, his lightness, the remains of the braces on his arms, which fell off him in pieces. His sense went: the whole quarry and the hill and the town below, gone, in one breath, so that he stood in the dark as blind as any man, as blind as he had been on the other side of everything. His reinforcement stayed. The inward thing, the knight's thing, the strength in his own arms and legs, stayed; that never went.

And the golem stopped.

Mid-stride. Its right arm half raised. Its violet joints went dark, all at once, every one, and it stood there on the quarry floor in the starlight with one foot forward and one arm hanging and one arm half up, a statue, as it had been for a thousand years; and Emeric stood in front of it with his chest heaving in the sudden dark and thought, That's it, that's it, it's done.

One heartbeat.

Two.

And there was a sound in its chest. A small sound. A crack: not stone. Glass.

And out of its ribs, along both sides of its chest, from slits he had seen in July and taken for seams in the stone, there came a breath.

Blue. Pale and cold, in the starlight. A mist, pouring out of the slits along its ribs, out and up, round its body, round its head, a cloud of it; and he knew it. He knew it at once. He had built it. A bulb of liquid mana behind a seal, and a sprung spike held back by a stone, and when the stone stopped, the spike went home and broke the bulb, and the liquid flashed to mist and filled the dead zone round it. His fire sprinkler. His inducer. The thing he had drawn at his table in the ninth year and been proud of, that sat at the head of every ship Kaldo had.

The old makers had built it a thousand years before him.

They had built against dead zones. Which meant somebody had used dead zones on them.

And the mana that was pouring out of its ribs, filling the cloud round it, waking its joints again inside the cloud one after another, violet, violet, violet: that was the mana he had poured into its port an hour ago. Out of Bram's canister. He had filled the tank that beat his own kill switch.

The golem moved.



Inside the cloud he could cast again.

He felt it come back to him as he stepped into the mist: the mana, all round him, thick, cold, the Band's mana, his. His shield came back. His lightness. But the cloud was all mana, so thick that his sense, when he opened it, met it like a hand meeting a wall of wool. It was the tank's fog. It was what Imani saw when she put Ward in the water: a cloud the shape of a man. He could feel the golem in front of him in the cloud, as a great dense shape in a dense place, and nothing else: not its edges, not its seams, not where its chest plate met its ribs. And the night was black. The moon was down. Bram's lamp was a smear of burnt oil on the stone. The stars did not reach through the mist.

He could cast. He could not see.

And the golem swung at the place where he was, and he went out of the way of a ghost that was only half a ghost now in the fog, and the stone arm went past his face close enough that he felt its wind; and he knew that he had a breath or two before it was close enough not to miss, and that he could not hit what he could not see, and that the chest plate over the seat was a hand's width wide, and that if he missed it he would break his own arm on the shell and it would not care.

And a light came on.



White. A single beam, narrow, hard, from the quarry's open side, from the long low bank of spoil at the edge where the old quarrymen had tipped their waste; across the quarry floor and through the blue cloud like a rod, and onto the golem's chest.

Electric. He knew it by its colour before he knew anything else: not a stone's light, not a flame. A battery lamp. One of the works' small hand-lamps, a reflector and a bulb and a cell in a tin case, the kind that went in every ship's hull because it did not care about dead zones or stones or anything at all except whether its battery was charged.

It lit the golem's chest like a stage. The grey plate over the seat. The seam round the plate's edge, a hairline, black in the white. The violet of the joints round it, pale in the beam.

The golem turned toward the light.

It turned all at once, the whole of it, its body swinging round on its hips toward the beam, toward whoever was holding the thing that had lit it up; and it went for it. Away from Emeric. Across the quarry floor toward the spoil bank, with its one good arm coming up and its broken one swinging, and its chest full in the beam, the seam lit, coming.

And the light did not move.

It did not swing, or run, or go out. It stayed exactly where it was, steady, on the golem's chest, as the golem came at it; and Emeric, behind the golem, running, with the shield up and the cloud round him, had one instant of a thought so cold it was not even fear, which was that whoever was holding it was going to stand there and hold it, and the golem's arm was going to come down on them.

The golem reached the spoil bank and swung.

Its arm came down on the place the light came from. On a great block of broken stone at the bank's top, as big as a man's chest; and broke it. Smashed it, in one blow, in a burst of chips and grit that went up white in the beam; and the beam went over, and spun, and fell, and lay in the spoil at the block's foot, still lit, pointing up, crooked, at the sky.

And there was nobody behind the block.

There had never been anybody behind the block. The lamp had been wedged into the rocks on top of it, alone, and pointed, and left; and whoever had wedged it there had gone, down the far side of the spoil bank, in the dark, the instant they switched it on.

The golem stood at the bank's top over the broken block with its arm still down, and its back to Emeric, and its chest turned toward the beam lying in the spoil, still lit by it from below, the seam black in the white.

He came off the Rail behind it. He went up the bank on one stride, light, and came down on the stone beside it heavy, and planted, and went round it with his shoulder low, inside its broken arm, to its front, into the beam; and put his right fist, with all of him behind it, into the plate over the seat, on the seam.

The plate cracked across.

He got his fingers into the crack. It cut them; he did not feel it. He tore the plate out, half of it, and threw it, and got his hand into the chest, into the seat, round the stone, grey and unlit and the size of a long small egg, and pulled.

The stone came out in his hand.

And the golem stopped.

Every joint went dark, at once, together, the violet going out of them all down its body like a row of lamps blown out. It stood there over him at the top of the spoil bank, mid-stride, for one heartbeat; and then, because nothing at all was holding it up any more, because only a control stone decides, and nothing in it was deciding anything now, it began to fall.

It went backward, away from him, slowly, the way a tree goes, over the edge of the spoil bank, down the far side; and hit the slope, and broke, at the hips and the knees and the neck, the way it had been made to come apart; and its pieces went rolling and sliding down the long bank of old quarrymen's waste in the dark, a long way, crashing, until they stopped.

The blue cloud thinned round him, and went into the ground, and the dead zone, its mana gone, began very slowly to fill again from the hill.

He stood at the top of the bank with the stone in his bleeding hand and the lamp lying in the spoil at his feet, still lit, still pointing at the sky.

"Who's there," he said.



"Me," said Wren.

She came up the far side of the spoil bank on her hands and knees, out of the dark, out of the long slope where the golem's pieces had gone rolling a moment before; grey to the eyes with stone dust, with her hair full of grit and a cut on her forehead bleeding into one eyebrow and her coat torn at the shoulder; and she stood up at the top of the bank beside him, and picked up the lamp, and switched it off.

The dark came back.

"Bram left it on the bench," said Wren. "In the Motion Shop. The battery one. I saw it when I was shutting up, and I knew you'd gone up the hill with him, because Tess said, and I thought, if they're going to wake it with a stone lamp and something goes wrong..." She stopped. "Ward said nobody was to come. I know Ward said. I wasn't going to come. I was going to leave it at the gallery gate. I got to the gallery gate and I heard it." She was quite steady. "So I came up the back of the spoil. Where nobody could see me. And I lay down at the top behind the big rock. And I watched." She looked at the lamp in her hand. "And when Ward did the thing, and all the lamps went, and the blue came out of it, I could see you couldn't see. You kept turning your head. Like you were listening. And I thought, he needs a light on it, and the light'll bring it." She looked down the bank where the pieces had gone. "So I put it in the rocks pointing, and switched it on, and went down the back." She was quiet. "It broke the rock I was behind."

He looked at her.

"It broke the rock you'd been behind," he said.

"Yes," said Wren. "I'd been behind it. Then I wasn't." She wiped the blood out of her eyebrow with the back of her wrist, and looked at it. "A bit of it hit me going down. It's only a cut."

He did not say anything at all. He found that he could not. He stood at the top of the spoil bank in the dark with the stone in one hand and his other hand on Wren's shoulder, gripping it, and could not let go, and could not speak; and she let him hold it, and stood there, and after a while said, quite kindly, "Is Bram all right?"



Bram was not all right. He was alive.

He lay on the quarry floor where he had fallen, on his left side, on the broken stone, with his eyes open, breathing; and when Emeric knelt beside him with his sense back, he found the collarbone first, broken clean across, and three ribs under it cracked, and then the head: a cut above the ear where he had come down on the stone, bleeding the way scalps bleed, a great deal, and under it, in the bone, a line. Not through. Not quite. A crack in the skull's outer plate like a crack in a cup's glaze.

"Don't move," said Emeric.

"Wasn't going to." Bram's voice was thick and slow. "The pin. Did the pin go?"

"The pin went."

"Told you it was out." He shut his eyes. "Told you."

Ward had come across the floor at a run, in the dark, from the north face. He knelt on Bram's other side without a word and put his hand flat on Bram's chest, as a soldier does, to feel it go up and down; and it went up and down; and Ward stayed there with his hand on it.

Then he looked up, over Bram, at the top of the spoil bank, where a girl of fifteen was standing in the starlight grey with dust holding a battery lamp, switched off.

Ward looked at her for a long time.

"Who taught you that?" he said.

Wren looked back at him.

"Nobody," she said. "Moths go to the lamp." She held it up, a little. "Not to whoever put it there."

Ward did not say anything.

He looked at her a while longer, across Bram, in the dark. Then he looked down at his own hand on Bram's chest; and then at Emeric; and something went across his face that Emeric did not know how to read, and was gone.

"Run for Imani," said Ward. "You know the way down in the dark. Faster than any of us." He did not raise his voice. "Tell her the smith's down. Collarbone, ribs, his head. Tell her to bring the stretcher and four men, and come to the gallery gate, and we'll carry him down to it. Tell her nobody else." He paused. "Then go home and wash your face. Your father'll be looking for you."

"Yes, sir," said Wren; and went, down the side of the spoil bank and out of the quarry's mouth and away down the dark hill track toward the lights, running, with the lamp in her hand, not lit; and Emeric, kneeling by Bram, felt her note go down the hill in his sense, small and bright and very fast, all the way to the hospital's door.



Rylan came up the hill track at a run at midnight, with a lantern, in his shirt, and found them carrying Bram down on the stretcher with Imani walking beside it holding Bram's head still between her two hands; and he stood aside on the track to let them pass, and then fell in behind, and said nothing all the way down to the gallery gate.

At the gate he caught Emeric's arm.

"She's at home," he said. "Nessa's washing her. She came in the back door at eleven with blood on her face and stone in her hair and said Bram was hurt and she'd run for Imani, and then she said she was going to bed." His hand was very tight on Emeric's arm. "She wouldn't tell her mother anything else. She said, ask Ward." He looked at Emeric in the lantern light. "I'm asking you."

Emeric told him.

He told it plainly, in the dark at the gallery gate, while they carried Bram on ahead toward the hospital: the lamp on the bench, the gallery gate, the noise, the spoil bank, the dead zone, the cloud, the light, the rock. The rock she had been behind, and wasn't.

Rylan listened to all of it without moving.

When it was finished he let go of Emeric's arm, and stood there at the gate with his lantern, and looked up the hill track at the dark where the quarry was; and his face, in the lantern's light, was white with a rage that Emeric had never seen on it, not in the boat's hold, not on the Isle, not anywhere. It stayed there for a long time. Emeric watched it stay.

And then it went. Not all at once. It went out of his face slowly, the way the blue cloud had gone into the ground, and left behind it something else, that Emeric did not have a word for, that made Rylan look, for a moment, very much older and very much younger at once.

"She put it in the rocks," said Rylan. "And moved."

"Yes."

"Before it turned."

"The instant she switched it on."

Rylan was quiet.

"I told her that," he said. "When she was nine. Hide and seek in the yard. She was always caught because she held the lantern. I said, Wren, the lantern finds you. Put it somewhere else and go." He looked at the lantern in his own hand. "Six years ago. In the yard. A game." He laughed, once, a short strange sound. "She said nobody taught her."

"She doesn't remember," said Emeric.

"No." Rylan looked up the hill again. "She remembered." He turned to go after the stretcher. "I'm going to shout at her in the morning," he said, over his shoulder. "For an hour. Nessa'll help. And then I'm going to go to the Motion Shop and ask Tess what she's teaching her." He was gone down the track toward the town, with his lantern, quickly; and halfway down, Emeric saw the lantern stop, and stay stopped for a while, and then go on.



The six sat at the table in the wine store at two in the morning, five of them, with Bram's chair empty and Bram in the hospital asleep under Imani's draught with his collarbone set and his head bound; and Imani came in last, at half past two, and sat down in her place, and put her hands flat on the table, and said, "He'll live. He'll keep the arm. The skull's cracked, not broken. A month in bed and he'll be shouting at his shop by the new year." She did not look at anybody. "He asked for the drawings. I said no."

Nobody spoke for a while.

Then Emeric put the stone on the table.

It lay in the lamplight in the middle of the table, grey, unlit, a long small egg, and they all looked at it.

"It was in the lines I didn't understand," he said.

He told them. The ornament. The little knots that did nothing, that appeared once and nowhere else, that he had set aside every night for a month as a maker's flourish; and what they had been. A guard. A lock, written into the one part of the stone that a stranger reading for what he understood would never read, because a stranger would read it as decoration and step over it. Waiting for a hand that was not the maker's to cut into the routines.

"I read every line I understood," he said. "I didn't read the lines I didn't. I thought that was the same as reading it." He looked at the stone. "It isn't. A guard somebody else wrote isn't in what you understand. It's in what you don't. You have to read every line before you change one. Every one. Especially the ones that look like they don't do anything."

"And the cloud," said Ward.

"The cloud." Emeric looked at him. "They built against dead zones. A thousand years ago. Somebody had been using dead zones on them, and they'd answered it, and put an inducer in every one of those figures at every door." He was quiet. "My kill switch was a dead zone. Theirs had already been answered, before anybody in this world was born. And I filled the tank that answered it myself, an hour before, out of Bram's canister." He looked at his hand on the table; it was bandaged where the plate had cut it. "A dead zone isn't a kill switch. Not for anything somebody clever built. Not for anything we build either."

"Then what is?" said Saban.

"Pulling the stone. The decider. Only a control stone decides; take it out and nothing decides, and it falls." He looked at the stone again. "So everything we ever build that walks, falls safe. Not like that. Sprung brakes on every joint that lock when the stone stops, so it stands where it is and doesn't fall on anybody. And a guard in every stone, like theirs. But ours fails dead." He looked round the table. "Theirs failed hostile. It saw a stranger's hand and it attacked. Ours will see a stranger's hand and lock, and lie down, and do nothing at all, ever again, until one of us comes. In my hand. Or yours, after me."

"And that one?" said Armand, looking at the stone on the table.

"I keep it." Emeric did not touch it. "I never write in it again. I read it. That's all. It's a book." He was quiet. "The best book I've got. I'll be reading it for years. But I'll never put a pen to it again."

Armand looked at the stone a while longer.

"It was their whole body," he said. "In stone. Muscle and all. Every joint." He turned his cup. "When the stones stopped, it stopped. When its own cloud ran out, it would have stopped again. They had nothing under it." He looked up. "No legs under the exoskeleton, as you'd say."

"No," said Emeric. "Nothing. Every muscle in it was a stone." He thought about Bram's bundle of oil and hair shaking Bram's hand in bay three, and Abel's cup. "That's the lesson. That's the whole of it. They leaned on the one thing that a dead zone switches off, and when somebody learned to switch it off, they built a way to switch it back on; and that was all they had. Ours have to stand up without it."

"Bram's fibers," said Imani.

"Bram's fibers."

Ferra had not said anything all night.

She sat in her place with her book closed in front of her and her hands flat on its cover, and had not opened it, not once, not to write a single line; and when Emeric looked at her she was looking at the empty chair beside her, Bram's, and her face was perfectly still, the way it went when she was adding a very long column.

"Ferra," said Imani, gently.

"I'm going up," said Ferra, and stood, and went out of the wine store and up the stair, and did not look back.



Imani did not go after her. Imani went back to the hospital, to sit with Bram until he slept properly, which she said he would not do for anybody else, because he would keep asking about the pin.

Emeric went up.

Ferra's room was warm. The floor was warm, under his boots, as it had been for ten nights now, from the pipe her father had laid on his knees. She was not in bed. She was sitting on the floor in front of the cold hearth with her back against the bed's foot and her knees drawn up, in her day dress still, with her book in her lap, closed, and her hands flat on it; and when he came in she did not look up.

He sat down beside her on the warm floor, and did not say anything.

She sat there a long time.

"He wanted to see the hip move," she said at last.

Her voice was quite steady.

"He told me at supper. On Sunday. That he'd drawn it eleven times and couldn't draw how it moved." She was looking at the hearth. "He said it was like knowing a man's name and not his voice." She turned her book over in her lap, and over again. "And he was bent over with his lamp, a foot from it, to see it move. And it moved."

"I know."

"You were there. Wren said. You got between." She turned the book over again. "You knocked him down. That's why he's alive. You knocked him off his feet and he broke his collarbone on the stone and that's why he's got a head, because if you hadn't he'd..." She stopped.

And then she was not steady any more.

It went all at once. The last time had been years ago, Emeric thought afterward; in the dark, after the dynamo house, after Tess. He had not seen it since. Ferra did not come apart. Ferra counted, and wrote, and turned her pen, and kept every column of the four baronies in her head at once; and he had seen her face go white, and go still, and go scarlet, and he had not seen it do this for years, and he had forgotten what it looked like.

She put her face down on her knees, on the book, and her shoulders shook, and she made no sound at all.

He put his arms round her. She let him. She turned into him, all at once, the way she did everything that mattered, and put her face against his neck and held on to his shirt with both fists, hard, the knuckles white, and shook, silently, for a long time; and he held her on the warm floor and did not say anything, because there was nothing to say that was not a lie or a sum, and she did not want either.

"He's my father," she said into his neck, at last, very low.

"I know."

"He's all I've got. Of then. Of before you. Of my mother." Her fists did not let go of his shirt. "He's the only one who remembers what she sounded like."

"I know."

"And Tess. She's mine too, now, she's my sister, she's been my sister since she was twelve and I never said so and I should have said so." She was crying properly now, with sound, the first sound. "And I sat at that table tonight and couldn't open my book. I couldn't write. I sat there and I couldn't write one line. That's never happened. Not once. Not when the dock burned. Not when Tess."

"Ferra."

"I need..." She stopped. She lifted her face off his neck and looked at him, close, in the dark, with her face wet and her grey-green eyes enormous. "I need you. Now. I need..." She did not finish it. She did not have to. She was asking him for something she had never in her life asked for; not to be held, not to be told it was all right. To be made to stop counting.

"Are you sure?" he said.

"Yes." She put her hand flat on his chest, where Imani always put hers, and held it there, and did not count. "Gently. Please. I don't want..." She swallowed. "Gently."

He was gentle.

He had never been so gentle with anybody in either of his lives. He did not hurry anything, not one button; he undid her day dress himself, every button, slowly, with his bandaged hand clumsy and his good one steady, while she sat on the warm floor with her eyes shut and let him, and her breath slowed under his fingers by degrees, as if every button were a figure being taken off a column she had carried too long. He did not take her to the bed. She did not want the bed. He laid her down on the warm oak floor her father had heated for her, on her own quilts, where she had lain with him ten nights ago laughing; and lay down with her, and held her, a long time, before anything else, with her face in his neck and his hand moving slowly up and down her back, until she stopped shaking.

And then it was very slow, and very quiet, and very short. There was nothing in it of the long night ten days before; no laughing, no hurry, no game. It was only the two of them on the warm floor in the dark, close, his weight on his forearms and his locs fallen round her face so that she was inside them, in the dark, where nobody could see her and nothing could reach her; and her hands in his hair, holding on; and her eyes open, looking at him, not counting, not anything, only there. He watched her the whole time. He watched the column go out of her face, line by line, until there was nothing left in it but him.

When it was over she did not let go of him. She lay under him on the warm floor with her arms round his neck and her face turned into his shoulder, and breathed, slowly, for a long time.

"Thank you," she said, very quietly.

He did not answer. He did not think it needed one.

The door opened at four.

It was Imani. She came in quietly, with her shoes in her hand and her scrubs on and her hair coming down, from the hospital; and stood in the door a moment, in the faint light from the stair, and looked at the two of them on the floor under the quilts. Ferra lifted her head off his shoulder and looked back at her, with her face still wet.

"He's asleep," said Imani. "Properly. I left Bettany with him." She put her shoes down by the door, beside Emeric's boots. "He asked about the pin again before he went. I told him it went. He said good."

She came across the warm floor and lay down on Ferra's other side, under the quilts, without a word, with her scrubs still on; and reached across Ferra, and found Ferra's hand where it lay on Emeric's chest, and took it, and held it, and did not let go.

Ferra held on.

They lay like that, the three of them, on the warm floor in the dark, with Ferra in the middle holding Imani's hand on his chest, until the window went grey; and Ferra slept first, as she always did, between one breath and the next, with her hand still in Imani's. And Imani slept next, with her forehead against Ferra's hair.

Emeric did not sleep.

He lay on his back on the warm oak, with the two of them against him, and looked at the ceiling, and thought about a guard written in the lines that looked like nothing; and a lamp wedged in the rocks; and a girl going down the back of a spoil bank in the dark a breath before a stone arm broke the rock she had been behind. And about Bram, with his lamp up, a foot from the joint, wanting to see it move.

Somewhere below the floor, very faint, the pipes ticked, as the steam came up the castle hill from the river, and kept them warm.
