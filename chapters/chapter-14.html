Chapter 14 - Four in Twenty

The chandler's book was a fat ledger with a cracked spine, kept on a shelf between the tallow and the rope, and the chandler let Emeric read it standing up in the shop while three people came in and bought candles around him.

The third page held the Marrow. Sixteen parts, ruled in a hand that had been dead sixty years, with every transfer since written underneath in whatever ink had been to hand. Bram's two parts, bought nine years ago from a man named Eddick. Widow Corbell's one and a half, inherited, with the half noted twice because somebody had argued about it. A line ruled through a part sold out of the parish and a note beside it saying refused by the rest, returned.

Emeric read the whole page twice and then read the four pages after it, which were three other boats and a fishing weir, and by the end of it he was standing in a candle shop feeling something close to embarrassment.

It was all there. Fractional ownership. Inheritance. Restricted transfer. A registry kept by a neutral party. The whole apparatus, worn smooth by a century of use, sitting on a shelf next to the rope, in a town he had privately been thinking of for four months as a place that needed things explained to it.

"How much to copy the page?" he asked.

The chandler looked at him for a second.

"You're the prince."

"I'm aware."

"Then it's nothing, and if you're asking me because you want it to cost something, it's a penny."

"It's a penny," Emeric said, and paid it, and the chandler wrote that down too.

He and Ferra spent five days on the terms and threw away three drafts.

The first draft was clean and he was proud of it for most of an afternoon. The harvest cut into twenty parts. Four parts to the barony. Sixteen parts to the field, allotted among the holders in proportion to the acreage each man brought in.

Ferra read it once and put her finger on the allotment clause.

"Halloway gets a quarter of everything the plain grows," she said. "For the rest of his life, and then his son does."

"He's bringing a quarter of the acreage."

"He's bringing a quarter of the acreage and four hands, one of whom is his son and one of whom is sixty." She turned the sheet round so it faced him, which she had started doing recently and which he had decided to be pleased about. "Maren brings eleven acres. She also brings herself and four grown sons who work like oxen because there's been nothing else for them to do since the levy. Under this she gets a fortieth and Halloway's sixty-year-old cousin gets more of the harvest than her four sons put together."

Emeric looked at his clean draft for a while.

"It pays the men who had land," he said.

"It pays the men who had land."

"Which is the exact thing Dunnel accused me of doing, and I wrote it down four days later without noticing." He pulled the sheet back and struck the clause through. "Say it to me plainly so I can't slide off it."

"You wanted them working harder. You've written a paper that pays a man for what he owned in the spring." Ferra shrugged slightly. "I'd work exactly as hard as I had to and not one hour more, and I'd be right to."

So the fourth draft had two kinds of part in it, and it was uglier, and it was correct.

Eight of the sixteen were ground parts. Allotted once, against everything a man brought into the field and could not take back out of it: acres, hedges grubbed, oxen, plows, the well on Corbell's corner. Ground parts were his, and his children's, and sellable, and no man could be made to sell one.

The other eight were work parts, recut every year against days worked in the season, tallied daily, posted where anyone could see the tally. A man who worked the whole year got the same weight of work part whether he had come in with four hundred acres or with a spade, and a man who sat in his house and lived off his ground parts watched his neighbors eat better than he did.

"Maren's four sons," Ferra said, running the arithmetic, "come out at a bit better than a ninth of the harvest between them. Under yours it was a fortieth."

"And Halloway?"

"Still the largest holder in the field by a distance, because he is. Just not a landlord about it." She made a note. "He'll count it, you know. That evening, on his own table. And he'll find that he's better off than he was this year, because a quarter of two thousand tired acres is less grain than a sixth of four thousand watered ones."

"Is he?"

"By about a third, on your own yield numbers, in the fourth year." She looked up. "You should let him find that himself. If you say it he'll assume you cooked it."

The four parts to the barony took the longest, because he kept trying to make them smaller and Ferra kept refusing to let him.

What they bought went on the paper as a list, because a fifth with a list next to it is a price and a fifth with nothing next to it is rent. The ring and everything that ever came after it. Seed, graded and bought. Plows and hand tools out of Bram's forge. The grain advance that fed forty diggers for a season in which those forty men grew nothing at all. Repairs, forever, on the barony's coin and not the field's.

And one clause that Ferra argued with him about for two hours.

"The barony's four parts are drawn last," she read out, "after the sixteen have been paid in full."

"Yes."

"Then in a bad year you get nothing and they get everything."

"In a bad year I get nothing and they get everything."

"You have a treasury you can see the bottom of. I've shown you the bottom of it. Twice." She set the sheet down. "A hard frost in the fourth week and you're feeding a garrison out of a hole in the ground."

"I know."

"Then why."

Emeric thought about how to say it and settled on the truthful version, which was not the version he'd have given a court.

"Because every man in that room has been on the wrong side of a bad year, and every single time, the lord's share came out first and the shortfall came out of them." He tapped the clause. "This is the only line on this paper that costs me something they can measure. I could write six pages of promises and they'd be worth less than this one sentence, because this one they can check by waiting. That's the whole point. They shouldn't have to trust me. They should be able to watch."

Ferra let it stand. She did, he noticed, copy the clause into her own book with the date next to it, which was either bookkeeping or a warning, and possibly both.

Ward found the flaw at the door, on his way out, in one sentence, the way he always did.

"Who counts the harvest?"

"Three tallies," Emeric said. "One in Ferra's book. One held by whoever they pick to speak for the field. One nailed to the mill door, rewritten every evening in the season, where any man who can count to twenty can stand and read it, and any man who can't can bring one who can."

Ward absorbed that.

"And the register?"

"Chandler's book. Fifth page, after the weir."

"Not the castle."

"Not the castle," Emeric said. "If it's in the castle it's mine, and the whole thing collapses back into rent with extra steps. In the chandler's book it's the same shelf as Bram's boat, and it survives me, and it survives whoever the crown sends after me, and if some future baron wants to tear it up he has to go into a candle shop and take a book off a shelf in front of the entire town."

Ward thought about that with visible effort and eventually nodded once, which from him was closer to approval than Emeric had expected to get all week.

Thirty-one people came on the eighth day, in a room he had expected to hold thirteen, and Rylan had to go and find more benches.

Ferra read the terms out. That was deliberate and it had been her suggestion. Emeric stood at the side with the chalk in his hand and did not speak for the first half hour, which was the single most difficult piece of self-discipline he had managed since arriving in Kaldo, and he was aware of both facts and only slightly ashamed of the second.

She read the parts. She read the allotment, the two kinds, the tally, the register, the transfer restriction, the barony's four and its list, and the clause about the barony drawing last. She read the numbers slowly and twice where they mattered and she did not once explain, because Emeric had learned by then that explaining is what a man does when he wants agreement rather than understanding.

Then the questions started, and they were better questions than he'd braced for.

Maren wanted to know what happened to her ground parts if she died with four sons and one holding. Halloway wanted to know who decided what got planted in which division, and looked unhappy when Emeric said the rotation was in the terms and the crop was the field's business. A cooper's widow at the back wanted to know whether a man could hold work parts without holding any ground at all, and when Ferra said yes, sat back and said something quietly to the woman beside her and both of them looked at Emeric in a way that made him deeply uncomfortable and which he was fairly sure was the first friendly thing anyone in that town had done at him in six weeks.

Dunnel waited until the end, as he clearly had all along.

"Who's Teague on this?" he said.

The room went slightly still. Emeric had not expected that, and he had to take a second with it.

"You've been talking to Bram."

"I've been talking to everyone. You'd know that if you'd ever come down to the Cross." Dunnel didn't stand up. "The Marrow's owned by nine of us and steered by a man who owns none of her, because on the water he's right and we're not. So. Who's Teague on your field. Who steers."

"On the water, me." Emeric said it fast, because he'd had eight days to get it clear and if he could not say it in one breath it wasn't clear. "The ring, the intake, the gates, the schedule, whatever goes on top of them later. That's mine, it's what the four parts are paying for, and when I say the west channel closes on a Tuesday it closes on a Tuesday and I don't take a vote on it, because if that ring silts up or breaks a bank in the wrong place you lose a section and I am the only man on this plain who currently knows why."

"And the field?"

"Not mine. You pick a man for that. Not me picking him, and not me approving him after. You pick him, and he runs the field, and he holds one of the three tallies, and he can stand in my long room and tell me no."

"Reeve," somebody said.

"That's the word," Halloway said. "That's what it's called. That's an old office and it's not a loved one, mind. The reeve's always been the man who counts your sheaves for the lord."

"Then this one counts them for you," Emeric said, "and tells me the number afterward, and if the two of us ever want different things out of the same day, I would like him to be a man who says so out loud in front of everybody instead of a man who works out which of us to please."

Halloway looked at him for a long moment.

"You'd not enjoy that as much as you think you would."

"Almost certainly not."

They put him out of the room for the choosing.

That was Dunnel's motion and it was carried before Emeric had finished agreeing to it, and Ward escorted him into his own corridor and stood beside him with an expression of profound satisfaction that he made no attempt to hide.

"You're enjoying this."

"Immensely."

"They're voting on a farm I designed, in a room I own, and I'm in the hallway."

"Aye," Ward said. "You'll want to remember how that feels. It's the first thing you've built here that doesn't need you in the room."

It took them under an hour. Halloway came out to fetch him, which was itself the answer, and Emeric had it confirmed anyway.

"They've had me for the reeve," Halloway said, without any pleasure at all. "Which I'd like on the record was not my idea and I argued against it for a good while."

"Noted. Who's holding the tallies?"

"Maren's got one." A pause. "Dunnel's got the other."

Emeric took a moment with that.

He would not have picked Dunnel. He would have picked somebody warmer, somebody easier to be in a room with, and it would have been the wrong choice by a distance he could see the whole shape of standing in that doorway. The tally that mattered was the one held by the man who assumed he was being cheated, because that was the only tally anyone in Kaldo would believe.

The room had just done, in under an hour, something he had spent eight days failing to do on paper.

"That's better than anything I'd have written," he said.

"I know," Halloway said. "Two conditions, and then I'll put my mark on it."

"Go on."

"One. I answer to you on the water and the works and the schedule, because you're right that you're the only man who knows why, and I'd be a fool to pretend otherwise. On the count I answer to them. If those two ever pull different ways, I'll tell you first and them second, and then I'll do what the count says." Halloway held his eye. "You'll want to know that now rather than in four years."

"Agreed. Second?"

"My grandmother's hedge on the east line stays. It's the parish boundary and there's people under it." He said it flatly, without any expectation of winning. "Take the rest. That one stays."

Emeric thought about the fall of the land on the east side, and the two divisions the hedge cut across, and what it would cost to bend the ring round it.

"It costs the field about forty acres of reach and it costs me a bad joint in the ring I'll have to inspect twice as often," he said. "Write it in. Not as a favor. As a term, so the next man can't undo it either."

Twenty-six marks went onto the paper that afternoon, and then eleven more over the following week as word went round, and the chandler ruled a new page and got the whole thing into his book in a hand considerably better than Emeric's.

Old Pell's nephew stayed out.

He came up, listened to the whole of it, asked two sharp questions about the transfer restriction that indicated he had understood the terms better than most of the men who had signed them, and then said no. His forty acres sat almost exactly in the middle of what the boards called division five.

Ferra wanted to know whether they could put pressure on. Emeric said no, and then said it again in writing, because a thing men cannot refuse is not a thing they chose, and a field with one holdout in the middle of it was a field that everyone could see nobody had been forced into. It cost him a jog in the ring, a set of gates he wouldn't otherwise have needed, and a permanent stand of somebody else's barley in the middle of his rotation.

He looked at the jog on the board for a while afterward and decided he could live with it, and made a private note that a man who understood transfer restrictions that well was a man to keep an eye on.

They broke ground on the intake nine days later, in a cold bright morning with half the town standing on the bank.

It was not much of a ceremony. Emeric had considered making one and then remembered where he was. Ferra read the day's tally out, which was forty-one names and the grain measure against each, so that the first thing ever recorded on the field was what the field owed its diggers. Halloway walked the line of stakes with the twine, checking the run against the marks, and stopped twice to argue about a peg and was right once.

Wren held the end of the twine, having been given the job on the grounds that it was the only job available that could not be done wrong, and took it with tremendous seriousness, and shouted at her brother twice for slack.

Emeric took the first spadeful himself because Bram told him to, and did it badly, and Dunnel said something from the back that got a laugh out of about nine people. Then the crews went in properly and the noise changed to the noise of forty men digging, which he stood and listened to for longer than he needed to.

Nine divisions. One field. Four thousand six hundred acres of reach when the ring closed, less forty for a hedge with people under it and rather more than forty for a man's barley in the middle.

And two hundred and forty hands, when the ring wanted five hundred and seventy to fill what it watered.

He had not moved that number by a single hand. Everything he had done in three weeks had been arranging the hands he already had into a shape where they weren't cheating each other, which was necessary and was not the same thing as more of them.

He wrote the letter that night and it was terrible.

It was four hundred words and had eleven numbers in it, and the numbers were correct, and he read it back at the point where he had explained the difference between ground parts and work parts in the second paragraph and understood that he had written a survey to a man with two children and a cart.

He took it down to Nessa's the next evening anyway, because Imani had been right about who would be writing it and there was no advantage in being slow about admitting that.

Nessa read it at the table with the candle pulled over, taking her time, and Rylan very carefully found something to do in the other room.

"It's a good letter," she said, when she'd finished.

"It isn't."

"No, it isn't." She set it down. "Can I say the true thing?"

"Please."

"He can't read." She said it without any weight on it. "Our mother had four and taught two. He'll take this to the miller at Wayford and stand there while a man he owes money to reads him a letter from a prince about parts of a harvest, and the miller will tell him it says whatever the miller wants it to say."

Emeric sat with that. It was, he thought, the single most useful piece of information anyone had given him in a month, and he had built an entire recruitment strategy on paper without once establishing whether the recipients could read paper.

"What goes in it instead?"

Nessa turned the sheet over, and took the pen, and wrote on the back in a plain slow hand, saying it out loud as she went, which he understood was for his benefit.

"The water's clean. My own two drink it and neither's been sick since spring." She wrote. "In Wayford you keep a fifth. In Kaldo you give a fifth and the rest is yours, and it's written in the candle shop where anyone can read it to you, not in the castle." She wrote. "Come before the sowing. Bring the children. I've a bed for the four of you as long as you need it."

She stopped, and looked at what she'd written, and added one more line.

"He hanged the baron. Everyone here has an opinion. Mine is that the water's clean."

Then she folded it and handed the whole thing back to him, his four hundred words on one side and her six lines on the other.

"You keep that side," she said. "I'll copy mine out fresh."

He walked back up the hill with the letter in his coat, and did not feel as bad about it as he had expected to, and could not entirely work out why until he was most of the way up.

Eleven numbers had gotten him nothing. Six lines and a bed would probably get him a cooper, a wife, and two children who had never had clean water, and there were four hundred families in Wayford working another man's field for a fifth, and precisely one person in Kaldo currently able to write to any of them.

He got the list out and put it under the empty space.

Find out who else here has a brother somewhere.

Then, because he had spent the afternoon watching forty men dig a ring that would water more grain than this barony had grown in his lifetime, and because he had learned by now to write the second thought down as well:

And if all of it works, in three years this place has a full granary, one road out, and no one to sell to.
