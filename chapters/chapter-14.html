Chapter 14 - The Shop, the Cousin, and the Ground

Two rooms off the lower market, a counter, a back room for curing, and a sign that said SOAP.

He had spent an evening on better signs and had thrown all of them away, on the grounds that every clever thing he could think of was clever in a language this town did not speak, and that a man who names a shop after himself has told you what the shop is for.

His name was not on it. Neither was his money, in any form a clerk could find. Abloh made. A rota of women from the north well cut and wrapped and were paid by the day, in coppers counted into their hands at evening.

And the counter belonged to Sela.

Imani made that offer, not Emeric, and she made it inside four seconds of the problem being described to her, before he had finished describing it. He had been about to suggest somebody else. By the end of the first afternoon he was extremely glad he had been slow.

Sela took it and was immediately and obviously good at it, because she had been running a household on nothing for as long as anyone at the north well could remember, and because nobody in Kaldo had ever argued with her about a price and got anywhere.

She still did not look at him.

When he came in she served him the way she served anybody, and charged him full, in front of the room, the first time very deliberately.

"Two coppers," she said, to the bars on the counter, at a volume meant for the four other people in the shop.

He paid it. He thanked her. He left.

He did not permit anything to happen to his face until he was outside, and then he laughed about it for the length of a street.

The next time it was the same, and the time after that. He paid it and never mentioned it.



He had been asking after Petram every few days for ten weeks.

Not visiting. Asking. What he ate. Whether he slept. Whether he had said anything to anybody about anything at all.

Declan, who looked in at the door most evenings on his way back from running levels for the well sites, reported it like weather. Nothing. Nothing. Ate less. Nothing. Asked for a second blanket and then said not to bother.

Emeric told himself this was patience.

Ward, in four words on a stair, did not agree with him, and did not push it either.

"That's not a plan."

He found Corliss himself, on foot, with Rylan, and spent a week beforehand working out what he had to trade and what he was prepared to threaten with.

He had held the card since the road. Petram had a cousin in Kaldo's garrison, which was a thing a frightened man says in the first hour and regrets in the second, and Emeric had written it down and sat on it for three months while it went on being worth something.

None of the preparation was necessary.

He got one sentence out, standing in the yard behind the guardhouse with the wind coming off the river, and Corliss said yes.

Immediately. Without conditions. Without asking what it bought him or what happened to him afterward.

He was a knight, of the kind a poor barony keeps, in a coat mended at both elbows, broad and quiet and somewhere about thirty. He had the face of a man who had been waiting a long time for somebody from the crown to come and find him and was relieved that it was finally happening, and he squared his shoulders while he said it, the way men do when they have rehearsed.

Emeric read that as guilt about his cousin, and filed it.

There was one other thing, and he could not make it file. When Corliss reached for his sword belt to settle it, his palms were blistered across the heel of the hand and the root of every finger, old hard blisters gone yellow and new ones under them, the hands of a man who works a spade. A garrison knight has nothing to dig.

Nobody asked him about it, so he did not say anything.



He put them in a room and stood outside it with the door open.

That was the compromise between decency and use, and he did not do himself the courtesy of pretending it was only the first one. A closed door is kinder. An open door is a record.

It was short and it was bad.

Corliss did not shout. He asked his cousin what he had done, and Petram told him, plainly, with nothing taken out and nothing excused, in the voice of a man reading a list.

The plainness broke him rather than the content.

Then Corliss asked the only question that had ever mattered to him.

"Did you give them my name? At the fire."

"Yes."

"Why."

And Petram could not answer that with a prince standing in the doorway, and did not try.

The thing Emeric had come for did not happen.

No name. Nothing about who had paid. Nothing about the road.

What happened instead came at the end, and he nearly missed it.

Petram asked a question of his own, which he had not done once in ten weeks. He wanted to know whether anybody had come to Corliss's door asking after him.

Corliss said no.

Nobody. Not once. Not a stranger, not a letter, not a boy with a message.

Petram sat back against the wall and something went out of him that had been holding him upright since the road, and Emeric, watching from the doorway, understood the whole shape of ten weeks of silence in the space of one breath.

He hated every part of how he had come by it.

Corliss came out past him into the corridor and stopped, and looked at Emeric once more, square, the way a man looks at a clerk who has a paper in front of him and has not yet turned it over. As though there were a question coming and he had already decided how he would answer it.

Emeric did not ask anything. Corliss went down the stair.

Afterward he was not pleased with himself, and he did not let himself be talked out of that.

He had used a man's family as a tool and got nothing. If he had got something it would have been worse, because then he would have had a reason.

Ward gave him the honest read on the way up, and did not absolve him of anything in the course of it.

"It worked. Not for what you built it for." He took the stairs one at a time, favouring nothing, because the arm had finally stopped being a subject. "Ten weeks he's been sat in there sure they'd go for the cousin if he opened his mouth. Couldn't think past it. He's not sat there now."

"He still won't give me a name."

"No. Not today."

Emeric decided that night to stop running Petram as a problem and start visiting him as a person, and was entirely aware while deciding it that this was both the decent choice and the more effective one, and that he could not separate the two by any method available to him.

He sat with that longer than he meant to, and arrived somewhere he did not care for, which was that this was probably what his father had felt like every day for fifteen years.



Rylan raised the other problem, because Rylan was the one who had to walk past that door six times a day.

There was a man in a bedroom in the keep who had tried to kill a prince of Randillia. He had been there ten weeks. He had not been charged with anything, because there was nothing in Kaldo to charge him in front of.

There was no magistrate in Kaldo. There had not been one in eleven years. The nearest sat at Wayford, three days' ride up the road, and answered to the same crown that had not replied to a letter out of this town since before the plague.

Emeric had no good answer and said so, which Rylan had not expected and took better than an answer.

It went on the list, on the page he was starting to think of as the page of things that would arrive whether he was ready or not.

Doran came up to the keep himself, in the evening, rather than sending for anyone.

Hale said afterward that he could not remember the priest doing that in twenty years, and Hale had been in that house for all of them.

He had brought the book.

He did not make a confession and Emeric did not invite one, and both of them worked quite hard at the not inviting.

What he said was administrative, and it was the most difficult thing he had ever said in his life.

The ground the rows sat on was consecrated, which meant it was the church's and not the baron's and not the crown's, and that mattered legally as well as otherwise.

The families would have to be spoken to one at a time. Not from a pulpit. Not by announcement.

It would take most of a season.

And there was a hollow outside the south-east wall that had never been used because it had never been convenient, and which he had walked twice that week and believed sat below every well in Kaldo and downstream of everything else.

Emeric did not say anything for a moment, because what he wanted to say was thank you, and thank you would have made it a gift given to a prince.

So he asked about the cost instead, and got a list, and the list was longer than he had expected.

Lime. Cloth. Diggers, and the diggers would have to be paid because no man does that work for nothing twice. New ground consecrated, which was time rather than money. And the fees.

"What fees."

Doran turned a page without any particular weight on it. The parish charged to bury, because the parish had to buy the ground it put them in and pay the men who opened it. It charged for the rite. It always had.

Then he stopped turning pages.

There were always some with nobody to speak for them. A dozen a year, more in a bad year. People who died with nothing and left nobody with anything. They had no entry in the book, because the entry came with the fee, and they had no rite, because the rite came with it too. And they were buried all the same. Somebody bought a box. Somebody dug, at night, and in the morning there was turned earth with no name over it. It had been going on for years, and he had never once asked who, and he did not look at Emeric while he said that part.

Emeric was interested in that for about ten seconds.

He wrote it in the notebook, in the margin, sideways, on a page about burial logistics, and moved on to the question of how much lime the kiln could produce in a month while running the soap.

He did not look at that page again for a year.

At the door, once, Doran asked the only part of it that was not administrative.

"Would they have died anyway."

Emeric could have said no.

It would have been true enough. The wedge was a shape with edges and the edges had been made by a decision, and he had drawn the thing himself and could have put a number on it inside a minute.

He was not going to spend a man's worst night on being accurate.

What he said was that Doran had buried four hundred people in eighteen months by himself, that nobody standing where Doran had stood had a better thing available to them, and that everything after it was a thing the two of them knew now and that no man alive could have known then.

Doran took that.

He did not believe all of it, and Emeric watched him not believe it, and he went home anyway.



Late, alone, Emeric wrote the day down.

The practice slate was on the desk beside the notebook, where it lived now. Three months of dry stylus lines, drawn and rubbed out and drawn again, no stone under them and nothing spent. The newest ones were finally straight. He allowed himself a minute of the particular satisfaction of a man who has been bad at a thing for a long time and has just stopped being quite so bad at it.

There had been something else that morning, at the dawn drill, and he turned it over now because he had not had a quiet minute for it all day.

Ward had come at him from the left, the same feint he had used fifty times, and Emeric's guard had come up to meet it before he had decided to raise it. Faster than three months of losing should have allowed. Ward had stepped back and looked at him.

Neither of them had said a word. Ward had come at him again, and he had lost the way he always lost, and they had gone in to breakfast.

He did not write that down.

He found his hand already in his pocket.

Closed on the grey carved stone, the way it had been most days since he arrived.

He had carried it constantly as a boy. Then the carriage, and the wall in his memory where the carriage should be, and fifteen years of it in a drawer untouched. He could not recall deciding to stop.

Then he had pulled it out the night before an exile for no reason he had been able to name at the time or since, and he had handled it every day for three months without once noticing that he was doing it.

There was a dream that had started coming back. His mother's hands over his, a slate, a voice he could not hear. It had come often enough since the road that he had started keeping count of it, and lately there was something under it, something with walls, that felt less like a thing he was making up and more like a place on the far side of the wall that he had been to once and mislaid.

He turned the other question over, which was why the wall was there at all.

Trauma was the obvious answer. It accounted for the shape of it, roughly. He had never in his life been satisfied by roughly and he was not satisfied now, and he had nothing else to put in its place.

He went to sleep with it in his hand, which he had been doing for weeks.
