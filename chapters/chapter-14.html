Chapter 14 - Two Sets of Books

He needed the barony's accounts read, and there was nobody in the castle he could hand them to.

That was the arithmetic of the mill road. Whoever had reached Tam had reach inside the keep, and the books lived inside the keep, and so did every clerk who could read them. He couldn't read thirty years of them himself and still run a soap yard, a well dig and a prisoner. And anyone on his own payroll was a man somebody could lean on, or buy, or find on a road one evening.

He said most of this to Bram at the forge, without the last part.

Bram didn't stop hammering. He finished the stroke, turned the bar, and looked toward the back corner of the forge, where a half-wall kept the sparks off a high desk and nobody was sitting at it.

"Ask her," he said.



Ferra heard him out at her own kitchen table with her pen in her hand. She didn't write anything down while he talked, which he took, correctly, as a bad sign.

"Paid," she said, when he'd finished. "Properly. Not in soap."

"Yes."

"I'm not a servant. I don't come up to the keep and stand in a corner waiting to be told."

"No."

"I work here. At this table. You bring them to me." She put the pen down, square to the edge of the page. "And the castle never learns my name. Not Hale, not the baron, not the boy who brings the wood. If I hear my name in that house, I stop the same day, and you can find somebody else."

He thought of Tam's name, and how long it had taken him to learn it.

"Yes," he said. It took him about four seconds, and three of them were spent wondering why she thought she had to ask.

She named a wage. It was fair, which made it harder to argue with than if it had been too high. He didn't.



Getting the books out of the keep turned out to be easy, which bothered him more than if it had been hard.

He went to Hale and asked to see what the barony had spent on water, for the well order, going back as far as there were records. Hale considered him, then took a ring of keys off his belt and went down to a vaulted room under the old hall that smelled of mice and damp. He came back with Rylan and Osric behind him carrying crates. Thirty years of ledgers. And on top, without being asked for, the current year's daybook, in Hale's own hand.

"The baron keeps the ledger," Hale said. "I keep the daybook. What's paid goes in when it's paid." He set it down with the others. "You'll want it."

He didn't ask what for. Emeric thanked him and didn't ask why not.

They went down the hill to Bram's house in a handcart under a sheet of sacking, Rylan pushing, Ward walking alongside as if it were nothing to do with him.

Ferra lifted the books out of the first crate one at a time and laid them across the table in a row, then stood looking at them with her arms folded.

"Three hands," she said. She touched the spines in turn. "That one's old. Before the baron. That one, from about here to here. Then that one, which is Hale's, from about eleven years ago." She opened one and turned pages. "No index. Not in any of them. No running totals, they add the year at the back and that's all. And this one," she held up a thin book in a grey cover, "has no year on it. Somebody forgot to write it." She put it down. "I can tell which it is. It'll take a day."

"How long for all of it?"

She looked down the row.

"Four months," she said. "If nobody needs me for anything else."



The plan was to do it in the room.

Everything she read would be there, the way everything he'd ever read was there for him, and they could lay thirty years side by side. Weeks instead of months.

It didn't work.

The first time, in the afternoon, at her table, with Bram awake on the far side of it and the lamp unlit, she put her hand on his shoulder the way her father had, and he went in, and she didn't come.

The second time, the same.

Each time he came out to her face close to his and her book open by her elbow, a new line in it in her own hand. First attempt. Eighty-one. Nothing. Second attempt. Sixty. Nothing.

The third time he leaned.

He hadn't meant to. He was in the room, and there was nothing of her there, and he wanted her there, and he pushed toward where she should have been, like a shoulder put to a door that had stuck.

Something was shut, and held from the other side. It gave a hair's width, and then it pushed back, hard and steady and nothing like a door, and he was out.

He was at the table with his head splitting. Across from him Ferra had both hands pressed flat over her eyes.

"That hurt," she said, through her hands.

"Yes."

"You did something."

"Yes," he said. "I'm sorry."

Bram had half risen from his chair. He sat back down slowly and didn't take his eyes off either of them.

The pain was a band behind the eyes and up over the crown, and it took most of an hour to go. Somewhere in that hour Emeric thought of a form on a clipboard in another world, eleven questions, all symptoms and none capability. The first one had been about headaches.

He looked at Ferra's book, at her two lines and the new one she was writing now, with the pen held a little too tight. Third attempt. Thirty-three. He pushed. Headache, both.

"You wrote the times down," he said.

"Somebody has to."

"Each time. Yourself." He touched the page. "You've got your hand on the count the whole way in. You're sitting here holding it."

She looked at him.

"Your father didn't know it was coming," he said. "He touched my shoulder and he was in before he'd had time to hold on to anything. You've been holding on from the start. You can't keep the count and leave the room."

She didn't answer. She looked at the book, at her own neat hand down the page.

That was what he'd got wrong. With Bram it had felt like showing a man round his workshop. But the room was built out of whoever was in it, nearer to sharing a head than walking into one.

"I'm not frightened of going," Ferra said at last. She said it to the book. "I'm frightened of not being able to count it."

Then she looked up.

"Then lean."

"No."

"It gave. You said. It gave a hair."

"It gave a hair and then it pushed back." He kept his voice level. "Think of your father's bar. Pull it and it stretches, and comes back when you let go. Pull harder and it stays stretched. Harder than that and it goes. And water. Push water apart fast enough and when it comes back together it crushes whatever's between. Pumps get eaten like that, from the inside."

She was listening now, properly, the way she listened to a figure.

"If I lean all the way," he said, "something gives. In you or in me. It might not come back, and we wouldn't know which of us. It would look like a stroke." He looked at Bram. "Your father knows what that looks like now."

Bram said nothing.

"No," Emeric said again.



She thought about it overnight. In the morning she had her terms written out on a clean page, and she read them to him.

"You never lean. Not a hair. Not if I ask you to."

"Agreed."

"He keeps the book." She didn't look at her father. "Not me. He counts. Past a count he's comfortable with, he fetches Imani, and nobody gets a vote."

"Agreed."

"And whatever's in there that's mine stays mine." Now she looked at him. "Unless I give it to you."

"Agreed."

She turned the page round and slid the book across the table to her father.

Bram looked down at it, and Emeric saw him understand that in all her years at the back of his forge, she had never once handed him this book. He put one big hand flat on it and didn't open it.

Ferra put her hand on Emeric's forearm. Not on the pulse, the way Imani would have. Flat, across the top of it, above the wrist, and left it there.

He went in.

And she was there.

She was standing beside the table in the room as if she'd been there all along, looking down at her own hand where it had been on his arm. She looked, he thought, faintly annoyed that it had been so simple.

Then she looked round, and stopped being annoyed.

The room had grown. It had shelves now, real ones, running back further than the room was wide, and they were full. He saw the ledgers first, because they were what he'd been thinking of: all thirty years of them, every page she'd turned at the table yesterday, standing open in a long row. Then he saw the rest. Pages he had never seen, never could have, in a hand that was hers. A smudge where her sleeve had dragged across wet ink. On one page, in the bottom corner, a thumbprint in soot.

And at the far end, a separate shelf of forge books, years of them, the brackets and the horseshoes and the iron bought and owed.

He looked at that shelf once, and didn't go down that end of the room at all.



He built her a spreadsheet.

He didn't call it that. He laid it out on the table, which had grown to fit it: a grid, every entry from every ledger on its own row, with date, amount, payee, what for, book and page. Across the top, a header that stayed put however far down you went.

"Watch," he said.

He sorted the first three years by payee, and nine hundred-odd entries rearranged themselves in a breath, each name's payments together in one block, in date order.

Ferra stared at it.

He filtered it to show only payments over a certain sum. He added a running total down the right.

She was quiet. He thought she was impressed.

"Nobody thought of this," she said. "Nobody." She turned to him, and she was furious. "Do you know, the winter before last, my father lost a payment from a carter, and I sorted two years of the forge's books by hand looking for it. On the floor. In piles. Eleven days." She looked back at the grid. "Eleven days."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Move." She took the pen out of his hand.

Within the hour she had broken it apart: one sheet for each year, the bottom of each carried forward to the top of the next. Fix an error eleven years back and the correction walked forward on its own through every year after, all the way to the present. He'd have got there in a week. She got there before he'd finished explaining the filter.

Bram shook them out of it, hard, by both shoulders, the way you'd shake a man asleep on a cart.

"A hundred and fifty," Bram said. He had the book open. "Your number."

"It felt like a quarter hour," Ferra said.

Emeric had thought nearer an hour and a half. Over the next fortnight it turned out there was no fixed ratio at all: it moved with how deep they went and how tired they were, and the hour of the day made no difference. And both of them, every time, came out believing less time had passed outside than had.

So the number stopped being a comfort and became a ceiling. Ferra wrote it at the top of the book, and Bram shook them at it every time, without mercy, mid-sentence if necessary. Twice it was.

Emeric wrote his own rule in the back of Armand's notebook. Contact. Consent. Nothing forced. No strangers. No enemies.

He didn't write that both times it had worked, with Bram and with Ferra, he'd been awake and sitting at a table.



At night, in his own room, he went on with the other work.

Somewhere in the long hours in the room, the coin had stopped being a problem. He could lift it and hold it now while thinking about something else, which was the whole point of doing a thing a hundred times. So in the second week of the books, with the shutters closed and Ward's cloak pinned over the gap under the door, he tried himself.

He'd expected it to be the coin a thousand times over. But he was only taking the weight away, and once it was gone, holding it gone cost far less than he'd feared. The glyph opened small and pale at his feet, and his heels left the boards, and then his toes, and he hung a hand's width off the floor of his room with his arms out like a man on ice, waiting for something to go wrong.

Nothing did. His nerve gave out long before his mana.

The next night he tried to turn.

Gravity went up and down. Armand's notebook said so in three places, and the coin had never once drifted an inch to either side. But a man wasn't a coin. Take the weight off his feet and leave it on his shoulders, and he had one push up and one down at opposite ends of the same body, and a pair like that would turn him.

Holding two at once was another matter. The first time, the half at his feet let go the moment he reached for his shoulders, and he sat down hard on the floor. The fourth time both held, wobbling, two small glyphs flickering like a lamp in a draught, and he tipped forward, slowly, head over heels, and kept going until the bedpost hit him in the back.

He lay on the floor and laughed silently, and stayed there because he couldn't have stood up if he'd wanted to.

The single hover cost him about what the candle did. Two holds pulling against each other emptied him like a long night in the room, and the headache after it sat behind his eyes until morning.

But lying there, he kept turning the other thing over.

Up and down he had. Sideways, a mage needed air, and air cost him three times what his own elements did. But fire was his. He'd bent the candle flame often enough to know how it felt when he pushed it hard away: a faint shove back, against the palm, so slight he'd taken it for nothing. Hot gas going one way. A hand going the other. Every rocket he'd ever read about came down to that sentence.

Hang in the air on gravity. Push with fire.

He was too tired to try it. He thought he could, though, some other night, somewhere no glyph could be seen. He could feel the shape of it, the way he'd felt the furnace before he drew it.

In the back of the notebook, under the night's counts, he wrote Fire for thrust? and left the question mark where it was.



What came out of the books took a fortnight.

Wexler had been skimming, for years. Not cleverly. Repairs invoiced twice and done once, or done halfway. The drawbridge chain had been paid for in full, twice, eight years apart, and Emeric had ridden in under the rope that replaced it. A grain contract at a price per measure that couldn't be true in any year Ferra could find in any book. And in the garrison's pay roll, sixteen names that drew a wage every quarter and existed nowhere else. Not in the parish book, not in the tithe lists, not in the garrison's own duty sheets.

Ferra laid it all out, flatly, in a column.

"It isn't enough to live on," she said.

"It's theft."

"It's theft," she agreed. "I'm saying it isn't enough. For thirty years of a baron. A man stealing to live well steals more than this, or he steals smarter." She tapped the column with her pen and moved on.

Then the garrison share.

Every year for nine years, the same guard had drawn small sums against the garrison's funds, over and above his wage. The same name, in the same hand, a few times a year, small enough that nobody would notice one of them, regular enough that the whole made a shape.

The name was Corliss.

"Look at that first," Ward said, when Emeric told him. "Before anything else. He's been in a room with your prisoner."

So Emeric looked. He checked the draws against the garrison's own book, and then against the quartermaster's slips, which he had Rylan fetch without saying why. He had Ferra count the coin the slips said had gone out against the coin the box said was there. He set Corliss's signature on the slips beside the one on his muster mark. Four ways, and all four said the same.

He found Corliss in the garrison yard, alone, oiling a buckle.

"Did you take it?" he said. "From the garrison funds. For nine years."

Corliss looked up at him. There was something in his face that Emeric had seen once before, outside Petram's door: a man who has been waiting a long time to be asked something, and is ready.

"Yes," Corliss said.

Emeric waited for more. Corliss didn't offer it. He only went on looking at Emeric as though there were a second question, and he was waiting for that one too.

Emeric had what he'd come for. He nodded and went back up to the keep.

In the same fortnight, sorting the year's outgoings by payee, Ferra turned up a block of purchases from the coffin-maker on Cooper's Row. A dozen or so a year, going back years, under no family's name. Just box, and a sum. She noted them in her column with everything else. Emeric read the line and moved on to the next.



And then Cobb.

He was in the books by name. Cobb, south road, a sum, every quarter, as regular as rent, going back years. Ferra had sorted the whole run into one block on the table in the room, and Emeric stood looking at it a long time.

At the bottom of the block, one entry was larger than all the rest. Several quarters' worth at once. The date on it was the date of the fire.

After it, nothing. The quarters stopped.

"There," he said.

"There," Ferra agreed. Then she said, "It isn't what you think."

He looked at her.

"Look at the rest of it." She ran her pen down the block. "Same sum. Every quarter. Eleven years. It never moves. Not in the plague, not in the bad winters, when every other line in these books moves. That's a retainer. He's paying to keep a man." She put the pen on the last entry. "And then this. One payment, on the day, and the account's closed. That's not what it looks like when you hire something done. When you hire something done, you see it coming. Money goes out early. Here, nothing changes until it's over." She looked up. "That's a discharge. He was settling an account. Eleven years of it." She paused, and chose the next words carefully. "He paid people who were already coming."

"He paid for the road."

"He paid. I don't know what for." She tapped the block. "It's worth pulling on. It's not what you think it is."

He thought of Petram's purse in a stable yard, the road, Garrin, the fire, and the large entry on the date of the fire, and the shape of it was so clear and whole that he could see every piece from where he stood.

"It's Wexler," he said. "He paid for the road."

Ferra didn't argue. She took her own book back from Bram's side of the table long enough to write in it. He read it upside down as she wrote.

Cobb account. A discharge, not a commission. Retainer unbroken eleven years. Said so. Overruled.

She dated it.

Then she wrote one more line under it.

Grain contract, Cobb retainer, Hale made steward, last magistrate gone. All begin the same year. Eleven years back.

He saw it. She didn't explain it, because she didn't know what it meant and wouldn't guess, and he didn't ask, because he had a prisoner in a cell under the keep and a name to put to him.

He went back up the hill. At the head of the stair Declan stood aside, and Emeric took the stair down to Petram with the lamp in his hand.
