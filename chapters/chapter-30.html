Chapter 30 - Ducatis

The letter went on the twenty-fourth of September and Emeric wrote it four times.

The first draft was the one he would have written a year earlier, which was a prince's letter, and he burned it. The second explained the year. The third explained the year and did not mention the fire until the fourth paragraph, and he read it back and recognised the shape of a man managing what somebody else was going to find out anyway, and burned that too.

What went was two pages. What it had in it was every number Ferra had, in the order she had given them to him, with the loss at the top and the harvest at the bottom and no argument anywhere in it at all.

"You have not asked them for anything," Ferra said, reading it.

"I've told them what's here and what's gone and what it costs to keep it alive until August. If they can't work out what I'm asking for they're not a lending house."

"And if they read the top of the first page and stop."

"Then they were always going to stop, and I'd rather they stopped on the second day than on the fortieth." He sealed it. "Everybody who has said no to me this month said no to something I dressed up. I'm running out of month."

Ducatis answered in nine days, which Ferra said was the fastest reply she had ever seen out of a lending house and which she did not think was a good sign.

It was one line. A factor would arrive.

Vantic came up the Wayford road on the eleventh of October, alone, on a hired horse, with one bag and a writing case, and he did not send ahead.

He was perhaps thirty-five, narrow, entirely unremarkable, in a coat that had been good and was now merely serviceable, and Emeric came out into the yard to meet a man he had been imagining for three weeks and found somebody he would not have picked out of a market.

"Your Highness." A small bow, correct to the inch. "Aldous Vantic. Ducatis and Sons. I have no authority to lend you anything and I want that said in the yard rather than in a room, because men in my position are frequently allowed to be misunderstood on the point for a fortnight and I find it wastes everybody's time."

"Then what have you got authority to do."

"To look at it, and to write down what I see, and to say what terms my house would want if it wanted any." Vantic handed his reins to nobody in particular and looked at the castle without any expression at all. "I would like to start with the burned building, if it is convenient. People generally show me that last."

He walked everything, and it took him six days.

He walked the granary walls and stood in the ash for a quarter of an hour and asked three questions about the thatch. He walked the graded approach from the joint down to the fence line and asked what the whitewashed stones at a hundred and twenty paces were, and wrote the answer down. He walked the new ground below the mill, where a hundred and forty-nine men lay in trenches Corliss had run, and took his hat off, and Emeric did not learn until much later that the taking off of the hat had gone into the report as well.

He spent a day and a half in the foundry.

He stopped in front of the bar on the wall of the charging floor for longer than he stopped in front of the third furnace, which made him the second man that year to do that, and Emeric noticed and said nothing.

"Explain the stick," Vantic said.

Emeric explained the stick.

"And the copies come back on the first of the month."

"Every one, and any that's drifted gets cut up in front of everybody."

"Who cuts it up when you are not here."

Emeric stopped, because in eleven weeks nobody had asked him that, including himself.

"The shift man," he said. "It's painted on the wall. It's drilled at both shift changes and it's been drilled twice a day since June."

"Then write down that it is the shift man and put the name in the book, because a thing everybody knows is a thing nobody is responsible for." Vantic was already moving. "I mention it because it is the only instruction in this building that is not written down anywhere and it is the one the other twenty depend on. Where is the second wheel?"

He spent two days with Ferra and the books and nobody else in the room, and at the end of the second day Ferra came out and sat down in the long room and looked at the wall for a while.

"Well," Emeric said.

"He found the shillings."

"Which shillings."

"The two I hid from you in March and June, which you found, and which I entered as found and did not enter as hidden." She rubbed her eyes. "He asked me why an entry in my own hand in March corrects an entry in my own hand in February and why there is no third entry explaining it. Nobody has ever read my books. I have kept them for eleven years for a baron who did not open them and a prince who reads them faster than I can hand them over and neither of you has ever once read them the way that man read them."

"Was he unpleasant about it?"

"He was worse than unpleasant. He was interested." Ferra looked up. "He said it was the best set of barony books he had seen in nine years and then he found four things wrong with them in two days, and I cannot decide whether I want to poison him or work for him."

The terms came on the eighteenth of October, in the long room, with Dunnel present because Emeric had asked for him and Ward present because Ward did not ask.

Vantic read them off a single sheet in a flat voice, and it took eleven minutes.

The principal was what Ferra had asked for and about a fifth more, and the fifth more was for the second granary, which he had put back in and named as a condition rather than a favour.

The term was eleven years. The interest was lower than Emeric had braced for and higher than anything he could service out of nothing.

The security was the harvest, all of it, every year, until the balance was paid, and the foundry, and the foundry's output, and the wheel, and the buildings on the eastern arm.

"And the covenant," Vantic said.

"Say it slowly."

"No new industry. Kaldo may operate everything it has. It may repair, replace, and maintain, and it may build the granary in the schedule because the granary is in the schedule. It may not commence any new works, trade, manufacture, or undertaking not listed on the second page while any part of the principal is outstanding." He turned the sheet round so Emeric could see the list. "My house has lent to eleven baronies in ninety years. Nine of them failed for the same reason, which is that a man who has built one thing successfully builds four more before the first one has paid for itself, and my house is then a creditor of a barony with five half-finished undertakings and no cash. I am not accusing you of anything. I am telling you what my house has learned about men who can do what you can do."

Emeric looked at the second page for some time.

The glasshouses. The kilns. The rapids foundry on the western arm that he had drawn in April and never shown anybody. Six of the nine deferrals off the list he had recited to Dunnel from memory in July, on the day he chose a water wheel over a second granary and wrote out his reasoning so that if it went wrong it would not have been bad luck.

All of it barred for eleven years, on the second page, in a clerk's hand.

"You've read my list," he said.

"I have read Mistress Ferra's book, which contains a column of things you have deferred and the reason for each." Vantic did not blink. "It is the most useful two pages in this barony and it is the reason my house is lending at all. A man who writes down what he is not doing and why is a man whose next four years I can predict. Do not take the covenant as an insult. It is priced into the rate."

"Say the rest of it," Ward said.

"There is a resident factor." Vantic set the sheet down. "Book rights and ledger rights, entry to any account of the barony or the venture at any hour, and a seat in any room where money is decided. Not a veto. I want that clear in front of witnesses, because it is the thing people most often believe and it is not true. I cannot stop you doing anything. I can write down that you did it, and my house reads what I write."

"For eleven years."

"For as long as any part of it is outstanding."

"And who is the factor," Emeric said, and then heard his own question land and answered it himself. "You're the factor."

"I am the factor. I have taken a house on the mill road for a year with an option on a second year, which I did on the fourteenth, four days before writing these terms, because I did not want to be the man who negotiates a covenant he intends to enforce and then discovers he has nowhere to sleep."

Dunnel spoke for the first time in forty minutes.

"The seed," he said.

Vantic turned to him with the specific attention of a man who has been waiting for somebody in the room to be the person he needs.

"The seed store is excluded from the security," Dunnel said. "It is outside the venture and it is outside the barony's pledgeable estate. It cannot be put in, valued, or pledged. It is written in the venture's own book in two sets of words on two pages, and I made him have it written twice because clerks misread things once."

"I have read both."

"Then say which one governs."

"Both. That is the point of writing it twice and it was competently done." Vantic took a second sheet out of the case. "My house does not want your seed. My house wants your harvest, and a harvest requires seed, and a security interest in the seed is a security interest in the ability to grow the thing I am lending against, which is the sort of clause that looks strong on a page and ruins a lender in the fourth year. I have excluded it in writing and I have excluded it more thoroughly than your clause does, because your clause protects it from the venture and mine protects it from me." He handed it across. "Read it. Object to it. I would rather you objected now."

Dunnel read it for eleven minutes while nobody said anything at all, and then put it down and looked at the man for a while.

"He's better at this than I am," he said, to Emeric, without particular pleasure.

"I know."

"Then sign it."

He signed it on the nineteenth of October in the long room with four witnesses, and the second granary went into a schedule with a completion date in June, and the glasshouses and the kilns and the rapids foundry went onto a page in a locked case in a house on the mill road, and the barony of Kaldo stopped being a place that owed nothing and had nothing and became a place that owed a great deal and could eat.

Afterward Vantic stayed behind while the others went out, and did the one unprofessional thing he did all month.

"May I ask you something that is not in the report."

"Go on."

"I was given a file in the capital before I rode out. It is not a secret, it is not slander, it is the ordinary sort of thing my house keeps on a man it may lend to, and it was three pages long, and I read it four times on the road because I have found that a file read four times tells you what the writer wanted." Vantic put his case down. "It said that His Highness Emeric had a tenth of a mage's output. It said he had been removed from the succession and sent to the poorest holding in the kingdom. It said, in the sort of sentence a clerk writes when he is repeating a room, that he was not expected to prosper, and it used a word about his character which I will not repeat in his own hall."

"You can repeat it. I've heard all of them."

"I would rather not, because the difficulty I am having is not with the word." Vantic looked at him. "I have spent six days walking a barony that has a foundry, a standardised measure, a threshing machine, a water ring, a drying shed that shuts itself, forty suits of interchangeable armour, and a plague that stopped. I have read a book kept by a woman who cannot be flattered. I have watched a priest hand you his church and a smith argue with you in front of your own garrison. Whatever was in that file was written by men who were describing something, and it was not this."

"They were describing me accurately," Emeric said. "They just weren't describing this."

"I do not follow."

"There's nothing in this barony I did on my own. Ward stopped me losing the harvest, and he did it by overruling me. Imani gave me the map and she'd had it five years. Bram filed the bar and built the breakers while telling me I was wrong, and he was right about the breakers and right about being wrong. Ferra prices everything I want before I want it. Halloway told me the straw was thin twice and I heard it as weather both times." He shrugged. "The file's about a man in a court with nobody standing next to him. That man is exactly what they said he was. I'd have been him for the rest of my life."

Vantic wrote something down. It was the only time in six days that Emeric saw him write anything without saying what it was.

"I will file the terms tonight," he said, picking up the case. "One matter of housekeeping. From tomorrow, when you decide a thing that costs money, I am in the room. Not for approval. For the record."

"Understood."

"You will hate it in about four months." Vantic went to the door. "Everybody does, and the ones who complain in the first week are generally fine, and the ones who are pleasant about it for a year are the ones who have another set of books."

Emeric got the list out that night and left the space empty at the top, and wrote underneath it:

Signed for eleven years against the harvest and the foundry and the ground under both. I read the second page four times looking for the thing that was unfair in it and there is nothing unfair in it. He has barred six of the nine deferrals and he found the nine on Ferra's page, which she keeps because I asked her to, which means the most useful thing I have written all year is the thing that has been used against me first.

The granary is in the schedule with a date in June. He put it in himself. I had it on my own list in July, second, and traded it for a wheel, and now a man from a lending house has made it a condition of the money because he can read a burned building. I have got the thing I should have built by losing the argument to somebody who was not here for any of it.

Dunnel wrote the seed clause twice in two sets of words in July because clerks misread things once, and it held against a professional, and then the professional wrote a better one and handed it to him to object to. That is twice this year that the only wall between this barony and something very bad has been a page written by a man who has put nothing into the venture and does not appear to like me.

He asked me about the file and I answered honestly and I have been sitting here for an hour deciding whether the honest answer was also the true one. I do not think I could have done any of this alone. I am not certain, and there is no way to test it, and the fact that I want there to be one is the answer to a question nobody asked me.
