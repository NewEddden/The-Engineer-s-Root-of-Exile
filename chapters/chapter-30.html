Chapter 30 - The Mill

In the first week of November, with the lower ward's tap newly running and the winter wheat not yet in, Emeric went down to look at the mill.

It had been on the pile since August: one of the leases Hale had put in front of him the week he came home, with the ground under it the barony's and the term run out in the summer, and nobody since then with the time to renew it. He had put it aside with the widow's barn and the boundary on the west side. Now he took it out again, for a reason that had nothing to do with leases.

The mill stood on the town side of the river below the east bridge, where the current came round the bend hard enough to see. It was old. The stone was old and the timber was older and the wheel was the oldest thing there, a great dark undershot thing taller than two men, black with wet and green at the hub, turning in the race with a sound like somebody breathing in a large room. Over the door somebody had cut a name into the lintel, and a date beneath it nearly a hundred years old.

HARL.

The man who came out from under it was past fifty and floury to the elbows, and stooped from a lifetime of bending to the stones. He was Harl as well. So had his father been, and his father's father.

He knew who Emeric was. Everybody in Kaldo knew by now. He did not seem to think it was any reason to stop work, and Emeric followed him in and stood out of the way while he finished a sack, and looked at the gear.

He stopped the stones once while Emeric watched, took a pinch of meal from the spout and rubbed it between his thumb and two fingers, frowning at nothing, and then lowered the runner stone by a turn of a wheel no bigger than a plate. He started them again. He did not explain what he had felt in the meal, and Emeric did not ask, because he had a fair idea that the answer would be thirty years long.

It ground for a town with half its fields empty. The stones turned perhaps a third of the day, and the wheel turned all of it, all night and every winter, in the dark, pulling on nothing. Harl lived alone in the rooms above the stones. His wife had gone in the first spring of the plague, Ferra had told him, and his two sons the summer after, and Harl had buried all three in the rows and gone back down the stair and ground the town's grain through the rest of it, because somebody had to and there was nobody else. In October, when Doran's carts took the rows down to the hollow below the wall, he had walked behind his three as far as the gate, and come back, and opened the sluice.

Emeric renewed the ground lease that morning, at the old rate, standing at the mill's door. He told Harl that it did not depend on anything else, and would not, and Harl looked at him as though he was waiting to hear what it did depend on.



What he wanted was the wheel.

Wire wanted a pull. Steady, heavy and patient, hour after hour, the same strength at the end of the day as at the start, and nothing in Kaldo gave that except the river. Horses and men tired. Stones ran down and had to be fed, and the man who fed them was a job, and Emeric had been in the capital and seen what that came to. The river did not tire and did not need feeding, and here it was, turning a wheel all night with nothing on it.

He did not say any of that. He did not say the other thing either, the larger one under it, which was that the river was where everything Kaldo would need in five years was going to have to come from, and that the man who stood on the river with a wheel would matter more than the man who stood in the keep.

Bram asked him what the wire was for.

"Later," Emeric said.

Bram gave him the look he gave a weld he did not trust, and let it go.



He bought a tenth of the mill the following week, with his own coin.

Harl would sell that much. He needed the money, the way everybody in Kaldo needed money, and a tenth was not the mill, and the price was what the town would have called a fair price for a tenth of an old wheel and a tired building with one old man in it. Ferra counted it out of the pouch and wrote it down, and Hale wrote it down again, and the tenth was Emeric's.

Then he let himself be seen.

He went down to the mill twice in the next week, in daylight, and stood with Harl on the bank looking at the wheel. He sent Bram down to measure it, and Bram went, with a rule and a slate and his usual face, and spent a morning on the bank where half the lower ward could see him doing it. The big plain carriage from the capital stood outside the mill door on two afternoons running while Emeric was inside, and he had not needed a carriage to go a quarter of a mile, and knew it.

"A quarter of a mile," Rylan said on the second afternoon, holding the carriage door. "You could have walked it in the time it took to put the horses to."

"I could."

"You want them to see the carriage."

"Yes."

Rylan looked at the carriage, and the mill, and the lower ward watching both from its doorways, and said that he had always wondered what princes were for.

He did not say a word to anyone about what the mill might be worth, or whether anyone else wanted it. He did not need to. That was the whole of the play, and he knew exactly how it worked, because he had watched it work in another life in rooms with better chairs. Nothing about the mill had changed. A prince had looked at it twice, and the looking was the news.

Within a fortnight the better-off of Kaldo were at Harl's door.

Pell came first. He had the biggest cloth business left on the high street, which was a smaller thing than it had been before the war, and he came down to the mill in his good coat and asked Harl, very civilly, whether he would consider selling a share. The widow Brannock, who owned more of the west farms than anybody but the barony, sent her steward to ask the same thing, and when the steward came back without an answer she came herself. And a man who lent money from a back room off the high street, and had never in his life been seen near a mill, sent his nephew to ask what a share might cost, in case his uncle should ever take an interest.

The widow came herself on a Thursday, on a grey pony, a small upright woman in black with a face like a drawn purse. She saw Emeric on the bank and inclined her head to him as far as his title required and not a hair further, and went in to Harl, and was inside a quarter of an hour. When she came out she stopped the pony beside him.

"You've cost my steward two pairs of boots," she said, and rode off up the bank before he could work out whether it was a complaint.

None of them had wanted a share of Harl's mill in April. None of them had wanted it in August.



Ferra put it to him at her table, on the evening the moneylender's nephew went down to the river.

"Nothing about that mill is different," she said, "except who's standing in it."

"I know."

"You know." She put her pen down, which she did not often do. "You're selling them your face. Pell's going to pay you for having stood on a riverbank. The widow's going to pay for your carriage."

"They're going to pay for a share of the strongest wheel on the river." He had been waiting for this since the first visit and found that he was enjoying it. "That was worth something in April. Nobody in Kaldo noticed, because nobody in Kaldo looked. I looked. Now they've noticed. Their money's been sitting in strongboxes since the plague doing nothing. If some of it goes into the mill, it's doing something."

"It's doing you something."

"It's doing copper. That's where it goes. Every coin of it."

"This time." She did not move. "And next time you want money, you'll know how to get it. Stand somewhere twice and wait for the knock. And next time it might not be a mill, and it might not be copper, and the price will still be made of nothing but you." She tapped the table once, beside her book. "When you stop standing there, the price comes down. Pell paid it. Pell's the one who loses it. He's a draper with a shop half the size it was, and he'll have bought a piece of your attention, and when your attention goes somewhere else he'll have bought nothing."

He had an answer to that, and it was a good one, about who had sold the mill short in the first place and who had let the wheel turn empty for four years, and he gave it to her. She had an answer to his answer. It was better than his. He found a hole in it and said so, and she agreed that the hole was there and then showed him that it did not matter, and they went at it for most of an hour across her table with the lamp between them and Bram coming in from the forge, listening for a minute in the doorway, and going out again.

It was the first time the argument had no sum in it at all. Every argument before this one had come down, in the end, to what the books said, and there she had always been right, and they had both known it. This one had nothing in it to look up. She was not right, exactly, and neither was he, and both of them were better at it by the end of the hour than at the start.

He did it anyway.

She knew he was going to. She had known it from the beginning, he thought, and had argued it to the end regardless, because it needed arguing.

When he had gone round it the last time and come out in the same place, she picked up her pen and turned to the page for the mill, and he waited for the line. Said so. Overruled. With the date.

She wrote the date and the price of the tenth, and nothing under it.

He looked at the blank space under the figure for longer than he meant to. She did not look up. She went on to the next page, which was the ash, and he did not ask her about the line she had not written, and she did not tell him.



He went down to the mill in the third week of December, a week into the frost, to make Harl an offer for the rest of it.

He had the numbers. The numbers were good. He had worked out, from the bidders, what the town now thought the mill was worth, and he offered Harl more than that, out of the money the bidders would pay for the share he meant to sell them, which was a piece of arithmetic he was quietly proud of. Harl would keep the rooms above the stones as long as he lived. He would keep grinding, and be paid a wage for it, and never have to wonder again whether a bad harvest meant a bad year. Emeric would have the wheel. Everybody would come out of it better off. He laid it out on the bench by the hopper, in the flour, carefully, with Ward in the doorway behind him.

Harl listened to all of it with his floury hands hanging at his sides.

Then he said, "No."

Emeric waited for the counter-offer. There was always a counter-offer. This was the part of it where the man named his price.

"You've had them all down here," Harl said. "The draper. The widow. The lender's nephew, that's never carried a sack in his life." There was no anger in it. "Every one of them at my door, because you stood on my bank. And now you."

"I'm offering you more than any of them."

"I know you are." Harl looked past him, up at the lintel over the door, where his name was cut and his father's before him. "I've buried my wife. I've buried both my boys. I ground grain through the whole of it with nobody to carry the sacks but me. There's nothing you can take off me now I haven't lost already." He brushed the flour off the bench where Emeric's figures had been drawn in it, slowly, with the side of his hand, until they were gone. "I've lost. And you can't buy it."

Emeric stood there with his good numbers and nothing to do with them.

He had read the economics right. Every piece of it had worked exactly as he had said it would: the tenth, the visits, the carriage, the knock at the door. He had read the man wrong.

"Then what would you take?" Emeric said.

"I don't know yet." Harl went back to his hopper. "Come back when I do."

Ward said nothing on the way out.

Behind them, in the dark under the mill, the wheel went on turning, as it had turned every night for a hundred years, for nobody.
