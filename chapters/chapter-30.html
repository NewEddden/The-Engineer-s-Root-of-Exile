Chapter 30 - Fifty-Eight Names

A year and two months after he said the farm eats first, Emeric went down to the lower ward to sort the garrison, and almost all of the work had already been done by a man leaning on a post.

Ward had stood in that yard since the muster. Not every day and not on any schedule, and never once with a sheet in his hand, and the men had stopped noticing him inside a fortnight the way you stop noticing weather.

What he brought up the hill in the second week of that winter was four pages in his own handwriting, which was bad, and it was the most complete document anybody had ever produced about the armed men of Kaldo.

Which of them carried. Which of them complained, and about what, because there is a complaint that means a man is tired and a complaint that means a man has decided the work is beneath him. Which of them were good with a crew and which had turned out to be good with nothing at all. Which of them, in the four hundred days since Wexler went north in a carriage, had shown that they had been Wexler's friends rather than Kaldo's soldiers.

The formal sort was confirmation. There was nothing in it that had to be discovered.

Emeric sat with the four pages on the Tuesday morning and allowed himself about a minute of being pleased with himself, because that was what the year of deferral had bought. He had not known which forty-two of the fifty-eight were real, and rather than find out with a roll and an interrogation in a cold season, he had put them all on a wage and let a year do it.

He was right about that. It is worth being clear that he was right about that.

The clean-out ran that afternoon, in the yard, with the whole garrison stood in it.

Sixteen names went first and went easily. They had been struck through in a magistrate's evidence in the capital a year ago and they had gone on drawing pay for eleven months afterward because nobody had done the paperwork, and Ferra's census had said as much a year and a half back, and the only thing that happened when Emeric read the sixteen names out was that four of the men standing there laughed, once, in the way men laugh at something that is not funny.

Forty-two left, and all forty-two of them had faces.

Then the ones who had been Wexler's. Not many. A handful who had carted grain for him out of the barony's own stores on nights when the barony's own stores were the only thing standing between the lower ward and a bad month, who had taken his money for it, and who would have taken anybody's, and one or two of whom did not seem to understand even now that this had been a thing rather than simply how a Tuesday worked.

Four of them he moved rather than turned out. Two to the mining hamlet, one to the west farms as a road warden, one onto the ferry. They were not good men and he did not pretend to himself that they were. They were men with families in this barony who had done a stupid thing under a man who had held their pay, and there is a difference between that and the rest of it, and Emeric drew the line where he drew it and wrote down where he had drawn it.

The rest went, and they went in front of everybody.

He did that on purpose, and the reasoning was sound and he would have defended it in any room. A garrison that hears about its own cleaning afterward learns that things happen to men in this barony behind a door. A garrison that watches it learns what the standard is and can measure itself against it.

So he stood in the yard with the ledger open and named them and told them why, item by item, with dates, and it took a quarter of an hour, and by the end of it nobody in that yard had any question at all about what the rules were.

The line garrison settled at forty. With Ward, Rylan, Declan and Osric, who were capital men and had never been on Kaldo's roll, the barony had forty-four.

The men who went out the gate that afternoon walked into the winter carrying an accurate and detailed picture of a place with forty-four armed men in it, and into the nearest hall that would feed them for it. Emeric did not think about them again for a year and a half.

Then he did the last item on the page.

He had been carrying it for a year.

It had come out of the ledger work in the autumn before last, in the middle of the Wexler accounts, when he and Ferra had been three weeks into the barony's books and finding something rotten roughly every second evening. It had not been a large thing. That was why it had survived a year in his head rather than getting dealt with in a week.

Draws against the garrison's provisioning line. Corliss had held that line for eleven years, because somebody has to buy the boots and the lamp oil and the winter salt and the flour, and it is the dullest job in any garrison and it is given to the man who will actually do it.

Sixty-one draws over eleven years that did not match anything that had arrived.

And Emeric had checked it. He had checked it properly, which is the part that matters and the part he would say afterward and which would not help him at all.

He had the ledger entries themselves, in Corliss's hand, signed. He had Ferra's reconstruction of what had actually been delivered against what had been paid for, built out of the supply book and three separate suppliers' receipts, and the gap was consistent and the gap was real. He had the timing, which fell on Corliss's own rotations and nobody else's. And he had gone and put it to the man directly, in the guardroom, alone, in the spring.

Corliss had said yes.

Not evasively. Not with an excuse attached. Emeric had asked him whether he had taken money out of the provisioning line and Corliss had said that he had, and Emeric had asked how much and Corliss had said that the number in the book was the number, and then Corliss had stopped talking and had not started again, and Emeric had taken that silence for what a silence like that is nine times in ten.

Four independent confirmations and an admission from the man's own mouth.

There was no error anywhere in his data. There was no error anywhere in his reasoning from it.

So he did it the same way he had done the rest of that afternoon. Publicly, with the ledger open, in front of forty men, because a garrison should watch its own cleaning.

He named Corliss a thief in the yard.

He did it without cruelty, which he was privately rather proud of. He laid the sixty-one draws out with the dates and the amounts and the gap against delivery, and he said that the man had admitted it in the spring, and he said that eleven years of it in a barony where people had been eating fish heads was the kind of thing he intended to be the last of.

Corliss did not deny it.

He stood there with his hands at his sides and let it happen, and when Emeric finished and gave him the room, which he did, deliberately, because he was not a man who condemned people without letting them speak, Corliss said that the numbers in the book were correct.

And then nothing else.

That was what convinced the yard. A man who is innocent argues. A man who stands there and confirms the arithmetic and declines to say another word has been caught, and every one of the forty men in that yard read it exactly that way, and so did Emeric, and so would anybody.

The reason he said nothing was that the money had gone to people whose families were standing in that town, four hundred yards away, and did not know it.

Ward came up the hill that evening and did not sit down.

"I would have asked him first."

"I did ask him. In the spring. He admitted it to my face."

Ward looked at him for a moment.

"You asked the ledger. Then you asked him to agree with the ledger. That is the same question twice."

He went out. He did not raise his voice and he did not stay to argue and he never mentioned it again for the rest of his life, and Emeric would have preferred an hour of shouting.

Doran came up the hill the following morning.

It was the second time in his life that he had done it. The first had been in the plague, to ask a baron for something, and he had not got it.

He was furious in the quiet way of a man who has spent forty years not raising his voice, and he stood in the middle of the hall and did not take the chair and did not take his hat off, and the first thing he said was a question.

"How many of the plague dead are in my book?"

Emeric said that he did not have the figure to hand.

"Nine hundred and four. That is what is written down." Doran's hands were shaking slightly and his voice was not. "Imani's mother counted more than eleven hundred before she died and she was not a woman who guessed."

He let that stand for a moment.

"Two hundred and eleven names in that book have a fee marked against them and no family that paid it. I told you about that. Standing in my own vestry, when you came for the death ledger, and you had a great many other things in your head that week and I have not held it against you until this morning."

Emeric remembered it.

That was the thing. He did not have to be reminded and he did not have to go and look. He remembered the vestry, and the cold, and the smell of the candle stub, and a priest saying, in the middle of a long administrative answer about burial fees, that the unclaimed ones had always somehow been paid for and that he had never known by whom and had long ago stopped asking, because the answer might have made it stop.

Emeric had filed it under the parish's own arrangements and had gone on to the next item, because he had been carrying two hundred items that week and a line item that resolved itself was not a problem.

"It was him," Emeric said.

"It was him."

Doran gave him all of it, standing up, in about four minutes, because he had been assembling it for eleven years without ever having anyone to give it to.

A body has to go in the ground. The parish cannot do it for nothing, because the parish has no nothing to do it with, and the fee was not a wicked fee, it was two men's day-wage and a plank. In the plague years there were households where everyone who could earn was in the same bed as everyone who could not, and there were households where there was nobody left to ask.

Somebody bought the boxes. Deal boards, cheap, not good, but boxes. Somebody dug the ground, and kept digging it, in the year when the men who normally dug it were in it.

And somebody paid the parish, in silver, at the door, quietly, every time, so that the name could be written down.

That was the part Doran came up the hill about. The digging could have been done by anyone with a spade and a strong stomach. The fee was what put the name in the book. A man who is not in the book was never here.

Corliss had a team. He never gave Doran their names and Doran never asked for them.

He had done it for eleven years out of the garrison's provisioning line, because there was no other purse in this barony and no honest way to ask for one.

"He could have come to me," Emeric said, and heard how it sounded while it was still leaving him.

"He could have come to Wexler for nine of the eleven years." Doran put his hat on, which he had not taken off. "He has had you for one. You have been busy. Everyone says so."

Emeric sat down after he had gone and went through it with the coldness that was available to him, which was considerable, because it was the only instrument he had that was working.

The money had moved. That was true. He had it four ways and all four were right.

The man had taken it. That was true. He had said so.

Every single thing Emeric had believed on the Tuesday afternoon was a fact, and remained a fact, and was still a fact now.

What was missing was a column. There had never been a column in that ledger, or in any ledger in the world, for where a thing went after it left. A ledger records what moved. It does not record why. He had spent one life in a discipline where the why could usually be recovered from the what if you were careful enough with the what, and he had brought the habit across without ever once examining it.

And he had been handed the answer. In a vestry, in the cold, by a priest who had walked him through the whole thing without knowing he was doing it, and Emeric had filed it under solved.

He had not been careless. That was the unbearable part of it and he could not find a way to make it mean anything else.

He did the apology on the Thursday, in the same yard, in front of the same forty men.

There was no other currency available. He could have gone to the guardroom and said it privately and it would have been worth nothing, because the thing that had been broken had been broken in the open.

So he stood where he had stood on Tuesday and said that he had been wrong, and said what the money had been for, as much of it as Doran had allowed him to say, and said that he had accused a man of theft on the strength of a ledger and a silence and that he had not asked the only question that mattered because it had not occurred to him that there was one.

It was a good apology. It was specific and it took the whole of the blame and it did not explain anything away.

It was not enough, and everyone in that yard knew it was not enough, including him.

He had unmade a man's name in public on a Tuesday and remade it on a Thursday, and the town heard the first one clearly, because a prince calling a guard a thief is a thing that travels by dinnertime, and the town heard the second one as a prince covering himself.

Both versions were in the market by the weekend and the second one did not displace the first one. It sat next to it. In four years there were still people in the lower ward who could tell you that Corliss had been caught at something and that it had been smoothed over because he had been useful.

Corliss accepted the apology. He said the words, properly, in the yard, in front of the men, because he understood what was being attempted and would not make it worse.

Then he went back to his post.

He was never warm to Emeric again. Not hostile, not sullen, and never once difficult about anything. He turned up, he did the provisioning, he did it well, and when he was asked a question he answered it accurately and completely and stopped.

It was the specific coldness of a man who had been right and had not been asked, and there was nothing whatsoever to be done about it, and Emeric spent some years finding out how little.

Rylan took it worse than Corliss did.

He worked it out about a day before Emeric did, which nobody ever knew, because he did not tell anybody and he did not go up the hill and he did not say a word in the yard.

He had sat in the parish house a year and a half earlier plotting dots on a sheet of Emeric's paper, marking where the dead had been, and he had come to a name and stopped, and Bram had put a hand on his shoulder and left it there for a while. A boy he had played with in the lower ward. Two streets over.

That family could not pay. Emeric knew that now because he had gone and looked at the book, in the vestry, on the Wednesday evening, on his own, with a candle, like a man checking a sum he already knew the answer to.

The fee was marked. The name was in the book.

Rylan said almost nothing about any of it.

What changed was the temperature. He answered when spoken to. He rode out when told. He did not stop being good at his job and he did not stop being the man the town opened for, and he never again, for a very long stretch, said the sort of thing to Emeric that he had said on the road back from Geddes's place about being unbearable since Tuesday.

It came back slowly. It did not come back all the way.

The law was the only good thing that came out of that week and Emeric wrote it inside four days.

Burials for those who cannot pay are a barony expense. Write it in the book, submit it to Ferra, and it is approved. No sneaking, no shame, no application, no assessment of whether the household deserves it, and no guard covering a line item for eleven years out of a purse he had to hide.

He read it out in the yard, and then he said the reason out loud, once, because he wanted it on a record somewhere rather than sitting in his own head where he could quietly amend it later.

Every man in this barony goes into the ground properly. Not according to what he was worth, or what he could pay, or whose side he was on. A body is the last thing a person has. What a place does with its dead is the clearest statement it will ever make about itself, and people can hear it from a long way off, and they remember it longer than they remember anything else you do.

Several of the men took him to be talking about the plague dead.

He was.

He also, without knowing it, said a sentence in a cold yard in the second winter that he was going to be held to some years later by a corpse he had not met.

He extended it past burials before the month was out, because once you have found one of these you start looking for others, and he found three. A widow's cartage on the west road that had been coming out of a crew boss's own pocket since the spring. The lamp oil for the parish hall on class nights, which Doran had been paying for out of the collection and had never mentioned. And the boat.

The ore quote came in from Grayrock that same week.

Saban brought it up with the winter goods and Ferra checked it against the autumn one and against eleven years of what Wexler had paid, and it was higher again. Not much. Inside what a hard winter at the workings would explain.

She put the variance in the same column she had put the last one in, and then she said it out loud, once, standing in the doorway of the small room with the sheet in her hand.

Emeric was in the middle of the worst week he had had since Tam and he did not hear her.

Petram stopped talking that month and Emeric did not connect it for a very long time.

The monthly visits had been running for a year. Three questions, always the same three, and a little more every time, and by that winter Emeric had something that nobody else in the kingdom had, which was a working picture of how the organization finds the men it uses.

The next visit after the yard was short.

Not hostile. Petram was never hostile. He answered what was put to him, briefly, and offered nothing, and when Emeric waited, which was the whole technique, Petram waited too, and won.

The one after that was shorter.

Then there was nothing.

Emeric put it down to the winter, and to the labor, which was hard and had been hard for a year, and to the plain fact that a man eventually runs out of things to say about the worst decision he ever made. All three of those were reasonable and he wrote the first of them in the book.

The week his cousin was called a thief in a yard in front of forty men was the week it stopped. Emeric had the date of the yard and the date of the visit written on two different pages in two different books and did not put them next to each other for years.

He wrote the sentence at the end of the week and he hated it enough that he put it on its own page.

A ledger records what moved. It does not record why. On Earth the why could usually be got back out of the what, given enough of the what, and here it cannot, because here the why lives in people.

And he read the people last. He had done it with Bram, and with the town, and with Sela, and he had corrected it each time and been pleased with himself for correcting it, and then he had done it again in a yard in front of forty men with a book open in his hands.

The other thing he did that winter he told nobody about, including Ward.

He went looking for the man who had walked out to the mill road. He had forty names now, real ones, with a year of a watcher's notes attached to each of them, and rotations, and a provisioning line that told him who had been where, and he went through all of it twice.

He found nothing. He went through it a third time in February and found nothing again, and concluded that he had missed something, and started once more.

There was nothing to find. The man had never been in the garrison. Emeric spent eleven weeks proving a negative and never once recognized that he was doing it.

The last item on the winter's list was armor and it got one line, because there was nothing to write under it.

What the capital sent a prince's guard was mediocre steel over good crystals, and he had known for a year exactly what that was worth on a bad day, and he had a foundry that was drawing properly and pouring badly and would not be making anything worth putting on a man for two years at best.

He wrote the word down, and the date, and nothing else.

By the end of that winter Kaldo had forty-four armed men, a wage board, a foot, a foundry, a farm in its second year, a trading arm, a school with no date on it, and a burial law that meant nobody in this barony would ever again be buried out of a purse somebody had to hide.

It had also, on a Tuesday afternoon, watched its prince stand in a yard with a ledger open and get a man's whole life exactly backwards, out loud, in public, with all of his evidence correct.

The town remembered the Tuesday.
