Chapter 171 - Storage

The cells were dying.

Tess had a column for it in her book. She had kept the powerhouse cells for four years, the jars of zinc and copper and acid that ran the telegraph sounders and the line room's bench and every instrument Kaldo had that wanted a small steady current and no dynamo; and a cell, she said, was a thing that ate itself to make you a current. The zinc went into the acid a little every hour, and when the zinc was gone the cell was a jar of dirty water. You threw it out and made another. On the telegraph that did not matter. A sounder took so little that a cell lasted months.

A bulb was different. She had lit the fourth bulb off the bench cells for a whole night, and the fifth, and by the morning after the fifth there were three jars on the bench that had nothing left in them.

"Three a night," she said. "For one light. The ship will have twenty."

"The ship can carry a dynamo."

"The ship can carry a dynamo that turns when the propeller turns." She turned her book round to show him. "When she's sitting in a harbor with the propeller stopped, it doesn't. When she's in a dead zone and the stones have gone quiet and she's coming down on the sweeps, it doesn't, and that's the night she needs her lights more than any other." She tapped the page. "Something has to hold it. Hold what the dynamo makes, so she can spend it when the dynamo's not turning."

"Yes," said Emeric. "That's the next thing."

"I know it's the next thing." She turned the book back. "I'm asking you what it is."



It was lead.

That was the beauty of it, and the reason he had left it so long: it was too simple, and he had not trusted it to be simple. Two plates of lead, standing in a jar of dilute vitriol, not touching. Put a current through them from the dynamo, in at one plate and out at the other, and something happened on their faces: the one went a dark chocolate brown, and the other a soft grey, and bubbles came up off them both. Take the dynamo away and put the two plates to a bulb, and the current came back out of them, the other way. And when it was spent, the plates went pale again and the acid went weak, and you put the dynamo back on, and it all happened again. Not eating itself. Storing. Filling and emptying, like a cistern.

"A storage battery," he said, at the bench.

"Lead," said Bram, from the door, in the tone in which he might have said dirt. Then, when nobody laughed: "We've got lead."

They had. The lead works at the river end of the brickfield had been making pipe for the water towers for three years, and sheet for the roofs, and shot for the garrison's slings, and the acid works beside it had been making vitriol since the second year. Kaldo had everything the thing needed lying in its own yards.

Which did not make it easy. The first plates were plain sheets, and they held almost nothing: a bulb lit off them for a quarter of an hour and went yellow. Plain sheet had only its two faces to work with. Emeric drew Bram a grid instead, a lattice of lead like a garden trellis, cast in a mould, and the grid's holes packed with a paste of lead oxide stirred up with the acid, so that the whole plate was face, all the way through, like a sponge. Bram cast the moulds and the grids and swore at the first ones, which came out of the mould with half their bars missing. Tess mixed the paste. It went on her hands, and Imani came across from the ward the next morning and stood at the bench and looked at Tess's hands and said a number of things, quietly, about lead, and the bench, and what lead did to a body that took it in through a cut or a mouth, that Emeric had told Tess himself and that Tess had heard without listening to. After that Tess wore gloves. Abloh's, oiled.

The first grid battery went on the dynamo line from the works on the ninth of February, at dusk, a row of six of Ivo's square glass jars on the line room floor with their plates in and their acid in and a smell coming off them like a struck match left in a cellar; and at dawn it lit two bulbs for most of the next night.

"How many times?" said Tess.

"Hundreds. If you don't drain it flat. Or let it freeze."

She wrote that down. Then she bent down beside her chair and picked up one of the jars, both hands, and set it on her knees, and looked at it.

"It weighs what a child weighs," she said.

"Yes."

"In glass." She put it down very gently. "Glass, in a ship that comes down on a slip."



She had the answer by the end of the week, and it was a barrel of tar.

Ferra had sold the tar from the coke ovens for two years, as she had said she would on the day she stood in the oven shed and told him he had been about to pour a barrel a week into a tub. Fletcher took it for pitch, and the salt boats took what Fletcher didn't, down to the coast, at a price under the pine pitch from the north that had made the pine men there say unkind things about Kaldo in every tavern on the shore. Ferra's line for it in her book had grown a little every quarter. It was not a large line. She was fond of it anyway.

And one barrel in five had not gone down to the coast. Tess had been keeping it.

She had told nobody why, except that she had a use for it. Ferra had let her, because the barrel went into the book against Tess's bench, as everything did, and Tess's bench had earned its keep more times than Ferra had bothered to count. And since the autumn Tess had been taking the tar down to the old whisky still at the bottom of the brickfield, with Haddon, on Sundays, and boiling it, and catching what came off it at each heat in a row of jars: a thin oil first, which stank; then a heavier one; then, at a higher heat, something that came over clear and set in the jar into long white needles as it cooled, with a sharp sweet medicinal smell that stayed on Haddon's coat for a week.

She had asked Emeric in November what it was. He had told her. Then he had told her, because she asked, what it did if you put it with something else, and what the something else was, and how you made that. He had told her it the way he had told her the bulb, on less than a page.

The something else was wood alcohol. The charcoal burners up the valley made it whether they meant to or not, a thin evil spirit that came off the wood in the kiln and that men drank sometimes and went blind of. You passed its vapour over copper gauze heated red, and what came out the other end, caught in water, was a liquid with a smell that made your eyes run and your nose sting, sharp and clean. Tess had made a jar of it in December and Haddon had refused to go into the shed with her the day she did.

Then the two together, the white needles and the stinging water, with a little lye, warmed in a pot; and the pot went thick, and then sticky, like warm honey, a dark amber; and the honey, poured into one of Bram's iron moulds and clamped in his press and heated, came out an hour later as something nobody in Kaldo had ever seen.

She brought it up to the line room in her lap, wrapped in a cloth, and unwrapped it on the bench.

A box. A small square box without a lid, the size of one of the battery jars, dark brown and smooth and faintly shining, as if it had been oiled, with the marks of Bram's mould on its sides. She picked it up and dropped it on the floor. It bounced. She picked it up and held the corner of it in the candle flame, and it went black at the corner and stayed exactly the shape it had been, and when she took it out of the flame it did not go on burning. She put the two wires from the bench cells to two sides of it with the galvanometer between, and the needle did not move.

"It doesn't melt," she said. "It doesn't burn. It doesn't carry. Acid doesn't touch it, I left one in vitriol for three days. Once it's set it's set, forever. You can't soften it again." She put it down. "I want to make all the battery jars out of it."

Emeric picked it up. It was warm still, from the mould, and lighter than glass, and it smelled faintly of the pot. He turned it in his hands. In the other life there had been a telephone on a desk in his grandmother's hall, heavy, black, with a dial; and this was the same stuff, near enough. The first of everything.

"Bakelite," he said.

"What?"

"It's what it was called. The man who made it first put his name on it."

Tess considered that.

"Well," she said, "I'm not doing that," and took the box back.

Ferra came to see it in the afternoon, because Haddon had told her about the white needles in the jars at last, now that there was no point in his not telling. She stood at the bench in her coat and turned the box over in her hands for some while without a word, and tapped it with a fingernail, and looked into it as if it might have something written on the bottom.

"From the tar," she said.

"From the tar," said Tess.

"My tar."

"The fifth barrel." Tess did not look up from her book. "You wanted to sell all of it."

"I sold four barrels in five," said Ferra. She set the box down on the bench, squarely, in front of Tess. "Which is more than anybody in this keep has ever sold of anything, without being asked twice." She looked at the box. "What does it cost to make one?"

Tess told her.

Ferra took her pencil out of her sleeve and wrote the figure down in the back of her own book, and below it another, and looked at the two of them. Then she looked at the box again, for a little longer than she needed to, and said nothing at all; and Emeric, who had known her for six years, understood that she had just priced it as a thing Kaldo would one day sell, and decided not to say so yet, and was annoyed with herself for not having thought of it in the oven shed.

By the end of February the battery jars were Bakelite, all of them, with Bakelite lids and lead posts coming up through the lids; and the telephone's two handsets had new Bakelite shells, which Tess said were warmer to hold against the ear than brass and which everybody in the line room agreed made a man's voice sound exactly the same as before. And on the right arm of Tess's chair, where the brass knob of her stick had been, which was cold in winter and had been cold every winter morning for two years, there was a new knob, dark brown and smooth and faintly shining. She did not mention it. Emeric did not either.



The first bulbs went where Imani said they went.

She had said it at the long table on the first of February, before there was a battery that would hold a night, as if she were stating the order of a ward round. First over the operating table. Then the school. Then, when there were enough, the keep; and the keep could have candles until then, as it had for three hundred years.

Nobody argued. Hale wrote it down in the minutes in the order she gave it.

So the first battery set that would hold a night went across the river to the ward on the twentieth, on a barrow, with one of Tess's linemen pushing and Tess riding beside on the motor; and a bulb went up over the table in Imani's cutting room, in a dish of Ivo's silvered glass that threw all its light down in a white circle the size of the table and none of it in anybody's eyes. The lineman ran the wire down the wall to a switch by the door. Imani stood in the door and watched him do it with her arms folded, and when he was finished she sent him away, and Tess too, and Emeric, because it was her room.

She sent for Emeric again at nine that night.

A quarryman from the Grayrock road had come in at dusk with his hand under a fallen block, the bones of it broken in more places than he could count and the skin torn to the wrist, and he was on the table with the ether mask over his face and one of her ward women at his head with the bottle and Imani's sleeves rolled to the shoulder. The white circle lay on the table and on the hand in it and on Imani's hands, and the rest of the room was dim; and the hand under the light was bright and clear and exact in every thread and splinter of it, and there were no shadows on it at all. Not hers. Not Mott's. Not anyone's.

Emeric stood by the door. She had not sent for him to help.

She worked for an hour and three quarters. He had watched her work before, many times, by lamp and candle, with somebody holding a light over her shoulder and moving it when she moved, and her head always a little to one side, out of her own light, and her neck aching after. Tonight she stood straight. She did not move her head once. She moved her hands, and the light stayed where it was, falling on them; and she did not have to ask anybody to bring it closer or lift it higher or hold it steady, because it was already closer and higher and steadier than anybody's hand had ever held anything.

When the last stitch was in she put her needle down in the dish and stepped back from the table and stood there, under the edge of the white circle, and looked at her own hands in it. She turned them over. She looked at the backs, and then the palms, the way a woman looks at her hands after she has taken off a ring she has worn for years, and Emeric saw how tired they were, and how steady.

"All my life," she said. Not to him. "My mother cut by a rush light. I learned on a rush light." She took the ether bottle off the side table and put the stopper in it. "I'll keep him three days. He'll keep the hand. He'd not have kept it by a lamp." She looked up at the bulb in its dish. "I wanted you to see it in use. Not on the bench."

"I've seen it."

"Then go and tell Tess." She went to wash. "And tell her the school's next, and I don't want to hear about the keep till the school has three."



The ship's lights would not last the night on carbon.

Tess had worked it out by the end of the month, on the bench, with the batteries and a carbon bulb and the galvanometer and her book. A carbon thread ran hot and white, but most of what went into it came out as heat, not light, and the batteries emptied into the heat. The K Discovery's set, at the weight Fletcher said she could carry low in her hull without changing how she sat, would light her saloon and her helms and her dials for half a night. And Ivo's wires still wept; the eighth bulb had gone out after four days on the bench with a faint grey bloom at the neck, where the air had crept back in round the copper.

"Half a night isn't the night," said Tess.

So it went in the forge's book: run fourteen. He did it on the twenty-sixth, alone, in the cellar, with the door shut.

There was a metal in the other life that melted hotter than any other metal there was, so hot that nobody could melt it in a furnace and cast it; you ground it to a powder and pressed the powder into a bar and heated the bar until the grains of powder grew into one another and became solid, without ever turning to liquid. Tungsten. A thread of it would run whiter than carbon, and give more of its heat as light, and last. The ore had come down from Grayrock's east workings in a sack in the autumn, heavy for its size, dark, and he had had the powder out of it by the slow way since, a little at a time.

The forge did what no furnace in the world could do yet. It took the powder in its cage, squeezed from every side at once in the lattice, and held it at a heat that would have run Bram's best steel like water, and he felt the grains go into each other one by one in the room's slow time, and closed them. A bar the length of his hand and the thickness of a reed, grey, heavier than lead. Then a set of drawing dies harder than anything Tess's plate had ever been made of, each hole a little smaller than the last. And last, a coil of a second wire, an alloy he cut in the cage to grow in the heat exactly as Ivo's glass grew, no faster and no slower, for the lead-in wires through the neck.

The rest was hers. She had the bar on her bench in the morning, and a gas flame, and Bram's swaging hammers; and she worked it hot, and drew it through the dies hot, one after another, slowly, a week of it, until what came off the last die was a wire so fine she had to hold it against a black cloth to see it. Tungsten filament. She coiled it round a needle, so that more of it fitted in the glass.

It ran three times as long on a battery's charge as carbon. Very nearly. She sat with it a night to be sure, and then another.

And Ivo sealed the new lead-in wires through his glass, and turned the bulb in his hand in the window, and put it on the shelf; and came back in a week and looked at the neck, and then in a second week; and said, grudgingly, that it had not wept.



Tess wired the ship in the last week of February.

She did it from her chair, mostly, with two of her linemen doing what the chair could not, which was lie on their backs in the hand's width of air between the inner hull and the outer and feed the cable along the ducts where Fletcher's ribs left room. She had it drawn before they started, every run of it. The battery set low in the hull aft of the stone-keeper's station, in Bakelite cases strapped into a timber cradle Fletcher built, twenty-four jars of it, which brought her stern down two inches until Fletcher moved four sacks of ballast forward and said nothing about it, ostentatiously. From the batteries, two runs of cable: one forward to the bow cockpit, one aft and up the ladder to the glass house on the dome. Switches at each station, Bakelite. Bulbs in Ivo's thickest glass behind brass guards in the saloon, the cabins, the passage, at the stair head and the stair foot. A shuttered lamp at the forefoot and another at the stern. A small bulb behind each dial. And the signal lamp, a shuttered bulb with a hand key, in the bow cockpit, so that Linus or anybody could flash code to the ground.

The batteries were charged from the shore by the works line, in the shed. In flight they were charged by a small dynamo that Bram geared to the propeller shaft in its arm, so that whenever the propeller turned, in the air or on the water, the batteries filled.

And on the same arm, on the other side of the shaft, the motor.

It was Tess's idea and Bram's iron, as most things were. An electric motor, not a large one, a dynamo running backwards, geared down to the propeller through a clutch that could be thrown in or out from the water helm. It would not fly her. It would not push her through the water at any speed worth the name; she was a hundred feet of oak and brass, and the motor turned her propeller the way a boy turns a mill wheel by hand. But it turned it. With no stone working at all. Silent, or as near as made no difference: a hum, under the water, and a trickle of bubbles at the blades.

They tried it on the night of the first of March, on the river, with the doors open and the ship afloat at the foot of the slip and every stone in her cases shut. Fletcher at the water helm. He threw the clutch in, and the hum came up out of the arm through the hull, and the K Discovery began to move. Out of the shed's mouth at a walking pace, into the dark, along the bank under the north wall, without a sound but the water at her stem; and Fletcher took her down past the water stair and round in a wide slow turn and back, and put her alongside the slip as gently as setting down a cup, and threw the clutch out.

"Three knots," said Linus, who had timed it from the bank. "Perhaps."

"Three knots," said Fletcher. He took his hands off the helm. "In a harbor that's all you want. Coming up to a quay at night, with nobody on it awake." He looked down at the water. "And if the stones go, she'll come home. Slowly. But she'll come home without anybody at the sweeps." He sounded, for Fletcher, almost moved. "I'll take it."



The first bulb inside the seal had lit before that, on the twenty-sixth of February, at dusk, and it was Maren's.

Not by any plan of Emeric's. Tess had wired the galley first because the galley run was the shortest and she wanted to try the whole circuit, battery to switch to bulb, on something short before she trusted the long ones; and Maren had heard there would be a light, and had come down to the shed with her apron on to see it, and was standing in her galley with her arms folded when Tess, at the station, threw the switch.

The galley lit. A small white light behind a brass guard in the ceiling, over the fire-stone range and the little oven and the copper sink, steady, clear, no flicker, no smoke, no smell. Every pot on its hook. Every knife in its rack.

Maren looked up at it.

Then she looked round her galley, slowly, at the range, and the shelf, and the back of the hatch, and the floor under the sink, as if she had never seen any of it before; and Emeric, standing in the passage door, saw her see it. She had cooked in this galley on forty night flights by the light of the lamp stone in the passage, which was the same small warm light it had always been, and which had never once reached into the corners.

She reached up and ran one finger along the shelf over the range, and looked at the finger.

"Well," said Maren. "I'll be down in the morning with a bucket."
