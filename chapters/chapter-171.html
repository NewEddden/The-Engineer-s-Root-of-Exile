Chapter 171 - Cloth

The levy book was the first book in twelve years that Ferra had not started herself.

Ward had started it. He had come to the long room on the first morning of summer with a book he had bought in the market, a cheap one with a grey board cover, and put it down in front of her open at the first page, and on the first page in his square hand he had ruled five columns and headed them Name. Of. Age. Signed. Mark. And he had said, "I'll want another one of these by the end of the week," and gone out.

She had four of them by the end of the month.

They came in at the Western Gate and at the market cross and at the camp's tally-shed on the flat, where Ward had put a table and a knight and a clerk from her own room, and they signed or made their mark, and the knight read them the terms, and they went off to the lower field to Enno. A Copper and a half a day and found, which was more than the quarry paid and less than the rail works. A coat. Boots, when there were boots. Three years, or until the barony discharged them. The terms were on a printed sheet nailed to every table and read aloud at every signing, and Imani had insisted on a line at the bottom about the lock and the hands and the ten count, and it was there.

Ferra read every page of every book at the end of every day, because she read every page of everything, and because somebody had to know who they were.

They were the reform's people. She could see it in the second column, Of. Freed tenants from the Wayford baronies, men whose debts had been claimed against their wages instead of their bodies since the notice, who had walked south because the wage was south. Men from the camp who had come for the quarry and the wall and stayed. Milford men. Grayrock men. Men off the island whose fathers had fished the lake and whose grandfathers had been Kaldo's own tenants when Kaldo was a ruin. Boys who wrote their age as eighteen and were not, whom Ward's knights sent home and who came back the next week to a different table.

At the bottom of the second book, on the eleventh day, in a careful joined hand she knew because she had taught the Kaldo primer to half the island's children and had marked this boy's copybook herself six years ago: Piers, son of Sela, of the island. Nineteen. Signed.

She read the line, and turned the page.

By midsummer there were six hundred and eleven names in the four grey books, and Ward said that was enough for the line, and closed them.

The coats came off the jenny.

There were four jennies now in the old granary by the mill, where the loom room had been since the spring, each one a frame of oak the size of a bed with eight spindles on it and a wheel at the side that a girl turned with one hand while she drew the carriage back and forth with the other, and on each one a girl did in a day what eight women did at eight wheels. The carpenter who had built the first one had named it for his wife, and the girls who ran them called all four by her name, and nobody in Kaldo called them anything else. The yarn went across the yard to the looms. The cloth came off the looms in grey lengths and went to the fullers at the lower water, and then to the dye vats at the ink works, where the woad was, and came back blue, and went to the long tables in the finishing room, where forty women on piece-work cut and sewed it to Ward's pattern.

Six hundred coats by the end of summer. Ferra had the figure in the book. It had not been possible a year ago. It would have taken the whole barony's wheels a winter to spin the yarn for six hundred coats, and there were not that many wheels in the whole barony, and the ones there were spun for the barony's own backs.

Now it took a month, and cost a third of what it would have, and six hundred men would stand in a line in blue.

The food came off Imani's register. That was the one line of it Ferra liked. Imani's students had noticed, keeping the ward's kitchen book, that broth sealed in a jar while it was still boiling hot did not go bad, not in a week, not in a month, and Imani had written it down and not known why, and Emeric had read it and known why and not said, and now the glasshouse was blowing jars by the hundred and the lower-water women were filling them with salt beef and pease and sealing them with pitch while they were hot. Ferra had the figure for the jars too.

Sela came to the long room on the last day of the second week of summer, in the afternoon, with a roll of paper under her arm, and asked for the prince.

Ferra knew what the paper was before she unrolled it. She had heard about it in the market for a fortnight.

Sela had never blinked at a prince in her life. She had been arguing with Emeric at the market cross, about one thing or another, for as long as there had been a market cross, and he had gone on listening to her, and it was generally agreed on the island that she was the only person in Kaldo besides Ferra whom the prince was a little afraid of. She came into the long room in her market apron and her good shawl and stood at the end of the table and did not sit, and unrolled the paper, and laid it flat in front of Emeric, and put a weight on each corner, and stood back.

It was a petition. It was headed in a clerk's hand, To His Royal Highness, from the spinsters of the baronies of Kaldo, Wayford, Milford and Grayrock, and under that there were three short paragraphs, and under those there were names. Columns of them, down the sheet and onto a second sheet pasted to the first and a third pasted to the second. Some signed. Most marked. Every mark with a name written beside it by whoever had carried that sheet round that village.

Ferra read the paragraphs over Emeric's shoulder. They were very plain.

The women who had signed it had spun yarn at home for money all their lives, and their mothers had before them, and it had been the money that was theirs, that came in at the door when the men's money did not, and that paid for the children's shoes and the salt and the doctor. Since the spring the yarn from the Kaldo jennies had come into every market in the four baronies at half the price of theirs, and the weavers would not buy theirs any more, and the factors would not, and a winter's spinning that had brought in a Silver last year would bring in a few Copper this. They did not ask for the jennies to be stopped. They knew they would not be. They asked what they were to do.

Emeric read it twice. Ferra watched him.

"How many?" he said.

"Two thousand and forty," said Sela. "I counted them."

He looked at the names for a long time.

"Your son's in the line," he said.

"My son's in the line," Sela said, "in a coat off your jenny. I know that. I'm not stupid. Piers's coat cost a third what it would have cost if I'd spun it, and I couldn't have spun it, not six hundred of them. I know that too." She put one finger on the petition, on the second paragraph. "And I know that Marta Crane at Milford has four children and no man and spun for their bread, and she can't sell a skein now to save her life, and it's not her fault and it's not yours, and I carried this round because she can't read and I can, and because somebody should."

Emeric did not say anything.

He did not say anything for long enough that Ferra, who knew his silences, knew that he did not have anything to say, and knew that he knew Sela knew it.

"I'll answer it," he said at last.

"When?"

"When I have an answer."

Sela looked at him. Then she nodded, once, as if that were more or less what she had expected, and more or less what she would have said in his place, and she turned and went out without taking the petition, and Ferra heard her on the stairs talking to Hobb about his mother's leg.

Emeric sat looking at the names.

"Give me the numbers," he said.

Ferra gave them to him. She had them already; she had had them since the first week the yarn went to market, because she had known this was coming, because it had come at Wayford with the ink, and at Milford with the paper, and it would come every time. The jennies' yarn at market, by the skein, against the price last year. The weavers' buying. The women's spinning by barony, from the old factors' books, which she had had Dace pull. What the four baronies' households had taken in at the door from spinning last year, as near as anybody could say, and what they would take in this year.

And on the other side: what six hundred coats had cost, and would have cost. What cloth cost in every market in the four baronies now, for everybody, for every child's shirt and every man's winter coat and every sheet. What the girls on the jennies were paid, and how many of them there were, and where they had come from, which was the same villages.

She laid the two sheets side by side in front of him.

He looked at them. She had known he would, and she had known what he would find, because she had already found it, lying awake. Both sets of numbers were true. Neither of them answered the other. You could not take what the four baronies saved on cloth and hand it to Marta Crane at Milford, because it was not in anybody's hand to give; it was in a thousand other people's children's shirts. And you could not give Marta Crane her spinning back without stopping the jennies, and if you stopped the jennies somebody at Aldane's end of the road would build one by next year, from a drawing, because anybody could.

"I don't have an answer," Emeric said.

"No."

"I'm going to say that to her."

"Yes," said Ferra. "You are. But not today."

The Crown's letter came in the same week, by the Crown's rider, since the line was dark.

It was from the chancery, not the king. It was on the chancery's good paper under the chancery's seal, and it was addressed to the lord of Kaldo, and it was very short. It noted that a levy of some hundreds of men had been raised in the barony of Kaldo under the barony's pay, drilled under arms, and clothed alike at the barony's cost. It noted that the realm's law, in the statute of the fourth king before this one, held that armed men raised and kept by a lord outside the Crown's summons were a private company. It noted that private companies were permitted within the realm, and listed the conditions under which they were. It did not say that Kaldo had broken any of them. It said that the chancery had entered the levy in its register, with the date on which the chancery had been informed.

It did not ask for anything. It did not summon anybody. It was not signed by any name Ferra knew.

She read it twice and put it in the book, under the date, with a line: The chancery has entered the levy.

"It's a record," Emeric said.

"It's a record," said Ferra. "Somebody's keeping one."

Vantic came for the summer quarter day at the end of the month, in the House's carriage, with her small book and her clerk and her courier.

She sat across the long-room table from Ferra as she always did, and read Ferra's book as she always did, and copied what the House needed, and asked about the sulphur and the second draw and the levy's pay, which she had read about in the book before Ferra could tell her. They talked about the price of wool. Vantic's hands were steady and her voice was the voice she always used on quarter day, dry and easy and a little amused.

At the end of the hour her clerk came up the stair with a letter that had come by the House's own rider that morning to the Western Gate, and gave it to her, and went down again.

Ferra watched her open it.

It was sealed with the House's seal, not the coast house's. Vantic broke it and unfolded it and read it, sitting at the table with her pen in her other hand, and Ferra, across the table, with her own book open and her own pen in her hand, watched her read it to the end.

It was not long. Ferra could see the shape of it through the back of the sheet: a page and a little more, in a large formal hand, with a paragraph at the end in a different, smaller one.

Vantic read it to the end, and then went back to the beginning and read it again.

Then she folded it, and put it into her own book between two pages, and closed the book on it, and put her pen down on the table beside it, and looked at Ferra.

"The House," she said, "wishes me to say that it is gratified by the barony's use of the first draw."

"I'm glad."

"It notes," said Vantic, "that the barony's city is now security for the House's loan, and that the House's loan is now, in some part, security for the barony's levy, because the barony is paying the levy out of the same purse the draw goes into." She said it in exactly the same voice as the price of wool. "The House would be glad of a full account of the city and the levy. Everything. The works, the stone, the men, the arms. Everything it lends against."

Ferra said nothing.

Vantic sat with her hand flat on her closed book. Her pen lay on the table beside it, and her clerk's inkstand, and the fresh sheet she always kept at her elbow for the House's notes, ruled and blank.

She did not pick up the pen.

Ferra watched her not pick it up. It was a small thing. It was the smallest thing Ferra had seen anybody do at that table in twelve years, and she knew exactly how large it was, and she knew that Vantic knew she was watching, and that Vantic was letting her.

"I shall write to the House," Vantic said, "that the barony's book is open to its factor every quarter day, as it has been for seven years, and that the factor has read it." She stood up and put the book under her arm. "That is true."

"It is," Ferra said.

"It won't do for long," said Vantic, and smiled, and went down the stair.

Ilsa found the mistake on the twenty-eighth day of summer.

She came to the long room at the end of the afternoon with her census book under one arm and Ferra's second book, the loan book, under the other, which she had asked to borrow in the morning to check the water rate against the households, and she put the loan book down on the table open at a page near the back and stood beside it and said, "I think this is wrong."

Ferra looked at the page.

It was the interest page for the House's loan. Her own hand, every line of it. The first draw, a hundred and twenty Platinum at one part in fifteen by the year, drawn at the start of last winter, and the interest on it by the season, carried forward. And then the second draw, at the start of spring, and the interest on the two together from that season, carried forward.

Except that it was not on the two together. Ilsa had her finger on the line. From the second draw onward the interest had been carried on the first draw's figure alone, as it had been the season before, as if the second draw had never been made. Every season since. A small figure, the difference, the first season. Larger the second. Larger the third.

Ferra looked at it. She did the sum on it in her head, from the bottom, and then from the top.

It was wrong. It had been wrong since the spring. She had carried a column wrong at the long-room table on a night in the spring, and every quarter since she had carried it forward from the wrong line, and read it back, and checked it, and not seen it, because she had checked it against itself.

"How did you find it?" she said.

"The water rate goes into the same purse as the draw," Ilsa said. "I wanted to see what the barony owed against what the households paid. The sum didn't come out." She looked at the page and not at Ferra. "I did it four times. I thought it was me."

"It wasn't you."

"No," Ilsa said.

Ferra looked at the girl. She was not yet twenty. She had come to the census from the tally house at Grayrock last autumn, because she could see a number at the far end of a weigh house that nobody else could see, and she had walked the barony every week since then with a book and a pencil, and she had found a family living under a dead family's name and a man with five names, and now she had found this. In Ferra's hand. In the book.

"Thank you," Ferra said.

Ilsa nodded, and gathered up her census book, and went.

Ferra corrected it. She corrected it in red, line by line, from the spring to the summer, and wrote in the margin in black, Carried wrong from the second draw, by F. Found by I. Renner, and the date, and the right figure. It was not a large figure. It would not ruin anybody. It would have been a great deal larger by next winter.

Then she sat at the table and looked at the book.

She kept the books for Kaldo, and for Wayford, and for Grayrock's tally house, and for the lower water, and for the works, and the loan, and the lane, and the city on the flat, and the levy now, and the box. Four baronies' work, near enough. With Hobb and Dace and Wenna, and Wenna in Wayford half the year, and one girl from the Grayrock weigh house who could see a long way.

She did not sleep that night.

She went down to the loom room at the mill in the dark, because the jennies ran till midnight now in the summer for the levy's coats and there was always a lamp in the counting room at the end of the granary, and because she could not sit in her father's house and not sleep and listen to him not sleep in the next room.

The counting room was small, with one window onto the mill yard and one door onto the spinning floor, and through the door she could hear the jennies running, the four of them, the long soft rushing sound of the carriages going out and coming back and the girls' voices under it, and the smell of the oil and the wool. She shut the door. She sat down at the desk, and put her book on it, and put her right hand on the band on her left wrist.

She had never done this alone. She had done it at the table with the others, with Emeric there, or Armand, since the bands were issued, a dozen times; had gone into her room with a hand on the stone and a voice to come back to. She had made the rule herself, years ago: nobody does this alone in a room, not in the middle of the night. Her father had broken it with a boiler, and she had sat beside him and counted and not fetched anyone, and she had let it stand.

She wanted to find where else she was wrong. That was all. She wanted to go into her room and go through every book in it, page by page, the way she had gone through the loan book in red, and find every column she had carried forward from a line she should not have trusted.

She did not try to picture the room. She knew that did not work; they all knew it now. She built the book instead.

She built it the way she built it every quarter day: from the first page. The ruling. The columns. The heading at the top in her hand. The first line, the date, the first entry, the figure. The second. She had kept Kaldo's book for twelve years and she had it by heart, most of it, and she built it line by line in the dark behind her eyes with her hand on the band, and when she got to the bottom of the first page there was a second page under it, and a third, and a shelf for the book to stand on, and a wall behind the shelf, and a room.

It was her room. She knew it. The long-room table, only it was her father's kitchen table, and the books along the wall, every one of them hers, and the one lamp.

And it had opened the wrong way.

She did not know how she knew that. She sat in it and knew it, the way one knows in the dark which way a door opens by the draught. The room was hers, and the door she had come in by was hers, and there was another wall, the far wall, that should have been the end of the room, and was not. It was a shelf. A long shelf, running the whole length of the wall, and on it, in no order she recognised, pages. Loose pages and bound ones, stacked and leaning, in a hand she knew better than her own.

She had not put them there.

She stood in her room and looked at them from where she was, and did not go nearer.

She knew what they were. She had known since the day the table was bound, in the year of the stones, when he had stood at the head of the table and told them all what the binding was and what it did, and what it could let each of them see of the others, and what it could let each of them see of him, because he was the one they were all bound to. And she had asked him then, in front of everyone, what stopped them, and he had said: nothing stops you but that you don't. Not taking them down is a promise. It isn't a lock.

She had written that down. It was in the book. It was the only line in the book in his words and not hers.

The pages were right there. The nearest was an arm's length from the edge of the table. She could see that it was written close, in his small quick hand, and that there was a drawing on it, and she could see the shape of a word at the top of it and did not let herself read it.

She did not take it down.

She stood there for a long time, in the room, with the jennies running somewhere very far off through a wall that was not a wall, and did not take any of them down, and did not look at them, and did not go.

When she came out of it the lamp had burned down by a third and the jennies were still running.

She was sitting at the desk in the counting room with her hand on the band and her book in front of her, closed. On top of the book, where she had put it in the afternoon to take home and had forgotten, was Sela's petition, rolled, with its three sheets pasted end to end, and two thousand and forty names on it.

She unrolled it and put her hand flat on it, on the names, and held it there, and listened through the door to the four jennies running in the next room, the carriages going out and coming back, out and back, eight spindles each, spinning.
