Chapter 230 - Keep Your Faith

Doran heard it from Armand.

Emeric learned that afterward. He had not told Doran himself; he had thought about it, in the days after the long room, and had decided that it was not his to tell, and had then spent three days not deciding who it belonged to instead. Armand had not spent three days on anything. He had gone across the inner ward on the Sunday after his first time aloft, in the afternoon, slowly, on his stick, to the parish house on the shoulder of the ward beside the church, and knocked, and been let in, and drunk two glasses of Doran's wine, which was as bad as it had been nine years ago, and told him. All of it. The five years and the cards and the long table and the thirty-five, and Rylan's three, and the rate. He had been there three hours. He had come back across the ward at dusk with his hands quite steady and said nothing to anybody about where he had been, and Emeric had only found out because Imani had seen him come out of the parish house door and asked him, and Armand had said, "I went to confession," and gone up the stair.

Doran came to the keep on the Monday.

He came at ten, which was when he came for anything to do with the burial ground or the school's hall or the sealed letters, and asked at the door for the Holder by that name, as he always did, and was shown up to the study; and he sat down in the chair across the table from Emeric with his hat on his knee, a grey man in his sixties now, tired in the way a man is tired who has performed other people's grief on a schedule for thirty-five years; and he looked at Emeric across the table for a long breath with an expression Emeric had never seen on him, which was something like a man who has been told the house he has lived in all his life was built on a mine.

"Armand came to me," he said.

"I know."

"He brought his sheets. Some of them. The still crew's." Doran turned his hat once on his knee. "I read them. I am not a man for figures; I keep a death book, and that is figures enough for anybody. But I read them, all afternoon, with him beside me telling me what each column meant, until I could read them without him." He was quiet. "I have been a priest thirty-five years, Highness. I have read the rite's doctrine every year of it, on the Sunday after the Ascent, from the pulpit, as the cathedral sends it: that the Light chooses whom it will, and those it does not choose it takes to itself, and that we are not to grieve, because a man the Light has taken is better kept than any of us." He looked at his hat. "I have told that to mothers. In that church. With my hand on their shoulders."

Emeric did not say anything.

"It's not true," said Doran.

He said it quite plainly, in the voice he kept for burial logistics; and then he sat there in the chair with his hat on his knee and looked at the window, at the summer sky over the yard, and did not say anything else for a while, and Emeric let him.

"It's a method," said Doran at last. "Armand's word. A method, run too fast. Those men did not die because the Light passed them over. They died because a mage at the head of a stone table filled them faster than a body can be filled, and nobody had ever thought to fill one slower, because nobody had ever counted." He turned his hat. "And the Church stood at the head of the table and called it a sacrament."

"Yes."

"My Church."

"Yes."

Doran nodded slowly, as if a sum had come out the way he had expected and he had hoped it would not.

"Rylan came to me," he said. "Five years ago. Six. After the fire in the yard, when he'd told it. He came to the vestry at night when the hall was empty and asked me to teach him his letters, because he had two messages in his head from two dead men and he wanted to write them down before he lost them. So I taught him. Every night for a winter. He wrote like a child of five. He wrote both messages out, as near as he had them, and he asked me where the men were, and I told him." He looked at Emeric. "I told him they were in the Light's keeping. That the Light had chosen to take them to itself. That he could take their mothers that comfort with the messages."

"He took them the messages," said Emeric.

"He took them the messages. And my comfort with them, because I'd given it to him and he was a good man and he'd not have thought to leave it behind." Doran's mouth moved, not in a smile. "Two mothers in two villages I've never seen have my lie in their houses. In Rylan's hand."

The study was very quiet. Down in the yard somebody was falling, over and over, and Ward's voice said something flat, and somebody fell again.

"What do I do?" said Doran.

He asked it simply, as a man asks a magistrate. He had asked Emeric for things before in this room, permissions and purses and a cart, and he had never once asked him anything like this, and Emeric saw that he knew it, and was asking anyway.

"Keep your faith," said Emeric.

Doran looked at him.

And then, quite slowly, the priest began to laugh. Not loudly. It came up out of him like water out of a spring that had been stopped, short and surprised, and then again, longer, and he put his hand over his eyes and shook with it in the chair with his hat sliding off his knee onto the floor; and Emeric, after a breath, found that he was laughing too, because it was funny. It was very funny. It was the funniest thing he had ever said in that room.

"You," said Doran, wiping his eyes. "You. Telling me." He found his hat on the floor and picked it up and held it in both hands. "You've never once been inside my church except to look at the drains. You told me in my own house in your first month that faith was not your subject and you'd not pretend it was."

"It isn't."

"And you tell me to keep mine."

"Yes."

Doran looked at him across the table. He had stopped laughing. His eyes were wet and quite clear.

"Why?"

Emeric thought about it, because Doran had asked him properly and deserved a proper answer, and because he wanted to get it right.

"Because the rite isn't your faith," he said. "It's a thing men did in its name. Men did it in a stone room for a few hundred years and called it the Light because that was the most useful thing to call it. That doesn't make the Light the stone room." He laid both palms on the table. "You buried four hundred people in eighteen months by yourself, in the plague, with nobody to help you and the road shut. You did it because of what you believe. You kept a book of every one of them so their families could find them. You've moved every one of the rows since, one family at a time, and paid the diggers on your own step. That's your faith. Armand's sheets haven't touched any of that." He held the priest's eyes. "Keep that. Throw out the stone room. They aren't the same thing, and the men who told you they were had a reason to."

Doran sat with his hat in his hands and did not say anything for a long breath.

"You'd let me," he said at last. "Preach. As I am. In this town. After this."

"I'd let anybody preach anything," said Emeric. "So long as nobody's will is forced, and nobody's harmed, people in Kaldo can believe what they like and practise what they like and tell anybody who'll listen. That's the rule. It was the rule before Armand's sheets and it's the rule after." He thought. "I'll ask you not to preach the stone room. I'll not tell you what to preach instead."

"And if the cathedral sends me the doctrine next spring, for the Sunday after the Ascent?"

"Then you'll read it or you won't." Emeric looked at him. "That's yours to decide. Not mine. I'd only say: whichever you do, do it as a man who knows."

Doran turned his hat round once more, slowly. Then he stood up.

"I'll think on it," he said. "I'll pray on it, if it's all the same to you." His mouth moved. "Which I suppose it is."

"It is."

The priest went to the door. At the door he stopped and looked back, as men in that room did; and his face had something in it that Emeric had never seen on it, and had to look at a while before he knew what it was. Comfort was the wrong word for it. It was nearer the face of a man who has been handed a heavy thing and finds, picking it up, that he would rather carry it than not.

"There'll be years ahead," said Emeric, before he had decided to say it. "Bad ones, I think. Worse than the plague, some of them, and not from a sickness. I'll need advice. From a decent man, who'll tell me when I'm wrong and won't want anything for it." He looked at the priest in the doorway. "If he wears the cloth, so be it."

Doran stood very still.

"I'll serve you terrible wine," he said, "and apologise for it twice."

"I'll drink it."

"You'll drink it," said Doran, and nodded, once, and went down the stair; and Emeric heard him on the stair, all the way down, and at the bottom, in the passage, he heard the priest laugh again, once, by himself.



Doran went to Rylan that evening.

Emeric did not see it. Nessa told Imani, at the dispensary, two days later, while Imani was looking at Wren's ear; and Imani told Emeric that night on the keep leads, because it was not a thing she thought he should not know.

The priest had come to the house in the river lanes at dusk, in his hat, and knocked, and asked for Rylan, and Nessa had brought him in and given him the good chair and gone to put the children to bed, and come back for the kettle and stopped in the passage, because of the priest's voice. Doran had told Rylan all of it. The vestry, and the winter of letters, and what he had said about the two men and the Light's keeping, and what Rylan had carried north with the messages because Doran had given it to him. He had told it standing up, Nessa said, with his hat in his hands, in the voice he used at the graveside, and at the end of it he had asked Rylan's pardon.

And Rylan had sat in his own chair with his boots off and his forearms on his knees and the old silver up the insides of them, and looked at the priest for a long while, and said nothing.

"Then he said the messages were true," said Imani. She was lying on her back on the warm lead with her hands behind her head and her eyes on the stars. "The two messages. That they were what the men said, as near as he had them, and that the mothers had them, in his hand, and that was a true thing in a true hand and nothing Armand's sheets said could make it not." She was quiet. "And then he said the other thing was the priest's and not his, and the priest could keep it, and he'd not go north again with a second letter to take the first one back. They'd had it five years. He'd not take a comfort off two old women with a letter that told them their sons were killed by a man in a hurry."

"What did Doran say?"

"Nessa didn't hear. She had the kettle." Imani turned her head on the lead and looked at him. "She said when she came in with the cups they were both sitting there with their hats in their laps, the priest with his and Rylan with his work cap he'd not taken off, not talking. And then Rylan said, 'Your wine's terrible, Father,' and the priest said, 'I know, I'm sorry,' and Rylan said, 'Then you'll have some of mine,' and got the bottle down." She looked back at the stars. "Nessa said they finished it. She said she'd never seen Rylan drink with a priest in her life and she never wants to again, because they sang."

"What did they sing?"

"The miller's wife," said Imani, and laughed, quietly, at the stars. "Kit Bastow's song. All the verses. The priest knew all the verses."



Armand had written it up.

Emeric found that out on the Wednesday, when he went up to the old man's room at nine with the night's figures from the ship, which was the hour he went now on the mornings after a flight; and found Armand at his table by the window with a stack of paper at his elbow a hand thick, written close in the small hand, every page, and a fresh sheet under his pen.

"The dissertation," said Armand, without turning round. "Five years. The method, the readings, the matching, Ottoline's machine, the result." He wrote a word. "The tower has a form for these. I have followed it. A master of the tower who finds that what the tower teaches is false writes it in the form and sends it to the tower, and the tower reads it in chapter and decides." He blotted the word. "I have written two before. They are still in the library."

"Will you send it?"

Armand put his pen down.

He turned round in his chair, and his hands, Emeric saw, were still on his knees; the Tuesday's flight was still in them. He looked at Emeric for a long while.

"Sit," he said.

Emeric sat on the end of the bed.

"Slow induction works," said Armand. "I have proved it. To do it at any size, for anybody but the men who carry stones in your works, you need a great deal of mana. More than the ground gives. The Band gives it. Nobody can reach the Band but you, in ships that only you can make, because only you can write their stones, by a thing that nobody alive has heard of and that somebody, I am now fairly sure, has gone to some trouble over a great many years to make sure nobody does." He did not say the word. Neither of them said it. "And even if all of that were nothing, you'll not sell the Band. You've said so. You'll not sell what you carry down, to anybody, at any price."

"No."

"Then if I send this to the tower," said Armand, "I send them a proof that the rite is murder and that there is another way, and no means of doing the other way. Except one: to fill a man as fast as his body will bear it and no faster, from whatever stones a lord can buy, under whatever mage he can hire. Slowly. Over years. In a cellar." He looked at the stack of paper. "I am a tower master. I know who reads what the tower prints. I am publishing a manual for the next cellar."

Emeric thought of a cellar in Wayford, and the white-haired, and the man by the window who did not speak.

"Then don't send it," he said.

"I shan't." Armand turned back to his table. "It goes in the box, with the sheets. Ferra can have the key." He picked up his pen. "And Kaldo publishes it the other way. By living it. Every man on your still crew is a page of it, and every child in Ward's yard eating Maren's biscuits, and they can be read by anybody who comes to the gate and looks." He wrote a word. "Which will not be soon. But it will be."

"You've thought about it."

"I have thought about very little else since Monday," said Armand, "except the Band." He did not turn round. "Tuesday next. You'll not forget."

"I'll not forget."



Ward kept the promise on the Friday.

He kept it in the yard at noon, with the class at Maren's dinner on the long benches under the awning and the older ones, Ward's no-rite apprentices from every Commencement since the first, standing along the armoury wall in their working clothes because they had been sent for from the plant floor and the stone shed and the still and did not know why: twenty-three of them, from twenty-one down to seventeen, who had chosen Ward's department in the square, one September or another, and been told on that day, in front of their families, that they would never be put through the rite, and that when there was a safe way they would have the first of it.

Ward stood in front of them with a sheet of paper. He did not look at it.

"When you chose," he said, "you were promised the first of a safe way. There is a safe way. Armand has proved it. It is the work you have been doing." He looked along them. "Carrying stones. Every day. For years. It is slow, and it is safe, and it works. The jobs that carry the most stone carry it fastest." He held up the sheet. "The plant floor. The stone shed. The still. And when there is one, a Band crew. Those are the jobs. You have first choice of them. Before anybody in Kaldo. Before the class." He did not look at the class. "Before the garrison."

The twenty-three along the wall looked at one another.

"What's a Band crew?" said one of them, a big slow girl from the stone shed.

"You will be told," said Ward, "when you have sworn the oath that goes with it, if you choose it. Not before." He held out the sheet. "Write your name against what you want. Ferra will enter it."

They came to the sheet one at a time and wrote. Some of them chose the plant floor, which they knew; some the still; nine of them wrote against the Band crew without knowing what it was, because Ward had said it last and in a particular voice, and they had been five years in his yard and could hear the particular voice.

Arthur was the first of them. He had stood in front of Ward in the square at the first Commencement and said knighthood before Sela had finished reading his name; he stood now at the end of the wall in his new coat with all its buttons, and when the sheet came down the wall to him he took it, and held the pencil over it, and did not write.

"I was promised once," he said, to the sheet.

"You were promised twice," said Ward. "Once in the square. Once on a cart, under an oak, by the Holder, to your mother, the night after." He did not look at the keep stair. "Write."

Arthur looked at the sheet, and then at Ward, and then, for a breath, up at the keep stair, where Emeric was standing, as Emeric always stood; and Emeric did not look away.

Arthur wrote his name twice. Against the plant floor, and against the Band crew. He wrote it the second time in letters so hard that the pencil went through the paper, and gave the sheet back to Ward, and went to his dinner.



Ned Tallis came to look at the ore lighter on the Thursday.

He came down to the slip at the end of his shift on hull two, in the evening, with his cap on the back of his bald head and his hands in his pockets, and stood at the foot of the slip and looked up at her for a long while, as he had looked at hull two the first time he saw her, before he spat. She was in her frames still, the iron ribs standing up out of the cradle black against the lake like the bones of a big fish, with the first of Bram's plate going on along the bottom: forty feet of her, near enough, flat as a barge and square as a box, with a single opening in her deck framing where a hatch would go, and aft, round a post for a helm, a square of heavier framing where something was going to stand that was not a hatch. Under her, in the cradle, the stubs of three legs lay folded up against her bottom like a heron's. And low in her bow, either side of the stem, there were two round holes in the plate, each the width of a man's arm, that went in and did not come out anywhere a man could see.

Fletcher was on the staging with a rivet gang. He saw Ned come and did not stop.

Ned walked all round her. He walked up the slip on one side and down the other, slowly, with his hands in his pockets, and stopped at her stern and looked up at the helm post, and at the iron sleeve that ran from the helm post down into her, to where, under the deck framing, the lift stones would sit in their cases. He knew it. He had a sleeve like it under his own hand on hull two four nights a week. He stood and looked at it a good while.

Then he took one hand out of his pocket and laid it flat on her iron flank, where the plate was on, and left it there.

"That's no ore lighter," he said.

Fletcher, on the staging, drove a rivet.

"I've worked lighters since I was twelve," said Ned. "I've carried ore in them, and coal, and stone, and a dead bishop once in a lead box. You don't put a sleeve down the stern of an ore lighter to a hole in her middle with nothing in it. You don't put legs on one. And you don't bore her bow like a flute." He did not take his hand off her. "And you don't build one on a slip that's never launched anything that floated the ordinary way."

Fletcher drove another rivet. Then he put his hammer down and looked down at Ned from the staging, for the first time.

"Forty feet," he said. "Flat deck. One hatch. A house aft. Legs, for beaching. Iron." He picked his hammer up again. "Ore lighter."

Ned looked up at him. Then down at the iron under his hand. Then, for no reason Emeric could see from where he stood at the shed door, at the sky, at the long pale summer evening going up over the lake into nothing at all.

"Who's at her helm?" he said.

"Nobody's said."

"Then I'm saying." Ned took his hand off her and put it back in his pocket. "Garrick can have hull two. He's been at her racks since she first flew, he knows her better than I do and he's younger. I'll have this one." He looked at her once more, up and down. "She's a barge somebody's been giving ideas above her station. I'll have her anyway."

He went back up the slip without waiting for an answer. Fletcher did not give him one. He drove the next rivet, and the next, and when Emeric came down from the shed door to the foot of the slip Fletcher looked down at him over the hammer with an expression that said, quite plainly, that he had not told Ned anything and had no intention of starting, and that if the Holder wanted to tell his own helmsmen what his own ore lighters were for, the Holder could climb his own staging.



The west ward began that week.

Not with the families; nobody moved, and nobody would until the new houses stood. It began with the dead rows, the long lanes off Foulard Street that the plague had emptied thirteen years ago and nobody had lived in since, roofless now, with elder growing out of the hearths and the doors gone for firewood in the first winter: the earth mages took them down in a fortnight, wall by wall, into the carts, and the carts took the rubble to the crusher for the roads, and where the rows had been there was flat ground for the first time in sixty years, and Linus's pegs went into it, white, in the lines of the town's map.

Emeric walked it with Linus on the Saturday morning. The footings were going in along the first of the pegged lines: the courtyard block that would stand round the first garden, four storeys, Linus said, round a square of grass with a well-pump and a bench and a tree, and a wash house on the corner with hot water from the works' steam, which had reached the school and the baths and the long hall since the spring, and would reach here. The trench for the footings was knee deep and smelled of wet clay. A crew from the kiln was laying the first course of the new brick in it, the hard red brick from the rotary kiln that had come up to heat for the first time on Tuesday, every brick the same as the last to a hair, which Bram said was unnatural.

A carter was watching them from the end of the next lane.

He was a big fair man with a whip over his shoulder and a team standing behind him in the shafts, waiting, with a load of bricks; and he stood at the end of his own lane, the third off Foulard Street, with his arms folded and his hat pushed back, and watched the footings go in, and did not drive on. Emeric saw the number on the door behind him, new enamel, white: nine.

"Holt," said Linus, quietly. "He's hauling for the crusher. He's been watching every morning."

Emeric went over.

The carter saw him come. He took his hat off, not quickly, and held it, and looked at Emeric's eyes, and then at the footings, and then back.

"Highness," he said. "Grace. Whatever it is now."

"Either."

"That's coming down?" The carter jerked his head at the house behind him, number nine, with its sagging roof and its new white number. "The lane?"

"The lane, yes. In a year. Two." Emeric looked at it. "Not before the new ones are standing. Nobody moves out until there's somewhere to move in. That's the rule."

"Whose rule?"

"Linus's. And the Chancellor's. And mine."

The carter thought about that. He looked at the footings, at the hard red brick going into the trench, all of it the same.

"My girl went up a wall," he said.

Emeric waited.

"Last week. In the yard. Up a wall twice her height, near enough, from standing." The carter looked at his hat in his hands. "She came home with her hands torn to the bone off the slates and sat at my table and cried. I thought she'd been hurt. I thought something had been done to her." He turned the hat. "She was laughing. Crying and laughing. She said Sir Ward looked at her." He was quiet. "She's carried my harness stones since she was four. Six to a team. I never once thought about it."

"She's good," said Emeric.

"She's my girl," said the carter, and put his hat back on. "I'll want to see the plan. The new house. Before I move into it. The Chancellor's map, in the hall."

"It's on the wall. Anybody can look."

"I know it's on the wall. I've looked." The carter turned back to his team. "I want to see what's under it. The pipes and that. Where my water comes from." He took the whip off his shoulder. "I'm a carter. I want to know where things go."

He drove on, with the bricks, toward the crusher.

Linus had come up behind. He was looking after the cart with his head on one side.

"He'll want the whole plan," he said. "Not the town's map. The real one."

"Yes."

"Ferra'll say no."

"Ferra will say no," said Emeric, "and then she'll ask him to the records hall on a Tuesday with the drainage sheet for his lane and nothing else, and sit with him for an hour while he reads it, and write down every question he asks." He watched the cart turn the corner. "And then she'll put them in the plan."

Linus looked at him sideways.

"You know her very well," he said.

"I've had nine years."

"Hm," said Linus, which was the most he ever said about anything that was not a level, and went back to his pegs.



Vantic had come at the quarter day, by the coast road, as her note had said, in the House carriage with no arms on its door; and she had not gone back.

She had taken the upper floor of the old wool merchant's house on the market square, the one with the stone stair and the iron shutters, for the House; and in the second week of July the carts began to come down the coast road behind her with the House's presses and its ledgers and two of its oldest clerks, who looked at Kaldo's square from the carts as men look at a country they have been posted to for their sins. Saban had found her the house. Ferra had found her the clerks' lodgings. Nobody had found her clients; she had brought those, and on the last Saturday of July she came up to the counting room with a list of them and laid it on Ferra's table and put her finger on three names.

"These have closed their books with the House," she said. "This month. Each with a letter." She did not sit down. "They give the same reason in different words. They will not keep their money with a House that sits in the machine duke's town, under the machine duke's seal."

"Aldane's sheet," said Ferra.

"Aldane's sheet. And their own good sense, by their lights." Vantic took her finger off the list. "Two of them owe the House more than they keep with it. I've called the debts. Politely. The third is a cathedral chapter in the north." She looked at Emeric, at the end of the table with the tape. "I've let them go. I'd like it written that I let them go, and did not ask the Holder for anything to keep them."

Ferra wrote it.

"What will it cost?" said Emeric.

"This year? A good deal." Vantic folded her hands. "Next year, less. The year after, the House will be sitting in the only town in the realm where the money doesn't leave when the lamps go out, and the clients who left will be writing to ask if they may come back." She inclined her head. "I'll let some of them."

Saban, who had come up behind her with his hat, made a small sound that in another man would have been a laugh.

"Speaking of the capital," he said, when Vantic had gone, and sat down on the bench with the hat on his knee. "My man in the linen office sends his duty. And a saying."

"A saying."

"They have a new one in the second ring. In the taverns. Since the investiture." Saban turned the hat. "When a grown man won't leave his mother's table. They say he's been very comfortable at home." He looked at the hat. "My man says the drovers say it. And the porters at the palace gate. And that a lady at the first queen's table said it last week at supper, about somebody's nephew, and the first queen laughed, and the Lieutenant got up and left the table."

Ferra kept her eyes on her book. Her pen had stopped.

"He'll not forget that," said Saban.

"Then he'll remember me for something true," said Emeric.

"That's what I'm afraid of," said Saban. "A man with a reason is easier to read and harder to stop." He stood up. "And the clerk's sister's husband has a brother who drives for a posting house on the north road. Who drove a man with a scar through his eyebrow, two Decembers ago, to a town I'd rather not name in a room with the windows open." He put his hat on. "It'll take the summer."



The dock quarter's bath and wash house opened the last day of the month, on the quay, in the old fish store that the dock quarter had refused to let anybody pull down; with a boiler from the works and the steam line run under the river to it in a lagged pipe, and twelve baths and a long wash room with coppers and mangles, and a woman from the quarter's own street council at the door with a box for the coppers. It was the first thing in the dock quarter that anybody from the keep had built with the quarter's leave rather than in spite of it, and the street council made a speech at the opening that lasted forty minutes and was mostly about roofs. The part that was not about roofs was made by a net-maker with one arm, who said that the quarter had asked for a bath before the plague and been told no by three barons and a steward, and had been told yes this spring by a duke who had never once come onto the quay without asking first; and that the quarter did not know what to make of that, and would think about it, and would let the duke know.

Imani came to it because it was a bath and a wash house and those were hers. Emeric came because Ferra had told him to, because the dock quarter had asked for him by name. He stood at the back while the council talked about roofs, and Imani stood beside him with her bag, and at the thirtieth minute she put two fingers on the inside of his wrist, under his cuff, where nobody in the crowd could see.

"Sixty," she said, very low. "You're asleep."

"I'm listening."

"You're asleep standing up. I can see it." She did not take her fingers away. "You were up till three with Armand on Tuesday and up till two on Saturday and you sat up after with the gauges, and Ferra says you came down a quarter before the second bell on Wednesday, which she's very cross about and won't say why." Her mouth did not move. "You've slept nine hours in four nights. I counted."

"You count everything."

"I'm the healer." She took her fingers away and put her hand back on her bag. "The council's going to ask you to cut the ribbon. Then you're going home and you're going to sleep until supper, and if you come down any stair tonight before I say so I'll know, and I'll tell her, and she'll tell you it's in the terms." The council paused for breath, and looked toward the back of the crowd, and somebody held up a ribbon. "Go on. Cut it."

He went and cut it. The dock quarter cheered, mostly for the baths. When he came back through the crowd to the door Imani had gone; he saw her on the quay outside, walking up toward the bridge with her bag, not looking back.

At the top of the bridge she stopped and turned round and looked straight at him, down the length of the quay, through the crowd; and made a small shape with her two hands, one on the other, flat, under her cheek, like a child playing at sleeping; and went on over the bridge.

He slept until supper.
