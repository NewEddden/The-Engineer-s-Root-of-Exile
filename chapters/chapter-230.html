Chapter 230 - The Dive Suit

Ward's gauntlet went to the bottom of the lake in the time it took to say so.

They had dropped it off the end of the works jetty on a line, at dusk, with Bram and Ferra and Ward and two of the rubber shed's men standing round to watch, because the prince had asked them to watch and would not say why until it was done. It was an old gauntlet, one of the first, from before the suits had hearts, and it went into the brown water with a small cold plop and was gone, and the line ran out through Bram's hands and stopped, and Bram hauled it up again, streaming, and laid it on the planks.

"That's one hand," Emeric said. "A man in plate is a hundred of them."

Ward looked at the gauntlet on the planks for a while. He had worn its brother on the first night anybody had flown.

"You're not going to fly into this one," he said.

"No. I'm going to go down into it." Emeric crouched and turned the gauntlet over with one finger. "Somewhere a long way from here, under a ridge, there's a way down that's full of water. A dead man went down it with a candle and a notebook and never came up. I want to go where he went and further, and come back, with the people I take." He looked up at them. "Steel sinks. We all know that. So whatever I wear down there isn't the suit I fly in, and the air I breathe down there isn't any air I can get with a stone."

"Air stones," Bram said.

"Move air. They don't make it." He had said it at Grayrock, on his knees in a gallery that had killed two men, and he said it again now in the same words, because they were the right ones. "Under water there's nothing for them to push. You can't stir a pot that's empty. The air has to be carried. All of it. Every breath a man takes down there, he takes down with him, on his back, and brings back what he doesn't use."

Ferra had her book open on her arm. She had written Under water. Air carried. at the top of a clean page before he had finished saying it, because she had seen it already on the sheet he had left on the long-room table a fortnight ago, three lines written in the dark in a hand that sloped downhill.

"And a line," she said.

"And a line."

It took the whole of the turn of the season, and it took, in the end, nearly everything Kaldo had learned how to make in three years, which was how Emeric knew it was the right time to make it.

It could not have been made before. He could see that, looking back along the benches of the fitting shop at the end of it, the way a man sees the path he has come up only from the top of the hill. It needed the rubber, and the rubber had needed the island. It needed the vials, a heart's worth of charge in a glass stick a man could clip to his thigh, and the vials had needed the band and Bram's pump and a winter of burned fingers. It needed glass that did not cut when it broke, and that had needed a boy with a bucket and three hundred and eighty panes. It needed a dozen things nobody had yet thought to put together, and all of them were in Kaldo, in one shed or another, waiting.

The suit started with the skin.

Bram's shed on the lake shore, the old rope walk, had been making island rubber for eighteen months, and the men in it could now lay a sheet of it as thin as a glove's leather and cure it in the long press so that it came out black and supple and would bend double and lie flat again. Emeric gave them a man to lay it round. Not a man; the shape of one, turned in wood in Bram's pattern shop from Ferra's measurements, which she had taken herself, of everyone, with a tape and a straight face. The rubber was laid over a suit of close wool, and a second skin of it over that, cemented at the seams with the same rubber dissolved in the lighter part of the island oil, which went on like syrup and dried to nothing, and the whole of it came off the form in one piece with the wool inside it, like a shadow that had been peeled off a man.

Ferra was the first one into it. She had not asked. She had simply been standing nearest when it came off the form, and had looked at it, and had looked at the prince, and had taken her coat off.

It fitted her like the water would. That was the only way he could think of it afterwards. It went on from the neck, stretched, and closed, and when she stood up in it in the middle of the fitting shop with her arms a little away from her sides, not sure what to do with them, it showed exactly what shape a person was and nothing else. The men in the shop looked at the benches. Bram did not look anywhere but at the seams, which was his way of being decent, and ran his thumb down the one at her side and nodded to himself.

"It'll want the plates," he said.

The plates were thin. That was the first argument, and Ward had it with him in the shop in front of everybody, standing with an MK2's breastplate in his hands and holding it up next to the pieces Bram had cut.

"That'll stop a knife," Ward said, of the breastplate. He put it down. He picked up the new chest piece, which was three curved plates on a strap, each overlapping the one below like the scales on a beetle's back, and bent it in his hands, and it flexed. "That won't stop a thought."

"It won't need to. Nobody's going to put a knife in anybody down there." Emeric took the chest piece from him and held it where it would sit, over the breastbone. "It's for rock. Look at where a man touches in a narrow place. He goes through on his belly and his chest's on the roof. He crawls and it's his knees and his shins on the floor. He puts his forearms up in front of his face when something comes at him in the dark, because he can't help it." He touched each place as he named it, on himself. "Chest. Belly. Hips. Forearms. Knees. Shins. The backs of the hands. The rest of him is rubber, and rubber bends round a rock and a plate doesn't. And every plate is small, so that if one's dented it dents on its own and doesn't jam the next."

Ward looked at the scales on the strap for a while.

"And if Kesrath finds you?"

"Then we leave. That's the whole plan for Kesrath. We aren't going there to fight anybody. We're going there to not be seen."

The second argument was the weight, and that one was not with Ward. It was with the water.

A man floats. Not much; a little, with his lungs full; less with them empty. A man in wool and two skins of rubber floats a great deal more, because the rubber and the wool hold air, and Ferra proved it that first evening off the works jetty, in the new suit without its plates, with a line round her waist and Bram at the end of it, when she walked down the steps into the lake to her chest and sat back on the water as if it were a chair, and could not have gone under if she had wanted to. She lay there on the surface of the lake in the last of the light with her arms out, looking at the sky, and said, in a voice that was trying not to be delighted, "Well, that's no good."

So the plates went on, and the plates weighed, and it turned out that that was what they were for as much as for the rock. Bram set them first, all of them, every scale and cap and guard, and put her in the lake again, and she floated less. Then the frame went on, a slim steel spine down her back from the shoulder blades to a belt at her hips, and she floated less again. And then Bram began to hang lead on her, a little at a time, in flat pieces on a belt with a buckle that opened with one pull, until she stood on the bottom of the lake at the jetty's end with the water over her head and neither rose nor sank but hung there, a hand off the mud, and turned slowly on her line like a leaf.

"The plates stay," Emeric said, when she was out and dripping on the planks. "The plates are fixed. The lead's what you drop. If anything goes wrong, anything, and you need to go up, you pull that buckle and the belt goes and you go up. Not fast. Slowly. Because the plates are still on you and they're most of what holds you down." He looked at the buckle. "That's why the plates first and the lead last. So that dropping the belt makes you rise, and doesn't throw you at the sky."

"Why not a stone?" said Bram.

He had known somebody would ask. He had hoped it would be Bram, because Bram would understand the answer with his hands.

"Because a gravity stone at her hips could hold her exactly where she is, and do it better than lead, and it'd be lovely," he said. "Right up to the moment it went dead. And then she'd go up. All at once. From as deep as she was, with nothing to stop her, as fast as a cork let go." He picked up a piece of the lead and weighed it in his palm. "Lead doesn't go dead. Lead is always exactly as heavy as it was this morning. Nothing that holds a diver up or down goes on a stone. Ever."

But the stones went on the suit anyway. That was the part that surprised them.

It could fly. It had to, because it would go into the cave from a ship's deck on a rope and out of it the same way, and a diver who could not lighten himself on a line was a dead weight on the winch and a danger to everybody under him. So the frame took gravity nodes, four, two at the shoulder blades and two at the hip belt, seated in iron under the plates as every stone on every suit was seated, and air stones in iron cups at the backs of both calves, where Ward's had been since the first harness. It took two hearts, front and back, fed from vials in steel sleeves on a clip at the thigh, three to a clip. It took a cape that was a wing, cut slimmer than the MK2's, and a parachute pack at the small of the back, and the two catches at the shoulders that let both of them go at once. It flew, the first night, off the works jetty, out over the lake and back in a long low curve, with Osric in it because Osric had asked, and came down on the planks as lightly as the old suits did and a good deal more quietly, because it weighed a third of what they weighed.

That was the air. The water was different.

Emeric had spent an evening in the lake with the old calves off an MK2, holding the iron cups under the surface and telling the stones in them to push. They pushed. He could feel them do it. They took the charge and bent themselves to the work exactly as they did in air, and nothing happened at all, except that the cups grew a little warm in his hands. There was no air in them to move. It was exactly what he had said on the jetty and he had still wanted to see it, because he had been wrong about stones before, in front of more people than this.

So he had gone back to the oldest stone Kaldo used.

There was a water stone in the pump house at the head of the irrigation ditch on the old farm, and had been since the first dry summer, lifting water out of the river into the top channel whenever the wheel was slow. It was the plainest thing in the barony. It moved water, and did nothing else. He had a pair of them cut small, the size of a thumb's end, and seated in iron ducts no longer than a hand, open at both ends, and Bram fixed one on each side of the hip belt, pointing back, and a lever at the left gauntlet's wrist that a thumb could reach.

Ferra tried it at the jetty's end on the fourth night. She let herself down the steps into the lake in the whole suit, plates and frame and lead, with the helmet on for the first time and the line round her, and hung there under the surface, a dark shape with a faint light at its breast. Then she put her thumb on the lever.

The line went out through Bram's hands so fast it burned them. He swore and let it run. Out on the dark water, thirty yards off, forty, a long ripple came up and spread and the faint light went away under it, straight out into the lake, as fast as a man could run, and then stopped, and hung, and turned round, and came back more slowly, and came up at the foot of the steps, and a gloved hand came up out of the water and took hold of the rail.

Emeric went down the steps and put his hand on her helmet and pulled her up the last of them. Through the glass her face was white and wet and very bright about the eyes.

"Again," she said, muffled, through the glass. He could barely hear her. He heard her anyway.

He made the rule that night, on the jetty, with the water still running off her, and Ferra wrote it down in the back of the ledger with her hands shaking, and she did not write shaking, though she wrote everything.

Stones may push you. Never hold you up.

"It's the same rule as the lead," he said to Bram and Ward and the rubber men, standing round the dripping shape on the planks. "Say the water stones die. What happens? She stops. She's where she was, at the depth she was, with the line in her hand, and she has to swim, or pull herself along the line, and it takes longer. That's all. That's a nuisance. A trim stone that dies throws you at the sky. A push stone that dies leaves you where you are." He crouched and touched the iron duct at her hip. "They point back. Never down. You don't rise on them. You rise on your hands, up the line, as slowly as I'll tell you in a minute. And in the cave you use them at a walk, not a run, because in a cave the thing you hit at a run is the roof."

"Then why have them at all?" said Ward. "If a walk's all you'll use."

"Because a walk's faster than swimming in all that. Because a diver who swims is working, and a diver who's working is breathing hard, and every breath is off what's on his back." Emeric looked at the lake. "And because the less time you spend down there the better. I'll come to that. It's the thing I've been putting off."

The suction had to change too, and Bram had seen it before the prince said it.

The pads on the old suits, the brass cups at the boots and the off hand with their rims of pitch, drew the air out of themselves with a small stone and gripped. Ward had gone up a wall at Wayford on them, and come off it once where the mortar was rotten. Under water there was no air in the cup for the stone to draw; only water, and a stone that pulled on water pulled the whole lake. So Bram went back to the glaziers' lifters he had used on the dome, a rubber cup with a lever on its back that you pressed flat against the glass and threw the lever, and the lever drew the middle of the cup back and held it there, and the cup held. No stone in it at all. It gripped wet rock if the rock was smooth enough, and it let go with a flick of the lever, and a man could hang from it by one hand with the other free. Bram made them with island rubber rims, in the palm of the off gauntlet and on the soles of both boots, hinged to turn in, as the old ones had been.

"Doesn't care if it's wet," he said, showing Emeric, pressing one to the side of the rain butt and hanging his whole weight off it. "Doesn't care if it's dark. Doesn't care if you're dead." He threw the lever and dropped off it. "Best thing on the suit."

The helmet was the one Bram had broken his heart over at the glassworks in the summer, and he did not break it again. He built the shell round it.

It was steel at the back and the crown, close to the skull, and it came forward round the jaw to a collar that bolted to the suit's neck ring on a gasket of rubber, and the glass sat in the front of it, the whole front, from ear to ear and brow to chin, in one piece, so that a person inside it looked out as if through a window with nothing round it. The glassmen had put a little green back into the melt for it. That had been the prince's asking and they had not liked it: a fortnight, years ago, spent taking the green out of Kaldo's glass, and now he wanted some back. But under the water, with a lamp at your chest, clear glass threw the lamp's light straight back into your eyes off every speck in the water, and a little green in the glass took the edge off it. So the helmet's glass was the green of shallow water over sand, and a face inside it looked like a face under a stream.

Ferra asked him, at the fitting, what the glass would do to the thing in him that felt the medium. She asked it quietly, at the bench, with the helmet in her lap.

"Glass holds," she said. "You showed us that. Round the flask. Round the dome. And this is going to be round your head."

He had wondered too. He put the helmet on, there in the fitting shop, and closed the collar, and stood with the glass in front of his face and the smell of new rubber and hot steel inside it, and let the sense out.

It went out the way it always did. Not through his face; it had never been through his face. It went out through his body, through his hands and his chest and the soles of his feet, the whole of him, like warmth off a stone, and the helmet was a small thing on the top of it. Inside the glass there was a breath of the medium, a thumb's worth, the little that had been in the air of the shop when he closed the collar, held there as a jar holds it. That was all the glass did. It held its breath, and he went on round it.

"It keeps what's in it," he said, taking it off. "And I go round it. It's a window I carry."

She wrote it down.

The chest lamp was the last thing on, and the only thing on the suit that nobody argued about: a light stone the size of a finger's end, set in a little iron cup at the breastbone behind a lens of the same glass as the helmet, so that when it was lit it put out a cold blue-green glow that went a long way in clear water and lit the rock in front of a man's chest, and the underside of his glass, and nothing else. There were two more on every diver, one at the wrist and one on the belt, because Emeric had said three lights each and would not be moved.

The breath was the hard part, and it was where the island's oil came in.

Kaldo had made breath once before, for the suits, when Emeric had passed the yellow gas through the soap house lye and got a pearly salt out of it that burned in a tin and gave off what a man needed to live for a quarter of a glass. Nineteen candles, and no more until there was lead for the acid. There was lead now, from Grayrock, and acid. But a candle was a thing you lit and could not put out, and it gave what it gave until it was gone. A man at the bottom of a cave did not want a quarter of a glass. He wanted as much as he needed, when he needed it, for as long as the dive took, and not a breath of it wasted.

So Emeric went to the current.

He did it in a shed on the lake shore next to Bram's rubber house, with a cable run down to it from the works. Two plates of iron stood in a trough of the soap house's strongest potash lye, a hand apart, with a wall of close linen between them hung from a bar, and the current went into one plate and out of the other, and both plates fizzed. Fine bubbles came off them, like the bubbles on the inside of a glass of new ale, and went up, and were caught under two iron bells, one over each plate, that stood in the lye with their mouths under it.

"Water," he said to Bram and to Imani, who had come down from the ward to see what he wanted the potash for, since it was her potash as much as anybody's. "Just water. The lye's only there so the current can get through it. It pulls the water apart. Two kinds of air come out of it, one off each plate, twice as much of the one as the other. The small one is breath. The pure part of the air, the part a candle lives on and a man lives on. That bell." He pointed. "The other bell's the big one, and that's the other thing water's made of, and it burns. It burns with a flame you can't see in daylight and it goes off like powder if it's mixed with air and meets a spark." He pointed up. From the second bell a pipe went up the side of the shed and through the roof and on up a pole, twenty feet, and ended in the air over the lake. "That goes up there and out and away, and nobody lights anything in this shed ever, not a pipe, not a lamp, not a forge. The lamps in here are stones. The door stays open."

Imani looked at the pole a long time.

"You're making something that burns," she said, "to throw it away."

"I'm making breath. The other thing comes with it. I'll find a use for it one day. Today it goes up a pole."

The breath went into a bag. Then it went into a bottle, and the bottle was the reason for everything else.

Bram made the bottles on the big lathe. He took a bar of the rail works' best steel as thick as a man's calf and bored it out from one end, slowly, a day to a bottle, until it was a steel flask as long as a forearm with walls as thick as a thumb and a neck at the top threaded for a valve. Every one of them was filled with water, before it was ever filled with anything else, and the water was pumped into it by hand until the gauge stood half as high again as the bottle would ever be asked to hold, and left there an hour, and looked at. Water did not go off like powder when a bottle burst. Breath would. The first bottle held. The third wept at the neck and went back on the lathe. Ferra entered them all.

Then the pump. It was the pump Bram had built for the vials, three iron cylinders each smaller than the last, with a coil of copper between them in a trough of water to take the heat out, and he built a second one for the breath, and set it in the gas shed. It could not be belted off a ship's engine here. It was belted off the bench engine's twin, the second of the little oil engines, which had come off the works floor in the spring and stood now at the end of the shed in a pen of brick, thumping, burning the island's light oil out of a tank on the wall. It was the first of the island's oil to do a day's work in Kaldo that anybody could hear, and it did it all day, filling bottles.

That was the oil in it. The oil was also the thing that nearly burned the shed down.

It was the Hesketh boy, Edwin, Bram's slowest and most careful apprentice, who had been given the valves to make because he was slow and careful. The valve on the fourth bottle was stiff. It turned, but it turned hard, and it squealed, and Edwin did what every boy who had ever worked in Bram's shop had been taught to do with a thread that squealed, which was to put a drop of oil on it. He took the oil can off the engine's shelf and put a drop on the valve's thread, and worked it in, and it turned sweetly, and he screwed it into the bottle's neck and put the bottle on the pump.

Emeric was at the other end of the shed. He heard the valve open, and a hiss, and then a sound like a sheet of paper being torn very fast, and turned in time to see a flame come out of the valve's side as long as a man's arm and as bright as the sun, white, roaring, with the brass of the valve melting in it like wax.

Edwin had his hand on the valve. He took it off faster than anybody in that shed had ever moved. Bram, who had been at the pump, threw the pump's cock shut with the flat of his hand, and the flame stopped, all at once, and there was a smell of hot metal and burned leather and a black star on the shed wall behind the bottle where the brass had spat, and a boy standing in the middle of the floor holding his right hand in his left and looking at the glove on it, which was scorched through across the knuckles.

Imani had him by the wrist before anybody else had moved. She stripped the glove off and looked, and let her breath out.

"Red," she said. "Not blistered. Cold water." She put his hand in the pump's cooling trough and held it there. Then she looked at Emeric over the boy's head.

Emeric was looking at the valve. What was left of it was on the floor, a lump of brass with its thread run to beads.

"What did you put on it?" he said. He did not raise his voice. He did not have to.

"Oil, Highness," said Edwin. He was white. "It was stiff."

Emeric picked the lump of brass up with his handkerchief. It was still hot. He turned it over.

"It's not your fault," he said. "It's mine. I should have said it on the first day and I didn't, because I thought it was too plain to say." He put the lump down on the bench. Then he turned to the shed, to Bram and the pump men and the lathe men and Edwin with his hand in the trough, and said it. "Oil burns in air. You know that. You've seen a lamp. In pure breath, oil doesn't burn. It goes off. It goes off hot enough to melt brass, and it goes off on its own, with no spark, the moment the breath hits it hard. Grease the same. Tallow. Pitch. A thumb with oil on it." He held up his own hand. "Anything that touches the breath is dry and clean. The valves. The threads. The inside of the bottles. The pump." He looked at Bram. "What's in the pump?"

"Soap and water," said Bram, "on the cylinders. You said so." He did not say thank God. His face said it.

"Soap and water. Never oil. The oil's for the engine, and it stays in the engine's pen, and the oil can doesn't come through that door." He looked at the black star on the wall. "Paint that round. Leave it. Every man who works in here looks at it on the first morning, and I tell him why."

Ferra wrote it in the back of the ledger under the other one, that evening, in the same small square hand: No oil on breath. Ever. And in the margin, small: Edwin. Right hand. Red. Not blistered.

The rest of the breathing set went together in a week after that, because it had all been waiting on the bottle.

It was a loop. That was what Emeric called it, and after a while everybody did. A man breathed out into a bag, and the bag was on his chest under the plates, a flat pouch of island rubber, and from the bag the breath went through a box of potash, and from the potash back into the bag, and he breathed it in again. The potash took out the part of the breath a body threw away, the part that had made the Grayrock gallery a grave; it was the same potash the soap was made of, dried and broken small and packed in a canister between two screens. And what a body used, which was only a little of each breath, a sip, the bottle on his back put back, through a valve into the bag, a little at a time, as the bag went slack.

"It doesn't bubble," Imani said, when he showed her. She had it in her hands on the long-room table, the whole loop, bag and canister and the two hoses of her own tubing that ran from it to the helmet. "Every breath you breathe out, you keep."

"Every breath. Nothing goes into the water but what I let go on purpose. No bubbles going up the passage to stir the silt off the roof. No noise. And a bottle that'd last a man breathing plain air a quarter of an hour lasts him two or three, on this, because he's only using what he uses." He turned the canister over in his hands. It was black, and hard, and smooth, and took the lamp's light like the back of a beetle.

That was the last new thing in the suit, and it had come out of Bram's shed by accident.

Bram cooked his rubber with a tenth part of sulphur, at the heat of steam, for a few hours, and it came out supple. That was the recipe. Emeric had asked him, in the spring, what happened if he did it wrong, and Bram had said he did not know, he had never done it wrong on purpose, and Emeric had said, try it. Put in four times the sulphur. Cook it all day. Bram had done it, because the prince asked, and what came out of the press at the end of the day was not rubber. It was black and hard as horn and would not bend at all, and when Bram put it on the lathe it turned like a good close wood, and took a thread, and polished. It did not rust. It did not rot in water. The potash did not eat it. Bram called it black horn, because that was what it looked like, and nobody had thought of a better word. He made the canisters of it, and the valve bodies on the loop, and the mouthpiece.

The mouthpiece was the thing Bram had asked for in the summer, at the glassworks, with his hand on the helmet's glass. It sat inside the helmet, below the chin, on its own short hose straight off the loop: a flanged bit of black horn shaped to go between the lips and the teeth, with two lugs for the teeth to close on. If the glass went, if the whole helmet filled, a man put his head down and took the mouthpiece in his teeth and bit, and breathed on it, and went home on the line. Bram had tried it in the lake himself, with the helmet's face off and the water on his face and his eyes shut. He had come up after a count of two hundred, and spat it out, and said nothing, and nodded.

The harpoon was Ward's, in the end, because Ward was the one who asked what a man was supposed to do down there if something with teeth took an interest.

"Nothing, mostly," Emeric said. "Eels. Big fish, if it's the kind of water that has them. They'll want nothing to do with you. You're a light and a noise." But he had drawn it already, and he showed Ward the drawing, and Ward looked at it with the first real pleasure he had shown since the gauntlet went into the lake.

It was a short gun. A stock and a barrel no longer than a man's forearm, and a spear in the barrel, steel, barbed, a little longer than the barrel, with a light line tied off at its tail and coiled on a reel under the stock. In the breech, behind the spear, in an iron seat, a force stone, cut from the same pale cloudy stuff as the one in the gate bar and with the same kind of working cut into it, so that when it was told to let go it let go one way, forward, all at once, and did not do it again until it was fed. And under the barrel, for when the stone was dead, two bands of island rubber on the stock, which a man could pull back by hand and hook on a notch in the spear, like a boy's sling, and it would still throw the spear, shorter.

"It's not for eels," Emeric said. "It's for rope. Somewhere down there, I'll want a line up a wall to a ledge I can't reach, or across a gap I can't swim. Stairs cut for somebody taller than us. I put this to my shoulder and it throws the spear with a line on its tail into the rock up there, and the barbs hold, and then I've got a line. The first line. After that it's hands." He tapped the drawing. "That's what it's for. It'll do the other thing, if it has to."

They tried it at the works quarry at dusk, the six of them and Bram's men, against the face of the old cut where the stone had come from for the first wall. Emeric put it to his shoulder and told the stone to go, and it went, with a sound like a door slammed in the next room and a kick that put him back a step, and the spear went into the quarry face thirty yards off with its line whipping out behind it off the reel, and stood there, quivering, in a crack.

And the chest lamp on his suit went out.

Not for long. For the time it took to draw a breath. It went out as if a hand had been put over it, and in the same breath so did the lamp at his wrist, and so did the light stone in Osric's hand six feet away, and Osric said something that Ferra did not write down. And then they came back on, all three, a little dimmer and then as bright as before, as the medium came back into the place round the gun from the air round it, the way water comes back into a hole dug in wet sand.

"It takes it with it," Armand said. He had come up from the tower for this, which was a thing he did now for the prince's tests and did not do for anything else. He had his reading stone out on its rod before the lamps were back. "When a stone empties like that, all at once. It doesn't only spend what's in it. It takes what's round it. A little hole. Like the dead patch in the rock after an overloaded fire stone, but small, and it fills again." He looked at the stone. "A breath. Perhaps two."

"Then that's the cost," Emeric said. "Every shot blinks every light near the man who shot. Three lights each, and they all blink. So you don't fire it with your partner next to you, because a kick in water carries like a blow, and you don't fire it without telling him first, and you never, ever fire it anywhere the roof's already cracked." He lowered the gun. "And when you've fired it, you hold still on the line till your lights come back."

There was one thing left, and it was the one he had been putting off, and he did it at the long table after supper, on the last night of the building, with all six of them there and a bottle of Kaldo ale on the boards in front of him that nobody had yet understood the reason for.

It was bottled ale, from the brewer by the old market, the strong sort that was put in stoneware with a spoon of sugar and corked down hard and left in a cellar to work, so that it came out lively. Emeric had had Hobb bring it up from the cellar without shaking it. He set it in the middle of the table and put his hand on the cork.

"Imani asked me a question in the spring," he said, "that I didn't answer, because I didn't know how to answer it without this. She asked what the deep does to a body." He looked at her. "It does three things, and I've told you two of them. Pure breath is a poison below about twenty feet. The lips twitch first, then the hands, then a man has a fit, and a man who has a fit under water drowns. So on pure breath we don't go below twenty feet. That's the first. The cold's the second, and the wool and the warm channels are for the cold. This is the third." He tapped the cork. "Everybody here has opened one of these."

"And worn it," said Osric.

"And worn it. Why?"

Nobody answered for a moment. It was the kind of question that had no answer because nobody had ever asked it. Then Bram said, "The work's in it. The air the working makes. It's shut up in there with nowhere to go, so it goes into the ale. And when you pull the cork it comes out."

"Where is it now?"

Bram looked at the bottle. "In the ale."

"Can you see it?"

"No."

"No. It's in there, all through it, and you can't see a bubble, because the cork's pressing on it. It's held in by the push." Emeric put his other hand flat on the table. "Water pushes. Every man who's ever dived for a dropped anchor knows it; it's in his ears. The deeper you go, the harder it pushes, on every part of you. And a man who breathes down there breathes his air at the water's push, or he couldn't draw it into his chest at all. That's what the bag's for. The bag's under the water's push, and so the breath in it is too." He turned the bottle a little. "Air isn't one thing. You know that now, from the candles. There's the breath, the small part, the part that keeps you alive. And there's the rest. Four parts in every five. It does nothing. It goes in with every breath and comes out with every breath and it doesn't feed a flame and it doesn't feed a man. The idle part. And when a man breathes it under the water's push, it goes into him. Into the blood, and from the blood into all of him, a little more every minute he's down there. The way the air from the working goes into the ale under the cork. And he can't feel it, and he can't see it, and it doesn't hurt."

He took his hand off the cork.

"Until he comes up."

He eased the cork. Slowly. He turned it in the bottle's neck, a little and a little, with his thumb over it, and there was a thin hiss, a long one, and he held it at that, and let the hiss run, for a count of ten, and twenty, until it stopped. Then he drew the cork out and set it on the table, and tipped the bottle, and poured a cup. The ale went into the cup quietly and lay there with a thin head on it and a few small beads rising in it.

He set the bottle down. Hobb, without being asked, had brought a second one. Emeric took it, and shook it once, and took the cork between his finger and thumb, and pulled it out all at once.

It came out of the neck in a white column. It came up over his hand and over the table and ran across the boards in foam, and Saban took his papers out of the way a moment too late, and the bottle stood there in the middle of it, still foaming, half empty.

"That's what it does in the blood," Emeric said, into the silence. "If you come up fast. The push goes off, all at once, and the idle air that went in quietly under it comes out, and it comes out as that. In the blood. In the joints. Under the skin. In the back, in the bone that carries the nerves. In the head." He did not look at the foam. He looked at Imani. "It doesn't happen in the water. It happens after. An hour after. Six. The man comes up and climbs out and says he's well, and he is, and then his knee hurts, or his elbow, deep, in the joint, and he can't straighten it. His skin itches and comes up in a rash like a map. His hands go numb. That's the lucky ones. The others can't feel their legs. Or they can't breathe. Or they fall down and don't get up."

Imani had not moved. "And the first way," she said. "The slow cork."

"The slow cork, nothing. The same idle air comes out, but it comes out a little at a time, into the blood and out of the blood into the chest and out with the breath, the way the hiss came out round the cork. No foam. If you come up slowly enough, and stop on the way up and wait, the body lets it go on its own." He pointed at the cup of quiet ale. "So we come up slowly. Hand over hand up the line. Never faster than the smallest bubble you breathe out, and you'll be breathing none, so never faster than I say. And on the way up from anything deep, we stop, ten feet under the top, by the knots on the line, and hang there, and wait, as long as the sand-glass says."

"How do you know how long?" said Ferra.

"I don't. Not well. I know it's longer the deeper you went and the longer you stayed. I'll give you a number and it'll be too long, on purpose, and we'll wait that long every time." He picked up the cork from the second bottle. "And there's this. The pure breath has no idle part in it. None. The sump at the cave's mouth is shallow, and we'll cross it on pure breath, and pure breath can't do this to you. It's the deep water past it that can. For that there'll be a second set, a mix, half breath, half plain air, because below twenty feet pure breath poisons you and plain air does this. Half and half is as far as I dare push either one." He put the cork down. "I've worked it. I'll work it again. I'll make it myself and I'll check it myself, at the surface, before anybody breathes it."

Ferra's pen was moving. She stopped it and looked at him.

"Who told you?" she said.

It was not the question he had expected, though he should have. He looked at the foam on the table. Hobb had a cloth and was mopping round Saban's papers and not listening.

"Somebody did," he said. "I know I was told. I know all of it, the push and the idle air and the slow cork. I don't know who, or where." He shook his head. "The same door."

She wrote something short. He did not need to see it to know what.

"And flying," Ward said. He said it in his flat voice, from his chair by the door, and it was not really a question; he had got there before anybody else, as he usually did, because he had been thinking about the ship since the gauntlet went into the lake. "The band's three miles up. The air up there's thin. You said. It pushes less."

"It pushes less." Emeric looked at him. "That's the cork. That's the cork coming out all at once. A man who's been down in the deep water carries the idle air in him for a day afterwards, a little less every hour. If he goes up into the band with it still in him, it's the same as coming up fast out of the water. Worse. It's coming up out of the water and then up again, three miles, on top." He put both hands on the table. "So here's the rule, and it's the one that the whole of the rest is built on. Anybody who goes into the water doesn't go up. Not in the ship, not on a line, not in a suit. Not for a day and a night after the last time they're wet, and when they do go, it's low, over the sea, in the ship that flies low. And anybody who's going to fly the band doesn't go into the water. Fliers don't dive. Divers don't fly."

The table sat with that for a while.

"So the ones who go down," said Saban slowly, "stay down."

"The ones who go down stay down. Till it's over."

Ward was looking at the foam on the table where Hobb had not got to yet. It had gone flat, and brown, and left a ring.

"A man who can't straighten his knee," Ward said. "Holding it. Bent over it."

"Yes."

"Then the men'll call it the bends." Ward looked up. "Whatever you call it. That's what they'll call it, by the second night."

"Then that's what it's called," said Emeric.

Ferra wrote the rules that night, all of them, in the back of the ledger under the older ones, in the order he gave them, and read them back to him before she closed the book.

Stones may push you. Never hold you up. Lead for trim.

No oil on breath. Ever.

Pure breath above twenty feet. Below, the half set, made and checked by E. at the surface.

Up slow, by hand, on the line. Stop at ten feet by the knots. Wait the glass.

Air stones off in water. They are dead weight there. Capes off at the cave mouth.

The line goes in, though he can see the passage.

Every rope that holds a lightened man is rated for his whole weight, because the stones are what will fail.

Turn back at a third of the breath, or at the glass, or when anyone signals to turn. No reason needed.

Fliers don't dive. Divers don't fly. (The bends.)

"The gravity nodes," she said. "On the suit. They'll still work down there."

"They'll work. The medium goes through the water like it goes through the air. I felt it at the jetty. They'll answer if I tell them to." He looked at the line she had written, Stones may push you. Never hold you up. "I won't tell them to. That's what the rule's for. It's for the moment I want to."

She closed the book on that.

In the fitting shop, when he went down to it late, as he did most nights now instead of the gallery, there were four of them hanging on their frames along the wall, headless, the helmets on the bench below them in a row, their glass catching the lamp. Three were made to measure. Ferra's, with its scales a little narrower than the others, and his own, and a third to the same pattern for whoever would be the third, which was a question he had not answered at the table and knew the answer to.

The fourth was made to no measure at all. Ferra had it in the book as the spare. He had given Bram the size for it himself, from memory, from the shape of a man seen across a room six weeks at a time a year ago and not since, and from the shoulders of a boy going out of a stable yard a long time before that; and Bram had cut it a little broad in the chest and a little long in the arm, on purpose, because the prince had said, when Bram asked, that it would be easier to take in than let out.

Ferra had written spare in the book. Under it, when he told her, she had written a second word, and not crossed out the first, because both of them were true.
