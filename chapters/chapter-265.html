Chapter 265 - Lantern Night, Year Ten

The Almanac left Kaldo on the half past seven trains on the morning of the sixth of March, eleven crates in the Milford van and nine in the Grayrock's, with the Milford Book packed in beside it and Wayford's share to follow on the evening through train; and the first man outside the press hall to read it said it was wrong.

He was the Milford train's guard, with a copy open on the van's step, and he looked up at the clock over the booking window and back down.

"Seven forty," he said. "It says the seven forty, mistress. She's gone at the half hour since June."

"From tomorrow," said Ferra.

She did not look up. She was standing on the platform in her grey coat with the crate list on a board against her chest, and a pencil going down it as the porters took the crates past her into the van.

"The spring table starts tomorrow. The Grayrock coal wants ten minutes at the junction, so you give it ten minutes. It's on the notice in your coat."

The guard found the sheet in his coat, and read it, and folded it up again rather smaller than it had been.

"Seven forty," he said.

"From tomorrow," said Ferra, and ticked the crate.

Emeric, who had come down because it was the first time Kaldo had sent a book to every house in the four baronies that had a roof and a field, and who had been told twice since breakfast that he was not needed, took a copy off the top of the next crate before it went in.

It was a thin thing. Sixty-four pages of the press hall's cheapest grey, sewn through the fold, and across the cover THE KALDO ALMANAC, YEAR ELEVEN, ONE COPPER, in ordinary ink, because it was leaving. Inside, the moon for the year to the hour, off Armand's tower tables; the planting dates for the river flats and the upland, which had never before been written down anywhere as not the same; Imani's page on boiling water and clean cloth, cut by half three times until it fitted on one; Ottoline's weights and measures; a year of the four markets' prices out of Saban's books; the Church's feast days, every one, because Ferra would not give the priests a missing saint to preach about; and Kaldo's own days beside them: Lantern Night, Commencement, the Long Table.

And the winds. Ferra's column of the river's winds by the month, nine years of it, with the barge pilots' word against each; and under March and April, in small capitals, A GUESS, because she would not have it printed otherwise.

And four pages at the back headed TIMETABLE. Every train on every line, both ways, the people's and the coal and the grain and the through train to Wayford, with the fares and the halts; and at the foot of the last page a line the yard's clerk had put in on his own: Lantern Night: the last train home to Milford and Grayrock waits for the fireworks.

He thought of a printer on the other side of everything who had grown rich enough on a book like this one to give up printing and fly a kite into a thunderstorm, and put the copy back.



The down train from Milford came in on the other road at twenty past seven, early, because its driver liked to be early on a festival and nobody had told him yet that the Almanac had an opinion about it.

It was full. The holdings came in for Lantern Night now, at a Copper each way, with their lanterns folded flat in their bags and the names already written at their own kitchen tables; and last off, slowly, because nobody hurried Hollis, Hollis and Farris and Hester Mull.

Hester saw the stencil on the crate going past her before she saw him. MILFORD BOOK. She stopped the porter by the arm, took the lid off, and took one out.

"That's counted," said Ferra.

"Then count it again."

It was thicker than the Almanac, bound in boards, a hundred and twenty pages; and on the first page, under the title: How Milford farms, as told by Hollis, Farris and Hester Mull, of Milford.

Hester read that twice. Then she held the book open where Hollis could see it without his spectacles.

"Page one," she said.

"My strip," said Hollis, who had known it would be and had come thirty miles to make sure.

Farris could not read. He had said so at the halt in the spring. But the night school at Milford had taught him his own name over the winter, that and nothing else yet; and he took the book from Hester as he would have taken a lamb, and ran his finger along the line under the title until it stopped on the shape it knew, and looked at it a long while, and gave the book back without a word.

Hester was turning pages, fast, looking for one thing. She found it two-thirds through.

"I crossed that out."

It was a line about byre floors: how deep the old muck went before you got to clean clay, and why a floor of rammed chalk and a drain to the midden put flesh on a winter calf. Hester had struck it through in October at her dairy table in front of the school's clerk.

"It's right," said Emeric.

"I know it's right. Paxton's byres are chalked and he's not lost a calf since." She put her finger on the line. "I crossed it out because you said it. It's our book. If there's a line in it out of your mouth and not ours, the first man at Grayrock who reads it will know, and he'll say it's the duke's book with our names on it for show."

Ferra, after a breath, took the pencil out of her book's spine and gave it to her. Hester struck the line through in that copy with a ruler's straightness and no ruler, and closed the book and handed it back.

"Now count it."

Ferra put it in the crate and wrote beside the crate's number, small: one, amended.

"Leave the rest," said Hollis to Emeric, quite kindly, as the crate went into the van. "They'll not know which ones are yours. I can't tell, and I was there."



The builders' yard lay between the station and the works, on the long walled ground below the brickfield, and they went up through it because it was the shortest way and because Ferra wanted to see whether anybody was in it on a Sunday.

Somebody was.

The long printer stood at the yard's far end under its tarpaulin, as it had since January, with frost still on its rails in the wall's shadow: two lines of steel rail the length of a terrace of four houses, and over them, on four tall legs with a wheel at each foot, a gantry of black girders as wide as a street. Under the tarpaulin the hoist arm, the hose on its drum, and the nozzle wrapped in sacking with its two steel trowels folded up either side like a heron's legs. The builders' yard had built it over the winter between runs of doors and stair treads. It had not printed anything yet. Emeric had written construction printer across the top of the first drawing; the yard called it the long printer, after the gantry; Bram called it the machine, as he had called the dynamo the machine for its whole first year, and would until it had earned better.

Bram was standing under it in his Sunday coat with his hand flat on one of its legs. Tess was in her chair in the door of the tracer shed at the rails' end with half the tracer table's linkage on her knees, and Osric beside her holding a box of small brass parts, with a clean bandage on his right wrist that he held up to show Emeric from thirty yards off.

"It's Sunday," said Ferra.

"I know what day it is," said Bram.

"The Works Rule's been on the gate since Friday noon. One day in seven a man's own. I copied it in a hand you could read from the lane."

"I'm not working. I'm looking. Nobody pays a man to look."

"I'm looking too," said Tess, with a screwdriver in her teeth.

"I'm carrying," said Osric.

Ferra opened her book and wrote something short.

"What did you put?" said Bram.

"That the Works Rule was two days old, and the first three to break it were the Secretary of Works, the mistress of bay three and a knight of Kaldo." She put the book under her arm. "And the Chancellor, who's been on the platform since six. I put myself in too."

Emeric put his hand on the leg beside Bram's. The steel was cold enough to hurt.

"She wants your station turned inside out," said Bram, as though they had been talking about it for an hour; which, between the two of them and Tess, they had been, in pieces, since the pegs went into the field in July.

"I want it to reach," said Tess, round the screwdriver.

"It reaches. The east half's been on my dynamos since the autumn before last and not gone dark a night. The trams want it my way. Your pots want it my way; you'll not make a button of the light metal on the back-and-forth, you told me so. Every motor on my floor but one wants it my way, and I can mend every one of them with my eyes shut."

"And it stops at a mile," said Tess.

"Then build it a mile from what wants it."

"Everything wants it." She took the screwdriver out of her mouth. "The Port Town. The suburb. Every farm on the flats. You'll not build a station a mile from every farm."

Bram did not answer. Emeric said nothing either, because Bram was right about every machine on his floor and Tess was right about the farms, and he had not yet decided which of them was right about the station.

Ferra was watching him.

"He'll decide on the wall," she told her father. "Tonight. He stands up there with a lantern and looks at the town as if it's a column that won't add, and in the morning there's a sheet on my table."

"Then I'll be on the wall," said Bram.

"You're always on the wall."

"Then I'll stand nearer," said Bram, and took his hand off the machine and blew on it.



Sorrel's hot-air balloon went up in the square at two.

It had never gone up inside the walls. Every year Sorrel had asked for the square, and every year Ward had said no, because a fire basket on a rope over a town is a fire basket over a town whatever is on the rope. This year he had said yes. There had been no thatch inside the walls for three years; the roofs round the square were slate and tile and the church's was lead, and he had the new class in a ring round the square's edge with a bucket every ten paces and Mira Holt walking the ring with a list. He stood under the church porch with his arms folded and watched the bag fill as if it owed him money.

It was the same bag, or most of it, patched so often in so many greys that from below it looked like a map of somewhere; the fire basket burning Tess's big cakes round the still's blue spirit lamp, a new willow basket wide enough for four, and the rope down to a winch off the builders' yard crane with four of the yard's men on the cranks.

Sorrel took them up three at a time, a hundred feet, ten minutes a turn, and who went was drawn from a hat. Sela held the hat. She had taken it over without anybody asking her, as she took over most things that needed doing in front of a crowd, and she drew each slip herself and read it out once, loudly, and not twice.

"Wren," she said, at the third turn. "Rylan's."

Wren came through the crowd at a run. Nessa came after her rather faster, and caught her by the back of the collar at the basket's side without breaking stride.

"Not on your own."

"Sorrel's in it."

"Sorrel's flying it," said Nessa. "That's not the same thing." She looked round for Finn, who was taller than his father now and standing at the back pretending he had not put his name in the hat, and then past Finn at Rylan.

Rylan had already stepped forward: a knight of Kaldo who had come off the keep's wall into the canal ring more times than anybody in the barony, and had done a number of things since that nobody in the square would ever be told about. He stepped up to the basket cheerfully, as a man steps into a boat to cross a river.

"I'll go," he said. "Somebody has to."

Sorrel looked at him over the rim with her bad leg cocked and her cropped head on one side, and something in her face that Emeric did not like on Rylan's behalf.

"In you get," she said.

The winch let the rope out a turn at a time, and the bag lifted off the cobbles with its patched belly glowing faintly in the daylight, past the school's upper windows, past the church's gutter, until the basket hung a hundred feet over the square with the whole town's faces turned up under it.

Wren was leaning over the rim as far as a girl can lean with one hand holding on, shrieking with pure delight and pointing at things, her own house among them, which she could not possibly have picked out. Rylan had his left fist in the back of her coat.

His right hand had the rim. He was standing in the middle of the basket very straight, with his eyes on the church tower's weathercock, which was at the level of his face and about twenty feet off, and he was not looking at anything else in the world; and his face, even from a hundred feet below, had gone the colour of the patches.

Ward, under the porch, unfolded his arms.

"Hm," he said.

They came down at the end of the ten minutes. Wren was over the rim before the basket had stopped, and ran to Nessa and began to tell her all of it, in order, from the beginning. Rylan climbed out more slowly, and stood on the cobbles with one hand still on the rim, as a man stands on a quay after a bad crossing, and let go of it.

"Da was grey," said Finn.

"He was green," said Nessa.

"I was neither," said Rylan.

"He was both," said Wren, and went on with her account.

Emeric went over and kept his voice down. "You've done worse than that. A good deal worse. In the dark."

"With something to do." Rylan was flexing his right hand and looking at it. "In the suit you've the glide and the flare and the ground coming, and your hands full. Up there," and he jerked his head at the bag, rising again behind them with three of the school's children in it, "you're stood in a basket with your life on a rope and four men you've never met on a crank, and nothing to do but think about it." He breathed out. "I'll not do that again."

"Sorrel knew."

"Sorrel," said Rylan, with feeling, "has been waiting since the day she came for that," and went to stand with his wife, who took his arm and did not give it back.



Tess went up at the last turn, at dusk, because Sorrel said so.

"The cakes are hers," Sorrel told Sela, when Sela looked at the hat. "And the wash on the bag. And the lamp in the basket. She's had the bag aloft five springs and never once been in it." She looked down at Tess at the winch. "Coming or not?"

Tess considered the basket the way she considered anything she had built and not tried, which was with deep suspicion. Then she held up her arms; not to anybody in particular, though there was never any doubt who would be there. Osric put his box of brass parts down on the church step, and lifted her out of the chair, and stepped into the basket with her and set her on the bench across one side of it, and sat down beside her with his bandaged wrist in his lap; and the yard's men cranked, and they went up into the last of the light.

The lamps came on while they were up.

They came on as they had every evening for a year and more, all at once, at the minute of dusk the board kept in its book: the square's posts first, then the lanes off it, white and steady, and the river street, and the long double line of them down the Gate Avenue to the South Gate; and the windows behind them, a great many, the same white. Nobody in the square looked at them.

Tess was looking. He could see her from below, small and upright on the bench, turning her head slowly from one side of the town to the other, as she turned it along a board to read a row of meters; and Osric beside her not looking at the town at all.

When they came down she had Osric set her in her chair before she said anything. Then she wheeled herself straight across the cobbles to Emeric, through the crowd, which got out of her way.

"It stops," she said.

"Where?"

"A mile, near enough. More east, where Bram's feeders are thickest. You can see it from up there; you'd not see it from the wall. It's like the line on the quay steps where the tide's been. White inside it, every lane. Then the Port Town's one street of gas, yellow. Then the suburb's pegs, with a house roofed here and there and a candle in it. Then the lock villages and the farms." She put her hands flat on her knees. "Then nothing. A candle, here and there. Bram's right about every machine on his floor. And there's nothing past the mile for any of them to run."

Bram had come up behind her while she talked. He listened to his best student tell the duke that he was right about everything and that it did not matter, and said nothing at all.



Maren had a barrow by the church steps, and a queue at it that went twice round the square.

The cold cream was at the school railings with a girl from the castle kitchen, because the town had decided three years ago that Lantern Night was when you ate cold cream, whatever the weather. Maren's own barrow had an iron pan on it as big as a cartwheel, with a lid she held down with her whole weight while a boy from the works shook it over the heat; and inside it a sound like hail on a tin roof, and then like a battle, so that the front of the queue stepped back every time and the back pushed forward to see why.

Popcorn. The town had first met it in the autumn, out of Maren's kitchen, and it had gone round Kaldo in a week, and nobody had got over it yet. Maren shovelled it into paper cones with butter from a jug and salt from her fist, a Copper a cone.

She gave him three when he reached the front with Ferra and Imani, and held out her hand for six.

"The castle pays double."

"It paid triple last time."

"It's been a hard winter." She looked at his head. "Somebody's done your hair."

"Somebody has."

"About time." Maren looked past him at Imani, who was eating popcorn one piece at a time and looking at nothing. "Next."

The square was full and filling: the holdings off the trains, the dock quarter over the bridge, the Port Town down the river road, the class off its bucket ring, the freed men from the Isle in a knot by the school railings in their new coats, watching. Everybody with a lantern folded under an arm, and the smell of butter and hot salt and three thousand people in their best wool on a cold March evening.

By the church steps a carter in a Milford coat had an Almanac open. He had bought it at the station, he told the people round him, because the one coming free to his steading would come in a week and he was not a patient man; and he was reading bits of it aloud the way a man reads the good bits of a broadsheet. The moon to the hour, which got a laugh. The Grayrock fare. When to sow beans on the upland, which a woman beside him said was wrong and a man beside her said was right. Then he turned to the days.

"Lantern Night. That's tonight. Commencement. The Long Table." He ran his finger down the column, and down again, slowly, and looked up with a great broad delight coming into his face, the delight of a man who has found a mistake in a lord's book.

"There's no day in here," he said, loud, "for the duke's wedding."

The people round him laughed. The people round them turned to see why, and were told, and laughed; and it went out across the square the way a ring goes out across a pond, in about the time it took Emeric to put a piece of popcorn in his mouth and decide not to swallow it.

"When?" said somebody by the railings.

"When, Your Grace?"

It was cheerful. That was the worst of it. Nobody meant anything by it but a good time on a cold night, and they took it up from every side until it was a game, people who had never said a word to him in ten years calling it across the square; and Ferra on his left went on eating her popcorn with perfect composure, and Imani on his right went on looking at nothing.

He swallowed.

"It's not in the Almanac," he said, loud enough for the church steps, "because the Almanac only has things in it you can put a date to. The moon. The beans." He held up the cone. "There's no hailstorm in it either, and we'll have one by June."

That got a laugh. It did not get him anywhere.

"That's a weather sign, not an answer!"

"It's the Chancellor's book. Ask the Chancellor."

"The Chancellor," said Ferra, to her popcorn, "prints what she can date."

The nearest heard it and passed it back, and the square laughed again; and then a woman's voice from the far end of the square called out the other question, the old one, the one the river inn had kept a slate on since the harvest dance.

"Which, then?"

"Which!" said the square, delighted.

He looked at the two of them. Ferra had a piece of popcorn between finger and thumb and was examining it as though it might be counterfeit. Imani was looking at him sideways, with her cone under her chin and the face she had in the ward for a symptom she had not seen before and found interesting.

So he told the square what he knew. It was not what they had asked for. It was what he had that was true and could be said in a square.

"The first winter I was here," he said, "the barony owed more than it took in, and the only person in Kaldo who'd tell me what it couldn't pay for was the smith's daughter, at the smith's kitchen table, with a pencil. She's told me every winter since. I've never once been glad to hear it, and she's never once been wrong." He let the laugh come and went on through the end of it. "And my first spring here the north well was killing the lower town's children, and I knew why, and the healer didn't believe a word I said until she'd tried it three ways herself. There's a good many of you in this square alive tonight because she tries things."

The square had gone quieter than he had meant it to.

"The trains run because one of them counts. You're alive to ride them because the other wouldn't stop boiling. I'd not have lasted a winter in this town without either of them." He stopped. "Nor would you."

For a breath nobody said anything.

"That's not when!" said the carter with the Almanac, at last, aggrieved; and the square let its breath go and roared.

"He's said everything!"

"He's not said the thing!"

"Ask Master Bram! Master Bram, when?"

Bram was at the edge of the crowd by Maren's barrow with a cone of his own. He looked over the heads at the voice for some time, chewing, with no expression whatever.

"Ask her," said Bram, and went back to his popcorn.

Emeric did not look at Ferra. He felt it at his left side as plainly as if she had spoken: she had stopped eating. She took the pencil out of her book's spine without hurry and opened the book, not at the day's page but at the back, past the river's winds, to the inside of the back cover, where she kept a column with no heading and believed he did not know it. She made a mark there. Small. Then she closed the book on the pencil and put it under her arm, and took another piece of popcorn out of her cone and ate it.

He did not ask. He could not have read it if he had turned his head, which he did not, and she knew he would not, which was why she had done it there.

Imani held her cone out to Ferra.

"Hold that."

Ferra took it. "Where are you going?"

"The river inn." Imani wiped her fingers on her handkerchief, one at a time. "I want to know what it thinks."



On Lantern Night the river inn's taproom was as full as a taproom can be and still have a door that opens.

Imani opened it. The noise came out into the street like heat out of an oven, and then, from the door inward, in a widening patch, it stopped.

Emeric came in behind her because there was nothing else to do. He had followed her down the river street at a distance meant to look casual, and the whole street had watched him do it.

The slate hung behind the bar: a schoolroom slate in a pine frame, chalked and wiped and chalked again so many times its black had gone grey. He had never seen it. He had heard of it from Saban at the long table, the year of the harvest dance, and had looked at the ceiling.

Saban was in the corner under the stair, where he always was on a festival night, because a stranger in Kaldo came to the river inn first and Saban liked to be sitting there already. He had his hat on his knee and a pot of ale in front of him, untouched, and he watched Imani come through the crowd with the air of a man who has been waiting since the sixth year for a particular delivery.

The innkeeper, a big soft man with flour on his apron, saw who was at his bar and what she was looking at, and his ears went red.

"Mistress."

"Which of us," said Imani pleasantly, "is the money on?"

The innkeeper opened his mouth and nothing came out of it.

"Evens, the Chancellor," said Saban from the corner, without raising his voice; the room was quiet enough now that he did not need to. "She was two to one on, the year of the dance. She's drifted. Six to four, yourself. You've come in." He turned his hat. "Three to one he marries somebody nobody here has met, for a treaty. Capital money, mostly, off the Wayford train. I'd not take it." He looked at the slate. "And a line for never, at eight, that a man from the dock quarter put a Copper on in the seventh year when he was drunk, and has come in every Lantern Night since to ask for it back."

"He can't have it," said the innkeeper, finding his voice.

Imani read the slate the whole way down, the way she read a chart at the foot of a bed.

"Is there a line," she said, "for both?"

The innkeeper looked at her.

"Both what, mistress?"

"Both of us," said Imani. "Same day."

There was a short silence in the taproom of the river inn of a kind it had never had on a Lantern Night, and then a noise so large that the pots jumped on the shelves: men laughing with their heads back, women hitting their husbands on the arm, somebody at the back standing on a bench to see. In the door, Emeric felt the back of his neck go hot and stay hot.

The innkeeper waited for it to finish. It took some time.

"There's no such line," he said.

"There is now," said Saban.

The innkeeper looked at Saban, and at Imani, and at the duke in his doorway; and then slowly, like a man who has understood that whatever he does next will be told in every inn on the Wayford line by Thursday, he picked up the chalk.

BOTH, he wrote, at the foot of the slate. SAME DAY.

"Forty," he said. "Forty to one, mistress. And that's generous."

"It's not," said Saban.

Imani took a Silver out of her bag and laid it flat on the bar and pushed it across the wet wood with one finger, as she pushed a dose across the dispensary counter.

"My name," she said. "And the date."

He wrote it. She watched him, and read it back off the slate, and nodded; and then she walked back through a crowd that opened for her in a long laughing lane, and stopped in front of Emeric.

"Imani."

"You had your turn in the square," said Imani, and went past him into the street.

Saban had not laughed. He was looking at the new line at the foot of the slate with the quiet attention he gave a ledger that has just told him something its owner had not meant it to.

"Best-informed money on the board," he said mildly, to nobody.



The trestles were along the inside of the river wall as they were every year, with their pots of lamp-black and the school's brushes, and the town was writing its names.

He walked them with Ward. Ward walked them because three thousand people with fire in their hands on a wall was a thing to be walked, and Emeric walked them because Ward did.

The freed men had a trestle of their own at the north end by the tower. They had carried it there themselves from the school's stack, and they stood round it in their new coats and wrote, many in hands that had not held a pen since before the Isle and some that never had, with the others spelling for them; and their lanterns filled the trestle and went on along the parapet behind it in a row.

Armand was at the head of the water stair in his chair, in two rugs and his fur, because Imani had said he could come up if he came in the fur, with a lantern on a board across his knees that he had written at his own table that morning and would not show anybody.

Edric was not with the freed men. He was at the school's trestle with his mother and his brother. Sela had her own lantern done and set aside on the trestle's end, and stood with her hands folded on her apron while Edric said names, low, one at a time, with a pause between each while he found the next, and a short careful cough now and then into his collar; and Arthur wrote them in his school capitals as fast as Edric said them. The lantern was full. Arthur turned it and went on up the other side. Sela took another lantern from the stack, opened it out, set it in front of Arthur without a word, and went back to where she had been.

They were not his names to hear. But Sela saw him across the trestle, and after a breath she picked up her own lantern and turned it with its writing toward him, and held it there.

TAM.

One name. She held it until he had read it, and set it down again, and went on watching her sons.

Ward had stopped walking.

He was at the garrison's trestle by the water stair, looking at the lanterns stacked on it, with his hand resting on one of the brushes.

In six Lantern Nights Emeric had never seen Ward at a trestle.

Ward picked up the brush. He opened a lantern on the trestle, and dipped, and stood with the brush held over the paper for some while, as Emeric had seen him stand at a door he was about to go through. Then he began to write. Small, close, in his square careful hand, name under name, not covering them, not hurrying.

Rylan came along the wall walk with Nessa and the children, and saw, and said something low to Nessa, and came and took a brush and a lantern and stood at Ward's left and wrote. Declan came a little after, from wherever Declan had been, and stood at Ward's right. Osric came last, and took a brush in his bandaged hand because it was the one he wrote with, and swore under his breath at it, and wrote anyway, slowly.

The four of them stood at the garrison's trestle with their backs to the town and wrote, and nobody on the wall walk went near them. Emeric did not read the names. He knew what floor they came from.

Tess had written hers at the works' trestle before any of them, the same two names as every year, and had come along the wall walk and stopped her chair at the end of the garrison's trestle with it on her knees, and waited there until Osric had finished.

He wrote his own at the counting room's trestle with Ferra beside him: Harl and Wick on the outside, where they were every year, and the third on the inside by the hoop, where it would face the flame and nobody else. Ferra wrote one name on the inside and folded the paper over it. Imani came back from the hospital's trestle with hers already written, in her square capitals, large, facing the town: her father's name, where anybody on the wall could read it.

"Forty to one," she said to Ferra, across him.

Ferra did not look up from her lantern. "Forty."

"A Silver. In my name. He wrote the date."

"You should have sent me." Ferra smoothed the paper over her name with the side of her hand. "I'd have had sixty out of him. You don't go in and ask. You go in and say you've heard the odds are better at the posting house in Wayford." She opened her purse, still without looking up, and took out a Silver, and put it in Imani's hand and closed Imani's fingers on it. "Tomorrow. Mine as well. At whatever you can get."

"He's standing right here," said Imani.

"He's looking at the river," said Ferra.

He was. He looked at it with great attention until he was sure that neither of them was going to say anything else he would have to be looking at the river for.



Ferra read the count from the head of the water stair, a little before the bell.

It had not been her idea. Ward and Ottoline had decided between them that the town should hear it, and nobody had ever won an argument against the two of them together. Ferra had agreed on one condition: no list. She had one sheet. She read it the way she read a rent roll, without raising her voice and without stopping, and the people on the wall leaned in to hear her and the people on the quay passed it back to the people behind them.

"Thatch inside the walls. None, three years. You knew that."

"Ward one, the west ward. Rebuilt. Ward two, the Gate Avenue. Rebuilt. Four hundred and sixty-three households in the new blocks between them, and every one moved into a finished house. Not one put out of an old one first." She did not look up. "Wenna Hallam's street on the river road has thirty-one households in it, built by its own men. Wenna wants it said that it's thirty-one, not thirty."

A cheer went up from the quay at the Port Town end, and was hushed.

"Water at the tap. Every house inside the walls." She held up a second sheet, printed one side. "And this. The Kaldo Standard. What every house is owed, and what the Works Office will come and fit if it isn't there. It's on the records hall door from tomorrow. If your house hasn't got what's on it, you take this to the Works Office and they'll come." She paused. "If they don't come, you take it to me."

That got a laugh, the long low laugh of a town that knew Ferra.

"The fevers." She gave them by ward, slowly, the old wards and the new. Then the year before, by ward. Then the year before that. She read three columns and stopped, and did not say what the difference meant.

The wall was quiet. Somebody on the quay began to ask somebody else what she had said, and was hushed.

Ferra folded the sheet and came back along the wall walk to where he stood between Imani and the parapet, and took her lantern back from him without a word.

"You didn't say what the fevers meant," said Imani.

"No."

"They'll work it out."

"That's the idea," said Ferra.



The bell went in the church tower.

All along the wall, and on the quay and the bridge, and on the far bank where the dock quarter's people who had not yet gone over to the Port Town stood on their own staithes, people struck matches. The boxes had been on every trestle since noon, Kaldo matches in Kaldo paper boxes with a rough strip down the side, and the wall walk filled with the small hard crack of them and the smell of the heads going off; and in the cups at the bottoms of the lanterns Tess's cakes caught one after another, and the paper glowed, and began to fill.

He held his own in his left hand and put the first two fingers of his right to the cake in its cup.

He had not thought about it. He had lit a hundred fires that way, in the cellar and the yard and the long shed, and once in the bottom of a lighter, with Tobe holding the tray and saying he would never get used to it; fire was the one magic in him the whole town had seen. A small red ring came up off his fingertips and was gone, and the cake caught, a clean little flame in the cup, and the paper over it went gold.

A boy at the next trestle saw, and shouted, and pointed. A girl of about seven beside him put her own two fingers to her own cake and pressed them there very hard with her face screwed up, and nothing happened, and her mother took her hand away from the cup and struck a match for her.

Ferra had seen it too. She struck her own match without looking at him, and lit her cake, and shook the match out.

"Next year," she said, "you'll use a match."

"Why?"

"Because it's theirs." She held her lantern up a little as the paper began to tug. "The bell and the matches and the names. It's the one night that's theirs and not yours." She did not say it unkindly. "Use a match. Like everybody."

He looked at the girl at the next trestle, who had her lantern lit now and was holding it up in both hands and had forgotten about her fingers entirely.

"A match," he said.

"I'll have it written down by morning," said Ferra, "so you can't say I didn't."

The wind came round.

It came with full dark, as it did one evening in two in March, as the barge pilots said and Ferra's column had said for nine years and the Almanac now said in print with A GUESS under it; it came round in the valley and ran down it toward the marsh and the sea, and the lanterns felt it and pulled, and the town let them go.

They went up off the river wall in a long slow drift, and off the quay and the bridge and the far bank, and the wind took them out over the water and away downriver, glowing, turning a little as they went, more than anybody could count; and the wall went quiet, as it did every year, along its whole length. Imani's went first, her father's name toward the town. Then Ferra's. Then his. Then Armand's, from his chair at the stair's head, which Imani had to let go for him because his hands would not open in time, and which went up crooked and righted itself over the water and went on after the others. And down by the water stair four lanterns went up together from the garrison's trestle, close, almost touching, and a fifth after them from Tess's chair, and the wind took them all the same way.

On the far bank somebody began the song the dock quarter sang at a launch, and the Port Town end of the quay took it up, and got the words wrong.

Ferra wrote the wind and the hour in her column on the parapet; and then she took an Almanac out of her coat, and found March, and put a small tick beside A GUESS.

"Printed," she said, "and held."



Fennick's display went up at nine from the marsh dyke, a long way from anything, because Ward had chosen the place, as he had every year since the first.

Fennick had told everybody in Kaldo for a month that this year nothing would burn: not a reed, not a rick, not a fencepost, because every shell would go so high and burst so clean that what came down would be cold before it was halfway to the ground. Ward had listened to all of it and written the powder in his book to the ounce, and had the dyke wetted for a hundred yards either side, and sent six of the class out with buckets anyway.

It went high, higher than any year before, rocket after rocket off the dyke and the burst far up over the black marsh: white, and a hard red Fennick had been after for two years, and gold above all, falling in long slow streamers like willow branches.

At the last a single shell went up very high and burst in a ring of gold, with a blot of gold in the ring a little off its middle, that hung there for three breaths and fell.

The wall puzzled at it.

"It's a rose," said somebody near the water stair, uncertainly.

"It's the rose!" said somebody else, with conviction; and the wall took it up, the gold rose, the duchy's own, and cheered it all the way down to the reeds; and out on the dyke, a small dark shape against the last of the gold, Fennick could be seen dancing.

Nothing caught. The six on the dyke stood in the dark with their buckets full and nothing to throw them on, and after a quarter of an hour they poured them out on the ground and came in.

Fennick came up the water stair at half past nine, black to the elbows, with his maimed hand in his armpit and a grin that took up most of his face, and went straight to Ward.

"Nothing," said Fennick.

"Nothing," said Ward.

"Write it down."

Ward wrote, and turned the book so Fennick could read it.

"The rick was the first year," said Ward, putting the book away.

"The rick was the wind," said Fennick. Then, still grinning, to Emeric: "Did you see the rose?"

"I saw the rose."

"It was meant to have petals," said Fennick. "I'll have petals next year," and went off along the wall walk to tell everybody who had seen it what it had been meant to be.



Saban came up onto the wall a little before ten with his collar up, and found the three of them by the river tower's door, where they had stopped because Armand was being carried down the water stair in his chair and Imani was watching every step of it.

"It's come in," said Saban.

"What has?" said Ferra.

"Both, same day. Forty when she put her money down. Six more took it before the fireworks. It stood at twelve to one when I left, and he was talking about wiping it." He took his hat off and looked into it. "One of the six was Maren. Two Silver, and she told him if he wiped it she'd know."

Imani, at the stair's head, did not turn round. Her shoulders moved.

"And one," said Saban, "was your father."

Ferra looked at him.

"My father doesn't bet."

"He does now. A Silver, on both, same day. Came in at half past eight, put it down, drank half a pot standing up and went out. The innkeeper asked him if he was sure." Saban put his hat back on. "He said, 'Write it.'"

Ferra stood quite still on the wall walk with her book under her arm.

"He'll have done it to annoy me," she said.

"Very likely," said Saban, and went down.

Imani had turned round. She looked at Emeric over Ferra's shoulder for exactly as long as Ferra took to decide what her father had meant by it, and to decide that she knew; and then she looked back down the stair at Armand's chair.

Emeric looked at the river. It had not changed.



The river tower was the oldest point on the river wall and the highest, with the watch's long glass on its stand on the leads. The watch had gone down to the quay for the late train's crowd, and Ward was at the foot of the stair, and so the top of it was theirs.

It was ten years and more since he had come in at the South Gate past a boy on the door, and nearly as many since a March evening at the corner above the east bridge, with a lantern swung three times for Tess at the lever. Then there had been eight arc lamps on the wall and a candle here and there under them, and the dark; and the dark had gone back a street at a time while he was looking at other things. He had counted thatch from this wall until he lost count and was glad to, and roofs the year Imani told him he had a face like Ferra's. There was nothing in the roofs to count now.

Inside the wall the town was lit, all of it. The canal ring round the keep lay white under its arc lamps, every bridge over it and every bench on its banks; and out from it the lanes, and the river street with its lamps doubled in the water, and the west ward's courtyard blocks round their gardens, four storeys of brick and Kaldo's grey concrete block, a lit window in nearly every one; and down the Gate Avenue the double line of posts from the keep to the South Gate, with ward two's blocks along it lit floor over floor and the young plane trees in their iron grates along the kerb. The hospital end to end on its hill. The school's windows. Out past the South Gate a tram going away down the river road on its wire with its windows lit and nobody in it but the driver, because the whole town was here; and far off at the arm's mouth, small and white and steady, the lighthouse, which had been lit in November and which people still went down to the quay in the evenings to look at.

He looked past the wall.

Tess was right. Now that he knew to look for it he could see it as plainly as she had: the edge. The town's light went out from the walls in every direction, and on a while past them along the roads and the river, white; and then it stopped, all round, at very nearly the same distance, as a tide stops on a beach. Along the river road past the port's new quay the Port Town's one finished street lay in a short line of yellow, its gas lamps burning the soft flat flames he had promised Ward at the long table in the eighth year; and beyond it the pegged streets that were not built yet, and the roofed houses among them with their windows in tallow. South-west, between the wall and the woods, the suburb's first houses on their dark grid, a candle in one window in three. West up the canal past the ice house, the lock villages. And out on the flats in every direction, the farms.

He counted them. He had not meant to, and he did not stop.

The Port Town: a hundred and eight windows with any light in them at all, and every one yellow. The suburb's: forty-one. The lock villages he could not separate, and gave up at sixty. The farms he could not count, because there was nothing out there to count; a steading's window at a mile was a spark and at three miles was nothing, and he knew from Ottoline's cards that there were more than four hundred steadings on the flats within ten miles of the wall, and from the river tower he could see, perhaps, nine.

"Bram's dynamos stop at a mile," said Ferra, behind him.

"They stop at a mile."

"And the little sets at the works for the west half? The back-and-forth ones?"

"They're small, and they're full, and they're at the works." He did not turn round. "It's not the dynamo. It's the wire. Push a current a long way down a wire at the pressure a house can use and the wire eats it. A mile and you've lost a good part. Ten and you've lost the lot, or you want copper as thick as my wrist." He looked out at the dark. "Turn it back and forth and you can make the pressure as high as you like at one end for the long road, and bring it down again at the other in a lane transformer, one of Tess's grey boxes at the head of a lane. Then a wire you could lift in one hand does what a wagon of copper couldn't."

"Alternating," said Imani. She had come up the last of the stair and stood at the parapet on his other side with her arms folded in her coat. "Tess says alternating. She told the whole ward at dinner on Thursday. Twice."

"Alternating current." He said it to the dark. "The power station, all of it. Up high for the long lines, down at the lane's end, and out along the rails to everything."

There was a man in the stair door. He had been there some while; Emeric had felt him come up the last turn past Ward and stop, a big slow note he would have known in any crowd in the kingdom, nearer, as he had said he would be, and not a word out of him.

"Every machine on your floor," said Emeric, without turning round, "will want it turned back the one way at the far end. A box at every motor."

"Then I'll build the boxes," said Bram, from the door.

"You said it stopped at a mile."

"It does stop at a mile. I never said there was nothing past it." Bram came out and stood at the parapet at Ferra's elbow and looked at the dark a while, at the edge his best student had shown him from the square. "She's right," he said. "I'll not tell her till June."

Ferra had her book open on the parapet. She wrote, and turned it so that he could see by the light that came up off the town, as she had turned it for him every time she had written down something he decided since the first winter: the date, and under it, in square small capitals, AC STATION, and under that, smaller, Decided on the wall. As forecast.

"You'll write it out properly in the morning," she said. "I'll not copy a word that long twice."

Bram read the page over her shoulder, all three lines, and grunted.

"I'll go and draw a box," he said.

"It's Sunday," said Ferra.

"Then I'll think about a box. Nobody pays a man to think." He went back to the stair door, and stopped in it, and looked at the three of them at the parapet for a breath longer than he needed to; and went down.

He took the long glass off its stand.

He swung it west first, along the line of the Milford road over the dark flats, slowly, with the glass full of nothing; and found it, low on the sky where the land rose to Milford's upland thirty miles off, where there had never in his life been anything at night but stars. A smudge. Faint and yellowish, no bigger in the glass than a thumbnail at arm's length: Milford's main street under its lamps, off the wind row on the ridge. He held the glass on it until his eye watered.

Then north, up the river's valley, past the hills; and there, forty miles off on a hill where the valley turned, a single hard point that did not twinkle as the stars round it did. Grayrock's pithead. The powerhouse on its own coal, with a lamp on the chimney head for the night shift to walk home by; and round it, so faint he could not be sure he had not imagined it, the rows.

He gave the glass to Ferra without a word. She looked, west and then north, with her lips moving, and gave it to Imani; and Imani looked a long time at the north, and handed it back.

"You can see Bryda's rows," she said.

"You can see something."

"You can see Bryda's rows. My nurses sat in those rows the winter of the pox with tallow dips, so the mothers could see the arms."



And then he talked. He had not meant to do that either.

He told them the station: the boiler house and the steam turbine he had been drawing since the summer and putting back in the drawer, and the coal landing beside it, where Tam's trains would run straight in under the hoppers. He told them the lines along the rails, because the rails already ran where the people were and a lineman could ride a wagon to any pole on them; Grayrock first, because Grayrock dug the coal and the copper both and had been last for everything. Milford would want a line too, wind or no wind, because the wind did not blow when Hester's cold store needed it. He told them that Wenna's thirty-one would have bulbs by the harvest, and the Port Town's pegged streets would be wired before they were roofed, because the long printer could set the empty pipe for the wire in the wall as it went, and nothing in Kaldo would need cutting into again. He told them the farms, a pole and a grey box at every lane's end and a bulb in every room that was lived in; and that the night school at Milford would not have to shut at eight because the tallow cost too much, and Farris could learn the word after his name. He told them Tess's ring round the wall, and the falls. He told them the carter on the church steps who had bought an Almanac because he was not a patient man, and that there would be a carter like him on every steading in the four baronies by Saturday, reading out the moon to the hour to people who did not need it.

He said a great many things.

They let him. Ferra stood at the parapet with her book open on the stone in front of her and her pencil in her hand, and the pencil did not move; she was not writing any of it down. Imani had her arm through his, and somewhere about the Port Town she had slipped two fingers under the cuff of his coat, against the inside of his wrist, as she did when she was not working and did not want him to know she was.

When he stopped, nobody said anything for a while.

Out over the marsh nothing moved. The lanterns were long down in the reeds, cold, as Tess had made them, and the last of Fennick's smoke had gone away on the wind.

"Forty-one," said Ferra.

He turned his head.

"Things," said Ferra. "About the town. I counted." She closed the book on the pencil. "Forty-one."

Imani took her fingers out from under his cuff.

"Seventy-four," she said, of something else.

He waited for one of them to say what the forty-second would have been.

Neither did. Ferra put the book in her coat. Imani put her hand in her pocket and looked out at the marsh with her arm still through his. He had heard it himself, going past it: the place in the list where a thing ought to have been and was not, as plain as a missing step on a stair you have gone up every night for years. He had gone past it at the Port Town, and at the farms, and at the end. They had both heard it. Nobody said.

Below them the town went on being lit, all of it, to the edge; and past the edge the dark, with nine farms in it and a candle in one window in three.

"Home," said Imani at last, "before you count anything else."

"There's the late train," said Ferra. "I want to see it go at eleven. The Almanac says it waits for the fireworks." She looked at the quay. "If it's not gone by five past, I'm writing to the yard."

It went at eleven. They watched it from the river tower, the three of them, its lamps going away up the Milford line in a slow bright string across the dark flats, past the mile and the edge and on into the farms, until it was a spark low in the west under the smudge that was Milford; and Ferra took out her watch and looked at it, and put it away, satisfied.
