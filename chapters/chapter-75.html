Chapter 75 - Who Carries It

Hale had cleared his desk for them, which in Hale's office meant that the pens lay in a row on the left instead of a row on the right.

Emeric stood at the end of it with Saban's lamp-oil bill flat under his hand. Ward had the wall by the door, as he always had a wall by a door. Ferra had taken Hale's chair without being offered it, and Hale had let her, and stood at her shoulder with his hands folded in front of his coat as if he were the one who had come to see her.

"Somebody has to carry it north," Emeric said. "The register, Ferra's page, the map, every date. Into a room in the capital where somebody can do something about it. Who?"

"Not the post," said Ferra.

"Not the post." He did not bother to argue that one. "Saban goes to the capital every spring."

"Saban goes to the markets in the capital every spring," Ferra said. "He can't put a matter before anybody. He's a man with two carts. They'd thank him for his trouble and show him the door, and he'd charge us for the walk."

"He would." Emeric moved his hand on the bill. "The House has its own riders. Vantic's letters arrive inside a fortnight."

"Take it to Keller," said Ward.

They all looked at him. He had not moved from the wall. He had said it the way he would have said that it was raining, and Emeric had not finished the list yet, and there were two more names on it, and Ward had not waited for either of them.

Emeric noticed that. He noticed, too, that it was the right answer, and that Ward had got there faster than a man should who had not already been thinking about it for some time. He did not ask. Whatever Ward had been thinking about, and for how long, it would keep until the road.

"I'll ask the House first," he said.



The tower stair had not got any wider since October. He went up the last turn of it sideways and found the door open its hand's width, as before, and knocked, as before.

"Come in."

He stayed in the doorway, as before. Vantic was at the table under the window with the light going grey behind her and her own book open, and she put the pen down and waited, and did not get up.

"Twenty-one letters from this barony to the crown have gone missing in two years," he said. "All of them through one house at Wayford. I need it in front of somebody in the capital who can act. Will the House carry it?"

She did not answer at once. She looked at him with the same face she brought to every question, the face that weighed it for exactly what it was and not one grain more, and he could see her taking the question to pieces and setting the pieces out in a row.

"The House reports to the partners on its security," she said. "What the barony pledged. What it earns, and whether it will go on earning. That's what my letters carry." She laid one hand flat on her book. "A borrower's quarrel with the crown's post isn't the security."

"And if it becomes the security?"

"If it becomes the security, I'll report it."

She said it with no weight on any word, as she said everything, and he understood it was the most she could honestly say and that she had said all of it. It was narrow. It was also, he thought, a door left open by exactly the width of a hand.

"Would you put your answer to my question of last month in writing? About your own letters. Six sent, six acknowledged, the dates."

She drew a clean sheet toward her and wrote it out in four lines, the days and the riders and the road, and dated it, and signed it with the name she used, and blotted it, and held it out to him without getting up, so that he had to come into the room after all to take it.

"Thank you."

"Good night, your highness."



Keller, then.

He said it in Hale's office with the sheet folded into the rest, and nobody argued, and nobody was pleased either. Keller had ruled on Wexler two summers ago because Ferra had put a document on his table, a clerk's hand three times over and a sum that did not add, and Keller had looked at the document and not at the prince and ruled on what the paper said. That was what Keller was. A pattern was not a paper. Twenty-one missing letters were not a document; they were the shape of a hole where documents should have been, and Emeric knew exactly what a magistrate who ruled on paper would make of a hole.

He was going anyway. There was nobody else.

"Post horses," he said. "The posting houses, the same as August. Seven days north if the roads hold."

"They'll hold," said Ward, "till they don't."

"You and me. Nobody else."

Ward nodded once. He did not need it explained. A prince who rode north with a column of his garrison was a prince somebody wrote down, at some posting house, in some book, and the book went north faster than he did. Two men on post horses were two men on post horses.

Ferra had been writing while they talked. She finished the line, and wiped the nib, and put the pen down in Hale's row with the others, on the left, which made Hale's hands tighten very slightly in front of his coat.

"I'm not coming," she said.

He had been going to ask. He had the question halfway made, and she had seen it coming across the desk at her and stepped out of its road.

"Three weeks out of the counting room," she said, "to carry a pattern that isn't a case yet to a man who only reads cases. And the quarter day's the last Tuesday in December, and somebody has to pay it, and it isn't going to be Hale." She stood up out of his chair. "No offence."

"None taken," said Hale, with every appearance of having taken a great deal.



Armand was in his chair by the fire with a blanket over his knees that he would have said, if asked, belonged to someone else and had been left there.

The room off the hall had been a steward's office once and it had not forgiven anyone for turning it into anything else. There was the bed, and the bench, and the case of stones, and the books, which had got into everything now, the windowsill and the floor beside the bed and the seat of the second chair, so that Emeric had to move a stack of them to sit down and then did not know where to put them, and held them.

"I'm going north," he said. "To the capital. Three weeks, if the roads are kind. I need someone to hold Kaldo while I'm gone. Sign for the barony. Hear the disputes on the Tuesday. Keep the garrison's orders standing, which Ward will leave you in writing, and which you'll be wise not to change."

Armand looked at him over the top of the blanket with the expression of a man who had been asked whether he could read.

"Boy," he said, "I have been holding things since before your mother was born."

He stopped there. Not for effect. The breath ran out of the sentence before the sentence was finished, and he sat with his mouth a little open and his hand flat on the arm of the chair and waited for it to come back, the way a man waits on a stair he has climbed too quickly, and it was a long time coming. The fire ticked. Emeric held the books.

"Has anybody," Armand said, when he could, "looked at what that ward is doing to its linen? Imani boils everything. Twice. I have seen her boil a spoon. The laundry women are in despair."

"I'll ask her."

"Do." Armand settled the blanket. "Somebody should."

Emeric let it stand. He had been letting it stand since April, the stair Armand no longer climbed and the second chair nobody sat in and the breath that ran out halfway through sentences, and he went on letting it stand now, because Armand had asked him to without saying a word, and it was the only thing Armand had ever asked of him.

"I'll want paper," Armand said. "While you're gone."

"How much?"

"A great deal." He did not say what for. He looked at the fire, mildly, as if paper were a thing an old man naturally ran through in a fortnight, and Emeric, who knew to the sheet what Armand had used since the summer, said he would tell Ferra.

He was at the door with the books still in his arms, having forgotten he had them, when Armand said, "Wait," and leaned forward with some care and took a letter from the bench beside him. It was folded small and sealed, and the seal was plain, and the name on the outside was a name Emeric did not know.

"There's a man at the tower," Armand said. "An old colleague. Give him that, in your own hand, and nobody else's."

"What is it?"

"A letter." Armand sat back. "Put my books down, boy. You're not taking them to the capital."



The ward smelled of lye and of the dried lavender Imani put in with the sheets, and the river was loud under it, high and brown with the last of the rain.

Tess was by the window in her chair, with the bench book open on a board laid across the arms and her pen going. She had been in the chair six weeks now. She had learned to stop it without going over the bar, and to turn it in its own length, and to lock one wheel with the heel of her hand and lean on it while she reached for something, and she had got thinner in the face and harder in the arms, and she no longer looked at the chair before she got into it.

"I want paper," she said, before he had said anything.

"Everyone wants paper."

"And the lamp column. I've lost three days."

"The lamps are lit," he said. "All eight. All night. The river's higher than it's been since you started the column."

"I know. I can hear it." She did not look up. "I still want the column. A river's not high because it's high. It's high because it's been raining in the hills. When it stops, it'll start going down, and I want to see what day."

He could not argue with that, and did not try.

"And I want to go down to the powerhouse," Tess said. "In this." She put one hand on the rim.

"No," said Imani.

She was at the next bed with a stack of linen, folding it in the way she folded everything, corners first, as if a sheet were a wound that had to be closed in the right order. She did not look round either.

"It's downhill," Tess said.

"It's downhill there," said Imani. "Who pushes you back up?"

Tess opened her mouth and shut it and wrote something in the bench book, hard.

"I'm going north," Emeric said to Tess. "For a while. Three weeks. If you want anything from the powerhouse while I'm gone, ask Bram. If you want paper, ask Ferra. If you want the chair to do something it doesn't do, write it down and I'll read it when I'm back."

"Where north?" said Imani.

She had stopped folding. She had a sheet in her hands, half done, one corner up and one corner down.

"North," he said. "The capital."

"Will you be back before the snow?"

It was not a question a healer asked. A healer asked how long, and what you were taking for the road, and whether your boots were dry. He heard that it was not one of those, and so did she, at about the same moment. She looked down at the sheet in her hands as if she had found it there.

"Before the snow," he said. "If the roads hold."

Imani put the sheet down on the bed, not folded. She picked up the kettle from the hearth, which was full, and took it out to the back, where there was nothing at all that needed boiling, and he heard her setting it on the fire out there with more noise than a kettle needed.

Tess, who missed nothing that happened in that room and had nothing to do all day but notice it, looked at the unfolded sheet, and at the back door, and at Emeric. Then she looked down at the bench book and wrote something very small in the margin, and closed it.



He came down late on the last night, because he could not sleep and the keep was cold.

The gate passage had a guard table at the inner end, under the one lamp, where the night watch kept its book and ate its bread. Ward was sitting at it alone. The man of the watch was up on the wall, and the passage was empty, and Ward was writing.

Not on barony paper. Emeric knew Kaldo paper across a room now, grey and soft, the kind that drank ink sideways if you let it. This was thinner and whiter, and Ward was writing on it in a hand Emeric had never seen him use: small and square, every letter the same height, close-packed, as if the page were being charged by the inch.

Ward did not stop when Emeric came round the corner of the passage. He finished the word. Then he laid his forearm across the page, quite slowly, the way a man in a barracks lays his arm round his bowl when somebody he does not know sits down at the table, and looked up.

Neither of them said anything.

Emeric thought about Hale's register, every line of it, every carrier. He thought that he had read the whole of that book, twice, and that Ward's name was not anywhere in the carrier column, not once, in two years and two months. He thought that a man did not write in a hand like that to his mother.

He nodded to Ward, and Ward nodded back, with his arm still over the letter, and Emeric went up the stair to bed and lay awake in the cold, and did not ask, and was fairly sure that not asking was the right thing, and could not have said why.



The two post horses were in the gate yard before it was light, steaming, stamping on the frozen mud, with the posting house's brand on their hips and the posting house's boy holding both bridles and looking as if he would rather be in bed.

The satchel was under Emeric's coat, against his shirt, where a rider could not take it without taking the coat. Hale had copied the register's crown entries into it overnight in his fair hand, twenty-one lines with the dates and the carriers and his few words on the matter, and had wrapped the copy in oilcloth, and then in a second oilcloth, and then tied it with tape and sealed the knot, as if the road might try to read it. Ferra's page was in it, copied, with its single empty line and the 21 and the rule under it. Saban's lamp-oil bill was in it. Vantic's four lines were in it, dated. Armand's letter was in the inside pocket of the coat, by itself.

Ward was already up. Ward was always already up.

Ferra was not there. Emeric checked the girth, and checked it again, and the horse turned its head round and looked at him.

Then she was there. She came up out of the lane in her coat with her hair tied back badly, the way she tied it when she had not been to bed, and she had a sheet of paper in her hand, folded once.

She held it out. He unfolded it.

The quarter day, the last Tuesday of December, with its date. Under that, in her small plain hand, the figure that would be paid on it, to the copper, and under that the figure that would be left in the strongbox afterward. Nothing else.

"So you know I'll have it," she said.

He folded it again and put it in the satchel, with the register and the map and the rest of the barony's case, and buttoned his coat over all of it.

"I know you'll have it."

She nodded, once, as if that settled an account, and stepped back out of the horses' way.

Ward swung up. Emeric swung up, less well. The boy let go of the bridles and stood back against the wall with his hands in his armpits, and the gate crew hauled on the bar, and the South Gate opened onto the dark and the road and the first grey line of the morning, a long way off in the east.

They rode out.
