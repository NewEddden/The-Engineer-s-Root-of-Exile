Chapter 75 - The Standing Order

The Standing Order was one sheet of paper. It was called that because Ferra had written the words across the top of her first draft, to remind herself what sort of document she was making, and a clerk had copied them onto the folder, and in eleven days nobody proposed anything better.

That was the whole of the naming.

The problem it solved was a hole, and Emeric had spent four years describing the same hole to other people without once turning round to look for it in himself.

A system that depends on one man has a hole in it the exact shape of the man. He had said so about the post, to a magistrate. He had said it about the ring after the stair, and written it down, and put a root under the wall so that no key could live in one pair of hands. He had sat at the side of a court in the capital and watched the principle work so well that nobody needed him in the room.

And the barony itself ran on a table.

It had been the table for four winters. Six people who came into the long room with their own work and went out with it, because that was how it had begun one February and nobody had since found a reason to change it. It worked because they turned up. There was no instrument behind it, no order among them, and not one line written anywhere that said who could act when the man at the end of the table was not sitting at it.

That had not mattered, because he was so seldom gone. In nine years he had been away from the keep for any real length of time on four or five occasions, and each of them had been an event, planned for months, with a date at the far end of it, and while he was away the barony had kept its head down and done nothing it could not undo until he came back up the road.

That arrangement had ended with a ruler and a line through a ledger.

Grayrock was a day upriver and had a sealed working in its hill that he was now permitted to open. Milford was a day west. Wayford was five days north, with a junction that every road in the south ran through and a clerk in its counting house who had been the most junior man in Sethe's employ a year ago, and the notebook he had kept for three years had changed its character overnight. The second column, the one listing what each frozen project would need on the day the freeze lifted, had become a list of the reasons he was going to spend most of the next several years on a horse.

He never once thought the word government. He thought of the post, because it was the same problem, and he had been right about the post.

So he asked Ferra to write the table down.

She took eleven days over it. On the second she asked each of the six, and him, for a sheet setting out an ordinary week hour by hour, so that she could see who covered what when somebody was elsewhere. She got seven sheets back. His was the worst of them and she returned it once, with a mark beside Thursday.

"Thursday is Thursday," he said.

"Thursday is three places at the same hour. Pick one."

He picked one.

They read it in the long room on the first hard morning of the winter, with the fire lit and a clerk's fair copy at the head of the table and a copy under every hand.

"This is the Standing Order," Emeric said, and it was the only time in his life he called it that out loud. "Ferra drafted it. She'll read it."

Nobody was impressed with anybody. That was what he noticed first and was most pleased by afterward. Six people who had sat at that table for four years listened to a woman read their jobs back to them in the order she had written them, and the feeling in the room was that of a household hearing the rota for the coming month.

Ward, Secretary of War. Ward did not move. It was the only title on the sheet that meant exactly what it said, and he had been doing it for nine years under no title at all.

Bram, Secretary of Works. The foundry, the roads, the wall, the yard, and everything in the second column of a notebook Bram had never seen and had heard a great deal about.

"How long is the column," Bram said.

"Longer than the first one."

Bram nodded as though that settled something, and it probably did.

Saban, Secretary of Trade. Saban had been a merchant for nearly thirty years and Kaldo's hands outside its own border for seven, and for the last four of those its ear as well. The ear was the job. The trade was the reason he could do it. A Secretary of Trade has an unanswerable reason to ask anybody anything about prices and carriage and routes and who was hiring and who was buying, and no man in any market in the kingdom thinks twice about being asked.

"It's the most honest lie on the sheet," Emeric said.

"I'll try to live up to it," said Saban.

Imani, Secretary of Health. Water, the wards, burials, the drains, and the standard for every one of them. She had been running all of it for years with nobody putting a word to it, and she listened to the word being put to it with her hands folded on the table and no visible opinion whatever.

Armand, Secretary of the Arcane. The mages, the crystals, the etching floor and the bench.

Armand's mouth went thin. It was the first reaction anybody had to anything, and it was a sharp one, because the old man had heard a title about wizards and concluded, correctly by the custom of every court he had ever sat in, that he was being given a chair to be ornamental in. He had crossed a kingdom in a hired cart to be of use. He said, not quite under his breath, that he had not come six hundred kilometres to be furniture.

"Keep reading," Emeric said.

Ferra read the sixth line, which was her own. Ferra, Chancellor.

Saban noticed it before anybody else, as a man who read contracts for their asymmetries would. "Why isn't she a Secretary?"

Emeric had decided to explain it once, so he did.

"A Secretary holds a column," he said. "The Chancellor holds the ledger the columns sum into. She's the only person at this table who sees every brief at once. If I called her a Secretary she'd be one voice in six with a bigger desk, and somebody would test that inside a month, and they'd be right to."

Nobody asked anything else about it. Ferra had not looked up from the sheet.

The seventh clause was Armand's, and she read it at the same pace as the rest.

In the absence of the holder from Kaldo, the Secretary of the Arcane holds Kaldo, with the holder's authority in all things save those this Order reserves, until the holder returns.

Armand had his own copy in front of him. He read the clause on it, and then read it again, and then he put one finger on the paper beside the line as though to keep it from moving. He did not say another word for the rest of the morning.

"Ward rides with me," Emeric said, to the table in general. "Whoever holds Kaldo has to be in Kaldo."

That was the reason, and it was true, and it was not the whole of it. The rest was not a thing he was going to say in a room. The rest was that Armand had come here because of him and for nothing else, and had never once asked for anything, and argued with him in public about baselines as if Emeric were a second-year with a bad habit, and had been wrong in front of this table twice and stayed in the room both times. That was a man you could leave a barony with. Emeric had been certain of it for a year and had never found a way to say it that did not sound like a speech, so he had put it in a clause instead.

The eighth set out the order among them when he was away and Armand could not act either, and Ferra read it without comment.

Then she read the ninth, which was not in her draft.

She had written eight clauses and a close. The clerk had copied eight clauses and a close, and one more, which Emeric had given him the night before in his own hand with instructions to set it in its place and to mention it to nobody.

The Chancellor may stay any act of the holder by a stay in writing. A stay so written stands. The holder may not set it aside.

She read it at exactly the speed she had read the eight before it. Then she read the close. Then she set the sheet down square to the edge of the table, the way she set everything down, and anybody who did not know her would have seen nothing at all.

Emeric knew her. He watched her understand it, and it took her about as long as the clause took to read. What she understood was what he had done with it, which was to put the one person who saw the whole of the barony where she could stop him, and to write it so that he could not argue.

She did not thank him. A thank-you would have made the clause a gift, and a gift is a thing the giver may one day ask for back, and she was not going to be the first person at that table to suggest it was one.

"The ninth wants a form," she said. "A stay that isn't in a form is a mood."

"Then write the form."

"I will."

That was all either of them said about it. Some days later he caught himself going back over the sentence and the way she had squared the sheet, for no reason he could put a name to, and put it down to relief that the morning had gone well.

Ward, who had not moved through any of it, said to nobody in particular, "That's two, then."

Nobody asked him two of what. Emeric did not need to. The factor held an instrument, permanent and entirely his own doing, that let her read every book he owned for as long as her house cared to, and now the Chancellor held one that let her stop him. One of the two women answered to a lending house. The other answered to him and was paid on his books, and her clause sat in an instrument that the next instrument he wrote could replace. It was the weaker of the two brakes, and he knew it was the weaker, and it was the best he could build from the inside.

The second thing on the table was the relay, and the first thing anybody said about it was what was wrong with it.

He laid it out over Ferra's map of the south, which was the best map of the south in existence because she had built it out of carriage lines. Stones bound in pairs and set in posts on high ground along the roads. At each post one stone listened back along the line and one spoke forward, and a trained man sat between them, read what lit, wrote it in a log with the hour, and sent it on. A stone had to be learned by hand before it could be spoken to at a distance, and the distance was not great, so the posts would stand at whatever interval Armand's bench said the stones would honestly bear, and not a yard further.

Grayrock first, a day of posts up the river road. Milford next. Wayford was the long one and would take a year.

"And the riders keep running," he said. "Every one of them, on the same days. If every stone on the Wayford road went dark tomorrow we'd be where we were last month, and nobody in this barony is to forget how to saddle a horse because a lamp learned to talk."

What it bought was time. A question sent to Wayford came back in something over a week if the road was kind, and Kaldo had spent three years learning in detail how often the road was not. On the relay it would come back in a morning.

"Signal," he said. "Not speech. The man at a post knows the signs his own lamps make and the signs he hands on to the next post, and nothing else. What passes through him is a string of marks that means nothing to the man writing it down. It turns back into words at the ends and only there. Each end holds the key to its own line and nobody else's." He put a finger on Kaldo. "The whole key is in two hands. Mine and the Chancellor's."

Ward heard that. He did not change his expression and did not say anything, and nobody at the table looked at him, and the meeting went on.

Ferra put a number on the Grayrock and Milford legs, and a second number on the Wayford road, and the second number was the kind that gets written down and then looked at for a while. It was money the barony had only that season stopped not having.

Saban waited until everybody had finished looking at it.

"A man at a post can't read you," he said. "Good. But whoever sits at the Wayford end holds Wayford's side of the key, and he can tell you anything he likes in Wayford's hand, and you'll have it in a morning instead of a week. And you'll believe it, because it came up your own line." He turned the map a little toward himself. "Today, if somebody wants to lie to you from Wayford, he has to do it slowly and on paper. You're about to let him do it fast, and from further off."

Nobody had an answer to that, and Emeric did not pretend to.

"Build it," Saban said. "I'd just like somebody to own the question."

"Then it's yours."

"I thought it might be."

"There's one more thing," Emeric said, "and I have kept it from three of you for eight years."

He had not planned how to begin, because every way of beginning he had tried in his head had come out sounding like a man setting himself up to be forgiven.

"From the other three I've kept most of it. I gave each of them the part I'd decided they could carry, and I decided it without asking them. That was mine to decide about my own work. It was not mine to decide about any of you, and I did it for eight years."

He put his hand into his coat.

"Before that comes out," Imani said.

She had not raised her voice. She never did in a working room. She looked across at Ferra. "I'd like this written down."

Ferra picked up the pen and turned to a clean sheet.

"Five years ago," Imani said, "in the room behind the church, on the fourth man, you put your hand on my wrist and took me somewhere." She said it the way she would have given the site of a wound. "The room didn't go anywhere. That's the part I've never been able to make anybody understand. The room stayed exactly where it was, and there was suddenly more of it, in a direction I hadn't had before, and the inside of that man was in that direction."

Nobody at the table moved. Bram was studying the grain of the wood.

"I worked out roughly what it was a long time ago, so I won't waste the table's morning asking. My question is why I was taken into it without being asked, on a day I couldn't have refused anything and couldn't have thought about it for a week after. And why, in five years, nobody has said one word to me about it."

He had known for five years that this was coming, and he had never got an answer ready, because it was not the kind of answer that improves with preparation.

"I'd spent twenty minutes describing a vessel to you three ways and drawing it wrong," he said, "and a man was bleeding, and taking you in was the fastest way to make you able to close it. That's why."

"I know that's why."

"Then here's the rest of it. That's the reasoning a man uses when he's decided somebody else's consent is a delay. I decided it. It wasn't mine to decide. And afterward I decided you didn't need to be told what had been done to you, which was the same decision made twice."

He did not add anything to it. There was nothing to add that would not have been softening, and she would have heard it as softening.

Imani nodded once, the way she did at the end of a history when she had got what she needed out of it.

"It worked," she said. "I learned to close a vessel in forty minutes and I've closed a great many since. I've spent five years unable to decide whether that makes it better or worse, and I've stopped trying." She turned her hand over on the table and then laid it flat again. "So this is why I'm sitting here, if anybody wondered. I'm not grateful and I haven't forgiven anything. The last two times you decided something about me, I wasn't in the room. I'd like to be in the room for the third."

Ferra wrote it down at the speed it was said, and read the last line back, and Imani said yes, that was it.

Of all the reasons anybody had ever given for sitting at that table, it was the one Emeric believed most completely, and it had nothing to do with loyalty. It was a woman putting her chair where she could see him.

He put the stones on the table.

There were six of them and they were not impressive and nobody said so. Grey-white, none of them bigger than the top joint of a thumb, the kind of thing a man picks out of a stream and throws back.

Then he told them what they were, and he told it badly, because he had never once told it from the beginning to anybody.

There was a place inside a stone. He had been going into it since his first autumn in Kaldo. An hour in there cost something like a minute out here, and not reliably. It held every page he had ever looked at, as it had been when he looked at it, down to the smudges. He could build in it, and had built the furnace in it with Bram, and the ledgers with Ferra, and the root under the ring. And it agreed with him about anything he believed, which was the most useful thing about it and the most dangerous, and he had not always been able to tell those apart.

Armand, Saban and Imani listened. Ward, Bram and Ferra did not move, did not look at each other, and did not look at the three who were hearing it, and the silence on their side of the table was so complete that it said more than any of them could have.

"Your model," Armand said. It was the first thing he had said since the seventh clause. "The one that agreed with you about the constant."

"Yes."

The old man looked at the six stones for some time. "I have been tired near your crystals for four years," he said. "Only near them. I priced it as being eighty." He sat back. "It appears I ought to have measured it."

"Each of these is bound to me," Emeric said, "and through me to that place. It's the same kind of binding Bram and Ward took on the ring, pointed the other way. Theirs let them reach into the wall. These let me reach you. With one on you I can bring you in without a hand on your wrist." He touched the nearest of them. "And carried long enough, against the skin, every day, it may do for you what the one I carry did for me, which was to let me in one night without anybody bringing me. I don't know how long that takes. Mine took months. I don't know if it happens for everybody."

Then he told them why he had waited, and that was worse, because he had never had to say it aloud and the sentences came out in the wrong order.

"I don't know whether I'm the first," he said. "I've reasons to think I'm not."

He did not give them. The reasons were a grey stone that had lain in a drawer for fifteen years, and a room he had recognised on the first night as somewhere his mother used to come, and past that a wall in his own head that he had never once got over.

"If I'm the first, this is a new thing in the world, and I've no way of knowing what it does once more than one or two people are carrying it. If I'm not the first, then it's old, and somebody knew, and the question after that is why the King of Randillia's third son has never heard of it. Every answer I can find to that question is a bad one."

He moved his hand on the table.

"Second. I've never found a word about it. Not here, not in the capital, not in Doran's archive, not in forty years of the tower's curriculum." He did not look at Armand. "Not from Vesk, who was bought and sold by men who used every mage they could get their hands on, and who has answered five years of careful questions without once saying anything that sounded like a room. An absence that complete is either an absence or a floor somebody swept, and I can't tell which from where I'm standing."

"Third." He stopped, and then said it. "I didn't know who to trust."

He said it to six people he was at that moment trusting with it, and heard how it sounded, and nobody helped him out of it. Ward looked at the ceiling. Bram looked at the grain. The sentence lay on the table next to the stones for what felt like a long time.

It was true of five of them. For the sixth it had been something else, a sentence he had gone looking for on three evenings in a room off the foundry office and never found, and that had not been about trust at all, and he did not say so.

"Sethe was one man with a grudge and a purse," he said. "Whatever kept the road dark before Kaldo was worth keeping dark isn't one man, and doesn't have a grudge. Somebody paid for a second war for three years and then stopped paying, and I still can't tell you why they stopped. I've waited eight years for things to get safer. They haven't. The only thing that's changed in eight years is how much there is to lose."

He looked at the stones.

"I wish I'd done it sooner."

He did not say anything after that, because anything he said after that would have been a list.

Ferra put the pen down.

"Is it a leash?"

She was not asking on the table's behalf, and nobody who knew her would have taken it that way. She had sat at her own kitchen table three times holding on and once letting go. She had taken a bond before she understood a word of what it was, and had worked alongside it for eight years without anybody giving her an account of it that satisfied her. She was asking a question she had already lived, out loud, in front of the three people who had not yet decided, before they decided.

"Specifically," she said. "Does the stone give you anything. Can you feel us. Can you find us. Can you read what we've read. Can you pull us in, and if you can, can you do it when we don't want to come. And does it end if we hand it back."

He answered in her order, because she had asked in it.

"I bound them, and I wrote nothing into these but the binding. I don't know everything a binding carries, and I won't tell you I do."

"Inside, I can feel you. Like weight. Outside, I don't know. I've never tried to find anybody by one."

"Can I read what you've read." He had known that one was coming from the moment she said specifically, because she was the only person at the table who already knew the answer. "In there, yes. Anything you've looked at with your own eyes is on a shelf in that room while you're in it, the way you saw it, down to the smudges. Ferra found that out on her first night, when the forge's books turned up stacked at the far end of my office and I had never opened one of them." He kept his eyes on the table. "I told the three of you it holds every page I've ever read. That was true, and it was the smaller half."

Imani's hand had gone still on the wood. She had kept a register of every body she had opened since she was nineteen, and there was a name against every line of it. Saban was looking at nothing in particular, with the face of a man going through a great many letters in his head and deciding which of them he would mind.

"I have never taken one of hers down without her standing there," Emeric said. "That's a promise. It isn't a lock. I haven't got a lock to give you."

"Yes, I can bring you in. That's what they're for."

"When you don't want to come." He looked at her, because she was the one at the table who knew. "I've brought three people in. Counting only the first time for each, it failed as often as it worked, and every failure was the same person, and she's sitting at this table. I know one reason it fails, which is that the person holds on. I don't know that it's the only reason. The third time, I leaned. It gave about the width of a hair and it put a headache into both of us, and I believe that with enough weight behind it, for long enough, it would have given all the way. I've never tested that and I won't. That isn't the same as telling you it can't be done, and I'm not telling you that."

"And handing it back."

"I don't know. Give it back in a week and I'd guess it ends. What mine built in me didn't go anywhere when I put the stone down. I don't know if yours would."

He sat back.

"So you have an incomplete account of a thing I don't fully understand," he said, "and I'm asking you to take it anyway. That's all this is. I'm not going to dress it as more."

He could feel what that did to the six stones on the table. Ten minutes earlier they had been a gift, or near enough to one that a man could have gone on calling them that. Ferra had turned them into a proposition with an unknown attached, in public, in front of the three who had to choose, and he had let her, and then he had helped her do it. Imani watched him let her. He did not look to see what she made of it.

"They stay here," he said. "Nobody picks one up in this room, and nobody tells anybody else what they've decided. If you want one, come and get it. If you don't, nothing changes about your office or your pay or your chair, and I will never ask twice."

Nobody picked one up. They went out of the long room one at a time, the way they always did, back to whatever the morning had interrupted.

Bram came up the next day before the fifth bell, which meant before the yard opened, which meant before anything.

He came into the long room and held out his hand, and Emeric put a stone in it. Bram closed his fingers round it, nodded once, and went back down the stairs. He had not asked a single question the day before and he did not ask one now, and it was the shortest conversation Emeric had with anybody that year.

Armand came an hour later with a sheet.

He had not slept much, by the look of him, and he had used the time. There were four questions on the sheet in the crabbed hand nobody in Kaldo could read at speed, all of them about the succession of authority, and all of them good.

Whether the authority in the seventh clause ran to the garrison left in Kaldo or only to the offices. To both, Emeric said. The senior man Ward left behind answered to whoever held the keep.

Whether the Chancellor's stay bound the man holding Kaldo as it bound the holder. It bound the chair, Emeric said, whoever was sitting in it.

Whether the man holding Kaldo might countermand a standing order of the holder's in an emergency, and how he was to know it was one. Yes, in writing, with the hour, and it would not be reversed on the holder's return except at the table. As to knowing, Armand had been deciding what counted as an emergency for fifty years and Emeric was not going to start telling him now.

The fourth question was what happened if Emeric died on a road.

He read it twice. He did not answer it, and Armand did not ask him to, and after a while the old man took the sheet back with three answers written beside it and one line blank, and folded it into his coat. He did not look at the stones on his way out. He had not come about the stones.

Nobody else at the table had written that question down. Neither had Emeric, in eleven days of reading drafts, and he noticed that on the stairs.

Ward took his in the yard four mornings later, at first light, between the fourth and fifth passes of the blade work. He held his hand out without looking at it. Emeric put the stone in it.

"Against the skin, if you can stand it," Emeric said. "That's how I carried mine."

"I know how you carried yours." Ward put it inside his shirt. "I watched you do it for a year."

They went on, and Ward left him alive for rather less time than usual that morning, and neither of them said anything else about it.

Armand came for his. So did Saban, and Imani, and Ferra, each on their own day and in their own words. He did not write down which day or what they said. It was the only courtesy he could think of that cost him anything. By the end of the fortnight there was nothing left on the table.

On the last evening of the fortnight he sat on in the long room after the others had gone, with the fair copy in front of him.

It was one sheet. It had a title across the top that a clerk had copied off a draft, and nine clauses and a close, and it was the first document he had ever made that was designed from its first line to work when he was not in the room. It would outlast every name written on it. In twenty years a man nobody at that table had met would take it out of a folder to check who was permitted to stop whom, and would find the answer in Ferra's order and a clerk's hand, and would have no idea there had ever been a morning when it was read aloud to a household like a rota.

He had spent nine years building things that did not need the man who built them. The bucket line, the burial rows, the census, the school, the post, the ring. This was the last of them and the largest, and with it he had made himself the most replaceable he had ever been.

He had assumed, for about as long as he had been assuming anything about it, that arriving here would feel like relief.

It did not. It felt the way a chair at the side of a court had felt in the capital more than a year ago, with nothing in his hands and the afternoon proceeding as it would have proceeded without him. He recognised it at once. He had not been able to do anything about it then either.

He put the sheet back into its folder, where the clerk's hand said what it was, and sat on for a while in a room that had just been arranged to run without him.
