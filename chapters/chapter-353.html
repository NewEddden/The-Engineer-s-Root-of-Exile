Chapter 353 - The Word for His Eyes

The flasks came across on the nineteenth of December in a crate of straw, twelve of them, each in its plated sleeve, and Fletcher's men carried the crate up the Cradle's ladder between them as though it were full of eggs. Liquid mana off the plant. A month of the head tank's feed if Imani's notches held, and less if they didn't.

Emeric was on the cabin bench with the dose register on his knee, because Ferra had gone to the stores and left it with him on the understanding that he wouldn't write in it. The guest was sitting on the trough's edge with her feet in the water. She had taken to sitting up for most of the day since the tenth, her back straight against the iron and her hands on her knees, and she watched everything that came through the door: the cook's boards, Wren with the solder pot, Fletcher's men with the deck hose twice a day. She watched the crate come in.

Fletcher set it down by the head tank and unbuckled the first sleeve and lifted a flask out to look at the seal in the light from the door. It was a fat glass bulb with a stoppered neck and a wire cage over the stopper, and under his sense it sat there like a cold coal, small and full.

The guest got up.

She crossed the iron floor in three steps, wet-footed and quite steady, and took the flask out of Fletcher's hands before he had decided whether to let her. He let her. She held it against her breastbone with both hands and turned, not toward the door or the trough, but toward the valve on the head tank, and the brass dial, and the row of notches Imani had scratched in it with a nail.

"That's not for drinking, miss," said Fletcher. He had called her miss since the cabin went up, and nobody had stopped him.

She put one finger on the dial. On the first notch. Then she drew it along them, one after another, past the thirty-fourth, where the needle stood that morning, to the last notch Imani had cut, and off the end of the dial onto the plain brass, and off the brass onto the iron of the tank's jacket, and along the iron of the wall, slowly, all the way to the frame of the cabin door. There she stopped, with her finger on the door frame and the flask held against her chest, and looked at Emeric.

Imani came up the ladder from the cell below with a bucket and saw her standing there.

"Put that down," she said, quite gently, and set the bucket down herself to show how it was done.

The guest didn't. When Imani came near she turned a shoulder, not unkindly. It was the way a child keeps a toy from another child without wanting a fight about it.

"She's not hungry for it," said Emeric.

"Everything in this room's hungry for it. It hums."

"She knows what it's for. Look where she started." He nodded at the dial. "Notch one. And where she stopped."

Imani looked at the finger on the door frame. Then at the woman's face, which had gone slate at the throat and stayed there.

"She wants it faster," she said.

"Or she wants to know how long."

"Both," said Imani. "I would." She held up one finger, and touched one notch on the dial, and put her cheek on her folded hands and shut her eyes: one, then sleep. Then one finger again. She did it twice. The guest watched it all the way through both times, and then set the flask down at the foot of the tank, very carefully, upright on the iron, and kept her hand on top of it.

Then she talked.

She had made sounds at them for six weeks. This was talk. It went on a while, low and quick, mostly in the throat, with a sort of soft knock in it now and then that Emeric felt in his own jaw. It had a shape. It went up at the end the way a question does in every language he had ever heard, and then it stopped, and she waited, looking from Imani to him with her hand on the flask.

Nobody in the room could answer. Fletcher took his cap off and put it back on.

"Well," said Imani quietly, after a while. "That's me told."



"No," said Imani, on the deck, ten minutes later. "Say it properly. Say all of it."

Emeric said it properly. A read. Given, not taken, or not at all. He would hold her hand and go in the way he had gone into Corbin, and Grier before him, and a dozen others, and she would take him where she chose and nowhere else. A given read searched the other person's own links; he could only find what she put next to what. And he would show her his own, through the same hand, the way he had once taken Harrold in to hear Amara: what he chose, and nothing he didn't.

"And she'll know what you are," said Ferra. She had come back from the stores with a sack of lime on her hip and put it down to listen. "What you can do."

"She'll know what I show her. She can't walk anywhere in me I don't open."

"And what she shows you might not be true."

"It'll be what she remembers. That's not the same as what happened. Grier was sure about the day." He looked at the cabin door. "But it'll be words. Hers for ours. I can't learn a language off a woman who can't tell me which noise means fish."

"And if her head's like the rest of her?" said Imani. "You said it yourself on the beach. Two of Armand, and then some. What's that like to walk about in?"

"Heavy, I should think. I'll come out with a headache."

"You'll come out when Armand says." She turned round. "Armand. You'll say."

Armand was at the rail with his cane hooked over his arm, watching the cook's smoke go along the shore. "I'd be delighted to say," he said. "I so rarely get to tell him anything."

"And she has to say yes," said Imani. "Like she did for me. Not because she's hungry and you're the one standing there."

So he went back in and did it Imani's way.

He sat on the iron a yard from the trough and held out his hand, open, and stopped a foot short of her, and left it there. When she looked at it he put two fingers of his other hand to his own temple, and then turned the open hand palm up again. She looked at the hand a long while. Then she put the tips of her fingers on it.

He showed her the smallest thing he could think of. The board on the bench end in lamplight, and the bones on its corner in a tidy pile, the way she had left them on the twelfth of November, seen from the hatch above.

She snatched her fingers back as if the hand had been hot.

Her skin went dark from the throat to the shoulders. She stared at him. Then at the bench end, where the board had been, and wasn't. Then at him again, and he held still and let her stare.

When she put her hand out the second time she didn't take his. She took his wrist, the way she had taken Imani's in the cell, with that cold grip that was much too strong for its size, and drew his hand in the last foot herself, and laid it flat against the side of her own head, above the ear, over the wet dark hair. She pressed it there once with her palm on the back of it. Then she let go.

"Tomorrow," said Imani, from the door. "After she's eaten. After you've slept. And I mean slept."



Ferra wrote his hours that night with the lamp turned down to save it, sitting up on the watch room cot with her knees up and the register propped on them. She showed him the column. It went on for some time.

"That's December," she said.

"That's not all December."

"It's December so far." She ruled the line under it. "You're going into her head in the morning. You'll want all your own wits for that, and you've been lending them out."

Imani, face down beside her with one arm hanging off the cot, said into the blanket, "Bring her back a word for fish. The cook wants to know what to call them on the board."

"She'll have six," said Emeric.

"Then bring back six." Imani found his hair with the hanging hand without looking and pulled it, not hard. "And bring yourself back. Ferra's ruled you a column. It'd be a waste."



He went in at nine on the twentieth with his back to the iron and her hand in his. Imani sat across the trough where she could see both their faces. Ferra sat on the bench with the register shut on her knee and a slate on top of it. Armand stood in the door with his eyes closed and his sense laid along the guest's note, thin as a hair, and said "Go on," the way a man says it to a horse.

Emeric had walked a good many people by now. Every mind he had been into had been a house, in the end, because a house was how his own mind sorted things and the sorting came along with him: a hall, a door, a room with the thing in it. Corbin's had been a house with the furniture worn bright where somebody else had walked. Grier's had been a house with the dates on the wrong doors.

Hers wouldn't be a house.

He reached for a door and found a depth instead. Things in her weren't beside one another but above and below, and the near ones were lit and the far ones went down into dark, and she moved among them the way she moved in the trough, without walking. And it was heavy. Not unkind. Heavy the way water is heavy at the bottom of a deep pool: it pressed on him from every side at once, evenly, and he felt the cost of standing in it start to come out of him like a slow leak.

He held the cue, the first time, because she didn't know how.

A cup of water from the watch room, on the table, with the light through it. Water, in his own voice. She gave him back a sound for it, low with a lift at the end, that he could hear perfectly and couldn't have spelled for a pension. He held it. Then fish: the heavy fish on the cook's board. She gave him a sound, and then, when he showed her a little silver one off the works pier, a different sound, and when he showed her the seal on its rock, a third, and a feeling with it that he understood very well, which was that she thought him a bit simple for using one word where any sensible person used three.

Then, because it was ten days old and still the clearest thing in his head, the pressed fish: the board on the trough's edge, the rice under the dark slices, the saucer. She didn't give him a word for that. She gave him a place. It came up from somewhere lower than the words, and quicker than she meant it to, the way a smell brings a thing back before you've decided to remember it. A flat stone, cold and smooth under small hands. Strips of something pale laid across it in a row. A bigger hand, grey, setting the first strip down in front of her and nobody else. Somebody laughing, a long way off through water.

Then she had it back. Not shut, the way she would shut home in a minute; only put away. And she gave him a word after all, and it wasn't any of the words for fish.

He wrote it down afterward as near as he could. The cook chalked it over the board in the mess the next morning, wrong, and the guest corrected her, and the cook chalked it wrong again in exactly the same way, and that was the one that stayed.

Hand. Stone. Hurt. Sleep. The sun, which he showed her the way a land man would, a white ball in a blue sky; and she took it and turned it into something else, light coming down from above through moving water in long slow bars, and gave him her word for that.

Home.

He showed her Kaldo's harbour wall from the Hod, because it was the first thing that came. She was quiet. Then she gave him, very fast and then gone, a glimpse of blue so deep it was nearly black, and lights in it. Some still and steady, low and gold, like lamps in windows. Some that moved, slowly, greener, on long stalks that leaned all one way in a current. Then she shut it, gently and completely, and he didn't go near the place again.

After about sixty words she took the cue from him.

He felt the change. It was like being taken by the elbow. She went down, past the lit near things into the dimmer ones, and he went with her, and she set him down in a memory that had been walked a great many times. He could tell. It was smooth with use, the way a step is.

She was small in it. There were other small ones near her, a row of them, and their skins were all the same pale grey and all, at the moment, a little paler than that. An old one was in front of them. He couldn't see the old one's face; she hadn't looked at it, being small and frightened, and so it wasn't there to see. What she had looked at was a stone.

It was etched. A flat dark slab as tall as a man, with lines cut into it and rubbed with something that glowed faintly: a long line wavering down one side, and beside it a wide empty space with no lines in it at all, and on the far side of the empty space a jagged edge he read as a coast. Where the wavering line met the empty space the small figures cut in the stone had tails. Past it, in the empty space, they had legs. None of the figures with legs were coming back.

The old one touched the empty space, and all the small ones went paler.

That was one lesson, and he understood it without a word of hers: the water goes thin there, and the thin water takes your tail, and you don't come home. It was true. He was sitting in a room built to prove it was true.

But the old one moved on to a second stone, and the small ones' fear moved with it and changed.

The second stone was older. He could tell from the way she remembered it: worn smooth along its top as if hands had rested there for a long time, the lines softer. Figures on land, many of them, close together, with long straight things held up. Fire along the shore, cut as a row of points. And in the shallows figures with tails, a great many, lying the way nothing lies that is alive.

The carving didn't need words, and she gave him none. She gave him what the small ones had felt in that row, looking at it, which was not the clean fear of the first stone. The thin water was a thing that happened. This was a thing somebody had done. The land past the empty space was where her people had been killed, by people who walked on legs and held up straight things, and who had stayed there afterward and were there still.

He stood in it with her.

It was a teaching. She hadn't seen it; nobody she knew had. It was what she had been told as a child, by someone who had been told it as a child, cut in stone so it couldn't drift, and a given read could be honestly wrong and still be the truest thing a person had. But he had stood in the cave and looked at the wall, and he had read Vey's second half with Ferra and Zale at the inn, and it fitted. The old races had been here. Something had killed them, so well that nobody in the kingdoms knew they had existed. Men had won it by numbers.

There was nothing to say to it. He didn't try. In the read there was no way to say it anyway, and he wasn't sure he would have used one.

She took him on, faster now. She wanted the next part over. Dark water, colder, and her own speed through it, which he felt in her as a kind of joy even here, even with what was coming. Something behind her. She didn't look round, and so there was nothing behind her to see, only the knowledge of it. Then the blow low in her back, two points, and the cold that went in after it and kept going in. And ahead of her, in her mind, laid over the water she was swimming through, the first stone. The wavering line, and the empty space past it.

She turned toward it. That was the thing she wanted him to have, and she held it in front of him until she was sure he had it: that she had known exactly what the empty space was, and had chosen it over what was behind her.

He took the cue back very gently, and showed her Ferra's hand in the register, writing a line on the sixth of November. Then the three of them on the cabin bench: Imani saying his name, and Ferra saying it, the way they said it at the end of a long day. Emeric. Then he held the place open, the way you hold a door, and waited to see if she would come through it.

She answered with one more memory, and this one she didn't let him stand in. She held it up, the way Imani held a dressing up to the light.

Somebody calling a name into the sea. He didn't hear the name. He heard the calling, and saw what came after: a shoal of small fish nearby turning all together on the sound, and the sound going on out of them into further fish, turning and passing it, a long way, faster than anything could swim; and far off at the end of it, big shapes in the dark, turning to face the way the small fish faced.

Then she shut it, and the depth closed over, and she pushed him out.



"Forty minutes," Armand was saying. "I'd have called you at forty-five. I was looking forward to it."

The cabin was too bright. Emeric's head hurt behind both eyes at once, evenly, as if somebody had put a thumb on each, and he was soaked to the waist; at some point he had slid down the iron and sat in the trough. Imani had his free wrist in her fingers, counting. Ferra had stood up.

The guest was very close. She had come forward in the water while he was coming out, and she was looking at him from about a foot away, at his face, in the hard light from the lamp Fletcher had hung for the read.

At his eyes.

She had looked at him first every day for six weeks. She had never looked at him like this, from this close, in a good light, straight in. He watched it happen. Her pupils went wide. Her skin went black from the throat to the hip, all at once, the way it had gone on the table with Imani's spreader in her back. She went back from him through the water until her shoulders hit the iron at the trough's far end, hard enough to sound, and stayed there.

"Suvarre," she said.

One word. Not to him. About him, or about what she was looking at; he couldn't have said which. Then she looked away, at the wall, and didn't look back.

Imani let go of his wrist. She touched her own eyes, two fingers, and turned her hand up toward the guest, asking. The guest turned her face into the water.

That was no. They had all learned that one.

Ferra wrote the word down in the register under the guest's line, spelled as near as she could get it, and drew a ring round it, and looked at it as she looked at a sum that wouldn't come out, and didn't ask him what it meant, because she could see that he didn't know.



"Sixty-one words," he said, on the deck, with a wet towel on the back of his neck and the cook's coffee going cold in his hands. "Maybe. Some of them won't stay."

"And her," said Imani. "What about her."

So he told them. The first stone, and the second. The fear of the first and the other thing under the second. He told it plainly and left out nothing, and when he got to the figures in the shallows Ferra put her pen down.

Saban was there. He had come down from the works at the noise of the lamp being hung, and he leaned on the rail with his hat in his hand and listened to all of it with his eyes on the water.

"Did you tell her it wasn't us?" said Ferra.

"No."

"It wasn't. Not anybody living. Not anybody whose grandmother was living."

"It was people on legs. On land." He turned the cup in his hands. "She doesn't know Kaldo from the Church from Duvrenne. She doesn't know there's a difference. We came out of the place on the stone, and so did they, and that's all there is to it from where she sits." He drank some of the cold coffee. "And I don't know it wasn't us. Vey doesn't say who. Nobody's written down who. That's rather the point."

Ferra looked at him a while. Then she picked the pen up again.

"And the curse is that," said Imani slowly. "Both. The water that takes your tail, and the people who did that. All in one place. No wonder they teach it on a stone."

"Half and half. I couldn't tell you which half she's more frightened of."

"The second," said Saban, without turning round. "You can't argue with water."

Then the name. Emeric told them the fish turning on the call, and the call going out through them, and the big shapes at the end of it.

"A name's a trail," he said. "That's what I think she meant. Say it into the sea and it goes. Whoever put that shard in her back is out there listening for it."

"In this water?" said Imani. "She couldn't make a gull turn round."

"She doesn't know how far it goes in thin water. Neither do I. Neither does she want to find out."

"Then she's right," said Saban. He put his hat on. "I wouldn't give mine either. I haven't, to most people." He looked at Ferra. "Don't ask her again. Don't let anybody guess at it, either. A guess said out loud is halfway to a name."

"She's a guest," said Ferra. "She's in the register as a guest. I've got nothing to change." She turned back a page and looked at it, the line from the sixth of November in her own hand, and under it now the ringed word that nobody could read. "I wasn't going to write down anything I can't spell anyway."

"And that?" said Imani. "The other word. Do you know what it is?"

"No."

"You frightened her."

"I've been frightening her for six weeks," said Emeric. "She just told me."



Fletcher built the tank between the twenty-seventh of December and the sixth of January, and it took the whole of the cabin's long wall where the trough had been. Nine feet by four, and five deep, iron-jacketed like everything else in that room and soldered at every seam, with a lip wide enough to sit on and a ladder of three iron rungs at one end. His men filled it from the deck pump, a foot an hour, with sea.

Imani dosed it the cabin way and no other.

"The room's at the mark," she said, when Fletcher asked. "The water sits in the room. It takes the mark up the way a bucket left out takes the cold. Slowly. Armand reads her and reads the water, and I move the valve by what he reads." She tapped the flasks in the corner with her foot. "Nobody pours any of that into it."

"It'd be quicker."

"It'd be quicker if I knew what liquid mana does in sea water. Bram doesn't know. Emeric doesn't know. The plant doesn't know. I'm not finding out in her."

Bram came across on the ninth with the iron for the tank's lid, because Fletcher had asked him for a sheet that wouldn't warp and Bram didn't trust anybody else to choose it. He went to look at the shard's box first, in the strongroom under the mill, and came out again having checked both locks and touched neither, and said nothing about it to anybody.

On the tenth the guest asked again.

She was sitting on the tank's lip with her feet in the sea, and they were all in the cabin for the morning reading: Imani at the valve, Armand in the door, Ferra on the bench with the register, Bram against the wall with his arms folded looking at Fletcher's soldering with an expression of grudging approval. Emeric was on the floor. Zale had come in behind Bram and stood just inside the door, as he always did, as though he might be needed on deck.

She looked at Emeric and said, in his own language, "Home."

It came out wrong. The h was a breath from much further down than anybody in Kaldo breathed from, and the vowel went round a corner on its way out. But it was the word. It was one of the sixty-one. She said it and pointed past all of them at the door.

Nobody answered at once.

"Right," said Ferra at last, and turned the slate over on her knee, and held out her hand for the chalk without looking up, and Imani put it in her hand. "Then she gets the sum. All of it."

"She can't read a sum."

"She can read a slate. I'll draw her the sum. You say it, so it's said."

So they said it, each of them their own part, and Ferra drew.

"Her back," said Imani. She stood by the tank with her arms folded. "The cut was the thirteenth of November. It's muscle, deep, and the sheet over it. Muscle mends fast and mends weak. She can walk now; she has been. She can swim gently from the middle of February, three months, if I'm watching. Swim hard, against a current, carrying anything, the middle of May. Six months." She looked at the woman's back, where the pale line was. "At a year it's as strong as it's ever going to be. That's November. And it won't be what it was, ever. Four parts in five. Nobody's is, after a cut that deep, and hers had a stone drinking out of it for weeks."

Ferra drew moons. A row of small circles across the top of the slate, and she struck through the first two, for November and December, and rang the one for May and the one for November after it.

"Her note," said Ferra. "I've got it." She opened the register and ran her finger down the column. "Two of Armand on the twelfth of December, at notch twenty-seven. Notch forty on the twenty-fifth. Two and a half on the first of January. Since then a notch every second day, not every day, because..." She looked at Imani.

"Because the top of a cup fills slowest," said Imani, "and that's where you crack it. I can feel the sides of her now. I couldn't on the beach; there was too much going out. She's a big cup and it's not half full." She looked at Armand.

"Six," said Armand. "Of me. Near enough. Imani says six and I've stopped arguing with her about anything."

"Six of Armand is her own," said Ferra. "Her proper weight. At a notch every second day, and every third past four, and nothing going wrong, which it will, three by March. Four by May. Five in August. Six in October." She drew it under the moons, a line climbing in steps. "Call it November, to be safe. And I'll call it that, and it'll be December."

"And then there's the water," said Emeric.

They looked at him.

"It's a thousand miles from the outermost coast to where the water goes thick. Give or take. All of it thin. She'll have legs the whole way, in water she can't swim properly in. Then the wall, where it thickens, and she'd have her tail back, and she'd be on her own, weak, in water where whoever put that thing in her is waiting." He looked at Zale. "And ships die out there."

"Aye," said Zale. "I've been to the edge. Where it goes green and thick and you can feel it in the tiller." He thought about it. "I turned round. I've met nobody who didn't, and come back to say so."

"So she can't swim it, and we can't sail her across it on top," said Emeric. "Something has to go under it. With her inside, in her own water, and us inside in ours, and air for us, and quiet enough not to bring the big things up." He heard himself say it, and it was out, so he gave it its name. "A submarine."

Bram unfolded his arms.

"What's it made of?"

"I don't know yet."

"Then it's not a boat," said Bram. "It's a word." But he took his little steel rule out of his apron pocket and looked at it, and put it back, which Emeric had seen him do before, at the start of things.

Ferra drew the last thing on the slate under the climbing line: a small hull shape, and a line under that, and a question mark, and then she held the slate out across the tank.

The guest took it in both hands. She looked at it a long time. She touched the struck-through moons, and the rung one for May, and the steps of the climbing line one at a time, and the boat. She touched the question mark, and looked at Ferra, and Ferra shrugged, which is the same in every language there is.

Then she laid her palm flat on the slate, over all of it, the way she had laid it on Imani's hand over her wound, and pressed once, and handed it back.



Zale had forty languages, more or less, and none of them had come out of a book.

He had got them the way he got currents: by being in the water a long time and paying attention. You stood in a market and you listened to what a woman said when a man tried to cheat her on the weight, and what she said when he didn't, and you said it back to her the next day and watched her face. That was all a language was. Words were where people kept what they cared about, and you found out what they cared about first.

Emeric had written the sixty-one words out for Ferra on the evening of the read, in his own spelling, with the thing beside each one in plain Kaldo, and Ferra had copied them clean. Zale took the copy off the cabin bench on the twelfth of January without asking and read it on his own deck in the last of the light, and saw at once that half the spellings were wrong. Not Emeric's fault. A man who'd heard a word once, inside somebody's head, with a headache coming on.

So he went and heard them again.

He went in the mornings, after the reading and before the fish, when Imani had done with her and before the cook arrived to stand in the door and make remarks. He sat on the iron floor a fair way off the tank with Ferra's list on his knee and said a word off it, and waited.

The first morning she didn't answer. She looked at him the way she'd looked at him over the rail in November: level and pale and still, as one pilot looks at another on a strange deck. He said the word for water again. On the third go she said it back, properly, and it was nothing like what Emeric had written: lower, with a stop in the middle of it like a lock gate, and the lift at the end a great deal smaller than he'd been led to think.

He said it the way she said it.

She looked at him a while longer. Then she said it again, slower, for him, and he got the stop right the second time, and she made a sound he hadn't heard from her: short and through the nose, and he thought it might be a laugh.

After that it went better.

By the end of January he had the sixty-one right, or righter, and another forty of his own. They weren't Emeric's kind. Emeric's were things: water, hand, stone. Zale's were the other kind, the ones in the market. No, not that. Again. Bigger. Further. A sort of click at the back of the throat that went at the end of a thing when she meant it, and was missing when she was only being polite; he'd known men in the spice ports who did the same with their eyebrows. He had a word that he thought meant the colour of water over sand and might mean shallow, and a word that might be cold and might be a particular sort of cold, the kind you meet coming up from below. He didn't know her name and didn't try for it. He wasn't in the market for it.

Wren came up with the solder pot one morning to do a seam on the lid, and stopped in the door, and listened to the two of them go back and forth over one word for a quarter of an hour, and went away again without doing the seam.

In February they started on the currents.

It began because he had a word that he was fairly sure meant moving water, the kind that carries you, and wanted to know if it was a current or a tide, and the only way to ask was to draw one. So he wet his finger in the tank and drew on the iron of the lid where it lay folded back against the wall: the island, rough, and the cane islands west of it, and the long set that ran south past the Sentinel in the warm months, which every pilot on that coast knew, and he put the arrow on it.

She looked at it and said no.

Not his no. Hers, with the click.

She put her own wet finger on the iron beside his and drew the same set, south past the Sentinel, his arrow, and then a second line under it, close beside, going the other way. North. Slow, she showed him, with her hand flat and moving slowly. And cold, the word that was the cold from below.

"That's not there," said Zale. "I've run that water for years. It sets south."

She drew the line under his line again, harder.

He drew his again, and the depth of a ship's keel beside it, and the line of the top of the water, and tapped it: this is where I am. This is what I know.

She looked at that. She tapped the top line, his, and said a word he didn't have yet. Then she tapped her own line, underneath, and put her hand flat on her own chest, and then the hand down low, a long way down, under the tank, under the iron. That is where I am.

They sat there and looked at the two lines on the lid.

"Both, then," said Zale.

She said something with the click in it. He didn't know what, but he didn't need to.

He went back to his ship that afternoon and got the lead line out and went and sounded the set south of the Sentinel, which he had never done, because why would anybody, and at a depth he'd never have let a keel near he found the line came up colder than it went down, and the lead hung a hair the wrong way in the water. He wrote that in his own log, and the date, and next morning he told her, in her words and his, and she gave him a look he'd seen on harbour masters, and said nothing at all.

The next morning he had something to say about a back-eddy off the cane islands that she had wrong. She was wrong about it. It took him three days to make her see it.



Emeric went home for the sixteenth of February on Zale's ship, in the rain, with Ferra and the written copy of Corbin's terms in an oilcloth on her knee.

The two rooms on the keep's second floor smelled of a fire that drew properly and of somebody's apple cores in the grate. Corbin had kept them tidy. Ward stood by the door because the door was Ward's. Saban was in the window seat with a sheet of his own on his knee, which was the watchers' year, and which he had already given to Emeric and Ferra that afternoon in the records hall and which nobody else in the room would see.

Corbin sat by the fire with his foot up on the stool, the anklet's leaves catching the light, and read the old terms through once more at the same pace twice, as he had a year ago. Then he looked up.

"Well?"

"The band stays," said Emeric.

"I'd assumed."

"The watchers are halved." He didn't look at Saban. "You've gone where you said you'd go. You've been where you were expected to be, mostly, and the times you weren't you were in the coffee house under the gate, drinking the coffee, which nobody does on purpose. You've said what you were thinking to the man who was watching you, more than once, which he didn't expect. You named him a confessor at the crowning."

"He was the only one there who wasn't listening to the music."

"Half of them go," said Emeric. "You won't be told which half. And the review's set again. This table. The sixteenth of February, year sixteen."

Ferra turned the new copy round on the table and put her finger on the last line, exactly as she had a year ago.

"In writing," she said. "Your hand."

Corbin read it. He signed. Then he sat back and looked into the fire, and Emeric waited, because he could see there was something else, and Corbin had never once in his life said a thing before he'd decided to.

"Her body," Corbin said. "The queen's. The one in the dome." He didn't look round. "Will it wake?"

Emeric didn't lie to him. "Imani doesn't know. She says she'd rather say that than guess, and she won't guess." He kept his voice level. "It's alive. It isn't getting any better. Bram and Tess are building her another one she can walk in. It isn't hers. It'll be the nearest thing we can make."

Corbin listened to all of it without moving.

"Thank you," he said, finally. "That's the first straight answer I've had about her from anyone since I was thirteen." He took his foot down off the stool. "Including myself."

Sabina had hers three doors down a quarter of an hour later. The same: the band, half the watchers, the review set again a year out. She checked the date of the new review against the date at the head of the sheet, as she had last year, and found them a year apart to the day, and signed, and this time thanked Ferra, briefly, without looking at her, for the coal.



He told Harrold the same night, in the dome, with Amara there and the knights fetched and Ward still in the door, because Saban had said in person and he had meant the door as well.

He told it plainly. The beach. The gills. The shard, and Imani's hands. The sixty-one words and the two stones of the teaching, and who the stones said had done it. He left out the word she had said at his eyes; he couldn't have said why, except that it was hers and he didn't know what it was.

Rylan, when he had got past the gills, said, "Wren knew. Since November. And she's said nothing at home." He looked at Saban. "Nothing."

"She kept it," said Saban. "Better than some of my own people would have. I'll want her, one day, when she's done with the mill."

"You can't have her," said Rylan, and after a while, quieter: "Well. Ask her."

Declan wanted to know how deep the tank was. Osric wanted to know whether the shard's box had Bram's lock on it or Ferra's, and was told both, and looked much happier. Ward said nothing at all until they were leaving. Then, at the door, to Emeric, he said, "Two stones." Then: "I'd have wanted to see them," and went out.

Harrold sat by the tree with his hand on the edge of Amara's mattress, as he always sat now, and Amara stood beside him in the dome's light, as she always stood. When they had all gone she looked at the place where the knights had been, and then at Emeric.

"A whole people," she said. "And we didn't even know there was anyone to be sorry to."

Harrold turned his gold eyes up toward his son and didn't say anything for a long time. Then: "Bring her home safe. Whatever home she means."



Ferra and Emeric slept in their own bed that night for the first time since the end of October, and she lay on her side with her cold feet on him and wrote, in the dark, by touch, a line in the small notebook she kept for nothing in particular.

"What's that?"

"A note for Imani. That the bed's too big without her." She put the notebook on the floor. "She'll laugh."

"She will."

"Good," said Ferra, into his shoulder, and was asleep before him, as she always was.



Imani wrote it herself, on the eleventh of March, in the register, in the column Ferra had ruled for remarks, in her own small square hand that was nothing like Ferra's.

She'll live.

She had the figures for it and put them in the line beside. A hundred and fifteen days in the register with clean gums. A belly that hadn't once cramped. Skin clean. Three of Armand that morning, three and a little, he'd said, and wouldn't say how little. And four weeks of swimming, gentle, the length of Fletcher's tank and back, an hour a day under Imani's eye, with the pale line low on the left back stretching and holding every time, and Imani's sense down along it the whole hour.

"That's not a figure," said Ferra, looking at the two words.

"It's the sum of the figures. I can do a sum." Imani shut the register and gave it back. "I've been wanting to write that since the beach. You have your columns. Let me have my line."

The guest had watched her write it from the tank's lip. She couldn't read it. But she watched Imani's face while she wrote it, and Ferra's while she read it, and Emeric thought, watching her watch them, that she knew exactly what it said.

She called them that night.

Not with a sound. Emeric had been asleep on the watch room cot for about an hour, with Imani along one side of him and Ferra along the other and both their elbows in him, and he woke because the guest's note had moved. Not dropped. Moved, the way it had moved toward the gull in November, a thread going out on purpose. It was going out toward him. It touched his sense and stopped there and waited.

He woke Imani, who woke Ferra, who said a word about the hour that she would not have written down, and the three of them went out across the Cradle's deck in their blankets in the dark.

The cabin's lamp was out. Only the little hooded one by the valve, that Imani left low for the dial. The guest was in the tank, in the water to her shoulders, with her arms on the lip. When they came in she looked at each of them in turn: Ferra, Imani, him. Not him first. She looked at Imani last and longest.

Then she let go of the lip and went under.

It took most of a minute, and he could feel it the whole time. Her note pulled in, all of it, the way a man pulls his breath in before a lift; the mana that had sat round her like heat round a stove all through February drew inward, every bit of it, and went down along her. In the water her legs drew together. Not quickly. The line between them closed from the hip down like a seam sewn shut from the inside, steady stitch after stitch, and her skin went dark over it from the waist down, nearly black along the back and pale underneath, and kept going past where her feet had been.

Imani had her hand on the tank's lip so hard her knuckles stood out. She wasn't watching the tail. She was watching the line on the woman's back, low on the left, which was above the place where the change had begun and was stretching as everything below it changed. Emeric could feel her sense on it, thin and fierce, ready.

It held.

The guest turned in the tank. Once, fast, all of her, and the end of the turn hit the iron at the tank's foot with a sound like somebody kicking a door, and the water went up over the lip onto the floor and over their feet. Then she came up at the near end with her arms on the lip again and the water running off her, and she was breathing hard through her mouth and through the slits under her ears both, and she looked straight at Imani.

"Armand," said Imani. Not loud. She didn't need to be. He'd been in the door the whole time; of course he had.

"Up," said Armand. "It went in and it's come back up. Every bit. Higher, if anything." He was quiet. "She could have done that a week ago, I think. She waited for your line."

Behind him, in his nightshirt with a boot on one foot and not the other, Fletcher looked at the water on his floor and at the long dark tail lying the length of his tank with a foot of it folded up against the far end.

"I'll need a longer tank," he said.

Ferra laughed. It came out of her before she could stop it, and she put her hand over her mouth, and the guest looked at her with the water still running off her hair, and said something short through the nose, and Emeric thought that one was a laugh too.

Imani crouched down on the wet iron, level with her, and put two fingers on the inside of the guest's wrist where it lay on the lip. The guest let her. Imani counted, with her lips moving, as she did on the ward. Then she sat back on her heels in the spilled water and wiped her face with the back of her hand and said, to nobody in particular, "Well, it's in the register," and didn't get up for a while.

Ferra had the register under her blanket. She'd brought it across the deck without thinking about it. She opened it on her knee on the bench in the low light and wrote the date and the hour, and in the column for remarks, under Imani's two words, three of her own: Shifted. On purpose.



On Lantern Night, at the end of March, the sworn staff lifted a few lanterns off the Cradle's deck into the lee, the cook and Fletcher's men and Wren and the mill's master, seven or eight of them going up crooked in the still air over the black water; and Fletcher propped the cabin door open, and the guest watched them go from the tank's edge with her arms folded on the iron and her tail moving slowly in the sea behind her, until the last one had gone out of sight above the island.
