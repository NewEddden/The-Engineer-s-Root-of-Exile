Chapter 154 - What He Could Not Write

His hands were steady on the fourth morning after the fire, and he wrote the first letter then, because he did not know when they would be steady again.

He had learned their calendar over the winter the way a man learns the moods of an old dog. After the flight in the autumn they had been still for two days, entirely still, still as a young man's, and he had sat in the keep's east room and looked at them on the table in front of him and not trusted them. Then on the third morning the smallest finger of the left hand had begun, and by noon it was the whole hand, and by the week's end both, and it had been both ever since, some days worse and some days less bad, and never still.

After the fire it had been worse. He did not need Imani to tell him why. He had stood on a stone landing above a lake with nothing left in him and turned the lid of a flask, knowing the rate, knowing exactly the rate, because he had named it himself at the table with his own mouth, and he had taken it anyway, because the market corner was going to go and there were people in the lofts over it. And it had gone into him the way it went into boys on a table in a stone room on the fourth hour, all at once, far too much, and he had felt it find the places in him that were empty and fill them and then go on, looking for more places, and there were none.

He had thrown the water. He did not regret throwing it. He had not been able to hold a spoon for three days.

On the fourth morning, for no reason he could see, they were quiet.

So he sent the girl who brought his tray for good paper and a new pen from the finishing room, and sat down at the table in the window, and wrote to the Circle.

It was a short letter. He had composed it in his head over the winter a great many times, and each time it had got shorter.

He wrote it to the Master of the Circle's second chair, who received papers on the old instruments and passed them where they needed to go, and who had been his pupil, forty years ago, for one winter, and had not been a good one. He wrote it in the Circle's own form, with the Circle's own opening and the Circle's own close, and in the middle of it he said one thing.

He said that in the course of his long study of the knight's rite, and of the men who had come through it and the men who had not, he had come to believe that the rite might be made safer without any change to its substance. That the same working might be done, and the same total given, and the same knight made, over three nights instead of one. That the chamber might be kept consecrated for three nights as easily as for one. That he did not propose this lightly, and did not propose it as a finding, only as a thing an old man who had spent his life in the tower thought worth the Circle's asking, and the Church's, whose office the chamber was. And he asked that it be passed, with his respects, to the cardinal's office that kept the rite.

He did not say why.

He had thought about why for a long time, and in the end he had decided that he would not put a single reason into the letter that he could not stand up in the capital and defend to the Circle's face. And he could not defend the real one. The real one was in a locked box in a cupboard in this room, six years of readings in his own hand, the etching floor and the foundry men and the ship's crew and the six at the table and the survivors in Imani's end room. It said that a man's capacity was not what he was born with. It said that it could be widened, slowly, safely, without a mark, by a rate the body could take. And it said, by plain arithmetic, that every man who had died on the table in four hundred years of the rite had died of the rate, and not of what he was.

That was in the box, and the box stayed shut, and the letter went to the Circle without it.

He sealed it himself. His hands were still steady. He looked at them doing it.

He knew what the letter was. He had known when he began it. It was the letter a man writes so that he can say afterwards that he wrote it. It asked the Church to change the oldest thing it held in common with the Circle, on the word of an old man in a southern barony, for no reason he would give. He knew what the answer would be, and roughly when, and in whose hand, and he wrote it anyway, and sent it by the ordinary post, and entered the date in his own book.

Then he took down the other box, the one with the names in it.

There were a great many names.

He had taught for forty years at the tower. Not all of them had been pupils; some had been boys he had assessed once, at fifteen, with a stone and a cup of water, and told what they had, and sent on to somebody else. Some he had taught for a winter and some for five years. Some had written to him afterwards, once, when they were posted, and some every year since, and some had never written at all, and he had kept a book of them anyway, because it was the kind of thing the grey robe did. It was a thick book. Its early pages were in a hand he hardly recognised as his own.

He went through it over three evenings, with a lamp and a pencil, and made a mark in the margin by every name he still trusted.

It was not the same as every name he still liked. He was careful about that. There were men in the book he liked very much whom he would not have trusted with a letter about the weather, because they talked, or because they had married into a house that talked, or because they had gone up in the Circle and gone up in the way a man goes up in the Circle, which was by remembering who had said what. And there were men he had never much liked who had never in forty years said a thing they did not mean, and those he marked.

He marked the earth mages first. He knew why, and did not make himself pretend otherwise. Emeric had not asked him for anything. Emeric had put a circle on the long room's table and said nothing about who would lift its wall, and Armand had sat at the end of the table and counted it with his thumbs before he could stop himself, and seen Emeric see him do it. A wall of that length, in earth. A mage of middling strength could raise a length of footing twice a man's height and as long as a cart in a morning, and set it for masons to face, and be spent by noon. He had done the sums over and over, in bed, in the dark, when his hands would not let him sleep. It came to a number he did not like to think about, and every time he thought about it he found that he had begun thinking, instead, of names.

By the third evening there were ninety-one marks.

He did not trust all ninety-one to come. He did not trust half of them to come. But he trusted all of them to read a letter from him, and keep it, and say nothing about it to anybody who had not been sent one too.

He wrote the first of them on the next morning his hands were quiet, which was eight days later.

He wrote it by hand.

He had considered the printer. It had occurred to him, standing in the finishing room one afternoon in the thaw watching the second frame lay down a page of Orrin's primer in its patient back-and-forth, that he could write the letter once and have ninety copies of it by evening. He had stood there for some time thinking about it. Then he had gone back to his room and taken out his own pen.

A printed letter would tell the Circle more than the letter said. That was the whole of it.

Every mage in the kingdom knew how letters were written. They were written by hand, one at a time, and a man who received one knew that somebody had sat down and written it to him, and nobody else. If ninety men in ninety posts across the kingdom each received a letter from the grey robe in a southern barony, and every letter was the same letter to the stroke, in a hand that was not quite a hand, on a sheet that was not quite any sheet a stationer sold, then the first two of them to compare their letters would know three things at once. That there were ninety. That somebody in the south could make ninety letters as easily as one. And that the grey robe had not thought them worth his own pen.

So he wrote them by hand. Each one. Every one different, because each was to a different man and he could not write the same letter to two men he had known for different reasons. He wrote on the steady mornings, which came perhaps two in a week, and on the mornings that were not steady he did not write at all, and read over what he had written instead, and burned some of it.

They were short. They said, more or less, the same thing.

That he was in the south, at Kaldo, and well, and old.

That there was work there. Walls. A city, which would want walls on a scale that nobody had asked of an earth mage in his lifetime or in his teacher's, and that it would pay, and pay well, and be honestly done.

That there was other work too, which he would not describe, because it could not be described in a letter.

That there was something at Kaldo he could not write. I will not write it, he wrote to one of them, and then crossed it out, because it was not true, and wrote instead, There is something here I cannot write in a letter, which was.

That if the man came, he would be shown it. That if he did not come, Armand would think no less of him, and would ask only that he burn the letter.

He did not promise anything else. He was very careful not to.

He hesitated longest over the ones with families.

There were twenty-six of them among the ninety-one. Men with wives in the capital, or in the market towns round a crown post, with children at the tower's own school or apprenticed to somebody. A mage of the Circle could leave a post the way any man could leave a trade; he had only to say so. His lord would be sorry, and there would be a dinner, and then the post would be filled from the tower's list and that would be all. Nobody would hold him. Nobody had ever held a mage. It was one of the few things in the kingdom that had never needed a law.

But a man with a wife and three children who left a crown post and a house in the capital for a southern barony where there was nothing yet but a quarry and some trenches, on the strength of a sentence in an old man's letter, was not leaving a trade. He was taking his family with him to find out what the sentence meant.

Armand sat with the twenty-six for a long time.

He wrote to eleven of them. He put the other fifteen at the back of the pile and did not write to them yet, and did not strike them out.

"You know what it is," said Ferra.

He had told her, because she had asked. She had come to his room on the fourth week of it with Hobb and a question about the cost of the post, because a letter south to north by the ordinary riders cost three Copper, and he had sent forty-seven of them, and it had come across her book as a figure without a reason. He had given her the reason, standing, with Hobb at the door. He had not meant to give her any more than that.

"I know what it is," he said.

"Say it."

He looked at her. She was standing by his table in her grey coat with her book open on her forearm and her pen out, and she was not writing yet.

"It's a schism," said Armand.

She waited.

"The Circle has taught for four hundred years that capacity is what a man is born with and that the tower's business is to find it and train it. Everything in it is built on that. The assessment at fifteen, the posts, the ranks, the rite. Every mage in the kingdom was measured once and told what he was." He did not look at his hands. "And I was the one who told most of them. And I am writing to them now to say come south, there is something here I cannot write, and every one of them who comes will find out that I was wrong for forty years, and that the Circle is wrong now. And he will not be able to go home and unlearn it."

"And you know that."

"I've known it since the first letter." He picked up the one he had been writing, and looked at it, and put it down. "It isn't a recruitment. I'm not asking men to come and work. I'm asking them to leave. The ones who come will not be the Circle's men afterwards, whatever they call themselves. They'll be something else, and I don't know what, and I'm the one who is going to have to answer for it."

Ferra wrote something in her book. She did not ask him whether she might. She wrote it the way she wrote the price of a barrel of iron salt, in the same hand, at the same speed, and then she looked at it.

"I'm entering it," she said.

"Entering what?"

"What you just said. The word." She did not read it back to him. "Not in the barony's book. In the box. With the date, and that you said it, and that you knew." She closed the book on her pen to keep the page. "Somebody's going to ask one day when this started, and what the people who started it thought they were doing. I'd like there to be an answer that isn't something somebody remembers."

Armand looked at her for a moment.

"You're a hard woman," he said.

"I'm a clerk," said Ferra. "It's the same thing, most days."

He did not do it because Emeric had asked him. Emeric had not asked him. Emeric had not said one word to him about mages since the morning of the circle, and Armand had noticed that, and knew him well enough by now to know that it was deliberate, and that the man would have gone on not asking until the wall was built by hand, one course a season, if Armand had never lifted a pen.

He did it for the reasons he did not put in any of the letters.

He did it because of the end room in Imani's ward. Because of Hew and Colley and Jem and Perrin and Hild and Senna, and Wat, who was in the lake-side row now. Because he had sat an hour a day with each of them for eight weeks, holding a stone between his hand and theirs and keeping the door narrow, and watched nothing come back. Because five people in a cellar under a wool hall in Wayford had worked out the same thing he had spent six years proving in a locked box, and had built it out of those six, and he had been the one who knew how and had not said.

He did it because of the band. Because he had gone up into it in the autumn against Ferra's no and felt himself filled, gently, the way a body is filled by warm water, and come down thirty years old, and spent two days with hands that did not shake. Because the band was there, every night, over every town in the kingdom, and nobody in the Circle had ever gone up to it, because nobody in the Circle had ever had a reason to believe it was there.

And he did it because of forty years.

That was the one he could least bear to look at directly, and so he looked at it on the steady mornings, when his hands were quiet enough that he could hold the pen and the thought at the same time. Forty years of boys at fifteen standing in front of him in the tower's cold assessment room with a stone in one hand and a cup of water in the other, looking at him, waiting to be told. And he had told them. Middling. Small. None to speak of. Knight-range, if the rite takes. You will serve in the capital. You will not. And every one of them had believed him, because he was the grey robe, and he had believed himself, because the Circle had believed it for four hundred years before he was born.

He had been wrong every time. Not about what they had. About what they could have.

He wrote to them now. One at a time. By hand.

The table asked him what they would be shown.

It came at the long-room table in the last week of spring, with everybody there, because Emeric had said it would not be settled anywhere else. The first answers had begun to come. Nine so far, by the ordinary post, in nine hands, and seven of them said yes.

"If they come," said Emeric, "what do they see?"

"The knights," said Armand.

He had thought about this. He had thought about little else.

"Flying," he said. "Not at a height. Not over the town. Ground to ground, a field to a field, two men in plate with their cloaks hooded and their faces covered, carrying something, because a man carrying something is at work and a man flying for nothing is a display. They'll understand it's done with stones. They won't understand how. Nobody in the tower knows a man can do it. Nobody in the tower knows a stone can hold a man up at all without a caster pouring himself into it by the minute. They'll see it and they'll know I wasn't lying."

Ward nodded, once.

"The printer," said Armand. "Yes. Running. They'll look at the page and think it's clever, and some of them will look at the head going back and forth with nobody standing over it deciding where it goes next, and that's the part they'll lie awake over. I did."

"And the ship," said Ferra.

"Never."

He said it before Emeric could.

"Not a word of it. Not a board of it. Not in the band, not on the lake, not on the lower water, and not anywhere near the new site." He looked down the table at Emeric. "Put her somewhere else while they're here. Put her on the far side of the coast if you have to. Every one of those men can reckon a distance and a day, and some of them are better at it than Ferra."

"Nobody is better at it than Ferra," said Saban.

"Some of them are as good," said Armand. "And they'll be here a long time. If a man who's supposed to be in Kaldo is seen in Emberfell four days later, one of them will sit down with a map. The ship stays off the site. The ship stays off the lower water while there's a mage on the flat. That's the whole of it."

Emeric looked at him for a long moment.

"Agreed," he said.

Nobody spoke about the stones.

Armand sat with his hands folded on the table and waited to see whether anybody would. The six stones at the heart of the six bands, and the seventh on Emeric's wrist, and the two of Amara's in Ferra's strongbox, the root that was a bead now and the chipped one still bound to nothing. The thing that made the printer a printer and not a clever frame. The thing he had watched lattice itself back over every new face of a stone in the forge in the autumn and grown, as if it were alive, and said out loud, in that room, nobody made these, or nobody made them the way we were told.

Nobody at the table said a word about them. Emeric did not. Ferra did not. Armand did not either. He sat and let the silence go past, and it went past, and the table moved on to the price of oak.

He sealed the last letter on a steady morning at the beginning of summer.

It was the ninety-first. He had written to the fifteen at the back of the pile in the end, all of them, in the last fortnight, on mornings that were not always steady, with his hand braced flat on a book. Some of those letters were in a worse hand than the others. He had let them go anyway. A man who had known him forty years would know why.

He pressed the seal, and held it, and lifted it, and looked at the wax.

Then he put it on the stack with the others waiting for the rider, and sat back in his chair by the window with his hands in his lap, and looked at the stack.

There were nine answers in the box on the shelf. Seven yes, one no, one that said only I will think. There were eighty-two letters that had gone out and not come back, and nine on the table that had not yet gone.

He found that he did not know, looking at the stack, how many of the men he had written to were still his.

He had thought, when he began, that he knew. That he had kept the book of names for forty years precisely so that he would know. But a man who had been his pupil for one winter at seventeen was forty-five now, and had lived twenty-eight years that Armand had not been in, and had taught his own pupils, and buried his own dead, and made his own peace with the Circle or not. The name in the book was the boy. Armand did not know the man. He had written ninety-one letters to boys.

And the ones who came would be men.

He sat there until the rider knocked. Then he gave him the nine, and paid him, and watched him go down the stair, and sat down again by the window, and held his hands still in his lap, and they were still for a while.
