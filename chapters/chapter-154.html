Chapter 154 - The First Train

Before anybody put water in the engine, Bram wanted to know what his gauges were lying about.

He said so at the long table in the last week of March, with his arms folded, when Emeric asked him when the boiler would be tried. Not when. First, said Bram, he wanted to know what the dial on its front would say when the boiler was at the pressure it was built for. Because the dial on its front had been made in his shop, by his own man, from a brass tube bent into a hook and a spring and a needle, the way every pressure gauge in Kaldo had been made, and he had never once had anything to try it against but another gauge his own man had made.

"And one of those," said Bram, "was on the drum in the oven shed."

Nobody at the table said anything to that. The drum in the oven shed had let go two winters ago with a man in front of it, a dock quarter man with a wife and a girl, and Ferra still paid his wage every week to the girl's mother, and would until the girl was grown. The gauge had come out of the wreck with its glass gone and its needle bent, and Bram had kept it. Emeric had seen it on the shelf over his bench.

"It read under," said Bram. "I've tried it since against the others and it read under them. Not by much. By enough." He unfolded his arms. "A gauge that's made by one man and tried against another gauge made by the same man reads that man's opinion. That's all it reads. I want to know what the steam thinks."



The forge made the heart of it on the twenty-ninth, at night, in the cellar, with Tess at the dial.

A piston and a cylinder. That was all, and they were nothing to look at: a short bar of hard steel and a short sleeve of hard steel that it slid into, the length of a finger, the bar's face ground flat. What the forge put into them was the one thing Bram's lathe could not. The bar was round to the width of a hair's split end all along its length; the sleeve was round inside to the same; and the bar's face was a known area, exactly, a square inch of the Kaldo inch to the last grain, lapped so true that the bar would slide into the sleeve on a film of oil and fall through it slowly under its own weight, like a stone sinking in honey, and not let a bead of oil past it in a day.

Bram built the rest by daylight in four days. An iron stand with the sleeve upright in it and the bar in the sleeve, its face down, on oil. A little screw pump at the side that pressed the oil when its handle was turned. A pipe from the oil to a fitting where a gauge could be screwed in to be tried. And a stack of iron weights, round, with a hole in the middle of each to drop over the top of the bar, each of them weighed on Harl's beam against the tally room's standard pound and stamped with its weight by Bram's own hand.

"A deadweight tester," Emeric told him, when it stood finished on the tally room bench. "That's its name."

"It's a scale," said Bram, "upside down."

That was the whole of it, and he had seen it at once, standing over the stand with his hands flat on the bench on either side of it. Put a hundred pounds of weights on top of the bar. The bar's face was a square inch. So the oil under it had to be pressing up at a hundred pounds on the square inch to hold the weights, no more and no less, or the bar would sink or rise. Turn the pump until the bar lifted off its stop and floated on the oil with the hundred pounds on it, and the oil in the pipe was at a hundred pounds to the inch, by the weights, by the area, by nothing a man had made a guess at. And a gauge screwed into the end of the pipe had to say a hundred. Or it was wrong, and you knew by how much.

They tried the engine's gauge first. It said ninety-four.

They tried the gauge on the works boiler, which had been running for a year and a half at what everybody believed was its working pressure. It said a hundred and nine, which meant that every day for a year and a half the works boiler had been running at less than its men believed. The powerhouse's said ninety-one. Grayrock's winding engine's, which had come down the line in a crate that morning wrapped in a sack, said eighty-eight, and Bram stood and looked at that for a good while and then said a word Emeric had never heard him use in front of Tess.

Tess had not said anything at all.

She was at the end of the bench in her chair with her data book, writing each gauge's number in a column as Bram read it out, and when Bram unscrewed Grayrock's gauge she reached up without a word and took the bent one down off the shelf over the bench. The one from the oven shed. She looked at it in her lap, at its broken glass and its needle bent back against the pin, and then she held it out to Bram.

Bram looked at her.

"Try it," said Tess.

He screwed it in. He pumped, slowly, with the weights on, until the bar lifted off its stop and floated, and the bent needle on the oven shed's gauge moved off its pin, stiffly, and went round, and stopped.

It said eighty-one, at a hundred.

Tess wrote it down. Then she closed the data book and sat with her hands flat on its cover, and nobody in the tally room said anything for as long as it took the bar to sink back onto its stop.

"Every gauge," Tess said at last. Her voice was quite steady. "Every one in the four baronies. Here, on this, before it goes on anything. And again every quarter, with the bolts." She looked at Bram. "And a card on each one, with the day and the number. Tied on."

"Every one," said Bram.

It took them a fortnight. There were sixty-three pressure gauges in the four baronies, which nobody had known until they were counted, and every one of them came to the tally room in a sack, and was tried, and was mended or thrown in the scrap bin, and went back to its boiler with a card tied to it in Tess's hand. The engine's gauge, the one that had said ninety-four, was taken apart and its spring reset and tried again until it said a hundred, and at two hundred it said two hundred, and at a hundred and fifty it said a hundred and fifty; and then, and not before, Bram said they could put water in the boiler.



The engine had been built all winter in the long shed at the works by Bram and Tess and nine men, and Emeric had drawn very little of it.

He had drawn the idea. On the long table, in October, after the low flights stopped: a boiler on wheels, and the engine to turn them. He had drawn the boiler the way a locomotive's boiler had been drawn on Earth, because he remembered it and because there was no better way he knew of: a long barrel lying down, with a firebox at one end and a smokebox at the other, and between them, running the length of the barrel through the water, a nest of tubes, so that the fire's heat went down the inside of the tubes and the water stood round the outside of them; a fire-tube boiler, the other way round from the water-tube boilers in the boats. Steel plate, rolled, from the converter's blows, because iron plate would not hold the pressure a barrel that size had to hold without being as thick as a wall. He had drawn two cylinders, one on each side, and the rods to the wheels. And then he had stopped drawing, because Bram had taken the pencil.

The rest was theirs. What the steamboat had taught them, coming up the river against the current two summers running on a boiler at twice the pressure of the works', with Bram in the engine pit listening to every knock: where a stay wants to crack, how a joint weeps before it goes, how much a hot cylinder grows. What the compound engine had taught them in the summer. Where the tubes went into the plates at each end, Tess had them expanded in with a tool she had made at her bench from Emeric's description and her own opinion of it, and then she had them welded round the outside, every one, with the arc, which nobody on Earth had done to a locomotive's tubes at the start, because nobody on Earth at the start had had the arc.

The water went in on the eighth of April. Cold, from the works' tank, until the barrel was full to the top and the air was all bled out of it at the high cocks. Then the hand pump, on a pipe to the boiler's feed, and the engine's own gauge with its card on it, and the tested twin of it from the tally room screwed in beside it so that there were two needles to watch and not one.

Bram pumped it to its working pressure and stopped, and walked round it.

Then he pumped it to half again its working pressure, cold, with no fire under it and nobody in front of the firebox door, because that was the rule Bram had made after the oven shed: a boiler is tried with cold water at half again what it will ever be asked to bear, and if it is going to burst, it bursts like a dropped bucket and not like a bomb. He walked round it again, slowly, with a lamp, close, looking at every seam and stay and tube end, and at the plates for any wet.

There was one. A bead at the root of a stay on the firebox's left side, no bigger than a tear. Bram let the pressure off, and drained it, and Tess's man caulked the stay, and they filled it and pumped it again.

It held. Bram walked round it a third time, and stood back, and looked at the two needles side by side at half again its working pressure, steady, the same, both of them telling the truth.

"Now you can light it," he said.



It went onto the rails on the tenth, in the yard at the Kaldo end of the line, below the works, where the engine shed stood new and roofless with its pit dug, and a water tower on four brick legs beside the track, and a stack of coal sacks at the foot of a ramp for want of the coal stage that was being built at the mine.

Bram had the crane brought down for it.

It was the crane from the gravity drive at Grayrock, come down the line on a flat wagon: an ordinary timber crane on a turntable with an ordinary jib, and hanging from the jib, instead of a hook block, an iron case the size of a cider barrel with a lever on its side and a hook under it. In the case, behind iron, were two of the mine's own gravity stones. Set the lever to Lighten, and the case took the weight off whatever hung from its hook, notch by notch, until the load floated, and four men on guide ropes could walk it where they liked.

They walked the engine. It came up off the timber cradle it had been built on, slowly, as the lever went over, the whole black length of it with its tall chimney and its brass dome, and hung a hand's width clear of the cradle, swaying very slightly, like a moored boat; and the four men on the ropes walked it across the yard as if it were a cart of hay, and set it down over the rails, and Bram, lying on his back in the pit with a lamp, called it left a little, and right, and down; and its flanges found the inside of the rails, and it settled onto them with a long iron sigh, and stood.

It had taken a quarter of an hour.

Then Bram had the yard lift the tender. The tender was the wagon behind the engine that carried its coal and water: a deep iron tank on wheels, nothing like the engine's weight but heavy enough. And Bram would not let them touch the crane's lever for it. He had them rig the old timber derrick from the river landing, with its block and tackle, and the long sheer legs, and the winch, and the guys, the way the yard had lifted every heavy thing it had ever lifted before there were gravity stones in Kaldo.

The yard did not like it. The yard had just watched a whole engine float across the cobbles in a quarter of an hour.

"And the day the stones in that case are flat," said Bram, to the yard, "and it's raining, and the engine's off the rails in a cutting twenty miles from here with a train of ore behind it, who's going to put her back?" He waited. Nobody answered. "Rig the sheers."

They rigged the sheers. It took them all the rest of the morning, and the tender went up and over and down onto the rails a foot at a time to the clank of the winch's pawl, with Bram walking round the guys checking every one, and when it was down the yard stood round it sweating and swearing and pleased with itself in a way it had not been with the engine.

Emeric watched from the shed door. He did not say anything. He thought he would remember, later, that he had not needed to.



Odo Pike had come down from Grayrock for it on the horse train the night before, with his stick over his arm and his back bent, and at two in the afternoon he climbed up into the first wagon behind the tender, onto a plank bench under a canvas hood, and sat down on the plank, and said that it was a plank.

"It's a seat," said Bram, from the footplate.

"It's a plank," said Odo. "I've sat on planks forty years. I know a plank when I'm sat on it." He shifted his bad back against the wagon's side and looked at the engine in front of him. "Go on, then."

Emeric was on the footplate with Bram, and Tess was on the tender in her chair, lashed down to the coal plate with two of Bram's chains through her wheels and her data book on her knees and a look on her face that dared anybody to say anything about it. Vantic was in the first wagon with Odo, on the other plank, in her grey, with her gloves on and the House's book in her lap, as upright as she sat at her desk. She had asked Bram on the tower stair a week ago when the engine would run. She had not asked whether she might ride. She had simply come down the hill at half past one and got into the wagon.

Bram opened the regulator.

It did not go at once. It hissed. Steam came out at the cylinders in two white clouds that rolled across the yard and made the crowd at the edge of it step back and laugh; and then the first beat came up the chimney, one, a hard flat cough with a puff of black smoke on it; and then another, slower than a man's walk; and the wheels turned; and the whole of it, the engine and the tender and Tess and the six wagons behind with Odo and Vantic and as many of the works as could cram into them, began to move.

The yard cheered. It ran alongside, the way crowds always ran alongside anything that moved without a horse, children first and then the men, cheering and keeping up easily, for the first hundred yards; and then not so easily; and then, as Bram eased the regulator over another notch and the beats came closer together and the chimney's cough became a bark, not at all. The last boy dropped back at the bend below the brickfield with his hands on his knees, and the engine went on without him, out onto the river road on Odo's rails, faster than he could run, and then faster than a horse could trot.

Emeric stood on the footplate with his hand on the cab rail and the fire's heat on his legs and the wind of their going on his face, and watched the river road come at them.

He had ridden trains in the other life. Thousands of miles of them, in seats with tables, behind glass, with the land going past outside like a painting someone was pulling sideways. It had never been anything. He had read on them, and slept on them, and once missed his stop on one because of a drawing. He had never once in the other life stood on a footplate in the open air in front of a fire with a man he trusted at the regulator and felt the whole weight of the thing come up through his boots off the rails, every joint, every sleeper, iron on iron, the beat of the chimney in his chest like a second heart.

Bram looked at him sideways, once, and away again, and opened the regulator another notch.



They made Grayrock in a little under three hours, with a stop at the halfway siding for water from a cask on a stand, which had been put there for the horses.

The horses took two days. The river boats took two days going up against the current and one coming down. The engine took three hours, at the speed of a galloping horse on the long straight stretches beside the river and the speed of a walking one on the climb up the shoulder into Grayrock's valley, where the rails went up the hill on the bench Paskin had cut and the drivers slipped twice on the wet and Bram swore and eased her and she found her feet again.

Odo complained the whole way.

He complained about the plank. He complained about the smoke, which came back over the wagons in long black streamers on the straight and got into everybody's eyes and hair, and about the cinders, which came with it and burned small holes in people's coats. He complained about the noise, which made it impossible to hear a man complaining. He complained about the joints, every one, the knock of every wheel over every rail end at every yard of the forty miles, which he said he could feel in his spine, and which were his own joints, laid by his own hand with his own gauge stick, and he knew it and complained about them anyway. At the halfway siding, while the tender filled from the cask, he got down off the wagon with his stick and walked very slowly round the engine, twice, and climbed back up onto his plank, and said nothing at all for the next ten miles.

Then the wagon came round the shoulder of the hill at Grayrock, and Odo looked down the valley at the landing and the shaft head and his own wagonway running down the hill between them, which had been a picture on a tavern wall forty years ago, and the engine whistled, which Tess had fitted to it for exactly this moment, a long high rising shriek that went off the valley's sides and came back from three of them; and he put his hand over his eyes.

"It's a plank," he said, behind his hand.

Vantic had not said anything all the way.

She had sat on her plank through the smoke and the cinders and Odo, upright, with her gloves on and the book in her lap, and watched the land go by. She had watched it the way she read a column, steadily, without hurry, taking every line; and at the halfway siding she had not got down, and had not looked at the engine. She had looked back down the line, the way they had come, along the rails going away into the afternoon toward Kaldo, and then up the line, the way they were going, toward Grayrock, and Emeric, coming back from the tender, had seen her lips move. Counting.

At Grayrock she got down off the wagon as if she were getting down from a carriage at a door, and stood on the landing with the book under her arm, and waited until he came.

"Three hours," she said.

"Nearly."

"The House lends money to people a long way off." She was looking down the line. "It always has. It's the House's whole trade. And it prices every loan by many things, and one of them, which nobody in the House ever writes down because everybody in the House knows it, is how far away the borrower is. How long it takes a factor to ride there and look at him." She was quiet. "A loan you can visit in two days is a different risk from one twelve days away. You lend to the first one cheaper. You'd be a fool not to."

Emeric waited.

"Grayrock was two days from Kaldo," said Vantic, "last week. It's three hours now." She turned her head and looked at him, directly, which she did not often do. "You've just made every loan along this line cheaper. Every one. To anybody. Whether you meant to or not." She turned back to the line. "The House will want to know which way the next one goes. Before anybody else does."

"Are you going to tell them?"

"I'm going to tell them that you've built a thing that makes distance cheaper," said Vantic, "and that they had better start thinking about what a factor is for when he can be anywhere in a day." She tucked the book a little higher under her arm. "And then I'm going to sit on a plank for three hours, and go back to my tower, and work out what it means. I haven't yet." She paused. "I should like to have done it before them."



He went up on the river wall that night, after they came home, with Ward.

He had told Ward he would. He had told him on the footplate coming back, quietly, under the noise, and Ward had looked at him and said nothing and been at the foot of the water stair at midnight with his cloak on.

The wall walk on the river side ran along the top of the town wall at the height of three tall men above the lane below it, the lane that went along inside the wall from the water stair to the north tower. At this hour it was empty. Nobody walked the river lane at midnight. The wall lamps lit the walk itself and threw the lane below into a long black trench with a gutter down its middle and the backs of the dyehouse sheds on its far side.

He had done the Stop every night for a fortnight in the stable yard. He could hang at the top of a jump now till his legs got bored. He could pick a stone on the coping and put his boots either side of the moss. The Light Step still threw him into the water butt one night in two, but the Stop was his.

So the Drop was only the Stop the other way up. Step off a height with the weight already off, so that he did not fall, he floated down; and then, a man's height above the ground, put a little of the weight back and brake, the way he braked at the top of the stable wall to come down onto his stone. The ground would come up to meet him at the speed of a man stepping off a stair.

He stepped off the wall walk.

It worked. It worked better than he had any right to expect: he went down past the face of the wall in the dark with the stones going by a hand's breadth from his face, slowly, slowly, as if he were sinking in water, the violet flicker at his heels no bigger than a coin; and a man's height above the gutter he let a little of the weight back, and felt the brake take, and slowed, and slowed; and he was coming down onto the lane at no more than the pace of a man stepping off a stair, exactly as he had worked it out.

And then the brake went. Not the gravity. His nerve. At the last moment, with the cobbles a foot under his boots, some old part of him that had been falling off things all its life and knew how it ended took over without asking, and he let the weight back all at once to get his feet on the ground, and the mana went down into his legs to meet it the way it had gone down into them in the stable yard two years ago: hard, and into the ankles, and nowhere else.

He landed. His ankles took it. His right knee did not. It turned in between the stiffened ankle and the stiffened hip, all the force that had nowhere else to go, and went sideways with a sound inside him like a green branch, and he sat down in the gutter with both hands round it and his teeth together.

Ward came down the water stair. He did not hurry. He came along the river lane in the dark and stood over Emeric in the gutter and looked down at him.

"You braced your ankles," said Ward.

"Yes."

"And left the knee to argue with them." Ward crouched. He did not touch the knee. He looked at it. "Can you stand?"

Emeric stood. It held. It hurt, a great deal, in a deep sick way, but it held, and the leg straightened, and he did not think anything was broken.

"Watch," said Ward.

He went back up the water stair, slowly, and along the wall walk until he stood over the place where Emeric had stepped off, a black shape against the wall lamp. And he stepped off.

He did not float. He fell, the way any man falls, the height of three tall men in no time at all, a knight in a cloak dropping off a wall into a lane in the dark; and Emeric, with his sense open, felt him do it.

He felt Ward's note change on the way down. It was always the same note, slow, even, a wall; and on the way down it moved. Not into the legs. Through him. All of him, at once, top to bottom, like a long wave going down a rope: into the shoulders and the back and the hips and the knees and the ankles and the feet, everywhere in the same breath, so that when he landed there was nowhere in him for the landing to go that was not ready for it. He landed in the lane on both feet with a sound no louder than a dropped sack, and his knees went down and came up again, soft, the way a cat's do, and he was standing.

He walked back to Emeric.

"It's not more," said Ward. "You put more into your ankles than I put into all of me. It's not more. It's everywhere at once." He looked at Emeric's knee. "And before. Not when you feel the ground. Before. When you see it coming."

"You've never told me that."

"You've never fallen off a wall in front of me before," said Ward. "Not on purpose." He put Emeric's arm over his shoulder. "Hospital."



Imani strapped it at half past seven in the morning, in the cutting room, under the north window, with the hospital's first bell going over their heads.

She did not ask him what he had done. She looked at the knee, which had come up in the night to twice its size and gone the colour of a plum on the inside, and put her hands on it, one on each side, and moved it, gently, this way and that, watching his face rather than the knee, and when he made a noise she stopped.

"Nothing's torn through," she said. "Stretched. Badly." She reached for the linen. "You'll walk on it in a week and run on it in a month, if you do what I say, which you won't."

She strapped it. She did it the way she did everything with her hands, without a wasted movement, the linen going round and round under the knee and over it in a figure she had done a thousand times on the docks and the works and the drill yard, pulling each turn exactly as tight as it needed to be and not a hair tighter. He watched her hands. She did not look up.

"You fell off something," she said, to the knee.

"Yes."

"Something high." She pinned the end. "That's not a stair. That's a landing. The swelling's all on the inside, where it goes when the leg comes down hard from a height and twists. I've seen it on men off scaffolds." She sat back on her stool and looked at the strapping, to see if it was right, and it was. "And you didn't come here till morning."

"Ward brought me at one. You were in the lying-in room."

"Then you should have waited." She did not say anything else about it. She did not ask what he had fallen off, or why, or why Ward had been there to bring him, which he saw her not ask, the way he had seen her put things away for three years and close the lid on them, and knew that she had put this one somewhere she could find it.

She put her hand on the knee. Flat, on the strapping, over the place where it hurt most. She left it there a breath longer than a healer needed to, with her fingers spread, and he felt the warmth of it through the linen.

"Next time," said Imani, "fall off something lower."

She took her hand away and went to wash.



Rylan found him in the keep yard a week later, walking up and down the length of it on the strapped knee, carefully, because Imani had said walk.

"You land like a sack of turnips," said Rylan.

"You've never seen me land."

"Ward told me." Rylan was grinning. "Ward says you've to learn to fall. For the suits. For when the chute opens and the ground comes up at you and you've no say in where." He pulled off his coat and threw it on the mounting block. "And I'm the one in the yard who's fallen off the most things, so it's me. When your knee's done." He looked at the knee. "Say a week. Then I'll teach you to roll, and you'll hate it, and Osric'll laugh at you, and it'll be the most use you've ever been to anybody."

"Ward said that?"

"Ward said the bit about the suits," said Rylan. "I said the rest."
