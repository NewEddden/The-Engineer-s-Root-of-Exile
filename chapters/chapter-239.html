Chapter 239 - Low Over the Water

They left the city the way they had found it, except for the pegs.

That was the sixth night's work, and it took most of the sixth night. The camp came down first: the five cots folded, the stores that were left packed back into the tarred bags, the empty casks, the spent canisters, the spoiled one with the hair of a crack across its shoulder, wrapped by itself for Bram. Then the hand lines, all of them, off every stair in the city, stair by stair, back through the levels, Sir Aled and Sir Gavin going up each flight on the line and coiling it down behind them as they came. The pegs they left. The pegs were driven into cracks in the stone with a mallet wrapped in rag, and to draw them would have meant the mallet unwrapped, and the rock did not deserve that. Nobody would come. And if somebody did come, one day, a long time from now, with a lamp, and found a line of small iron pegs going up the side of every tall stair in a dead city, that would be the least strange thing they found.

The line in the shaft came up last. Emeric went down on it himself to the first knot and cut the weight off it and came up, and coiled it, and the shaft lay black and still behind them with nothing in it but the water, and at the bottom, sixty feet down at the turn, an empty clip on a bolt where the stage bottle had hung. Zale's own bottle was still down there too, on the floor at the shaft's foot where Emeric had cut it loose, spent, with its hose trailing. Nobody went down for it. It would lie there on the clean stone in the clear water, a red-banded steel bottle bored out of the rail works' best bar, for as long as the city lay there. Ferra had it in the book already. One half set bottle, Zale's. Left.

He went to the little room in the autumn of the wall, last, alone.

Ansel Vey sat where he had sat, against the wall under the niche, with his hands in his lap and his collar up. The satchel was in his lap under his hands. The candle stub in its tin, the flint, the pen, the little crusted bottle, all in their row beside him on the floor, where he had set them. Only the book was gone. Zale had it, in oilcloth, in two layers of oilcloth and a third of tarred canvas, sewn, inside his suit against his chest where Ferra kept hers.

Emeric did not say anything to him. There was nothing to say that the man had not said himself, better, on his last page. He stood in the doorway a moment with his lamp, and then put the lamp out, so that the room was dark the way it had been dark for all the years before they came, and went back out through the painted autumn into the hall, and did not look at the wall.

The sump was easy, the last time. That was the thing about the last time.

They went down the flooded stair in the order they had come, with the guideline under their hands and the talking line in its rings beside it, Ferra first now and Emeric last, because the last man took the line up behind him. He came down the steps into the black water after the others with the reel on his belt, and at every peg he stopped and untied the line, and wound it in, coil by coil, onto the reel; and it came up off the flooded stair behind him as he went, so that when he came up the far side into the passage under the ridge there was nothing in the water behind him at all. The steps going down into the dark, and the silt on them, and his own boot marks in the silt, the only marks there had ever been; and those would settle, in a week, in a month, and be gone.

Ward was at the top of the passage, in the slot's mouth, on his knees, with the talking line's end in his hand, and when Emeric's lamp came up the last of the tall steps into the reach of his, he did not say anything. He took the reel off Emeric's belt, and weighed it in his hand, and nodded, and put it in the bag with the others.

Then they were in the hollow under the ridge, the five of them and Ward and Wes, in the cold desert dark, with the stars over them in their thousands and the lanterns of the Kesrath row strung along the benches far above, a mile of yellow points, and the tink, and the tink, of their picks coming down the ridge very faint on the wind. Nobody on the lowest bench. Nobody ever on the lowest bench. Ward drew the slab back across the slot, and heaped the spoil at its foot the way it had been, and went round the hollow backwards with his brush of grey shrub, and there was nothing.

"Walk," said Emeric.

They walked. That was the price of the rule and they paid it: a mile west across the island in the dark, on foot, the five of them in their Dive Suits with their helmets under their arms and the bags on their backs, with Ward and Wes going ahead in the good suits a foot off the ground on Lighten and waiting for them at every rise. Divers do not fly. Not a foot. Not on a line. They walked the whole mile, across the sand and the crunching white crust of the flats, in the dark, with the Kesrathi lanterns at their backs and the sea sound ahead of them, and nobody complained, and Zale, who could not have complained above a whisper anyway, walked the whole of it with his hand pressed flat on the front of his suit where the book was.

The Wain was on her legs on the flats, where she had been on the third night, with her belly door open and her ramp down and the last of the bales going up it on men's backs, and she stood there against the stars like a church somebody had set down in a field.

Imani was at the foot of the ramp.

He had not known she would be. She had crossed to the Wain from the Discovery that afternoon in the band, on a tethered carry with Osric, a line between the hulls and a man on Lighten to take her across it, because Imani did not fly in a suit and had said so for years and had not changed her mind; and she had crossed it anyway, three miles over the sea, with her bag on her back, because there were divers coming off the island who could not come up to her, and so she came down to them. She stood at the ramp's foot in her dark coat with her bag at her feet, and when the five of them came in off the flats she did not say anything at all. She took Zale by the jaw, gently, in one hand, and tipped his face up to the lamp stone Fletcher held, and said, "Open," and looked in his mouth.

Then she looked at his elbow. Then at his other elbow, and his knees, and his hands, and she made him walk up and down the ramp twice while she watched his feet. Then she did Ferra, and the two knights, and Emeric last. She did not ask any of them how they were. She looked. At every joint, at the skin of every forearm, at the backs of every neck; she pressed on every knee, and asked, "There?" and "There?" and when she had finished with Emeric she stood back and looked at the five of them in a row at the ramp's foot in the starlight, and he saw her shoulders go down, a quarter of an inch, which was as much as Imani's shoulders ever went.

"Up," she said. "All of you. Lie down in the hold. Flat. You're not to stand up on this ship till I say. And you're not to go higher than I can spit." She looked up at Ned, on the deckhouse rail. "Ned. How high?"

"Masthead," said Ned. "Less."

"Less," said Imani, and picked up her bag, and went up the ramp after them.

They lay in the hold on the bales, the five of them, in the dark, with the smell of the grey shrub all round them and the oil moving in the baffled tank under the floor with a long slow sound like the sea. Ned brought the lift on. The legs came up out of the crust. She rose, a little, and a little more, until she was just off the flats, a few hundred feet, the height of a tall ship's mast; and there Ned held her, and turned her, and put her nose west, and took her out over the dunes and the black stinking pools and the white line of the surf and away over the sea, low, so low that Emeric, lying on the bales with his face to the open belly door, could see the wave tops going by under her in the starlight, a long way down and not a long way.

Above them, somewhere, three miles up, the Discovery was going home high. He could not see her. He knew where she was. She would be over the sea by now, in the band, with Osric at the levers and the six in their Skins asleep in her bunks and Rylan's hand on the rail, going home the fast way, the way she always went, and would be lying at the yard quay by the grassed mound a night ahead of them. The Wain would not. The Wain would go home low, over the sea, in the dark, the long way, the slow way; and lie out the day on the open water a long way off any coast, like a barge becalmed, with her legs down and her lift off and her crew asleep in the hold, so that anybody who saw her from a ship's masthead would see a barge; and come in over the bar the next night behind the Marsh Hen's lamp. A day and a night, from the last time any of them was wet. That was the rule. He had made it on the jetty with an ale bottle, and he was lying on it now.

Zale was on the next bale. After a while, in the dark, he whispered, "Low."

"Low," Emeric said.

"I thought flying would be higher."

"It is, usually."

Zale was quiet a while. The waves went by under the open door. "I like it low," he whispered. "You can see the sea."

Ferra did the count on the open water in the afternoon, sitting on a bale in the Wain's hold with the belly door shut and the sun coming in hot through the deckhouse ports and the ship rocking on the swell like the barge she was pretending to be. She had Ned's gauge readings and Rylan's book from the dune and the vial rig's tally that Osric had sent down with Imani, and she did it the way she did everything, on a clean page, in two columns, with the figures going down; and when she had finished she did not read it out. She turned the book round and held it out to him.

Oil. By the tank. Five loads, one a night from the second to the sixth, off the drum by the hose, into the one tank, a night's pumping at a time; Fletcher had built it long enough to take the drum's summer, and it had. Two thousand gallons a night, near enough, by Ned's gauge, and the last night less, because the drum was low. Ten thousand and some. Near thirteen hundred of the old casks. And beside it, in the margin, small: Two winters of the old runs.

The grey shrub. Bales. Three hundred and forty, two landings. To the rubber shed.

Vials. Filled on the rig in the band, six dawns. Spent in the Skins, and the two good suits, and the dive suits' hearts on the drop line, and the Wain's rails on the two landings. A hundred and seventy-one. Four boxes and some.

Breath. Pure, eleven bottles of fourteen. Half set, six bottles down; one left, at the shaft's foot. Canisters, nine of twelve; one cracked.

Line. All recovered but the pegs.

Lost.

And under it, nothing. A space. And then, under the space, in the same small square hand as everything else, as if it were one more figure in the column, which it was:

Nobody.

He looked at it a long time.

He had said it. At the long table, a year ago and more, the first time the cave came up, with the table round him and the thing not yet planned: nobody dies on this. He had said it the way he said the things he had decided. He had meant it. He had planned for it, every rope rated for a man's whole weight, every drill done fifty times, three lights each, the stage bottle, the glass run out and half again at ten feet. And he heard it now, reading Ferra's page, the way it had sounded then, in the long room, in his own voice: easy. Easy, the way a man says a thing he has not yet had to pay for. A thing Zale had paid for with his throat, and Ferra with a helmet full of lake, and all of them with the hair of a crack across a black horn shoulder that nobody had seen.

Nobody. It was true. It had come out true. It had not been easy, and it had not been certain, and it had come within a breath, twice, of not being true at all; and he had said it as if it were a figure he could promise.

He gave her back the book.

"Write the drill," he said. "Beside it. The drill and the collar and the crack. So that whoever reads nobody in twenty years knows what it cost."

"I have," said Ferra. She turned the page. It was on the next one, all of it, and his own two slates to Imani copied under it. "I wrote it first. Before the count. So the count would have something under it."

Ned gave him the slate that night, on the deckhouse, with the Marsh Hen's lamp in sight ahead of them over the dark water and the bar beyond it. He had not given it to anybody for four days. He held it out without a word, and Emeric took it to the lamp stone and read it.

Third night. Second hour and a half past midnight. Lantern on the ridge, west end, stopped. And: Moved at the fifth hundred. Stood up. Went back east to the others. Could have been resting. Could have been looking. Don't know.

He read it twice. Then he stood at the rail with it in his hand and looked at the Marsh Hen's lamp.

He knew exactly what somebody had seen. He could see it himself, from the end of the ridge: a mile off, on a flat with nothing on it for a mile in any direction, a long black roof against the stars, higher than a barn, standing on legs. Longer than a tithe barn. He knew, word for word, who else had sworn to a thing like that, in a court, on a hill north of a stream, with his captain laughing at him. A duke's scout. And now a Kesrathi on a ridge, who had stopped cutting stone for five hundred counts, and sat, and watched, and got up, and gone back to his mates, and would go home on the dawn boat in four days with the rest of his row, to a harbour on the Kesrath shore, where there were taverns, and men who bought men drinks.

A man who sat and watched. That was what Emeric had felt with the sense, the night of the stream: a rider on the hill north of it who stopped, and sat, and watched, and rode north. Two men on two hills, a year and more apart, in two kingdoms. The same shape against the stars. The same pause.

"You did right," he said to Ned. "Writing it."

"I didn't know what it was," said Ned.

"Neither do I. Not yet." He gave the slate back. "Give it to Ferra. She'll want it in the book, the way you wrote it. Don't know. Don't change a word."

He went to the dome first. Before the palace, before the table, before anybody. He went up from the yard in the grey of the morning after they came in over the bar, alone, still in the clothes he had put on in the Wain's hold, and went up the palace stair and along the gallery to the pass-box, and stood at its glass.

It was there.

He had known it would be. Bram had kept it; Bram had kept everything, the whole week, the collar and the cartridges and the screens at dusk and dawn and the gauge at Ferra's red line, and written every one of them down in the dome's book in his square heavy hand, because Ferra was not there to write them. He had known it would be there. He stood at the glass and looked at it anyway, a long time, as if he had not known.

The tree. A forearm high. Grey-stemmed, with the fine net of crossing lines in the grey. Twelve leaves, dark, flat, open, in their spiral up the stem; and a thirteenth, new, at the top, not yet all the way open, folded along its vein like a hand not yet unclenched. The three burned ones still on, a little way down, bronze at their edges. The two grey half-shells at the foot of the stem, held out on either side, cupped, open, holding nothing. In the morning light that came down into the round room from the glass overhead like light into a well.

Alive.

He put his hand flat on the pass-box glass.

He had stood under a tree as wide as a house three nights ago, dead, in a round room under a pale roof, with its leaves in drifts ankle deep on the floor and its granary swept. And he had gone through the bins with his hands and found no seeds, because the people who had tended it had taken every seed and gone. And there were none in dealers' trays but dead ones, a few, and none in any drawer or treasury that he knew of but the handful he had. There was this. A forearm high, in a glass bowl in the middle of his house, grown from half a book and a dead woman's seed. It might be the only living one in the world. He did not know that. He could not know it. But he had been to the only other place anyone had ever written down, and it was dead there.

And there was nothing else to plant. No seeds had come home. There was a husk in Ferra's bag, and a sliver of dead wood in his, and a bottle of old air. There was nothing to put in the ground but what grew here, if it ever set pods, if it ever lived long enough to set pods, if a thing that had been drawn standing in the sun in fields of its own kind could be kept alive alone in a glass bowl by people who did not know what it was.

He walked the seams.

He had not meant to. He found he was doing it. Along the gallery, round the drum's head, the whole way round, slowly, with his hand out, not touching the glass, and the sense out, low, a crack in the door, feeling the medium in the dome lying on the inside of every pane, and every node, and every seam of pitch; feeling for the thread of it going out anywhere, a hair's width, through a seat, through a bruise at a corner. The way the heart had breathed out through its cracked roof, slowly, for years, after the first rush, until the thing in the middle of it starved.

The dome was a pocket. That was all the heart had taught him, and it had taught him it the hard way, with a tree as wide as a house. A pocket with a skin, and the skin was only glass and pitch and iron, and a pocket was only a pocket as long as its skin was whole. The heart had had no door. Somebody had made it one with a pick. His had a door. The pass-box, the collar, the vent: three doors, all of them his, all of them shut.

Nothing went out anywhere. He went all the way round, twice, and came back to the pass-box, and it was whole.

Bram was there, at the gallery's end, in his leather apron, with the dome's book under his arm. He had come up to do the dawn screens and found the prince already there and had waited. He did not ask anything. He looked at the prince's face, and at the tree behind the glass, and back.

"It drank," Bram said. "Every day. Thirteenth leaf came on the fourth morning." He held out the book. "It's all in here."

"I know," Emeric said. "Thank you."

Armand had Ferra's bottle in his hands within the hour, and did not put it down all morning.

He could not open it. That was the first thing he said, and the second, and he said it with a kind of grief. A bottle of glass held what was in it the way the dome held what was in it; and the moment it was opened in ordinary air, what was in it would go out into the ordinary air of his study and be nothing, a breath, gone. So he did not open it. He had Bram make him a box of the dome's glass, the tempered kind, the size of a bread crock, with a pass-box of its own no bigger than a man's two hands and a pair of rubber sleeves to work it through; and a reading stone on a rod inside the box, and a strip of brass beside it marked in his notation. And when it was sealed, and the air in it read ordinary, he put his hands in the sleeves, and put Ferra's bottle through the little pass-box into the big one, and drew the black horn stopper under the glass.

The reading stone moved.

It moved a long way. It moved past the mark for the band, and past the red line Ferra had painted on the dome's gauge, and went on; and it went on, a little, and a little more, while Armand watched it with his old face very close to the glass and his hands still in the sleeves; and it stopped a good way past anything he had ever written on the brass, and stayed there.

That was the bottle's worth of it, opened into a box a hundred times its size. That was what was left, in the heart, after twenty years of breathing out through a crack.

Armand took his hands out of the sleeves and sat back and looked at the brass, and did not say anything for a long time.

"I shall need a longer strip," he said at last.

Declan had kept the barony, and Declan had one thing to tell him, and he told it at the long-room door in four sentences, the way Ward told things.

"The licence man came," Declan said. "Fenner. On the fourth night, at the powder works. Midnight, with his clerk, under the licence, the way he does." He looked at Emeric. "He asked for the lord of Kaldo."

"And?"

"I told him the truth." Declan did not smile. "I said the lord was away on the barony's business. He asked where. I said on the barony's business, Master Fenner, and I'll tell him you called. He wrote it down. The clerk wrote it down. He walked the powder house and the magazine and the nitre beds and signed the book at the gate and went back up the road." He paused. "He was civil. He's always civil."

Emeric stood in the doorway and thought about it.

It was true. Every word of it. The lord was away on the barony's business. It was the truth that said nothing, and a Crown officer had heard it, and written it down in a Crown officer's book, in a Crown officer's hand, with the date, and the hour, and the place. Honest paper. Every other line Fenner had written in that book since the licence was granted said the same few things in the same few words: the powder house seen, the magazine seen, the beds seen, the lord at the works, or at the palace, or at the table, and was sent for, and came. Line after line of it, quarter after quarter. Clean. And now, in the middle of all that clean, one line that said the lord is away, and did not say where.

It was not a lie. That was the whole trouble with it. It was the only line in the book that asked a question.

"You did right," he said to Declan.

"I know I did," said Declan. "I didn't like it."

He took them all in that night. All of them. The table, all six, Armand included, in his chair with his hands on his stick; and his brother.

He had thought about where for a long time, and in the end there was only one place it could be. Not on paper. Paper could be seized; and there were men in the capital who read a barony's paper for their living, every line of it, and Kaldo wrote more honest paper than any barony in the kingdom, and Emeric was not going to put a painted wall under an island on a sheet for any of them to read. It went nowhere anyone else could read. It went on no page at all.

It went into the room.

He brought them to it the way he always brought the table, and they came the way they always came, through their own doors into his, the six of them, and stood in it; and then he put his hand on his brother's shoulder, at the table in the long room, with the lamps low, and Zale looked at him, and did not hold on at all this time, not for a breath; and Emeric brought him in. Not to the grey place. To the room above Kaldo. His.

Zale came into it standing by the door, of course, and looked down the length of it, at the shelf behind his brother with everything on it that his brother had ever seen, years and years of it, the island and the plague and the wall and the ship and a boy with a bucket and a burning valve and a tree in a glass bowl; and he looked at it all for a long moment, and then he looked at his brother, at the head of the room, and did not say anything. He did not need to. Emeric had said it for him, by bringing him.

And along the near wall, the way they always did when somebody came into a room, the shelves put themselves up, one for each of them; and on three of them, the same thing. Three. Because three of the people standing in that room had stood in the great hall under the ridge with their lamps held up.

The wall.

He built it. He did not paint it; he did not need to. He took it off the three shelves, all three, at once, and set it up round them in the middle of the room, the great round wall of the hall under the ridge, as high as it had gone into the dark, with the tree at its centre and the branches going round through the year, and the tall ones and the broad ones and the beast and the others, and the bare places. Three times. Laid over each other, like three sheets of the same drawing held up to a window.

"Look where they agree," Ferra said.

She said it before anybody else had said anything. She was standing in front of the tree with her arms folded, looking not at the wall but at the edges of it, the places where the three sheets lay over each other. "That's the wall. Where all three of us saw the same thing, that's what was there. Where we don't," she looked at the edge of a branch that was a hand's width higher in one of the three than in the others, "that's us. That's what we brought to it." She looked round at the table. "One man's word is a story. Three men's word, where it agrees, is a record. It's the first thing anybody learns who's ever had to audit a book kept by three clerks." She looked at Emeric. "So look where it doesn't."

They looked. Most of it agreed. The tree agreed, to the leaf, to the pod, to the eighth bump. The tall ones agreed. The beast agreed. The bare places agreed, all eleven, and the heel and the hand and the shoulder at their edges; Ferra's own memory had them a little sharper than the others, and Zale's a little softer, but they were all there, all three times, in the same places.

The broad one at the roots, with the hammer on his shoulder, looking back, did not.

It was the same figure in all three. That was the first thing. Short, broad, heavy in the shoulder and the arm, standing square with the whole weight of him down through his heels, turning to look over his shoulder at whoever had come in. Bram's shoulders. Bram's way of standing. All three of them had seen that. It was on all three sheets, the same.

But in Ferra's, and in Zale's, the face that turned to look over the shoulder was a face. A broad face, painted in a few strokes, faded, with a heavy brow, and nothing in it that anybody in the room had ever seen.

And in Emeric's, it was Bram's.

He saw it. He stood there in his own room, in front of his own memory laid over two other people's, and saw it: that the face on his sheet was Bram's face, the nose and the jaw and the set of the mouth, exactly, and that the face on the other two was not. That it had never been. That he had stood in front of the wall with his lamp and seen Bram's shoulders, and Bram's stance, and somewhere between his eyes and the shelf in his head his own mind had painted Bram's face in over the one that was there, because it expected to. Because it had been expecting to since his first week in Kaldo, when he had looked at a smith at a bench and thought: built like something out of an old story.

He could not trust it. Not even to himself. Not even in here. The likeness he had seen on that wall, with his own eyes, was a thing he had brought to it.

He did not say any of that. Nobody asked him to. Ferra looked at the three faces laid over each other for a long moment, and then at him, and then away, and said nothing either.

Bram had come round the tree to stand in front of the figure. He looked at it for a long time, all three sheets of it. He did not look at the face. Emeric watched him not look at it. He looked at the hammer on the figure's shoulder; and then at the thing in the figure's other hand, down by its hip, that Emeric had taken for a tool he did not know, long and flat with a notched end.

"That's a swage," Bram said.

Everybody looked at him.

"For drawing down a bar. On the anvil. You set it on the hot iron and hit it, and it shapes the bar round." He put out his hand toward it and did not touch it. "Nobody's held a swage like that in my lifetime. Handle's wrong. Too short for the weight of the head. You'd break your wrist." He looked at it a while longer. "Unless your wrist was different."

It was a smith's remark. It was the remark any smith in the world would have made, looking at that tool. It confirmed nothing. Bram turned away from it and went and stood by Ward, and did not look back at the wall again all night.

Imani read the bodies. She went round the whole wall, slowly, all three sheets, the way she went round a ward, and looked at the tall ones and the broad ones and the ones on four legs who talked with their hands and the small ones in crowds like mice; and at the joints of them, and the reach of them, where the weight sat in each, how the shoulder met the arm, how the knee bent.

"Whoever painted these knew bodies," she said at last. "These aren't made up. Look at the broad ones' knees. That's a knee that's carrying twice what ours carry, and it's built for it, the whole leg's built for it, the way a draught horse's leg is built. And the tall ones: look how the hip sits. Look where the weight goes when that one reaches." She shook her head. "Nobody who was making it up would think to do that. You'd have to have watched them walk."

Ward went round looking for weapons. He went all the way round, all three sheets, every figure, and came back to where he had started, and stood there.

"There aren't any," he said.

"Any what?" said Saban.

"Weapons. Not one. Anywhere." Ward looked at the wall. "Tools. Baskets. That hammer, that swage. Nobody's holding anything you'd hold to kill a man. Nobody's fighting. The beast's not fighting." He was quiet a moment. "A people who painted everything, every season, every job, every kind of them. And no fight. Anywhere on it."

Nobody answered that.

Saban asked what they traded. He asked it of nobody, looking at the tall ones carrying their baskets of pods down out of the branches, and the ones on four legs with things on their backs. "Where did it go? All that. The seed. Who did they sell it to? Who came for it? You don't grow that much of anything for yourself." He looked round. "Who were they trading with?"

Nobody could answer that either.

And Armand stood in the middle of the room, in front of the tree, with his hands on his stick, and looked at the wall for longer than anybody; and Emeric watched him, because Emeric had wondered for a long time what Armand would see.

"I know these," Armand said at last. He said it slowly, with a kind of pleasure, like a man meeting old friends in a strange town. "Some of them. Not all. That one." The beast. "And those, with the ears. And the little ones. They're in the old books. The children's books; the ones with the pictures. I had one as a boy, with that one in it," the beast again, "curled round a tower. And on the old maps, the very old ones, at the edges, where the drawer didn't know what was there. That's where they put them. At the edges." He smiled a little. "I have one of those maps. On my shelf. With that one on it, in the sea, off a cape that's been drawn wrong for three hundred years."

"Were they people?" said Zale.

It was the first thing he had said in the room. His voice was still a whisper, and it was the only voice in there that sounded like it was hurting.

Armand turned his head and looked at him.

"People?"

"Peoples. Kinds of people. Like us. Other than us." Zale nodded at the tall ones. "Were they real, and other than us?"

Armand looked at him, and then at the wall, and then back at Zale, with his old face perfectly courteous and perfectly puzzled, the way it went when a student asked him a question in a language he did not speak.

"Other than what?" he said.

Zale opened his mouth, and shut it again.

Nobody explained. Emeric did not; he did not know how. He watched the old man stand in front of the wall with his hands on his stick, looking at it with pleasure and with nothing else at all, the way a man looks at the pictures in a book he had as a child: not puzzled by them, not frightened, not curious, only glad to see them again; and he felt the question go past Armand like a bird past a window, and not come in.

Then he told them about the heart. In words, only. He did not build it. He stood by the wall and told them: the shaft, and the level, and the stair going up into the weight; the tree as wide as a house, six-faced, dead, not long; the granary swept; the last panel drawn and not painted; the crack in the pale roof, and the pick head, and the mark on its haft. He told it in order, the way Ward told a road, and when he had finished nobody said anything for a long time, and Ferra took the pick head out of the bag at her side, where she had carried it into the room because she carried everything, and it was there, on the shelf by the door, rusted, with the tower and the line under it burned into the stub of its haft; and they all looked at that instead of at the wall.

Ferra's sheets went into the box that night. The numbered sheet from the hall, twenty-three outlines and eleven empty oblongs; and the sheet from the heart, of the panels, with the last one drawn in outline, the way it had been; and her own notes, of the bare places, and the edges, and what she had seen in them.

She would not burn a record. She had said so a hundred times. She would not leave it lying about either. So it went into the box, under the silk with Amara's stones in it, in with Keller's copies and Armand's readings and the Vantic papers and Ansel Vey's two books, the fair one and the last one, side by side in their oilcloth; and she locked it, and put the key on its cord round her neck, where it always was.

Emeric watched her do it from the door.

"Everything we can't say," he said. "In one box."

"Everything we can't say," Ferra said, "and everything that's true." She stood up. "They're the same box. They usually are."

Keller's letter was in the next morning's bag.

It came up with the rest, in the ordinary bag, in Keller's own hand, which was small and dry and slanted a little backward like a man leaning away from what he was writing. It was short. Keller's letters were always short.

My lord, it said. For your information, and on no instruction of mine or anyone's. His Eminence the cardinal Augustine has this week asked the chancery, in writing, by his own clerk, for fair copies of every quarter's rolls returned from your barony since the settlement, each man with his parish; and for a copy of the entrance list of your island school, as printed. The request is in order. The chancery will comply. I thought you would wish to know that it had been made. I have the honour to be, et cetera. Keller.

He read it twice. Then he put it down on the long-room table, flat, and put his hand on it, and sat there with the morning coming in at the windows and the sound of the city waking up below, and the dome on the palace's crown with the light coming down through it onto a tree a forearm high, and did not move for a long time.

Every quarter's rolls. Every man with his parish. Honest paper, every line of it true, signed and sent. And the entrance list, as printed: three hundred copies, one at the cross, one in the school's book, one in Ferra's, one gone north. Aubrey, second son of the lord of Esterby, north riding. Eighth.

He had printed it himself.
