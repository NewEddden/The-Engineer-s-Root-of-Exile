Chapter 239 - Cold Water

There was ice in the eddy under the water gate on the last morning of November, a thin grey skin of it along the edge of the stone where the current did not reach, and the class stood on the steps above it in the dark and looked at it.

Sixty of them. Kit Bastow had a cold and Imani had sent him home before he got to the steps, laughing. The rest stood on the water gate's stair in their shirts and breeches with their boots off and their feet on the frosted stone and their arms wrapped round themselves, sixty young people who had been told the night before to be at the water gate at half past six without breakfast and had come, every one, because Ward had said so in the particular voice.

On the bank below the stair, on the gravel where the river ran out of the town past the water gate and on toward the bridge, the medic had a fire going. A good one, driftwood and coal from the works, built up the evening before and banked and blown up again at five. She was one of Imani's, a broad steady woman from the lower ward who had been a laundress until the ward took her, and she had a stack of blankets on a trestle by the fire and a kettle on a stone, and she stood at the water's edge with her skirts tucked up and a boathook in her hand and the face of a woman who had pulled men out of worse.

Imani was at the top of the stair with her basket.

She had the twists in it, sixty of them, each with a name in pencil: a portion of Maren's Band oatcakes from the locked press, weighed on Ottoline's balance now, not Ferra's letter scale, each one different by the body and the last reading. Rylan had taken his already. He stood on the bottom step with his shirt off and his breath going up white in the lamplight and the flush coming up his neck from the oatcake, looking at the river as if it had personally insulted him.

"Eat," said Imani.

They ate. They sat on the stair, at her word, for her count of a hundred, while it came up in them. Emeric watched it come; he had seen it at the split days all summer and still watched. The flush. The breath going deep. The eyes going bright. Sixty young bodies on a cold stair filling up from inside past what they would carry sitting down, with nowhere to put it.

That was the point. Nowhere to put it but in.

"Two minutes," said Imani, at the end of the count. "Not one breath more. I've a glass." She held up the sand glass from the ward, the two-minute one she timed pulses by. "When it's run, you come out, whether you've found anything or not. If you've found it, you'll know. If you haven't, you'll come out blue and shaking and go to the fire and try again tomorrow. And if anybody stays in past the glass to prove something," she looked down the stair, "she'll pull you out with a boathook, and I'll send you home for a day, and Ward will let me."

Ward, at the top of the stair behind her, said nothing very loudly.

"Found what?" said a levy man, halfway down.

Rylan, at the bottom, turned round.

"The fire," he said. "Inside. A knight on a winter road doesn't freeze. You've seen Ward ride in out of the snow with his coat open and steam coming off him like a horse. You've seen us." He rubbed his bare arms. "We don't cast it. We don't think about it. The body's full and it's cold and it spends the full on the cold, inward, where spending costs nothing, and you get warm." He looked at the river. "You're full. You'll be cold. Let it spend."

"How?"

"I don't know how," said Rylan, honestly. "I didn't know how the jump was either. It's like the jump. You'll feel it or you won't." He turned back to the river. "Watch the duke. He's going first."

Emeric had not told anybody he was going first. He had not told Rylan; he had not told Ward. He had come down the stair in his shirt and his breeches with his boots in his hand, and put the boots on the step, and taken his shirt off, and Rylan had seen him do it and drawn the obvious conclusion out loud in front of sixty people, which was Rylan all over.

He went down the last steps onto the gravel and into the river.

It was cold.

That was all it was, for the first breath: cold, the whole of him, at once, the knees and then the waist and then, when he went down to the shoulders in the eddy under the stair, everything, a fist closing round his chest and squeezing the breath out of him in one long gasp that he could not stop and did not try to. His heart went up like a startled horse. The ice at the eddy's edge touched his shoulder and he did not feel it, because there was nothing left in him to feel anything with but the cold.

And then he did what he had done at four miles in the Hod's hold in August, standing in the rimed iron dark with the Band pouring into him: he let it go in.

Not out. Not a glyph; not a thread of fire at the end of a finger. In. Into his hands and his feet and his face and the fist round his chest, the way a knight does without thinking and a mage never learns because a mage has never needed to; and he was warm. Not warm. Not cold. Level. The river round him was the river, and the cold was in it, and he was in it, and the cold came at him from every side and met something coming the other way and stopped there, at his skin, like weather at a window.

He stood up to his chest in the Kaldo river at dawn at the end of November and breathed.

In the other life there had been a man who did this, he remembered. Who sat in ice in front of cameras and climbed mountains in his shorts and taught people to breathe a certain way so that the cold could not reach them, and half the world had called him a fraud and the other half had paid to sit in his ice baths, and Marcus had watched it on a screen at two in the morning and thought it was mostly nonsense with a little true thing in the middle. Here the little true thing was the whole of it. Here it worked for a reason, and the reason was sitting on a stair in sixty twists of paper.

The sand glass ran. He came out.

He came up the gravel past the medic's fire without stopping at it, and steam went off his shoulders into the lamplight in a long white plume, and the class on the stair watched it go; and he stopped at the foot of the stair and looked up at them, and did not say anything, because there was nothing to say that the steam had not said.

Rylan went in next, and came out steaming. Then Imani turned the glass and said, "First ten," and the first ten went.



Most of them did not find it.

That was the first morning. Most of them went in, and gasped, and stood in the river with their arms round themselves and their teeth going and their faces going from red to white to a colour like a bruise; and the glass ran; and they came out blue and shaking and stumbling on the gravel with their feet gone dead, and the medic threw blankets at them and put them at the fire and poured tea into them from the kettle, and they sat on the gravel round the fire with the blankets over their heads like a row of grey tents, steaming only from the tea.

Arthur found it. He had been finding things first all autumn and had stopped being surprised by it, and he went in and stood still, and at about the minute his breath slowed and his shoulders came down from round his ears and he looked, from the stair, like a man standing in a warm bath wondering what all the fuss was about; and he came out steaming and walked past the fire, as Emeric had, and stood at the foot of the stair, and did not look at anybody.

Mira found it at the end of her two minutes. She had gone in with her plait over her shoulder and her teeth set and stood with her eyes shut, very still, the way she had stood in front of the stable wall in July looking at the slate with the chip in it; and just before the glass ran Emeric saw her shoulders go loose, all at once, and a thin wisp of steam go up off her wet hair into the cold air, and her eyes open in astonishment.

Bren saw it too. He was beside her in the water. He did not find it.

Noll Finch was the last out.

He was the last into the water of his ten, because he had stood on the bottom step looking at it a long while; and in the river he had not found anything, and had stood with his eyes screwed shut and his thin arms wrapped round his thin chest and shaken so hard that the medic took a step toward him with the boathook before the glass was half run; and when Imani called the glass he did not come out. Not because he was proving anything. Because his legs had stopped answering him. He stood in the river up to his chest and tried to walk and his legs did not do it, and his face, under the lamp, went from blue to a kind of dull white Emeric had seen on the faces of the men the levy had carried in off the Stream Ground; and the medic waded in to her knees and put the boathook through the back of his breeches and walked him out of the river like a sack.

He sat at the fire under three blankets with his teeth going and tea spilling down his chin, and stared at the river.

"Again tomorrow," said Imani, crouching beside him with her fingers in his wrist.

Noll nodded. He could not speak. He nodded again, harder, so that she would know he meant it, and Imani wrote something in her book and went on to the next.



On the Tuesday Mira found it in the first half minute, and stayed.

She did not stay past the glass. She stood in the river with her plait down her back and steam going up off her shoulders like smoke off a field in the morning, and her eyes open, looking at the far bank, quite calm, while the levy men on either side of her gasped and shook; and when the glass ran she turned and walked out, and past the fire, and up the gravel, and stood at the foot of the stair beside Arthur, and they did not look at each other, and both of them were steaming.

Bren was beside her in the water again. He had not found it on the Monday. He did not find it on the Tuesday either, for the first minute, and Emeric, from the stair, watched him watch Mira find it, and saw his jaw set.

When the glass ran, Bren did not come out.

He was not shaking any more. That was what made Emeric go cold on the stair in his coat: Bren had stopped shaking, which he had read about in the other life in a book about men who went into the sea, and it was not the good kind of stopping. Bren stood in the river with his arms down by his sides and his lips the colour of a slate roof and his eyes on the far bank where Mira had been looking, and his chin up; and he was not finding anything. He was standing there because she had.

"Bren," said Imani, from the top of the stair.

He did not turn round.

Imani came down the stair. She did not run; Imani never ran on a ward. She came down the frosted steps two at a time with her skirts in one hand, past the class, past Emeric, past the medic with her boathook coming up, and straight into the river in her boots to her knees, and took Bren Tallis by the collar of his shirt with one hand and turned him round as if he were a child of six and walked him out of the water.

He came. His legs did not work very well. She walked him to the fire and sat him down on the gravel and put her fingers in his wrist, and counted, with her lips moving, and her face did not change, and then she put two blankets over him and the medic's tea in his hands, and stood up.

"Two and a half minutes," she said. "And a bit."

Bren looked at the fire. His teeth had started again. That was good, Emeric knew; that was the body deciding to fight.

"Ward," said Imani, without turning round.

"Home," said Ward, from the top of the stair. "For the day. Not the yard. Not the plant floor. Home." He paused. "You will come back tomorrow and you will come out at the glass."

Bren did not say anything. He sat at the fire under his blankets with the tea shaking in his hands and looked at the river, and Emeric could see in his face the whole of it: the shame, and the anger, and under them both something that had nothing to do with either, which was that he had stood in a river for half a minute longer than he could stand it because a carter's daughter from Foulard Street had stood in it for two minutes and found something he had not, and he would rather have died in it than come out first.

Mira had come back down from the foot of the stair. She stood on the gravel a little way off with her own blanket round her and the steam still coming off her hair, and looked at Bren, at the fire.

She did not say anything to him. After a while she took her blanket off and dropped it on the gravel beside him, and went back up the stair in her wet shirt, steaming, without looking back.

Bren looked at the blanket until the tea in his hands had stopped steaming. Then he picked it up and put it over the two he had.



On the Wednesday, Noll was not the last out.

He had not found it on the Monday or the Tuesday. He had gone in both days and shaken and come out at the glass, the second day on his own legs, just; and he had sat at the fire both days with his tea, and both days, Emeric saw, he had watched the ones who came out steaming, not with envy but with great attention, as he had watched the chipped slate in July, like a man who expects to be asked to draw it from memory.

On the Wednesday he went in with his ten and stood with his eyes open.

That was the only thing Emeric could see that was different. He did not shut them. He stood in the river with his arms round himself and his teeth going and looked at the far bank, where Mira had looked; and at the minute nothing happened; and at a minute and a half nothing; and then, just before the glass, his arms came down from round his chest. Slowly. As if he had not told them to. And there was no steam off him, not much, a thread, off the top of his cropped head; but he stopped shaking, and it was the good kind of stopping, because his face was not white. It was red. He was standing in the river with his face going red.

The glass ran. He walked out.

He walked out on his own, up the gravel, with the water running off him, past the fire; and he looked at the fire as he passed it, and at the medic with her blankets, and kept walking; and stopped at the foot of the stair with Arthur and Mira and the dozen others who had found it by then, and stood there, red, dripping, with a thread of steam coming off his head, and his thin chest going in and out.

The last one out of the river that morning was a levy man from Grayrock, big as a door, who came out blue and swearing and went to the fire, and was given his tea, and drank it.

Noll stood at the foot of the stair. He did not look round at anybody. He did not need to; they were all looking at him. Emeric saw Rylan, on the bottom step, look at him, and then at Ward, and Ward did not look back, which was Ward saying a great deal.

Imani wrote something in her book. Then she came down the stair to Noll, and stood in front of him, and put her fingers on the inside of his wrist.

"Seventy," she said. "Strong."

Noll looked at her.

"That's all," said Imani. "Seventy, and strong. Go and get your breakfast." She took her fingers away. "You'll go in again tomorrow."

"Yes, Mistress."

"And you'll come out at the glass."

"Yes, Mistress." Noll's voice went, at the end of it, up into the boy's register it had been leaving all autumn, and he went red to the hair, redder than the river had made him, and turned and went up the stair two at a time with the steam coming off him, and nobody laughed.



The building trades had gone indoors for the winter that week, as the plan said they would. No more trenching until March: the ground would freeze and a trench opened in December was a trench that would heave and crack in February. So the plumbers and the masons and the wiremen went to the builders' yard behind the works, where Linus had put up three long sheds in October with stoves in them, and made in the warm what would go into the ground in the spring: doors in runs of forty, window frames by the hundred, stair strings, sinks off the pan kiln, pipe cut and threaded and laid in racks by length. Ferra's count on the noon tape went from houses to parts, and she said at the long table that she liked it better, because a door did not argue.

And in the long shop, at the bench at the end where nobody went who did not have to, the guitar was finished.

It had taken a month, in the evenings, by a great many hands, and nobody but Emeric and two others had known what it was until the last week. That had not been a secret. Nobody had asked.

Fletcher had made the body. He had been given a drawing, a shape like a figure eight with its waist pinched in, deep, with a round hole in the top, and he had looked at it a good while and said that it was a box, and asked what wood, and been told a hard one for the sides and the back and a soft one with a straight close grain for the top; and he had gone away and come back a week later with maple for the sides, bent on his own steambox round a former he had cut for it, and a back of the same in two halves joined down the middle so the grain mirrored, and for the top a piece of pine from the old tithe barn's roof, the same barn that had given the Hod's racks, two hundred years seasoned and so straight in the grain that Fletcher had held it up to the light and said it was too good for whatever this was. He had braced the inside of the top with thin ribs in a cross, because Emeric had drawn the cross; and then, without being asked, he had shaved the ribs, an evening at a time, tapping the top with a knuckle and listening and shaving again, until it rang to him; and he had not said what he was listening for, and Emeric had not asked, because Fletcher had built boats for thirty years and knew more about how a thin board sounded when you knocked it than anybody in Kaldo.

"A box with a neck," said Fletcher, when he handed it over. "For a noise."

"Then it'll make a good one," said Fletcher, and went back to the K Discovery's shed, and did not ask.

The neck was maple too, with a steel rod down the middle of it under the fingerboard that Bram had made and that could be screwed tighter, at the head, against the pull of the strings; and the fingerboard was a dark close wood from the coast that Saban had found in a chest of the old baron's that nobody had opened in thirty years. Bram drew the frets: brass wire, hard, a little T in its section so that the foot went into a saw cut in the board and the top stood up above it, rounded. And the strings. Steel, the thinnest of them, drawn through the forge's dies until it was as fine as a hair and as hard as a spring and sang when Bram plucked it on the bench, which made him jump and swear; and for the three heavy strings, a steel core with a fine bronze wire wound round it, close, in a coil with no gaps, that Ottoline had done on her little lathe with a feed she rigged out of a clock's train, and that she had been very pleased with and had not said so.

Ottoline made the tuners, too. Six of them. A brass worm on a shaft with a small key on the end, turning a brass wheel with a peg through its middle that the string wound round, so that a turn of the key moved the peg a hair and the string held where it was put; six in a row down the head, three each side. She made them out of her own clock stock in two evenings and brought them to the long shop in a twist of paper and laid them out on the bench in a row, and turned each one with her thumb, and looked at Emeric over her magnifying glass.

"What's it for?" she said. "I've made six things that turn a peg a hair at a time against a pull, and I can't think of anything in the world that wants that but a clock."

"Music."

Ottoline looked at the tuners a while longer.

"Hm," she said, and put the twist of paper back in her pocket, and went home; and Emeric heard afterward from Ferra that she had gone to the census hall and sat at her table till midnight with a pencil, drawing a thing that turned a peg a hair at a time, and then a thing that plucked a string, and then given it up.



The frets were Ferra's.

He had not meant them to be. He had meant to do them himself, at the counting room table one evening in the middle of November, with the neck on the table in front of him and the scale's length scribed on it, and the sum on a sheet of paper; and Ferra, at her own end of the table, had watched him do it for a quarter of an hour without saying anything, and then got up and come round and stood behind his chair and read the sheet over his shoulder.

"What are you doing?"

"Where the frets go."

"Why's it a sum?"

"Because each one's closer to the next than the last was." He put his finger on the neck. "A string makes a note. Stop it at half its length and it makes the same note again, higher. That's the twelfth fret. Halfway." He moved the finger. "Between the nut and halfway there are all the others, and every one of them takes the same share of what's left of the string. Not the same length. The same share. So they get closer together as they go up."

Ferra looked at the sheet.

"What share?"

"About a part in eighteen. Less a hair." He showed her the figure; he had it from a page in the other life, a woodworker's handbook, where some man who made instruments had written it down to three places. "Seventeen and eight hundred and seventeen thousandths. You take the whole length, divide by that, and that's the first fret from the nut. Then take what's left and do it again."

"Again and again."

"Up to the top."

Ferra pulled out the chair beside him and sat down in it. She took the pencil out of his hand, without asking, and took a clean sheet, and wrote the length at the top of it, and began.

She did it faster than he had. She did it in her small square hand, a column of figures down the page, each one the length left, divided, carried, the next; she did not write any of the working that he had written, because she did not need it, and she did not stop, and in a quarter of an hour she had twenty figures in a column, one for every fret, from the nut to where the neck met the body. Then she laid her sheet beside his, and ran her finger down both at once.

At the fourteenth she stopped.

"You've carried a one," she said, "that wasn't there."

He looked. He had. In the fourteenth line, a slip of a digit in the second place; and every line after it was wrong by the same small amount, compounding, so that by the twentieth his fret would have been a hair and a half from where it ought to be.

"It's a hair and a half," he said.

"Would it be wrong?"

"It would be out of tune. Up there. A little." He thought of it, a note at the top of the neck a little sour, and every time he played it for the rest of his life he would know why. "Yes. It would be wrong."

Ferra crossed his sheet out. One line, corner to corner, the way she struck anything; and pushed hers across to him.

"Use mine," she said.

He used hers. He scribed every fret on the neck from her column with Ottoline's best rule, and Bram cut the slots, and when the frets were in and the strings on and the first chord rang, at the top of the neck, where his own figures would have been a hair and a half out, it was true.

She did not ask what it was for. She had known since the kitchen. She had written a heading in her book, he suspected, the night of the burned sugar, and not shown him.



Tess wound the coils.

That was the other half of it, and the half he had wanted most and had expected least to get. He had drawn them for her on the back of a works order: two long thin bobbins of fine copper wire, wound thousands of turns, with six short steel slugs standing up through the middle of each, one under each string, and the slugs magnetized. A string of steel moving over a magnet moved the magnet's field, and the field moving through the coil made a current in it, very small, that went up and down exactly as the string went up and down. That was all. It was the dynamo, small enough to fit under a string; it was the telephone, that turned a voice into a current. He had told her so, and she had looked at the drawing and said, "Then it's a microphone for a wire," and he had said yes, exactly that, and she had wound them in the winding room in an afternoon each, on the little machine she wound telephone coils on, counting the turns on its counter. The slugs she magnetized on the field of the big dynamo in the engine house, holding them against the pole pieces in a pair of brass tongs while the needle on the board went over, and came back to the winding room with six steel slugs that would pick up a nail.

"What's it for?" she said, too.

"Noise."

Tess looked at the two bobbins in her hand, and at the guitar on the bench with its strings on and its round hole, which she had walked past four times that week without asking about.

"A microphone for a wire, under a string," she said slowly. "That turns the string into a current. And then the current goes..." She looked at him. "Where?"

That was the hard part, and it was the part he could not do properly, and he had known it from the first drawing. In the other life the current would have gone into a box of glass bulbs with a third wire in them that made a small current into a large one. He had no such bulbs. He had thought about it for a week, and in the end he had gone to the telephone.

The telephone line north was too long to talk down without help; the voice faded before it got anywhere. At every relay post along it, as at Lisle's, a pair of stones took the line's whisper and gave it again stronger; and Tess, who did not like anything on her line that a dead zone could put out, had spent the autumn building the other way on her bench to go beside them: a telephone's receiver, with its iron diaphragm, set face to face against a telephone's microphone, with its little button of carbon grains, and a battery of jars driving a current through the carbon. The faint voice coming down the wire shook the receiver's diaphragm; the diaphragm pressed the carbon; the carbon, pressed and let go, let the battery's current through it in great swells and ebbs that went up and down exactly as the faint voice went; and the swells went on down the wire to the next post a great deal louder than the voice had come in. It hissed. It made a voice sound as if it were talking from inside a barrel of gravel. But it worked with no stone in it anywhere, and that was the point of it.

He asked her for one of those, and a big receiver with a brass horn on it, the horn off one of the old speaking tubes from the works' office.

Tess looked at him for a long breath.

"You want to make the guitar talk down the north line," she said.

"I want to make it louder."

"It'll sound like gravel."

"I know," said Emeric. "That's the sound I want."

Tess did not believe him. He could see her not believing him. She built it anyway, in a box, in two evenings, the repeater and a battery of jars and the big receiver with its horn; and the first time she put the guitar's coil across it and he touched a string, the horn screamed.

It screamed with no string touched at all. A high thin howl that climbed and climbed in the long shop until Bram, at the far end, threw a rag at it; and Tess sat in her chair with her hands over her ears and her face screwed up, and then, quite suddenly, took her hands down.

"It's talking to itself," she said.

She was right. The horn's noise was going back into the guitar's top, and shaking the strings, and the strings were going into the coil, and the coil into the box, and the box into the horn, and round, louder every time, like the cold air in Bram's column getting colder. She turned the horn away. She turned it to face the wall, and stuffed a folded sack into the guitar's round hole so the top would not shake so easily, and moved the box the length of the bench away; and the howl dropped, and dropped, and stopped.

He touched a string.

It came out of the horn. Loud, and rough, and buzzing at the edges like a hornet in a jar, and not at all like the string; like a string heard through a wall, shouting. Bram, at the far end of the shop, put down his file.

Tess sat and looked at the horn.

"It sounds terrible," she said.

"Yes."

"You said that's what you wanted."

"It is," said Emeric, and he played the string again, and then two together, and the horn growled at the long shop like a dog behind a gate; and Tess looked at his face, and then at the horn, and then at his face again, and began, very slowly, to laugh.



He played it for them on the first of December, in the cellar, after the second bell.

The cellar because the rock kept everything in. Nothing that happened in the forge room had ever been heard in the keep above it, not the forge's hum or Bram's swearing or the night the bank came out; and he did not want the castle hearing this, the first time, any more than he would have wanted the castle watching him learn to walk. He carried the guitar down in a case Abloh had made for it from the felt off the flying suits, without being told what it was for, and Tess's box and the horn on a handcart down the canal shaft's ramp, and set them up by the bench under the lamp with the forge cold in its frame and the tank glowing faintly at its slit in the niche; and sat on the bench, and waited.

They came down together, a little after the bell. Ferra had her book under her arm out of habit and put it down on the bench at once, as if she had not meant to bring it. Imani had nothing. She sat down on the cellar step, on the third one, above the fourth, and folded her arms on her knees; and Ferra, after a breath, sat down beside her.

They looked at the guitar on his knee.

"That's it?" said Imani.

"That's it."

"It's a box," said Ferra.

"Fletcher said that."

"Fletcher was right." She looked at the neck. "Those are my frets."

"Those are your frets."

He tuned it. He had tuned it already, that afternoon, in the long shop with Ottoline's keys and his own ear and the sound of the strings against each other; but he tuned it again, because his hands wanted something to do, and because he was, he found, very nearly as frightened as he had been at nineteen in a corridor in another world with a borrowed guitar on his knee and a girl at the end of it who had not looked up.

Then he played.

He played the first of the four. It was the first thing he had ever learned, that winter: a bright quick song with four chords in it, the same four round and round, that every boy in every college corridor in the world he had come from had learned first, because it was easy and it was about a girl and it made people want to sing along whether they knew the words or not. His fingers knew it. They knew it better than he did; they found the chords before he thought of them, and the strings rang under the vaulted rock, bright, much brighter than he remembered, in the cold stone room.

And he sang it. He had not meant to. The words came with the chords, as they always had, and they were in the other language, the one he thought in when he was not watching himself, the one nobody in this world had ever heard him speak for longer than a word under his breath; and he sang it to the two of them on the cellar step in the language of a country that did not exist, badly, a little out of breath, with his face going hot.

They did not move.

He finished it. The last chord rang under the rock and went on ringing a while, as things did in that room.

Nobody said anything.

"That's what it sounds like," said Imani at last. "Your other tongue. When you say it all at once."

"Yes."

"It's ugly," said Imani. She did not sound as if she minded. "All the edges on the front of the words. Like a man chopping wood." She tilted her head. "Say something in it. Not singing."

He said something in it. He said that she was the bluntest woman in two worlds, and Ferra the most exact, and that he had not known when he got up that morning that he would be sitting in a cellar at midnight with a guitar trying to impress them; and Imani listened to it with her head on one side, as she listened to a chest.

"What did you say?"

"That you're blunt."

"That's more words than blunt."

"It's a language with a lot of words."

Ferra had not said anything. She was looking at his left hand on the neck, on her frets.

"What was it about?" she said. "The song."

"A girl."

"They're all about a girl," said Imani. "You said so in the kitchen."

"This one's about a girl who's leaving on a bus in the morning and a boy who won't ask her to stay." He thought about it. "A bus is a cart. A big one. For everybody."

"Does he ask her?"

"No. That's the song."

"Then it's a stupid song," said Imani.

"It is a stupid song." He played the four chords again, quietly, round once. "Everybody loved it."

He played the other three. Quick, all of them, bright and silly, the songs of a second winter in a cold building far away when he was nineteen and thought four songs might change his life. One about a dog. One about a car, which he did not explain, and which had a part in the middle where everybody in the corridor used to shout, and he shouted it, alone, in the cellar, and Imani put her hand over her mouth. One that he could only half remember the words to and sang the other half in nonsense, and Ferra knew he was singing nonsense, he could see it in her face, she had heard enough of the language now to know when it stopped making shapes; and he did not care.

At the end of the fourth Ferra said: "Did it work?"

He looked up.

"The girl," said Ferra. "Down the hall. The one who played. Did she hear you?"

"Not once."

"Not once," said Ferra. She sat on the step with her chin in her hand and her face perfectly straight and her eyes doing what they did when she was enjoying a column. "A whole winter. Four songs. A bus."

"She went home in the spring."

"On the bus."

"On a ship, I think. I never asked."

Imani laughed. Not the dry one; the other one, the one that came out of her on the floor of a ship over the sea the first night, helpless, and had to be let finish; she put her forehead down on her folded arms on her knees and shook with it. Ferra did not laugh. She sat beside Imani with her chin in her hand and looked at him across the cold cellar with an expression he had seen on her face exactly once before, in Bram's kitchen, under the bulb, looking at the meter.

"It's working now," she said.



"You said electric," said Imani, when she could. She lifted her head off her arms. "In the kitchen. You said there was a kind with a wire. Tess has been telling everybody in the works there's a box that screams."

So he plugged it in.

He coupled the guitar's coil to Tess's box, and the box to its jars, and set the horn on the bench facing the far wall, the way Tess had set it, with the folded sack in the guitar's round hole; and the box began to hiss, a faint steady gravel in the horn; and he touched a string.

The cellar roared.

It came out of the horn and hit the far wall and came back off the vault and the rock and the forge's frame and the iron jacket of the tank in its niche, all at once, from everywhere, a great rough buzzing growl that was the string and was not the string, that shook in the chest; and the forge's little glass window in the chamber door hummed in its frame, a thin high hum on top of the growl; and Ferra put both hands over her ears and her mouth fell open; and Imani, on the step beside her, sat straight up with her hands flat on her knees and her eyes very wide.

He played the car song on it.

He did not even try not to. He played it the way it had been played in the other life, loud and rough and too fast, with the strings bent and the chords crashing into each other, and the horn snarled it off the rock like a bear in a barrel, and the window hummed, and the tank glowed at its slit, and somewhere in the middle of it Ferra took her hands off her ears and began to laugh, out loud, helplessly, and could not stop; and Imani did not laugh at all. Imani sat on the step with her eyes wide and her hands flat on her knees and her lips moving very slightly, and he realized, playing, that she was counting. Not his pulse. The beat. She was counting the beat of the song, the way she counted a heart, and her foot, on the fourth step, was moving with it.

He stopped. The growl went on a breath after his hand left the strings, and then the hiss, and then he reached over and pulled Tess's wire out of the box, and the cellar was silent, ringing.

"A hundred and forty," said Imani.

"What?"

"To the minute. That song." She looked at the horn as if it had done something to her. "That's fast. That's a man running."

"That's what it's for."

"Again," said Imani.

Ferra wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist. "Not again," she said. "My father lives behind a forge, not on the moon. He'll hear it through a mile of rock."

"He won't."

"He'll hear it in his bones," said Ferra. "He hears everything in his bones." She got her breath. "Not again. Once was enough to be impressed."

"You were impressed?"

"I was deafened," said Ferra. "I'm entering it as impressed."



He took the sack out of the round hole, and set the wire aside, and sat on the bench with the guitar on his knee and played the last one quietly, without the horn.

He had not decided to. It was the one his fingers went to when the others were done, the way they had gone to it in the other life late at night in a cold room by himself, when there was nobody in the corridor to impress and nothing to prove: a slow song, in three, low, a lullaby nearly, that every child in the country he had come from had sung once a year in the dark of the year with a candle in its hand, standing next to somebody, whether it believed a word of it or not.

He did not sing it. He played it, slowly, once through, and then again, the strings soft under the vault, and the notes going up into the rock and staying there.

Nobody said anything while he played it. Ferra had stopped laughing. Imani had stopped counting. They sat on the step side by side in the lamplight, and at some point in the second time through, Imani's head went down onto Ferra's shoulder, which he had never once seen it do, and Ferra let it, and did not move.

He finished it, and put his hand flat on the strings, and the cellar was quiet.

"What's that one?" said Ferra.

"A midwinter song. From home."

"About a girl?"

"No." He looked at the guitar. "About a night. A quiet one. Everybody sang it, once a year, in the dark, the longest nights. With candles." He thought about saying more, and did not; it was not the night for the rest of it, and he did not know yet whether there would be one. "My family sang it. Badly. My father sang it worst of anybody I ever heard."

"Worse than your mother?" said Imani, from Ferra's shoulder.

He looked at her. He had two mothers, he thought, and two fathers, and she had just asked about both and knew it.

"Worse than either," he said.

Imani did not lift her head. She lay against Ferra's shoulder with her eyes half shut and looked at him across the cellar, at the guitar on his knee and his hand flat on its strings.

"Play it on the day," she said.

"What day?"

"Whatever day you sing it, at home," said Imani. "When it comes. Play it then. Here, or anywhere. For us." She shut her eyes. "And not the car one."

Ferra did not say anything. She sat with Imani's head on her shoulder and her own hands folded in her lap, very still, looking at him; and then she reached down beside her on the step, without looking, and found her book where she had put it, and opened it on her knee one-handed, so as not to move her shoulder, and turned to a page near the back.

He could not see what was on the page. He could see her write two words, small, and close the book.

"What did you write?" he said.

"Arithmetic," said Ferra. "I'll tell you when I've finished it."
