Chapter 390 - Lantern Night, Year Seventeen

The band played for the first time at three in the afternoon on Lantern Night, in the Motion Shop, with the doors shut and the frames pushed back against the walls, and it was the worst noise Emeric had heard since Bram's first boiler.

It wasn't the instruments' fault. The instruments were good. Bram had built the drum kit in the winter in the Works' carpentry shop, in the evenings, without telling anybody what it was for until Bren saw it: a kick drum out of a cut-down beer cask with a ship's drumhead stretched over the end, a side drum with a coil of fine wire under the bottom skin that rattled when you hit the top, two cymbals of spun bronze that Bram had spun himself on the big lathe and then stood listening to, ringing, for a long time. He'd built Mira's bass too, the long-necked four-string with the body cut from one piece of Milford walnut, and had made her hold it three different ways while he measured her arm. The amplifier was Ambrose's, out of the radio work: a valve set in a steel case the size of a bread oven, with a dial on its face and a red lamp that came up when it was warm, and two wooden cabinets with paper cones in them taller than Tolliver, that Tess's people had wound the coils for. Abloh had made felt covers for all of it, against the salt, and was standing by the door now with his arms folded watching to see if anybody spilt anything on them.

And Emeric's guitar, the one with Tess's two pickups in it that had howled like a dog through a brass horn the first time and growled like a dog behind a gate the second, and that he'd played perhaps twenty times in the years since, alone, with the horn turned down.

It was the players. That was Ferra's diagnosis, and she gave it from the stool by the bench where she sat with her book open and a pencil, keeping the list.

"Again," she said. "From the top. Arthur, that's not a chord, that's an accident."

"It's the chord on the sheet."

"It's the chord on the sheet played by a man wearing gloves."

"I'm not wearing gloves."

"Then I don't know what to tell you," said Ferra, and wrote something down.

Arthur was the lead. That had been settled in January by Arthur, who'd come to the Motion Shop one evening after a night watch and asked if he could hold the guitar, and then asked if he could hold it again the next night, and the next, and had been practising on the K Amara's night watches ever since with the amp unplugged and his fingers bleeding, and was bad, and wouldn't stop. Emeric could play better. Emeric could play the four songs he'd learned one winter as a boy on the other side of everything, and could play them perfectly, because if he'd once seen a thing done clearly he could make his own hands do it again, moving his own fingers from the inside like a man working a puppet; he'd called it the Strings once, as a joke, at this bench, and it had stuck. But copying a thing wasn't knowing it. He could play the four songs exactly as he'd seen them played and couldn't have changed a note of them to save his life, and he knew it, and he'd said so the first night, before anybody else could. Arthur was learning. By the end of the voyage, Emeric thought, Arthur would be the one who could actually play. That was the joke, and Arthur didn't know it yet, and it would be very good when he did.

Bren on the drums was steady. He'd always been steady; he kept time on drill like a clock. Mira on the bass was quiet and very sure, the part you felt in your chest more than heard, and seemed to like that. Zale had his squeezebox and Finn the concertina, and between them they had all the sailors' songs Zale had brought back from seven years at sea and a good many he'd made up on the way, and they kept wanting to play those instead of the sheet, which Ferra kept writing down. Tolliver had the tambourine, because it was the only instrument nobody minded him dropping, and he dropped it twice in the first hour, and both times everybody cheered.

Imani had refused. She sat on the bench by Ferra with her feet up on a crate and a cup of tea, watching, and when Bren held out a pair of sticks to her she looked at them as though he'd offered her a dead fish.

"No," she said.

"Go on. You'd be good."

"I'd be very good," said Imani. "That's why no." And drank her tea.

They played the first of Emeric's four songs four times and it got a little better each time, and then much worse, and then Bren broke a drumstick, and they stopped to argue about it, and in the middle of the argument Wren, who had been sitting on the end of Tess's bench all afternoon with her chin on her knees, listening, and hadn't said a word, said, "You've got no words."

Everybody looked at her.

"It's a song," said Wren. "You keep playing it like it's a song and there's nobody singing it. There's a hole in the top of it where somebody ought to be." She unfolded herself off the bench. She was twenty and taller than Tess now, long-legged, with oil on her wrists that never quite came off and her hair tied up in a scarf. "Play the other one. Zale's. The one about the girl in the harbour who wouldn't wait."

Zale looked at her.

"That's not on the sheet," said Ferra.

"I know," said Wren. "Play it anyway."

Zale played it. He played it on the squeezebox the way he played it on the K Amara's deck at night, slow, and after the first verse Finn came in on the concertina under him, and Mira found the bottom of it on the bass, and Bren came in very soft on the side drum with the brushes, and Emeric, after a moment, found where it went on the guitar and played under all of them, quiet, the amp barely warm.

And Wren sang it.

She stood in the middle of the Motion Shop's floor between the frames with her hands at her sides and her oily wrists and her scarf, and sang Zale's song about the girl in the harbour who wouldn't wait, in a voice nobody in that room had ever once heard her use. It wasn't big. It didn't need to be. It went up into the Motion Shop's roof among the hanging chains and the half-built arms and filled it, clear and low and true, a little rough at the bottom, the way a voice sounds that's only ever sung in an empty workshop at night when nobody's there; and she didn't look at any of them while she did it. She looked at the far wall.

When it finished nobody played anything for a moment.

Tolliver dropped the tambourine. Nobody cheered.

"Where," said Arthur, "in the name of everything..."

"In here," said Wren. "At night. When I'm doing the joints." She shrugged. "Tess knew."

Everybody looked at Tess. Tess, at her bench, on her stool, with her stick against her knee, didn't look up from the relay board she was soldering.

"I've been listening to it for four years," she said. "You lot never come in at night."

"Well, that's it, then," said Bren. "That's the band. That's the singer."

And then the room remembered, all at once, the way a room remembers a thing it's been carefully not saying, and went quiet again, a different quiet.

Wren was staying. Wren was on the stays-home sheet in Ferra's book between Tolliver and Tess, with the Motion Shop beside her name and the frames' joints and Sorrel's table of offsets and her own two motorcycles, and had been since November. In a fortnight the band would go a thousand miles west and its voice would be here, on the end of a bench, doing the joints.

Arthur took the guitar strap off his neck, slowly.

"Then we'll be bad till we're back," he said.

"You'll be bad anyway," said Wren. She looked at him, and her mouth went sideways. "You'll be bad when you're back. But you'll be less bad." She sat down again on the end of Tess's bench and put her chin back on her knees. "I'll teach you the words. You can sing them yourselves. It won't be the same."

"It won't," said Zale.

"No," said Wren. "It'll be yours." She looked at Ferra. "Put that on the list."

Ferra wrote it down. Emeric read it upside down, later: 31 Mar, first rehearsal. Bad. W. sings (nobody knew but T.). W. stays. They'll be ours.



Lantern Square was full by dusk.

There were more lanterns than anybody could count, and Ferra had tried. The paper shop had sold out by Saturday. The clerks' table by the fountain had six clerks at it, writing names for the ones who couldn't, though there were fewer of those every year, and Doran in his old brown coat stood at the end of the table with the first candle in his hand, as he did, talking to somebody about the price of something, as he did. The steps of the records hall filled from the top. The Milford carts stood at the top of the square with people standing up in them. Ivo's shop had its doors shut and Ivo outside it in his hat.

Emeric stood on the top step with a lantern in his hand and didn't write on it for a long time.

He'd written the same three names every year since the first. Harl. Haddon. And a third, small, on the inside of the paper where it faced the flame, that nobody had ever read and nobody ever would. He wrote those three again now. And then he held the pen over the paper a while longer, and thought about the stair, and the frame, and the small boy who had sat on the bottom step and who wasn't dead and wasn't in his head either.

You didn't write the living on a lantern. That was the rule. He knew it. He put the pen down.

Below him on the steps, Sela was writing Tam, the way she did every year, in her schoolmistress's hand, very upright, without hurrying. She'd done it on this step every year there had been a Lantern Night. She didn't look up while she did it, and nobody on the steps watched her do it, which was how the steps had always helped.



Zale walked her down from the bay house at dusk, along the shore line, with her arm through his.

It was the way she walked with him now, on land. She'd started it in the autumn without saying anything about it, on the tram platform at the Works one wet evening when the step was slick, and had simply never stopped, and he had never once mentioned it in case she did. Her hood was up. The lamps were coming on along the sea wall one after another ahead of them, and up in the town the first lanterns had begun to go, early ones, children's, wobbling up off the roofs into a sky that was still a little green in the west.

About halfway, by the pilots' steps, she said, "Your heart is fast."

"It isn't."

"It is. Since the gate."

"It's a hill."

"It's flat." She didn't look at him. "All the way. Ferra says the shore line is flat. It's why the trams like it."

Zale walked on for a few yards. A man going the other way with a lantern under his arm touched his cap to them both and then, a step later, looked back.

"You can't hear a man's heart through his coat," he said.

"I don't hear it." She tightened her arm a little in his, so that her forearm lay along the inside of his. "You forgot I'm of water. You are water. In a skin. Mostly." She said it the way she said the names of fish, as a plain fact about the world that it would be odd to argue with. "All of you, the dry people. Water in the blood, and water round the blood, and in the eyes. When it moves, I feel it go. Like the swell under a boat. Close. Like this." She moved her arm against his, very slightly. "Your swell is fast. Since the gate."

"Then you've got it wrong."

Ilenne stopped walking. She turned and looked up at him from under the hood, which was not very far up, and her eyes were perfectly serious and her mouth was not.

"You forgot I'm of water," she said again. "I don't make errors when it comes to water."

He didn't have anything to say to that. He'd been in the water since he was four, on every kind of water there was, and he had never once won an argument with it either.

She seemed satisfied. Her throat went faintly blue under the edge of the hood, and she walked on, and he walked on beside her because his arm was attached to her, and his heart went on doing exactly what it liked.

At the top of the shore line, where the lane turned up between the chandlers' into Lantern Square and the noise of the square came down it like warm air out of a door, she stopped again.

"They're writing the dead up there," Zale said. "You can keep your hood up. Nobody will mind."

She thought about it. Up the lane the lanterns were going up off the fountain in threes and fours, and the light of them came down the walls.

"Ferra says the names go up where everybody sees them," she said. "So they're seen." She put both hands up and pushed the hood back off her hair. "Then I'm seen too. It's polite."

"It's going to cause a scene."

"Good," said Ilenne. "Then nobody looks at your heart."



Emeric saw the square notice before he saw her.

It went across Lantern Square the way a gust goes across a field of barley: a quiet, starting at the corner by the chandlers' lane and spreading, people half turning with their lanterns in their hands and their pens stopped, and then not quite looking, and then looking. A child on somebody's shoulders by the clerks' table pointed with its whole arm, and its father took the arm down and then, Emeric saw, looked himself.

She came into the lamplight on Zale's arm with her hood down, and the first thing anybody saw was how tall she was.

That was always the first thing, and Emeric had stopped seeing it about a year ago, the way you stop seeing the height of a door you go through every day. Six feet of her, long and straight, level with Zale's ear, in the grey cloak, open, and barefoot on the cobbles the way she always was. The lanterns going up off the fountain caught her skin and did something to it that the lamps in the long room never had; it took the gold of them and gave it back with the pearl underneath, silver-blue where she moved, so that she looked lit from somewhere inside, the way the sea looks at the edge of a lamp. Her hair was down her back, nearly black, heavy, tied once at the nape. The gill slits lay shut along both sides of her neck, four thin lines under each ear, and in that light they looked less like anything a person ought to have than like something a jeweller had done on purpose. She walked through the middle of Kaldo's Lantern Night as if she'd been walking through it all her life, unhurried, with her chin level, and the square opened for her without being asked.

He looked at it and thought, with a kind of distant surprise: oh. Of course. And then went back to looking for Ferra, who'd gone down for more candles.

On the step below him, Bren had stopped with his lantern half lit and the match burning down toward his fingers.

"Is that..." he said. "That's..."

"That's her," said Arthur. He hadn't stopped anything. He'd simply stopped. "Hood down."

"I've seen her a hundred times."

"You've seen a hood a hundred times. And the water. You've seen her in the water." Arthur watched her go past the clerks' table, past Doran, who took his eyes off the price of something for the first time all evening. "She's..."

"Beautiful," said Bren, and then, because the match had got there, "Ow."

"She's the most beautiful woman in this square," said Mira.

She said it the way she said a bearing off a chart, plainly, sitting on the step with her bass case between her knees and her chin on her fist, watching Ilenne cross the square; and then she seemed to hear it, a moment late, and the two of them were looking at her.

Bren put his burnt finger in his mouth and took it out again.

"Well," he said. "Well, well."

"Don't."

"We never knew," said Arthur gravely, "that you were a refined gentleman. Like us."

"A man of taste," said Bren. "A connoisseur. All these years on the Hod, and we never knew you could appreciate such a sight."

"I've got eyes," said Mira. "Unlike you two, I can use them without my mouth falling open."

"Her mouth didn't fall open," Arthur told Bren.

"No," Bren agreed. "It went the other way. It went sort of soft."

"She's gone red, look."

"It's the cold," said Mira.

The two of them looked at each other with enormous delight, because that was Zale's, and everybody in Kaldo knew it was Zale's, and nobody in Kaldo had believed it once in a year. Mira saw them see it. She put her face down on her fist and said something into it that Emeric, two steps up, was glad not to have heard properly; and then her eyes went away from all three of them, down the steps and across the corner of the square to the paper shop's trestle, where somebody in a knitted jacket was arguing about the price of a lantern, and stayed there a moment, and came back. Emeric didn't think anything of it. He'd think about it later, a great deal, but not then.

Over by the watch house, where the fire company had its two pumpers drawn up in the lane with their hoses flaked out ready, because a square full of paper lanterns with candles in them going up over the town's roofs was a thing Osric had refused to call anything but a fire drill since the first year he'd had the company, Osric himself was standing on the running board of the near pumper with a speaking trumpet under his arm, watching the lanterns cross the roofs. He'd stopped watching the lanterns.

"Is it rude," he said, to Tess, who was sitting on the pumper's step with her stick across her knees, "to look?"

"Yes."

"Everybody's looking."

"Then everybody's rude," said Tess. "Watch your roofs."

Osric looked at his roofs.

Imani had come up the steps beside Emeric with her own lantern, written and not yet lit. She stood there with it and watched Ilenne stop at the edge of the clerks' table, and Zale go on to the fountain, where his brothers were, and Ilenne stay where she was, alone in a little cleared ring of square with everybody not looking at her.

"She is, isn't she," said Imani.

"Is what?"

"Beautiful." Imani said it slowly, as if she were reading it off a chart she'd only just been handed. "I've had my hands on every inch of her for two years. I took a piece of a fork out of her back. I know where her ribs are better than she does." She shook her head. "And I've never once thought it. Not once. She was always dying, or drying out, or angry with me about something. You don't look at a patient. You look at what's wrong with her." She watched Ilenne turn her head toward the fountain, the long line of her neck in the gold. "There's nothing wrong with her tonight."

"No," said Emeric.

Ferra came up the steps behind them with a fistful of candles and stood on Imani's other side and looked where they were looking.

"Oh," she said. "Well. Yes. Obviously." She looked at Emeric. "You didn't look."

"Look at what?"

"Exactly," said Ferra, to Imani, and Imani laughed.

"I looked," said Emeric. "I saw her. She's tall."

"She's tall," said Ferra. "The whole square's stopped writing its dead, and the king of Randillia's considered opinion is that she's tall."

"I was looking for you."

"You were looking at your lantern."

"Then I was looking for you." He took one of the candles out of her fist, and then her hand, the one with the rest of the candles in it, wax and all. "I've been looking for you since about eight minutes after I got here. You're very hard to find. You keep going down for candles."

Ferra looked at him for a long moment with her mouth pressed together, trying not to let it do anything.

"That," she said to Imani, "was very bad."

"It was terrible," said Imani. "Look at you."

"I'm not doing anything."

"You're doing it with your whole face," said Imani, and took the other candles.



The brothers were at the fountain.

He didn't see them come together. He saw them there, when he looked down from the steps between two lanterns going up: Zale in his sea coat with a lantern he hadn't lit, and Corbin beside him in his good grey coat with his hands behind his back and no lantern at all, the two of them standing at the fountain's rim a little apart, the way men stand at a rail, looking at the square and not at each other. And then Waylon, coming through the crowd to them from the clerks' table with Alina on his arm and three lanterns in his other hand, because Waylon always brought too many.

Emeric went down.

He didn't mean to listen. He meant to go to them, and then he was at the fountain's other side, behind a family from the lower ward with a pram, and they hadn't seen him, and he stopped.

"You'll hold it," Zale was saying. He said it to the square, not to Corbin.

"I'll hold it."

"A year. Most of a year."

"I've held worse for longer." Corbin was quiet for a moment. "Badly. I'll hold this one better."

Zale turned the unlit lantern round in his hands.

"I left because of you," he said. "The first time. You know that."

"I know."

"Seven years at sea. Because I'd seen what you were turning into, at the palace, and I couldn't stand in the same hall as it." He looked at the lantern. "I'm going again. In a fortnight. I'll not be back for most of a year, and when I am, I might not stay." He paused. "It's not because of you. Not this time. I wanted you to know the difference."

Corbin didn't say anything for a while. Behind them a lantern went up off the fountain's rim and hung there a moment, and then went, slowly, turning, over the roofs.

"I know the difference," Corbin said at last. "I've had three years to learn it." And then, drier: "She's very bright when she looks at you. Your mermaid. Has anyone told you?"

Zale stared at him.

"It's the cold," he said.

"It isn't," said Corbin.

Waylon arrived between them with the lanterns. He looked from one to the other, and seemed to understand most of it without being told, the way Waylon did, and held out a lantern to each of them.

"You haven't got one," he said to Corbin.

"I've nobody to write on it."

"Then write somebody else's," said Waylon. "Everybody's got somebody else's." He held it out until Corbin took it. Then he looked across the fountain, past the pram, straight at Emeric, and raised his eyebrows a quarter of an inch, and Emeric went round the pram and stood with them, and the four of them stood at the fountain's rim with three lanterns and Alina, and nobody said anything about families, or crowns, or what any of them wanted or didn't, which was right. That was for later. Corbin wrote something on his lantern, after a while, with Waylon's pen, shielding it with his hand, and lit it from Zale's when Zale lit his, and let it go. Emeric didn't see what he'd written. He didn't try to.



Ilenne didn't write a name.

She'd stood at the edge of the square for a long while after Zale left her for the fountain, with her hood down and the whole square not looking at her, watching, with her colour doing slow careful things at her throat. She'd asked Ferra in the morning what it was for. Ferra had told her: the dead, their names on the paper, and the paper lit and sent up, so that they went up into the sky where everybody could see them go. And Ilenne had thought about it, and nodded, and said that it was a good thing, and that her people did a different one.

So when Zale came back to her from the fountain, she took his hand and walked him out of the square, down the shore line toward the bay, past the lamps, to the end of the pilots' row where the bay house stood with its garden wall and its gate to the shingle; and Emeric, who had been sent after them by Ferra with a lantern, a candle and a pen because Ferra had a feeling, followed at a distance, and stopped on the shingle when they did.

She took the lantern from him without a word. She didn't write on it. She lit it, and held it in both hands a moment, and then went down the shingle to the water's edge and crouched there, in her cloak, with the tide coming in round her bare feet, and set it on the water. Not up. Down. On the sea.

It floated. The tide took it, slowly, and turned it, and it went out across the bay on the black water with its little light shining up off the swell, away from them, west, toward the mouth.

"My people's dead go down," she said, to Zale, without turning round. "Not up. Into the deep. Where it's dark and quiet and nobody is hungry." She watched the light go. "This is for all of mine. In the thin water. Who came after me and didn't..." She stopped. Her colour went, briefly, a deep sad dark at the collarbone, and then softened. "And for Sela's Tam. And the king's Harl. And the dead of the dry people who are in the sea. There are many. More than you think." She stood up. "Now they have one that goes down."

Zale stood beside her on the wet shingle and watched it go.

"Fortnight," he said.

"Fortnight."

"Then home."

Ilenne turned and looked at him in the dark. Her throat had gone that faint deep-water blue, the one she couldn't hold still, the one she'd told him in the Band was the cold.

"Then home," she said. "Your home also. I said. My mother said." She did the turned-up hand, very small, at the level of her waist, where nobody but him could see it in the dark; and Emeric, thirty yards up the shingle with an unlit lantern, looked away at the bay, and walked back up to the town without being asked.



Maeve's lantern wouldn't light.

He found her on the records hall's lowest step with Mira, when he came back up, both of them crouched over it with their heads together: Maeve in Mira's knitted jacket, which she'd been wearing since December and nobody now remembered was Mira's, striking matches one after another and swearing at them in the court voice she used for swearing, and Mira holding the paper steady and laughing at her.

"It's damp," said Maeve. "It's a damp lantern. They've sold me a damp lantern."

"It's not damp. You're holding the match upside down."

"I'm holding it the way it goes."

"Give it here," said Mira, and took Maeve's hand with the match in it, the whole hand, and turned it round, and held it there under the wick until it caught, the two of them with their hands together round a little flame on a step in the dark; and the lantern filled with light, and they both looked at it, and then at each other, and then, a little quickly, at the lantern again.

Emeric, three steps up, stopped.

He didn't know what he'd seen. He knew he'd seen something. It was the thing he'd heard come out of himself at the ride-up in October, on the lighter's deck, when Maeve had said next time about Mira's suit: the noise Bram made at his daughters' young men at the Works' gate. Something in him had pricked up its ears, the way it did, and gone looking for the reason.

He looked round for it.

Bren was on the step above Mira, with a lantern of his own, grinning down at the two of them with the cheerful idiot face of a man who has just watched his squad leader light his king's sister's lantern for her. He saw Emeric looking. His grin faltered.

"What?" said Bren.

Emeric looked at him for a long, level, kingly moment.

"Nothing," he said.

"You're looking at me like Bram."

"I'm not."

"You're looking at me exactly like Bram. I've seen Bram do it to the lads at the gate." Bren looked down at Maeve and Mira, who were standing up now with Maeve's lantern between them, and back at Emeric, and his face went through bewilderment and alarm and arrived somewhere near offended. "Majesty, I have never in my life..."

"I didn't say anything."

"You didn't have to," said Bren, with feeling, and went off up the steps with his lantern held out in front of him like a man warding something off; and behind him, at the foot of the steps, Maeve let go of her lantern and it went up, steady and bright, over the square; and Mira watched it go with her hand on Maeve's elbow and didn't take it away.

Ferra, on the top step, had seen all of it. She wrote something in her book. She didn't let Emeric read it.



They went up to the dome at eleven, the three of them, when the last of the lanterns had gone over the walls and the square was emptying and the paper shop's man was sweeping candle ends into a bucket.

They'd talked about where. Ferra had wanted the dome, because the tree was there and the tree had been at the beginning of all of it, and because Ilenne's people did it in the water before witnesses and the dome was as near as Kaldo had to a sea with something old in it. Imani had wanted their own rooms, with the door shut and the stove going and no witnesses at all, not even a tree, and her own bed under her. They'd done it the way they did most things: they went to the dome, and sat a while under the tree with his mother's body asleep on her mattress against the trunk and the made one gone home to the castle for the night with Harrold, and the glass full of stars over them, and the hum; and then they went home.

The stone was his mother's.

That had been Imani's idea, and Ferra had agreed at once, and he'd sat with it for a day before he could say yes. It was the little grooved stone he'd carried in his pocket since he was a boy without knowing what it was, that had been his mother's before that, and that he'd had cut at last and strung on his band as a bead among the others, where it had held the delve room for him for twelve years. A control stone. Old, and small, and the only one any of the three of them had ever carried that nobody had given them for a job. Ilenne said the stone was the door and not the store; that it kept no piece of anybody; that her people kept theirs afterwards like a ring. He'd had it on his wrist for twelve years. It could go on being on his wrist.

He unstrung it from the band at the table in their room, with the stove ticking and the lamp low, and held it out on his palm.

Ferra put her hand over it. Then Imani. Three hands, one stone, in the lamplight, his under, theirs on top, Ferra's freckled and ink-stained at the second finger, Imani's brown and square and steady, the fingers laced.

"Nothing lost," said Ferra. It was half a question.

"Nothing lost. It's closed. You send, you take back. Each of us to each of us." He'd said it before. He said it again, because she'd want to hear it in the room. "If anything feels like it's going out and not coming back, stop. I'll feel you stop. We'll all stop."

"I know," said Imani. "I read Armand's notes. Twice." She looked at him across the three hands. "Stop telling us. Do it."

He laughed. He couldn't help it. And they were both looking at him, Ferra with her chin up and Imani with that look she had on the ward at three in the morning when a thing had to be done and she'd decided to be the one who did it, and he stopped laughing and shut his eyes, and opened.



It wasn't like a delve.

He'd thought it would be. He'd delved both of them before, a hundred times, for the work, through the bands, with their consent, the way the six did: going in, looking, coming out. This wasn't going in. It was like opening a door and finding them both already on the other side of it, opening theirs.

The stone between their hands was nothing. A doorframe. He felt it the way you feel a threshold under your foot, and then he was through it, and so were they, all three at once, and there was a moment where he didn't know whose hands were whose on the table.

He sent the piece.

He'd been afraid of that, a little, since the small room. Since he'd knelt by the stool and found a piece of himself in a stone that didn't know him. He'd been afraid that this would feel the same: a thing cut off and sent away. It didn't. It went out of him toward Ferra the way warmth goes out of your hand into somebody else's when you hold it, and as it went, something came back. Something of hers. It came back up the same road his had gone down, and it was tied, and so was his, both ways, and nothing was cut, and nothing was gone; his piece was in her and still his, and hers was in him and still hers, and he could feel the line running between them like a held breath.

And to Imani, the same. And Imani to Ferra, and Ferra to Imani, and the whole thing closing, the three lines of it pulling up tight into a ring with the stone in the middle of it like a knot.

A closed loop. He'd drawn it on paper for them in October. It didn't feel like the drawing.

It felt like this: Ferra, all at once, from the inside. Not her thoughts. He'd been told that and he'd believed it and it was true; he didn't hear a word. But he felt her the way you feel a room you know in the dark. A hard clean edge to everything, like a ruled line. A great many columns, all of them balanced, and the small fierce satisfaction of each one coming out. And under that, very faint, the way a smell brings back a place, something he'd never seen: a page of figures in a hand he knew, on a desk too high for her, and the lamp too far away, and somebody's voice in the next room, and the feeling of being very young and getting a sum right and nobody coming to see.

And Imani. Warm. Steady. A ward at three in the morning with the lamps low. Hands that knew where everything was. And under it, faint, a girl on a bed with a burn on her arm who wouldn't sleep, and a voice counting her down, one, two, three, slow, and it was Imani's voice, and it was Imani's memory, and it was in him now, and it would be in him, faint, the way it was faint in her, for as long as either of them lived.

He felt them find him.

He felt it happen, the way you feel someone's hand find yours under a table: Ferra first, quick and direct, going through him like a clerk through a ledger; and then Imani, slower, careful, narrow, the way she went over him with her sight. They went through the rooms. They didn't mean to; it was what the sharing did, at the start, he'd been told, a house opened to people who'd never been let all the way in. The lathe. The dome. The cottage. The counting house window, and Ferra's own face at it, and he felt her see herself through him and go, briefly, very warm. Imani's hands. The yard. His mother on the gravel.

And the stair.

He felt them both come to it at once. He felt them stop.

The palace. The tower. The long stone stair going up to his mother's door with the afternoon light coming down it. Every edge. The worn place in the stone. The wax on the banisters. And the bottom step, and nothing on it. A frame with the picture taken out. A place where a room had been, the edges all there, the shape of a small boy kept perfectly, and nobody in it.

Ferra stood at it. He felt her stand at it. He felt her reach, the way you'd reach out to straighten a picture on somebody else's wall without thinking, and find nothing to straighten, and take her hand back.

Imani didn't reach. She'd seen it before, in February, from outside, with her sight. She stood at the edge of it, now, from inside, and looked at it for a long time, and he felt her understand that this was what they'd carry: not the boy, because the boy was gone, but the shape of where he'd been. They'd each have it now, a little, the way they each had everything of his a little. A gap with his brother's outline. And none of the three of them could put anything in it, ever, because the thing that belonged there was dust in a small room off the long room, and the only person living who remembered the boy on the step was the boy.

He felt Ferra's anger at it, sharp and brief, like a pen snapped.

He felt Imani's grief, which was slower, and went deeper, and didn't stop.

And then he felt them both, at the same moment, turn from it, the way you turn from a grave when there's nothing more you can do there; and turn to each other.

That was the part he hadn't been able to tell them, because he hadn't known. He knew now. He felt it go between them, across the ring, Ferra to Imani and Imani to Ferra, a piece of each in the other, tied both ways; and it wasn't what it was between either of them and him. It was nothing like it. It had no heat in it and wasn't trying to. It was the thing that had begun on the Rowena trip, when two women who had each wanted the same man had decided, between themselves, to want him together instead; the thing that had made them stand side by side at Saint Edwy's in two dresses; the thing that made Imani take the book out of Ferra's hand on Lantern Night and put it in his pocket so that Ferra would have to put her hand in after it. It was that, made into a line, and tied. Two people who would never once in their lives want each other, and who would each, now, always know where the other was.

He felt Ferra feel it and be astonished, and then furious at being astonished, and then not.

He felt Imani feel it and not be surprised at all.

And then the three lines pulled up tight, and the ring closed, and held, and he opened his eyes.



The lamp was where it had been. The stove was ticking. The stone was a little warm under their three hands and kept nothing; he could feel that it kept nothing.

Nobody said anything for a while.

Then Ferra took her hand off the stone, and looked at it, at her own hand, and turned it over, and then looked at Imani across the table, a long, strange, level look, as if she were seeing her for the first time and was going to have to do some accounts about it.

"Oh," said Ferra. "Oh, that's very irritating."

"What is?"

"I know where you are." Ferra pointed at Imani. "You're there. I know you're there. I've known you were there for sixteen years, I can see you, you're a yard away, and now I know it. Here." She put her hand flat on her own breastbone. "As if I'd counted you."

Imani considered this, with her head on one side.

"You're cold," she said. "Your feet. They've been cold all evening. I can feel them."

"I am not going to have you diagnosing my feet from across a table for the rest of my life."

"I'm afraid you are," said Imani, and smiled, slowly, the whole of her face.

Ferra looked at her a moment longer. Then, quite suddenly, she laughed, and put both hands over her face, and laughed into them, and Imani reached across the table and took one of the hands away from her face and held it; and Emeric, sitting between them with his mother's stone warm on his palm, felt the two of them, one on either side of him, the way he'd feel his own two hands, and felt the gap in himself where the stair was, and felt them both feel it, and not look away from it.

"Is it all right?" he said. "Is it..."

"Stop asking," said Imani.

"Stop asking," said Ferra, through her fingers. "You'll know. You'll always know, now, you great fool. That's the whole point. We'll never be able to tell you we're fine when we're not." She took her hands off her face. Her eyes were wet and very bright. "Nor you us."

"Nor me you," said Emeric.

He strung his mother's stone back on the band. It took him three tries, because his hands weren't steady, and Imani did it for him in the end, and tied it off with a surgeon's knot, and put the band back on his wrist where it had been for twelve years. Then she stood up, and pulled him up by the band, and Ferra stood up on his other side, and between the two of them, with a great deal of unnecessary pushing, they got him across the room.

It was a while after that. A good while. The bed was solid and old and creaked, and the stove ticked and went down, and Imani said at one point that if he so much as thought about the pull she'd have him on a diet, and at another, much later, nothing at all, and Ferra laughed into his shoulder with her hair coming down round both their faces, red in the last of the lamp; and he found, with the two of them knotted round him and the ring of the three of them pulled tight and humming under everything like the tree, that he could feel what they felt, not the words, never the words, but the warmth of it and the want, and that they could feel his, and that there was nothing at all to hide and nobody wanted to. It was very undignified. It was the best night of his life.

Afterwards they slept, both of them, at once, the way they always did, Ferra across his chest and Imani in the curve of his arm with her face in his neck and her cold-footed, warm-handed weight on his side; and he lay awake, the way he always did.

He didn't mind. He lay in the dark with their two bodies on his, and their breathing, and the shared heat, and for the first time he could feel them sleep: not just hear it, but feel it, two slow tides going out in him, one on either side, very far down and very quiet. Imani dreamed of something, briefly, a ward, a lamp. Ferra didn't dream at all; she went down into sleep the way a stone goes into a pond. And under both of them, if he went looking, the stair, and the frame, held now in three people instead of one, and still nobody in it.

He went to sleep at last a little before four. When he woke at seven they were both up and dressed and looking at him from the table, rested and pink and smug, and Ferra had already written it in the book, and Imani said, "You look dreadful," with great satisfaction, and handed him a cup of coffee.



The Catch went at ten past six that morning, while he was asleep.

Fletcher had taken the raft out from South Sentinel in the rowing launch at first light, with the yard's rejects in it, two dozen cracked stones lashed under a red tarpaulin, the way they'd done it last June when they'd let the boat go out the first time; and cut it loose half a mile up the Pull, and rowed hard for the slack water under the cliff; and the Catch had felt the raft come into its line, and turned to it the way a sleeping dog's ear turns, and taken it. It had spent itself in a quarter of a minute. Fletcher said the water over the reef had gone flat as a table and then slid, and the raft had gone in at the pen's mouth like a cork into a bottle, and then nothing, and the gulls had come back.

The island's set rang the line room at half past six. The line room rang the keep at seven, while Imani was pouring the coffee.

Ferra took it standing, at the window, with the handset to her ear and the book open on the sill under her other hand, and listened, and said, "Spent? All of it?" and listened again, and said, "Thank you, Fletcher. Tell the boys," and put it down.

She stood at the window a moment longer. Then she turned the book round on the sill and wrote, and Emeric got up off the bed with his coffee and came and read it over her shoulder, with Imani's chin on his other shoulder reading it too, and the three of them, without any of them saying so, knowing exactly where the other two were.
