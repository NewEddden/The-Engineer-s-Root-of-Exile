Chapter 100 - Inventory

Paxton's letter came on the third of December, by hand, in the coat of a Milford drover who had ridden two days to bring it and would not give it to anybody but the prince.

He was a big slow man with a face burned dark by the weather and a sheepskin over his shoulders, and he stood in the back room of the exchange with his hat in both hands and dripped on Ferra's floor until Emeric came down from the keep. Then he took the letter out of the inside of his coat, from a place where it had been sewn in under the lining, and cut the stitches with his own knife, and gave it over.

"He said to put it in your hand," the drover said. "Not the window. Not the clerk. Yours."

"Thank you."

"He said you'd know what it was." The drover looked at the letter in Emeric's hand as if he had wondered all the way from Milford what it was, and had decided not to know. "He said to wait for nothing. There's no answer."

He went. Ferra had a cup of the spirit put into his hand at the door, which he drank standing up in the square in the rain, and then he got back on his horse and rode out of the South Gate the way he had come.

Emeric stood in the back room with the letter.

Paxton had told him, in the gate yard in August, that he was not going to write him letters. It was a single sheet, folded twice, sealed with plain wax and a thumb. He broke it.

It was not a letter. It had no greeting and no ending and no sentence in it anywhere. It was four lines, in a square, heavy, careful hand, each line beginning with a date, as a steward begins the lines of a stock book.

The twenty-second of November. Two wool carts, Milford's, with their loads, taken on the western road nine miles from the coast. Carters beaten and left in the road. Horses taken.

The twenty-third. Ninety-one head of cattle driven off the western grazing toward the hills. Two herdsmen hurt, one of them since dead.

The twenty-fourth. The barn at the boundary stone, burned to its walls, with the barony of Kaldo's two hundred sacks of wheat and Milford's winter fodder.

The twenty-fifth. Dunnel, Milford's factor, and the boy who drove for him, dead on the coast road a dozen miles short of the coast house. The cart burned.

And under the four lines, with nothing between: Paxton, of Milford.

That was all of it.

He read it twice. Then he put it down on Ferra's trestle in front of her, flat, the right way round for her to read, and she read it, once, slowly, with her pen in her hand and her pen not moving.

When she had read it she did not say anything.

She got up and went to the shelf and took down the year's book and opened it on the trestle beside Paxton's sheet, at the end of November, where on four pages in her own hand were four lines she had written as they came through her window. Milford wool carts, two, taken on the western road. Heard from the coast buyer. Paxton's border barn burned. Heard from Hollis's cousin. The carrier dead on the western road. Heard from Saban's man. Milford's western herds driven off. Heard from the hide seller. Each with the date she had heard it, and not the date it had happened, because nobody had told her that.

She laid her pen across the year's book, beside the four lines. Then she reached past him to the other end of the trestle, to the long narrow book she kept for the twin year, the index, and opened it at the line for the fifteenth of November, the one with the word in its last column, and set it down beside the other two.

Three books in a row. She still had not said anything.

He looked at them.

The fifteenth of November, the crown's copy of the report, into the bag at the post house on the north road, a little before ten in the morning, with the gate clerk's slip to say so. Two pages in it about Milford. And then nothing. Then, seven days after, the twenty-second, and the twenty-third, and the twenty-fourth, and the twenty-fifth. Four nights. Four nights in a row, every one of them Milford's, every one of them on Milford's western side, the side nearest the coast and furthest from Kaldo, and the last one Dunnel, who carried Kaldo's twin in his bale.

It had come to him in four pieces, out of order, over a week, from four mouths, each with its own small sensible reason. The western road had been bad since the plague. Hungry men burned what they could not carry. A factor with a fat purse on a lonely stretch in November. He had taken each piece as it came and put it where it seemed to go, and none of them had had a date on it, and so none of them had stood next to any of the others.

Paxton had put dates on them. That was all Paxton had done. He had not written a word of what he thought. He had written down what he had lost, in the order he had lost it, and sent it two days by hand in a drover's coat, and said there was no answer.

"Ward," Emeric said.

Ward came from the door, where he had been standing since the drover came in, and looked at the three books on the trestle for some time without bending to them.

"How long from the post house to the crossing?" Emeric said. "For the bag."

"Three days. In the winter, three. The bag changes horses at Wayford."

"And from the crossing to the far side of Milford. To the western grazing. For men on horses, who know the way."

Ward thought about it. He thought about it as he thought about every road, with the miles laid out somewhere behind his eyes, and the fords, and the places a horse would want water.

"If it is read at the crossing the day it comes," he said, "a man rides to Sethe's hall that night. A day to find the men and send them. Three days' ride to the western grazing, round Milford's north, on the hill road, where nobody would see them go by." He said it as he would have said the weather. "They would be there on the evening of the twenty-first. They would wait for dark."

The twenty-second.

Nobody in the back room said anything for a while. Rain ran down the window. In the square somebody was arguing about the price of candles.

"Fifty items," Ferra said at last. She said it to the books, quietly, in the voice she used for a total. "Fifty items prove letters are disappearing." She put one finger on the line in the index for the fifteenth of November. "One proves somebody's reading them."



He had thought the letters were being burned.

He had thought that for a year. He had built the whole of the twin year on it, the slips and the fold and the nick in the seal and a pair of letters going out every week, and every one of them had been built for a court, for Keller's cold eye, to show some day that Kaldo's letters had gone into the post and not come out of it. Lost. Thrown in a stove by a clerk who was paid to throw things in a stove, or tipped into a ditch by a thief looking for coin. He had said so to Ferra, and to Saban, and to himself, and believed it, because it was what the facts had looked like, and because it was easier than the other thing.

Nobody burned a letter and then rode three days to the far side of Milford to burn a barn.

Somebody had read it. Somebody at the crossing had opened Kaldo's November report to the crown, and read the two pages about Milford, and understood what they meant: that Paxton of Milford, who had answered Sethe's letters about the weather for fifteen years, had warned Kaldo in the spring of the war, and stood his men off by an understanding, and was Kaldo's. And then the report had gone wherever the others went, and four nights later Milford had begun to burn.

Sethe still thinks Milford is his, Paxton had said. It's the only thing I own that he hasn't priced. I'd thank you not to spend it for me.

He had spent it. He had spent it in an afternoon, at his own table, telling himself what Paxton had meant, and Ferra had written it in her book with the date, and he had read it upside down and gone on. And the price had been ninety-one head and a barn and two wool carts and a herdsman he had never heard of and Dunnel, who had stood up on a moving cart to wave his scarf, and a boy of fifteen who had wanted to see an engine that did fourteen horses and had been told to come in the spring.

The twin had burned with them. It had been in the middle of the bale, deep in the fleece, because he had told Ferra to put it there.

He stood with his hands on the edge of the trestle and waited for Ferra to say it.

She did not say it. She sat with her finger still on the line in the index and her eyes on Paxton's four dates, and did not look up, and did not say anything at all. She did not need to. It was in her book, in her hand, under the date of the report. Paxton asked that it not be spent. Said so. Overruled. She did not turn back to that page, and she did not have to, and the not turning back was worse than if she had.

After a while she took her finger off the index and closed it, and put it in the box on the left of the trestle, on top of the gate clerk's slips, where it lived. Then she picked up Paxton's sheet and looked at it a moment longer, and put that in the left-hand box too, on top, flat.

"That's evidence," she said.

"It's a dead man."

"It's both." She closed the lid. "Keller won't care which. He'll care that it's dated."



Ward said it on the keep stair, going up, where nobody else would hear it.

"The crossing," he said. "We know where it is now. We know it is one room. Twenty men. Two nights' ride if we change horses. We could have the man who opens the bags in a cellar in Kaldo before Sethe knew he had gone."

Emeric stopped on the stair.

He had thought it already. He had thought it in the back room with Paxton's four lines in front of him, and the thought had come with a heat in it that he recognised and did not trust, because it was the same heat that had once walked him into a yard to name a man a thief on the strength of a column.

"It's the crown's post house," he said. "On the crown's road. With the crown's bags in it."

"Yes."

"Then the day we put twenty men through its door, Kaldo is the barony that attacked the king's post. Not Wayford. Us. And Sethe gets to write to the crown about it, and his letters arrive." He looked down the stair. "That's the war he wants. One he can call the crown's."

Ward did not argue. He had not expected to win; Emeric could see that. He had said it because it needed saying by somebody, and he was the one whose work it was to say it.

"And the man in the cellar talks," Ward said. "To whom?"

"To us. And it's worth nothing, because we made him." Emeric started up the stair again. "Keller doesn't want a confession out of a cellar in Kaldo. He wants a year of slips with dates on them. We've got ten weeks of it left." He stopped at the turn. "And now we've got one that proves it."

"Then we wait."

"We wait. And from now on nothing goes in a report that can hurt anybody but us."

Ward considered that. Then he nodded, the way he nodded when a horse was sound but he did not like its owner, and went on up to the line room to look at the board.



He wrote to Paxton that night, in his own room, at the table under the window where he had written the report.

He wrote it four times. The first was an explanation, and he burned it, because it explained, and Paxton had not asked for an explanation and would not want one. The second was an apology, and he burned that too, because it was written to make him feel better and would not make Paxton feel anything at all. The third said too much about the crossing, which he did not yet know enough to say, and could not risk putting on paper in any case, even paper carried by hand. The fourth he sent.

It said what he had done. That on the fourteenth of November he had written into his report to the crown that Paxton of Milford had warned Kaldo in the spring of the war, and stood his levy off by understanding with Kaldo, and should be read by the crown as Kaldo's. That he had done it to put Milford's defence on the crown's file before any ruling, so that Paxton should not lose his land under the law for having taken the field. That the report had gone to the post on the fifteenth, and the first of Paxton's four nights was the twenty-second. That he believed now that the report had been opened and read on the road, by someone who wished Milford harm, and that the four nights were the answer to it. That Paxton had asked him in August not to spend the one thing Sethe had not priced, and that he had said he would not, and then had, against his word and against the advice of his clerk, who had written down that she objected. That the barony of Kaldo would make good the two hundred sacks in the barn without payment, and anything else it could make good that Paxton would let it. That he did not ask to be forgiven, and would understand if Milford's gate was shut to him.

He signed it with his name, not his title.

Saban took it in the morning. He did not give it to a carrier. He read the outside of it, and looked at Emeric's face, and put on his hat, and said he would take this one himself, because there were roads into Milford from the south that nobody watched and he knew all of them, and he would put it into Paxton's hand and nobody else's, as the drover had done. He was gone before noon, on his own horse, with the letter inside his shirt.



Ferra found him in the afternoon on the wall above the South Gate, which was where he had gone because it was the place in Kaldo from which he could see the west road go down behind the first rise.

She had never come up onto the wall in the daytime. She came up now, in her old coat with the year's book under her arm because she did not go anywhere without it, and stood beside him at the parapet for some time, looking at the west road, and the bare fields either side of it, and the rise.

"Keller's year isn't done," she said.

"No."

"It's done in February. The second week." She did not open the book. She did not need to. "Ten weeks. Ten pairs, or eleven, if nothing goes wrong." She was quiet. "The next one goes on Thursday. The ore account and the quarter's tallies. Nothing in it about anybody."

He understood what she was asking. She was not asking whether. She was asking whether he would be fit to sign it.

"Yes," he said.

She nodded. She looked at the road a while longer.

"It was a good report," she said. "Every line of it was true." She said it flatly, as a fact about a document, the way she would have said that the columns added. "That was the trouble with it."

Then she went down the wall stair again with her book, carefully, because the steps were wet, and he stood at the parapet and watched the west road until the light went off it, and the lamps came on along the wall behind him, all eight.
