Chapter 100 - Tethered

They drew the fires in the outside foundry two days before midsummer, so that the floor would be cold by the night, and on the evening itself Bram's men spread the sand.

It went down a hand deep in a square forty feet on a side across the casting floor, raked level, with the four posts sunk at its corners where Bram had cut the floor for them the week before, oak as thick as a man's thigh, each with an iron ring at the top of it. When the men had gone home Bram shut the big doors and dropped the bar across them himself, and the foundry was a long dark barn of a place with the smell of cold iron in it and the lake moving somewhere beyond the wall, and four lamps on hooks, and a square of pale sand in the middle of the floor like a field under snow.

Emeric chalked a line round the sand a pace outside its edge, and stood behind it.

Imani sat on a stool by the east wall with a canvas roll across her knees, and in the roll, he knew without looking, were the splints. Armand sat beside her with the sheet on his knee and his hands folded on top of it, where Imani could see them. Bram stood at the doors. Ferra stood with him, with her book shut under her arm. She had weighed the pack that afternoon on her own scales in the keep, full and fastened, with the harness buckles through it, and written the figure at the head of a clean page, and above the figure, as she had at the head of every trial's book since the first, three words.

WHAT WENT WRONG.

It had gone wrong on every page so far. She had never once left the heading off.

Ward walked out onto the sand in the harness.

He had put it on in the pattern store with Bram's help and walked it down the bank in the dark, and he came out into the lamplight the way he walked into anything, without hurry, and stopped in the middle of the square, and let Bram's two lads clip the four lines to the four rings, two at the shoulders and two at the hips. The lines ran slack across the sand to the posts. They were long enough to let a man go up to the tie-beams and no further, and not one of them was in anybody's hand.

He was in dead plate. The capital's crystals sat in Bram's steel under the node plates with every breaker open, and nothing in the whole of it was going to help him but the stones on the outside. Twenty lamp stones in a felt-lined box on his back, charged by Orrin over four evenings. Six blanks at his shoulders and hips and heels. Three air stones, at the small of his back and on his calves. A deciding stone on his breast, under its cover, with a brass lever either side of it.

Emeric opened the medium.

They were all there in it, the way the four stones under the section had been there on the bank in the thaw: six dense strange hot places on the shape of a man, and three steadier ones, and the pack on his back like a small warm hearth.

"When you like," Emeric said.

Ward put his left hand on the left lever and moved it forward. One notch.

Nothing much happened. The six blanks took something, and the deciding stone shared it among them, and Ward stood on the sand exactly as before. He moved it another notch. Another.

"Lighter," Ward said.

He said it flatly, the way he would have said the wind had gone round. Another notch. He was standing very straight, the way a man stands on ice he does not trust, and the sand under his boots had stopped taking the shape of his heels.

Then his heels came off it.

It was not much. The width of a finger. He hung over the sand in full plate with his toes just touching, and the toes came off it too, and he was hanging, an eighth of a ton of man and steel with nothing under it, a finger clear of a floor, turning very slowly on his heel toward the east wall as though he were looking for Imani.

Nobody said anything at all.

On the morning Emeric was sent south the sky over the capital had been full of traffic, hulls going over the inner rings every one of them with a man standing up in it pouring himself away, and he had crouched by a carriage and worked out how it was done, and it had been done that way for longer than anybody could remember. Ward could not pour into anything. He could not lift a cup. There was nothing in him that could have done this and nothing on the sand but the stones.

Armand had stood up. Emeric had not seen him do it. He was standing by his stool with the sheet in one hand and the other hand held flat against his coat, as if to keep it there.

"Count," Emeric said.

"One," said Ward.

He began to sway. It came up out of his own body, the way a man sways standing on a wall, a little forward and then back, and in the medium Emeric felt the deciding stone let it go, a hand forward, because that was what he had told it to do, and then answer. Once. The shoulder nodes took a little more and the heels a little less and Ward came back upright and swayed the other way, and the stone let that go a hand, and answered again, and after the fourth or fifth time Ward had stopped swaying, because he had worked out, the way a body works these things out, that it was cheaper to stand still.

"Twenty," said Ward.

He went up. He did it himself, a notch on the lift, and rose off the sand to the height of a man, and to the height of two, slowly, with the four lines lifting off the sand behind him and hanging in long curves to the posts. He stopped there. He looked down at them, and then along the foundry at the tie- beams, and his face in the lamplight showed nothing at all.

"Forty," he said. "Push."

He put his right hand on the right lever and moved it off its stop.

The air stones woke. It was a very small sound. It was the sound of a bellows breathed out slowly by a man who did not want to wake anybody, from the small of his back and the backs of his calves, and the lamp flames nearest him leaned away from it, and Ward began to go forward.

He went forward and over. He had been standing upright in the air with a push at his back and his calves, and the push was behind him and below his middle, and it turned him the way a push at the bottom of a door turns the door, slowly, forward, onto his face. The deciding stone felt him go and answered with the shoulders, and he stopped going over, and hung there at a slant, going forward, a man lying on the air as if on a slope of water.

"Lean," Emeric said.

"Which way?"

"Whichever way you want to go."

Ward leaned. He leaned back and went up, and too far, and brought the push off and hung, and tried it again. He leaned his shoulder left and went left, and too far, and the line on that side came taut with a small sound and held him. He did not say anything. He tried it again. He did it the way Emeric had once watched the boys in the lower town learn to swim off the quay, which was badly and then less badly and then all at once, as if the water had been explaining it to them the whole time and they had only just stopped arguing with it.

By a count of a hundred he could go where he meant to go.

By a hundred and twenty he was flying a circle, slowly, under the tie-beams, the four lines swinging after him in long loops across the sand, his shoulders toward the middle of the square and the lamplight going across his plate as he went. He went round once. He went round again, a little faster, and leaned into the turn without being told, the way a rider leans a horse, and the push breathed at his back, and he came round past the doors where Ferra stood and Bram stood, and past the east wall where Imani sat with the canvas roll across her knees, and past Emeric at his chalk line, a man in the Crown's own harness going round a barn in the air at the height of a roof beam, with nobody pouring anything into him anywhere.

It was the most beautiful thing Emeric had ever made, and he was not watching it.

He was watching the pack.

It had been thinning since the first notch, which was right. It was thinning much faster than it should. He had been calling it every twenty counts, low, for Armand's sheet, and at Ward's count of eighty it read, in the medium, where the arithmetic had said it would read at two hundred and fifty. At a hundred and twenty it read where the arithmetic had put it at three hundred and fifty, and the arithmetic was his and it had been checked against a whole evening in the forge with the pound going on and off the plate, and it had been true there. It was not true here. The charge was going out of the twenty stones as though something were drinking it from the other end of the grooves, and not all of it was coming out the other end.

There was heat in the pack. Not in the stones. Between them. The lamp stones sat in the felt in their rows, each one a small steady place in the medium, and between every stone and the next, along the grit that joined them, there were thin hot lines, like the lines of fire that run along the edge of a paper when it catches and has not yet flared. The same lines ran in the leather at the hips. The same, fainter, at the backs of the knees. Every join the charge had to cross was running warm, and the warmth was charge. It was going nowhere. It was going into the leather and the felt and the air, and it was coming out of the pack as fast as the stones were giving it.

In the forge the grooves had been a hand long, cut in firebrick. In the harness they ran the length of a man, through leather, across more joins than he had counted.

"A hundred and eighty," said Ward, from the air.

He had put three hundred on the plan, which was a third of what the arithmetic allowed, because the arithmetic had been true on a bench before and a man had caught two tons over a river for it. The arithmetic was not going to be true tonight, and he could feel exactly how untrue it was going to be. The pack was more than half gone. The rest of it would go faster, not slower, the way the stones under the section had gone faster at the end.

He did not wait for the count.

"Down," he said.

Ward did not ask why. He came out of the circle at the far side of the square, took the push off, and hung. Then he brought the left lever back, a notch at a time, and came down through the lamplight toward the sand the way a leaf comes down in still air, the lines settling in loops around him.

A foot off the sand he put his hand on the lever and took it all the way back, past Bram's notch.

The shoulders and hips let him go. The heels went heavy.

He came down the last of it all at once and landed, and the sand went up round him in a ring, and his boots went into it to the ankle and through it to the floor, and he stood there. He did not stagger. He did not take the half step every man takes when he lands from any height at all. He landed as if he had been set down there by something that weighed ten times what he did and had no intention of moving him again.

Ward looked at his feet. He tried to lift one, and it would not come, and he stopped trying.

"Nobody's pushing me off that," he said.

He said it as he said everything, and Emeric heard what was under it, because he had spent ten years hearing what was under Ward. A man at a gate. A man on a wall with a ladder coming up against it. A man in a line, when the other line comes on. It had been built for putting a man on the ground now and not presently, if the stones went, and in the time it took to land Ward had found a use for it that nobody had asked for and that Emeric had not once thought of, and would not have written down if he had.

"The pack," said Imani, from the wall.

"A quarter left. Less."

"Then he stays down."

"He stays down," Emeric agreed. "On the ground, heavy, on the lines. One thing more, and then it's empty."

Imani looked at him for a long moment, and then at Ward, standing in a hole in the sand with his feet on the foundry floor, and did not say yes, and did not say no.

"Full push," Emeric said to Ward. "From standing. Heels where they are. For a count of three, and then stop, whatever it's doing. I want to know what all of it is while you've got the floor under you, so nobody ever finds it out for the first time in the air."

Ward put his right hand on the lever.

"Three," he said, and pushed it all the way.

The foundry roared.

It was not the bellows any more. It came out of the small of his back and the backs of his calves as a single sound, huge, as though the whole building had drawn one breath and let it go through a gap the width of a hand, and the sand behind him went up in a sheet and hit the far wall like hail on a shutter. The flames of all four lamps lay over flat on their hooks. The lines went taut all four at once and sang. Ward leaned into it, planted, bent forward like a man holding a door against a gale, and did not move one inch from where his heels had put him, and the doors at the far end of the foundry, barred and shut, shook in their frame and let the sound out through every crack in them, over the bank and across the water, into the midnight.

"Two," said Ward, somewhere inside it. "One."

He pulled the lever back.

The silence afterwards was very large. Sand was still falling off the far wall in small dry runs. One of the lamps had gone out. In the medium, the pack on Ward's back was nothing: twenty cold places in a row, and between them, for a few breaths longer, the thin hot lines along the joins, going out one after another. His heels went last. He felt them go before Ward did, and then Ward felt them go, and lifted one foot out of the sand, and then the other, and stood on top of it in dead plate, a tired man in heavy armour, breathing through his nose.

Bram was the first to him. He came across the sand at a walk, and did not ask anybody, and put his bare hand flat on the pack on Ward's back, and then on the leather at the hip, and then on the pack again. He moved his palm along it slowly, from stone to stone.

"The stones are cold," he said. "It's the mortar that's hot."

"I know."

"Hot as a pan." Bram took his hand away and looked at it. "That's where it went."

"Most of it."

Imani had Ward's wrist before Bram had finished. She held it for a count of her own, and looked into his eyes one after the other in the lamplight, and asked him to breathe in, and out, and found nothing, and let go. She rolled the canvas back up without having opened it.

Ward unbuckled the pack himself and let it down onto the sand. It landed with a sound like a sack of nails.

"Well," Imani said to him. "How was it."

Ward thought about it for longer than Ward usually thought about anything.

"The heaviest thing on me," he said, "was the stone that let me fly."

He said it to Imani, and then looked at Emeric across the sand, and Emeric looked at the pack lying in the sand between them in its iron box, with the felt coming out at one corner where the heat had scorched it.

Armand had not sat down. He wrote on his sheet standing up, and when he had written it he held it out to Emeric without a word. Every twenty counts of Ward's, the reading Emeric had called low across the sand, a third gone, a half, and beside each one, in the crabbed hand, what the arithmetic had said. At the foot, under a line: Nobody pouring. Nobody. And under that, because he was a man who wrote down what was true, the count the arithmetic had promised and the count they had got.

Ferra weighed the pack again in the morning, on the same scales, empty. It weighed to a grain what it had weighed full. She wrote that down under the first figure and looked at the two of them for some time, and then drew a line under both, and wrote nothing else on the page. It was, he understood later, the beginning of a piece of arithmetic, and she did not intend to show it to anybody until she had finished it.

Saban had the fifth version inside the week.

He brought it up to the long room with evident pleasure, and set it out on the table beside the other four, as though they were a set of weights. The lower town had heard the roar at midnight across the water, and had heard nothing else, because the doors had been shut. By market day the roar had become the new furnace at the foundry bursting, and nobody hurt, which the lower town put down to the prince's luck. By the day after, on the quay, it had become the prince building a thing to fly to the capital in, and the thing had burst.

"It's the first one," Saban said, "that's got flying in it."

He said it lightly. He was not smiling when he said it, and nobody else at the table smiled either.
