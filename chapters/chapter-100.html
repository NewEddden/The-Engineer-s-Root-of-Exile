Chapter 100 - Raw Stone

The printer ran every day now.

It stood in the finishing shop on its iron bed under the high window, and through the last of the spring it ran from the hour Jorrin unlocked the shop until the light went: primers for Orrin's school, ruled sheets for Ferra, and for most of one week the barony's standard for a sound well, which Imani had written in eleven lines and Emeric had set into a stone in a night, and which went up on the church door of every parish in four baronies with the same eleven lines on every copy. Emeric went down most afternoons and stood by the bed for a while. He told himself it was to watch the head, which was true, and was not the reason.

The carriage went along the bridge a division at a time and stopped and blotted and went on. It was a small, dull thing to watch. It put a drop of ink at one point on a sheet, exactly, and then the next drop at the next point, and it did that because a stone on the bridge told it where and four stones at the ends of the rails pushed it there, and nobody stood over it at all.

There was a line in the notebook he had carried since the winter, in the column for things he could not answer himself. Heat and form. Can they be taken off me the way the hold was? Since the spring there had been a second question a few lines below it, which had come out of the line for heavy, and he had looked at both so often that his eye had begun to slide off them.

Watching the carriage, he could answer half of the first.

The heat was a thing at a point. In the pattern store it had been a chip of fire stone on a copper wire, which he carried to the one spot on the steel where he wanted the steel to move, and held there, and carried on to the next. That was all the head did with its ink. A chip on a carriage, on true rails, pushed from point to point by stones that a deciding stone told where, would lay heat on a billet the way this one laid ink on a sheet, and it would not need him in the room. Bram could build the rails. Jorrin had built the last ones.

The form was not a thing at a point. It was a shape.

It was the second of his three drills, the blade of air he had first thrown across a line of men at forty feet, taken down over eight years until it had stopped being a blade at all and become a pressure with an outline round it. He had cut the bore of the printer's head with it. And in all those years it had never been anywhere but in him. He had put it into steel a great many times. He had never once put it into anything that could keep it.

He went up to the room off the foundry office to ask the question he had been saving since the autumn.

Armand heard it without getting up.

"In the autumn," Emeric said, "you told me the tower gave up on raw stone before your grandfather was born. Did you mean the black stone, or every stone?"

"Every stone." The old man did not need to think about it. "Every stone the tower has ever sold was carved first. That is what the tower is." He set his pen in the tray. "A raw stone given work will do something with it, and nobody can tell you what. A raw fire stone flares, and nobody can say how hot. A raw force stone kicks, and nobody can say which way, or how hard. There is a bench in the tower with a hole through the top of it the size of a man's fist, and they show it to the first-years. There is a hole of the same size in the ceiling above it, and they show that to nobody." He looked at Emeric across the brass. "The carving tells a stone what it may do and no more. The tower is a very long time spent learning to carve."

"The black blank did what it was told."

"Once. Because you told it, and then let go of the thread, which no man in the tower has done on purpose since the founding." Armand folded his hands on the ledger. "What would you tell a force stone?"

"A shape."

Armand waited, which with Armand meant go on.

"The thing I did to the head in the pattern store. The heat, and the other thing, the one that went on through the billet into the oak when the billet slid." Emeric laid his hand flat on the bench. "Everybody who's seen it thrown calls it a blade of air, because at forty feet air is what it moves. At the size of a bore it isn't air. Air doesn't go through steel. What's left when you take the air out of it is a push with an edge round it. A shape that presses." He took his hand away. "The stone for a push is force stone. The etching floor cuts the gate workings into it. Nobody has ever given one a shape."

"Nobody has ever given one anything," said Armand, "raw."

He was quiet for a while. Then he asked the question Emeric had been waiting for since the winter, and had been dreading a little less than he expected to.

"And how do you know it might work?"

Emeric found that he had an answer, and that it was the true one.

"I don't. That's why it's a trial."

Armand looked at him the way he looked at a baseline, for rather a long time, and seemed to find that he could not argue with it, and did not enjoy that as much as he usually did.

"If it kicks," he said, "it kicks through whatever is in front of it."

"Lead, a hand deep. The stone in an iron housing open on one face, and that face looking down into the lead. Whatever it does, it can only do it one way."

"And your hands."

"Nowhere under it."

"Imani will want that on a sheet."

"It's on a sheet," said Emeric. "Ferra has it. So does Imani, by now, I should think."

Armand made the small sound in his nose that was the nearest he came to laughing, and picked up his pen.

The force stone came off the etching floor.

It was the stone the floor cut its kinetic workings into, the ones that dropped the outer bar across the gate and drove the pins home: a pale, cloudy stone like old ice, which Saban bought by the box at the coast at a Silver Rand and a half the piece, and which nobody in the barony had ever once used without a working cut into it first. Emeric went down with Nevin to draw it, because he wanted to choose the piece himself, and Vesk had the issue book open on the long table under the north window.

Vesk wrote the piece against the stock, and then held his pen over the column where the working went.

"Carved for?"

"Not carved."

Vesk's pen stayed where it was for a moment. Then he wrote raw in the column, in a small neat hand, and blotted it, and turned the book round for Emeric's initial, and did not ask anything else. He had been in Kaldo five years, and he was very good at knowing which questions to leave on the table.

Nevin cut it in the pattern store the next day. It went faster than the black stone. It was a softer, simpler stone and did not fight the saw, and he squared it to the size of the end of a thumb with six flat faces, and laid it on the bench, and looked at it with the expression Emeric had seen on him once before, of a man who has made a thing correctly and does not know what for.

"Do I cut the working now?"

"No."

"Tomorrow?"

"Perhaps never."

Nevin nodded slowly, as though that were a reasonable thing to be told about a piece of work, and swept up after himself, and went home.

Bram cast the lead: a block the width of a spread hand and as deep as one, poured into a sand mould and planed flat on top, and set in an iron trough bolted to the bench. Over it, on an iron bracket, he hung a housing off the any-size drawing from the winter, a thick iron cup open on one face only, and Jorrin turned the collar that screwed down over it. With the blank inside, the open face looked down at the lead from a finger above, and there was iron on every other side of it.

The shape was Emeric's choice, and he chose it for one reason.

Every gauge rod in the felt-lined box in the finishing shop had the master's mark struck into its end: a K over a short bar. Jorrin had cut it into the face of a steel punch the spring the gauges were made, and had struck it into every rod himself, one blow each, with the number beside it. It was the only shape in the barony with a true copy that anybody could pick up and lay beside it. Whatever came out in the lead, a man who could not read a word, and had never seen the delve, and did not trust Emeric any further than his own hand, could hold an inch rod next to it and say whether it was right.

He asked Jorrin to bring one.

They did it at night, with the door locked on both keys.

Armand sat on his stool against the wall with a clean sheet on his knee. Ward stood at the door. Nevin had come back, because he had cut the stone and wanted to see what it was for, and Armand had let him stay. Jorrin stood at the far end of the bench with the inch rod in his fist like a man holding a candle in church.

Emeric put his hands out over the housing, well above it, and opened the medium. The blank was there in it, small and pale and nothing like the black stone. Where the black stone had been dense and strange and hot, this was steady and even, like a line drawn taut.

He went down into the delve.

The plain table stood where it always stood, in the plain light, and he put the mark on it the way he put the true object there when he worked: the K and its bar, a straw deep, as crisp as the punch in the finishing shop. He looked at it for what felt like a long while and was a breath in the pattern store. He held it. Then he reached out through his hands, the way he had reached for the black blank in the yard, and put the form into the stone instead of into steel.

The stone took it. He felt it take the shape and hold it, and he let go of the thread and drew nothing back.

For a moment the lead did nothing. Then a shadow came onto its face under the housing, faint, like breath on a cold window, and deepened, and went on deepening, and the lead gave under it without a sound. It did not bulge or tear. It went down where the form said down and stayed where the form said stay, and the edges stood as clean as if a graver had cut them. In the time it took Ward to shift his weight from one foot to the other there was a K over a short bar pressed a straw deep into a block of lead, with nobody's hand within an arm of it.

Nevin let his breath out through his teeth.

Armand wrote. Emeric came up out of the delve and stood looking at it, and found that he was smiling, and did not care who saw.

Jorrin came down the bench with the inch rod. He laid it beside the trough, end on, with its stamp next to the print, and bent over the two of them, and looked from one to the other for some time. Then he straightened, and looked at Emeric, and looked back at the rod, with the face of a man who has found a fault in his master's work and would very much rather have found it in his own.

"Mine go the other way, Your Highness," he said.

Emeric looked.

The K faced the wrong way. It was perfect. Every edge was true, the bar sat under it at the right length to a hair, and it stood in the lead as crisp as the punch had ever struck it into any rod, looking back over its own shoulder like a K in a glass.

He knew why before he had finished seeing it, and it was very nearly funny, and then it was entirely funny.

Every seal-cutter in the world knew it. Every boy who had ever pressed his father's ring into warm wax knew it. A seal's face was the other way round from its print, because it met the wax face to face. Jorrin knew it; he had cut his punch backwards, the whole K turned about on the steel, so that it would come out right on the rod. And Emeric had laid the mark on the table in the delve the way he wanted to read it, looking down at it from where he stood, because that was how he had always held the form: from his side, onto the work. The stone did not stand where he stood. It pressed out of its own face, like a seal, and a seal's face is a mirror.

He had held the form from his side. The stone had pressed from its own.

"You cut your punch backwards," he said.

"You have to," said Jorrin, and then, because he was a fair man and could see the rest of the sentence coming, stopped.

Ward looked at the lead from the door without moving. "It's still a K."

"It's a K in a mirror."

"Then hold a mirror to it," said Ward.

Armand wrote a second line under the first, and Emeric, reading it over his shoulder, did not argue with it. Held true. Held backward. Maker's error.

The stone was still pressing. He had it in the medium, and it had not let go. It was holding the mark down into lead that was already the shape of the mark, spending itself steadily on a thing that was finished, the way the plate in the pattern store had once hung spending itself against Bram's iron fingers with nothing on it. He could not take it back. There was nothing to take it back by. They left it where it was, on its bracket over its backward K, and in the morning Nevin cut another.

They ran the second the following night, with a fresh block under a second housing and the same five in the room.

In the delve, Emeric did not change the mark. He walked round the table.

It was a thing he could only have done there. He went round the plain table to its far side, and then down, until he was under it and looking up at the mark through the wood, the way the stone would meet the lead: face to face, from the other side. From there the K looked back over its own shoulder. That was the one he gave it.

The lead took it in the same breath and the same silence, clean and a straw deep. Jorrin laid the rod beside it and bent over the two, and this time he stayed bent for a long while, moving his eye from the stamp to the print and back. When he straightened he did not say anything at all. He nodded once, and set the rod down on the bench as though it had done its work and could rest.

It read true. A stone could hold the form.

Armand did not go to bed. He sat on his stool beside the bracket with the sheet on his knee, as he had sat beside the pan all one night in the autumn, and wrote the hour every hour. Emeric stayed until the second hour and then went up, because nothing was going to change, and nothing did. The mark was made in the first breath. After that the stone held it down into lead that was already the shape of it, and spent itself doing so, steadily, on nothing, exactly as the first one was still doing on the next bracket.

In the morning, on the way up from the yard, he went through the finishing shop.

The first block was on the shelf beside the felt-lined box, where anybody who took down a gauge rod would have to see it first. Its backward K faced the room. Jorrin was at the second lathe with his back to it and did not turn round.

"For the next man," he said, before Emeric could ask.

Armand came up the stair a little before noon, which he did not often do, with the night's sheet in his hand.

He laid it on the corner of the table and did not sit. The first stone had spent a little after the fourth hour. The second a little before the fifth. Both marks unchanged since the first minute. At the foot, in the same crabbed hand: A raw stone holds a shape. It holds it until it dies, whether there is work under it or not.

"You will want a feed," Armand said.

"Yes."

"And something to tell it when."

"Yes."

The old man nodded, as though he had expected nothing else and was not sorry to be right. He did not pick the sheet up again. He stood looking at it for some time, with his hands behind his back.

"In a year," he said, "I have written two things in my own hand that the tower never found. A stone that holds with nobody holding it. And this." He did not touch the sheet. "When a man in the tower finds a thing the tower does not know, he writes it to the tower. That is the other rule they never relax. They teach it in the same year as the first, and I have kept it for fifty years, and I was proud of it. I have written nothing."

Emeric said nothing. He had wondered, in the autumn, how long it would be before a letter went up the road in that hand, and he had never asked, because it was not a thing that could be asked.

"I will not write to the tower," Armand said.

He looked at the sheet a moment longer.

"Not yet."

Then he went back down the stair, slowly, one hand on the wall, and left the sheet where it was.

Emeric sat with it for a long time.

He could have gone after him and asked what yet meant, and what would end it, and Armand would have told him. He could have asked for a promise, and the old man would very likely have given one, and it would have been worth exactly what a promise is worth when it is taken from a man who has just told you the truth without being asked. He did not go. A man who tells you his own clock is running has told you the most useful thing he can, and you do not thank him for it by trying to stop it.

He opened the notebook at the column for things he could not answer, and found the line from the winter. Heat and form. Can they be taken off me the way the hold was? He put a line through it and wrote underneath, small: Heat, on a carriage. Form, in a stone. Told once. Spends.

Then he found the question from the spring, a few lines down, and read it again. Who tells it twice? It was the same question. He had the hold and the form now, both of them lying on a bench where anybody could pick them up, and the heat would ride a rail, and not one of the three could be told anything after the day it was set. He added two words under the question and left it there.

And feeds it.

There was one more line he could have written, and it would have gone in the column for things he could not answer. He did not write it anywhere.
