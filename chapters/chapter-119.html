Chapter 119 - Three Hours

There was snow on the dunes at the lower water on midwinter morning, and frost on the lagoon in a thin grey skin that the boat broke going out to her, and the whole of the coast was white and still under a low sky.

They went aboard after breakfast. Nobody had eaten much of it except Imani, who had eaten a great deal and made Emeric eat twice what he wanted, and Maren, who had cooked it and then eaten standing up in the galley of the cottage because she said she could not bear to watch anybody else waste an egg.

She steamed out through the gap in the bar on her boiler's stones with nothing coming out of her stack, and Fletcher at the wheel under the dome did not look up at the stack once, which Emeric took to be an effort. A dozen miles out, with the coast gone to a white line behind them and no sail anywhere on the grey water, everybody came in under the roof.

That had been the rule since the autumn, and Ward had said it again at the gap: nobody stood on an open deck at speed. So they stood in the day room, at the ports, holding the rail that ran along the bulkhead beneath them. Ten of them, with Tobe below at the engine, and room for all of them, and the lamps lit in their cages although it was morning, because the shutters were half down against the glare off the sea.

Ward had the levers. Emeric stood beside him, near enough to reach, and opened the medium to read her, as he did on every lift.

Inside her, where the iron kept everything else out, the medium was hers. The sixteen stations low in her frames, the hundred and eighty cells in their rack, the reserve under the deck beams, cold and waiting. The house bank forward and the boiler's bank amidships, full. The reading stone over its dish. He had it all at once, the way a man standing in his own kitchen in the dark knows where the table is.

She ran on the top of the swell, lightening. The screw raced and came up. The sea went away under her.

At the rail beside him, Vantic's hand closed.

He heard it before he saw it. She had watched this from a beach in the autumn with her book held flat against her, and had never once felt it, and when the deck left the water her stomach went with it, as everybody's did the first time, and she took hold of the rail the way Ferra had in the autumn, hard, and did not let go. Her face did nothing at all. Her knuckles were white.

Ferra did not take hold of anything. She had done this before. She stood at the next port with one hand resting on its frame, and she did not look out of it at the sea going down. She was watching Vantic.

And in the medium, among the stations and the cells and the rack, there was something Emeric had never felt in it before. It was small and very close and very fast, a flutter in the edge of the medium beside him, and it took him a moment to know it for what it was: her heart, going hard against her ribs under her coat, a hand's breadth from his own.

He had not known the sense could do that. Nothing he had ever read with it had been so small, or so near, or so alive, and before he could stop it his mind had run straight on ahead of him to the next question, which was whether a sense that could find a heartbeat through a coat could find what was under the coat. He shut it. He shut the medium on her at once, the whole of it, embarrassed to the ears, and it was the first time in his life he had closed it on a person on purpose.

He opened it again only on her stones, tight, the stations and the rack and nothing that breathed, and read the ship that way for the rest of the climb, and the work took him, and he let it.

Then Vantic laughed.

It was a girl's laugh. It came out of her all at once, high and short, as the coast went down under the port and turned from a line into a map, and she let go of the rail and went to the port the way a child goes to a window, and put both her hands on its frame, and said something half-finished about it going up, it was going up, look at it, it was going *up*.

She heard herself.

Emeric saw her hear it. It went through her like a missed step on a stair. She took her hands off the port's frame and turned round, and her face had come back to where she kept it, very nearly.

"One would want to note the rate of ascent," she said. "For the valuation."

She got two words further than that. Then she stopped, because she could see that nobody in the day room believed a word of it, and she knew it.

Osric laughed first. He had been holding it since the rail, and it came out of him in a bark, and then Imani went, and Saban, and Orrin, who laughed the way he talked, at length, and Maren in the galley doorway, and Ward, silently, with his shoulders, at the levers. Emeric was laughing too. He found he could not stop.

Vantic went red to the ears and held her book very flat against her chest.

When it had died down she looked at him across the day room, still red, and entirely composed.

"Your Highness is laughing at a creditor," she said.

"Yes."

"It will be in the report."

"Will it?"

"No," said Vantic, and turned back to the port.

Nobody said anything more about it. She stood at the port for the rest of the climb with her book held flat against her and her eyes on the coast going down, and her face did nothing it had not been given leave to do. But she did not take hold of the rail again.

He did not steer for Emberfell.

He had Ward take her straight up instead, over the sea, until the probe stone behind its pane in the port vein began to glow, and the air in the veins came in cold and full, and the note of her changed. At the bottom of the band he had her levelled, and held there.

It was the standard now. He had written it in the notebook the week before and Ferra had copied it into the book that held the rules: every flight began this way. Climb, and fill, and then go. In the medium the cells in the rack, which had thinned on the way up, stopped thinning and began, slowly, to fill, as they had in the autumn, as every breath of the band that went past every neck in her paid for the one after. He said so.

"Taking on coal at sea," Ward said, watching the probe stone, "without stopping."

"No ship ever has," said Fletcher, from the wheel.

"That's the point," said Emeric.

It was warm under the roof. In the autumn it had been cold enough up here to stop a hand, and it was not now. The dial on the bulkhead by the chart table stood at its mark, and the floor under their boots was warm, and the air coming up through the grating at the end of the room was warm, and nobody was in a coat. Imani went and stood with her hand over the grating for some time. Then she asked him exactly how it did that, and he took the cover off the housing on the twig and showed her the fire stone in it and the air stone behind it, and the strip of brass and iron behind the dial that bent as the room warmed, and she looked at all of it with the face she used for a new kind of splint.

Maren brought round something hot at the bottom of the band, two miles over the sea, in mugs, off a hob with no fire under it. It was a broth, and it was very good, and nobody said so because their mouths were full, and Maren saw that and went back to the galley satisfied.

Then Ward took the push off its stop, and the note in the veins went up, and she went.

The coast went by at fifty miles a glass.

Emeric turned Neta's glass at the headland below the bar, the way he had in the autumn, and watched the white coast of midwinter unroll under her, long beaches under snow and marshes gone grey with frost and the mouths of rivers standing out black where the ice had not closed them. Ward flew most of it. He flew it on the levers, with his hands on them, as he had always flown her, and she held her line and her height the way she had in the autumn, and the cells stayed full.

Emeric took one stretch himself, from a headland Fletcher named to one Fletcher named after it. He did it from where he stood, beside Ward, with his hands at his sides, and the levers moved. It was easy for the first quarter of an hour and it was not easy after that, and when he gave her back to Ward he was tired behind the eyes, the way he had been in the foundry. Ward took the levers without a word and did not look at him, and Emeric knew that Ward had noted it and would not mention it, and was grateful on both counts.

Ferra stood at a port for most of an hour, with her book shut under her arm, reading the coast.

She had never seen it. She had read it. There was a chart in the day room on the table, and she had looked at it once when they came aboard, and she was reading the coast under them against the chart in her head the way she read a column against a total, and twice she named a headland before it came up out of the haze under the bow. The second time, Fletcher at the wheel looked round at her.

"You've been along here," he said.

"No."

He looked at her a moment longer and went back to the wheel.

Emeric went and stood at the next port. The coast was going by below them, white and grey.

"We'll be there before the letter," he said.

"A fortnight before." Ferra did not look round. "It'll get there and tell her we're coming, and we'll have gone."

"You sent it anyway."

"A queen should be told." She watched a river mouth go under them. "It isn't for her. It's for the book. When somebody asks one day whether Kaldo gave her notice, the answer's yes, with a date, and it isn't my fault the sea's slow."

He laughed, and she did not, and he saw the corner of her mouth move, and then the next headland came up and she named that one too.

Across the day room Imani was standing at the far port with her back to the coast, watching faces.

He saw what she was doing after a moment. She went from one face to the next, not staring, the way a woman looks round a room for somebody she knows, and at every face she looked at the lips. Vantic's. Saban's. Maren's in the galley door. Orrin's, who was talking. Fletcher's at the wheel. They were all the colour lips ought to be under a roof full of the veins' thick air. She had learned that from him. She had come up the ladder onto the ship on the lake in the autumn and seen Ward's mouth, and seen where Emeric was looking, and had not asked him what he was doing, and had worked it out, and now it was hers.

He did not say anything. She saw him see her, and went on doing it.

In the long quiet stretch after the second glass, with the talk in the day room gone low and Vantic writing something in her book at the chart table, he stood at a port by himself and let the medium open again.

Not on anybody. On the band. The air in the veins was the band's air, pushed through her from the mouths at the bow, and he held the sense on it, and it was thick. It was the thickest thing he had ever felt, thicker than the air over the lake in the autumn, pressed full of the medium on every side as far as he could reach. After ten years of ground, where the medium lay thin as a mist on a field, it was like putting his hand into deep water after a lifetime of rain.

And up here, he thought, a dead zone could not last.

He had never seen one in the band. He had never seen one anywhere but on the ground, and on the ground a dead zone lingered, because the medium round it was thin and filled it slowly, from the edges, a little at a time. But here there would be no edges thin enough to be slow. A hole in this would close the way a hollow closes in deep water, from every side at once, almost as soon as it opened. The deeper the water, the faster.

So the question was not whether a man could survive a dead zone. The capes and the chutes answered that. It was whether a man could carry the band down with him.

A box of charged stone, sealed in iron. Iron kept the medium in, as it kept the sky out; the cells had come through the top of the band whole because they rode in iron. Open a box like that into the middle of a dead zone and it would not give up its charge through a narrow neck, slowly, the way a cell did. It would bleed out all at once, the way the rails had bled into the nothing above the band in the autumn, everything they carried gone in a breath. Only this time there would be something to bleed into that wanted filling. Enough to close a small zone. Or if not close it, then hold a pocket of the medium round one man for long enough.

He thought, before he could stop himself, of the chemical candles men burned in sealed holds in another life, to make air where there was none. He did not say it aloud. There was nobody in the day room he could have said it to.

And past that, the question under the question. A cell held more than a rough stone of the same weight, and a round stone more than a rough one. How much more could a stone be made to hold? Could the medium be pressed denser than any stone held it at all, into something that was not stone and not air, something like a liquid, that would flash off into the air the moment it was let out, the way water flicked onto a hot pan flashes to steam?

He did not build anything. He took out the notebook, and turned to the column for things he could not answer, and wrote three lines under the others: *A box of the band. Dead air, opened into. Denser than a stone.* Then he closed it and put it away, and went back to the port.

An hour out, Emeric and Ward and Osric went aft.

They went by the inside passage, below, through the doors in the iron walls with their dogs, past the galley and the boiler and the engine, into the hold, and Ward unlocked the armoury door with the key from round his neck. The suits stood on their stands with their capes furled and their helms on the shelves above them. The hoses off the vein had been breathing into their hearts since the band, and the panes over the caps were bright.

Emeric had brought the brooch.

He had taken it off the coat that morning, where the coat hung in the stern cabin, and he stood in the armoury with the silver star in his hand and found the catch at the back that Bram had made, and opened it, and the grey stone came out into his palm. He opened the small iron door in the breast of his own suit and set the stone in its seat, and closed the door on it, and felt the deciding stone come up against it inside the iron.

They helped each other into the plate, the way they had in the pattern store. Then the capes, drawn up and round and closed at the throat, so that they hung as cloaks, and the hoods up. The helms stayed on their shelves. A winged helm would not go under a hood, and a man with a steel helm on under a hood was a man with something to hide.

Osric looked at himself in the dark glass of the armoury door with his hood up, and turned his head to see the fall of the cloak.

"I look like a man who's come about a debt," he said.

"You look like a man who's cold," said Ward.

They went back forward through the passage and came up into the day room in black, hooded, with nothing showing of what was under the cloth. Vantic looked up from the chart table at the three of them as they came in, one after another. She looked at them for a long moment. Then she looked back down at her book, and did not write anything in it.

They came down out of sight of the inlet.

Fletcher knew where it was from the coasting men's talk and from the shape of the coast, which had changed under them in the last glass: hills coming down to the sea, and dark rock under the snow where there should have been sand, and then no snow at all. Far ahead there was a haze over the land that was not cloud. He took her well out to sea, round a headland, where nobody standing at the inlet mouth could see her, and Ward brought her down, a notch at a time on the levers, the way he had brought her down in the autumn.

The sea came up grey and wide. At the height of a man Fletcher said, "Now," quite quietly, the way he had said it the first time, and Ward held her there, and let her down onto it, and she sat down on the sea as though she had never been anywhere else.

The screw went down. Tobe, below, woke the boiler's stones, and the engine came up under their feet with the old knock of it, and she steamed in toward the land with nothing coming out of her stack at all.

They came round the headland. The land opened in front of them.

It was an inlet, long and straight, running inland between two lines of dark hills, wider than the river at Kaldo and very much longer, grey water under a grey sky. There were boats on it, a great many of them, and a smell coming off it on the wind that nobody aboard could put a name to except Saban, who put his head up at a port and breathed in and said nothing.

And at the far end of it, where the hills came together, there was a mountain, and the mountain was smoking.
