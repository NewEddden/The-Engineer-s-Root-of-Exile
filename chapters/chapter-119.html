Chapter 119 - Summons

The summons came on the eighth of July, and it came by a crown rider, and that was how the town knew before Emeric did.

Crown riders did not come to Kaldo. In four years one had come, in the first August, with the single execution dispatch that ended Wexler, and the town had not forgotten him: a man in the crown's grey on a horse better than any horse in the barony, riding up the high street at a trot without looking left or right, with a leather case on his hip. This one was the same. Grey coat, good horse, leather case. He came in at the South Gate at noon, and by the time he had reached the inner ward's arch half the high street was standing in its doorways to watch him go up, and Maren had come out of the castle kitchen with flour to her elbows to see who had died.

Nobody had died. He handed the case to Hale at the foot of the keep stair and asked for a receipt, and got one, in Hale's hand, timed and dated, which he looked at with some surprise, as if receipts were not usually offered him in the south, and rode away again to the posting house to sleep.

There were two sheets in the case.

The first was to Emeric. It was on the crown's heavy paper with the court's seal in red, not the chancery's black wafer, and it said, in a clerk's formal hand that did not hurry, that the holder of the barony of Kaldo was required to attend upon the matter numbered, at the crown's court, on the twenty-sixth day of July, with such papers and persons as he thought fit, and that his failure to attend would be taken as his consent to whatever the court then found. It was signed by Keller.

The second was a copy of the first. Not to Emeric. To Sethe, holder of the barony of Wayford, required to attend upon the same matter, on the same day, at the same court. Sent to Emeric, Ferra said, reading it over his shoulder, so that he would know the other party had been called. That was the rule. She had read about it in a book of Wexler's on procedure that nobody had opened in twenty years.

"Both of them," Emeric said.

"Both of you." Ferra took the copy and held it up to the window and looked at the seal. "By number. Not by name of the matter. Nobody reading it on the road would know what it was about." She put it down. "Keller doesn't trust the road either."



The Standing Order sat that night, though it was not the first Monday, because any two of the six could require it and Ward and Ferra did.

It did not take long. There was very little to decide that had not been decided in the winter, when Hale read the sheet. The holder would go. The Chancellor would go with him, because the papers were hers and so was the book, and Keller would want to ask questions about both that nobody else could answer. The Secretary of War would go, because the holder did not go anywhere without him and nobody at the table proposed that he should. Ward would take Rylan, and one other.

"Rylan," said Imani, from the end of the table. She did not say it as a question.

"Rylan," said Ward.

Imani looked at him. Ward looked back. Something passed between them that Emeric did not follow and was not meant to, and Imani nodded, once, and did not ask anything else.

The seventh clause did the rest.

Armand held Kaldo. He said nothing when Hale read the clause again, aloud, into the minutes, the holder's authority in Kaldo while the holder was away from it. He sat at the fire end of the table in his chair with his hands on the blanket and looked at the fire, and when Hale had finished he nodded, as a man nods at the reading of a will he has known the terms of for years.

"And everything else," said Ferra, "Imani holds."

Imani looked up.

"The books go with me," Ferra said. "Somebody at the table has to sign the pay sheet on the last Friday, and Hale won't sign a thing he isn't named for. And somebody has to sit at the trestle on post days, and see the window, and decide what's a matter for Armand and what's a matter for the cooper." She did not look at Emeric. "Armand holds the authority. Somebody has to hold the town."

"I've a ward," said Imani.

"You've two women who can sit up and a girl who can sew a wound as well as you," said Ferra, "because you taught her to, in the winter, when you knew this was coming. I saw her name in your column." She put her pen down. "You hold the town. You've held it since the plague. You just never had it written down."

Imani sat back in her chair and looked at Ferra for some time, down the length of the table, and Emeric could not tell whether she was angry, and suspected that Imani could not either.

"Write it down, then," she said at last.

Ferra wrote it down.



Vantic came up to Ferra's room the next morning with a letter.

Emeric was there, because he was always there that week. The room had become the place where the case was built: the three deal boxes from May along the wall with their padlocks, and Ferra at the trestle with the index open and the barony's book open and a fresh book she had begun for the summons, ruled into columns he had not seen before. Witness. What they will say. What Keller will ask them. What they should not say.

Vantic knocked, which she did not always do, and came in, and stood just inside the door in her grey coat and grey gloves with the letter in her hand.

"The House requires," she said, "that its factor report any summons of the borrower to the crown's court. Within a day, by the first courier." She held the letter up. "This is my report."

"Of course," said Ferra, and went on writing.

Vantic did not go.

She stood there with the letter. Emeric had seen her letters to the House a hundred times: every week, on a Thursday, a single sheet folded small and sealed with the House's grey wax and the factor's seal, carried down to the House's courier at the coast road by her own hand. He had never seen one open. Nobody in Kaldo had. They were the House's, and the House's factor wrote what the House's factor wrote, and that was the covenant.

This one was not sealed.

Vantic crossed the room and laid it on Ferra's trestle, open, the right way up for Ferra to read, beside the index. Then she stepped back to the door again and folded her gloved hands in front of her.

Ferra stopped writing.

She looked at the letter, and then up at Vantic. Vantic looked back at her with no expression at all.

Ferra read it.

Emeric did not. He was standing at the window, and the letter was not his, and Vantic had not laid it in front of him. He watched Ferra read it, which took her a little longer than it would have taken her to read a page of her own; and watched her get to the bottom, and stop, and go back up to somewhere in the middle, and read a line again.

Then she looked up at Vantic.

"That's fair," she said.

"It's accurate," said Vantic.

"It's fair," said Ferra. "You could have written it otherwise. A summons to the crown's court in a matter with a neighbouring baron, a year after a private war, with the security in the summoned barony. You could have written that the House's position was at risk." She put one finger on the letter, not on any line, only on the paper. "You wrote that the borrower attends at the crown's request upon a matter in which the barony is the complaining party, and that the factor has seen the papers and finds them in order."

"I have seen the papers," said Vantic. "I saw the twin year's dates come down my own line. I find them in order." She looked at the letter on the trestle. "The House will read that sentence in the capital before the court sits. It will know that the House's factor, under the covenant, has examined the borrower's case and does not doubt it." She paused. "There are people in the capital who will ask the House what it thinks of Kaldo, between now and the twenty-sixth. I would like them to be told the truth."

Ferra looked at her for a while longer.

Then she folded the letter, carefully, along its old creases, and held it out across the trestle, and Vantic came forward and took it.

"Thank you," said Ferra.

"For a report?"

"For letting me read it."

Vantic held the folded letter in her gloved hand. She looked at it, and then at Ferra, and something went across her face very quickly, the way it had gone across it in the shed in January when she said a thing about the hull she had not meant to say, and was put away.

"I have written to the House every week for nearly two years," Vantic said. "From a room in the east tower. And sealed it before anybody in this barony saw a word." She turned the letter over in her hand. "You keep a column on everyone. It seemed to me that one week you should be able to keep it on me."

She took out her seal, and the stick of grey wax, and a little candle from her coat, and sealed the letter there, at Ferra's trestle, in front of both of them, with the factor's mark. Then she put it in her coat and went out, and they heard her steps go down the stair, even, unhurried, toward the gate and the coast road and the courier.

Ferra sat looking at the door.

"She didn't have to do that," Emeric said.

"No," said Ferra. She picked up her pen and looked at the witness column in the new book for some time without writing in it. "That's why she did."



Paxton's answer came on the twelfth, after five months.

It came the same way the four lines had come in December, by hand, sewn into the lining of a drover's coat, the same big slow drover with his face burned dark, who stood in the back room of the exchange and cut the stitches with his own knife and put it into Emeric's hand and not the window's.

"He said you'd be wanting it," the drover said. "On account of the court."

"How did he know about the court?"

The drover looked at him as if he had asked how Paxton knew it was July.

"He's been summoned," he said. "Same as you. By the same rider, two days before. Witness, it says." He put his hat on. "He said to wait for nothing. There's no answer."

It was a single sheet, folded twice, sealed with plain wax and a thumb. Emeric broke it standing by the trestle, with Ferra beside him and the drover gone out into the square.

It said, in Paxton's square heavy hand:

I had your letter. I'll say it in court.

And under that: Paxton, of Milford.

That was all of it. He read it twice. Then he put it down on the trestle in front of Ferra, and she read it, once.

"He hasn't forgiven you," she said.

"No."

"He's going to stand up in front of Keller and say that he warned you in the spring of the war, and stood his men off by understanding, and that you put it in a letter to the crown, and that the letter was read at the crossing, and that four days after it was read his barony began to burn." She looked at the sheet. "In front of Sethe. In front of the court. Everything you spent."

"Yes."

"He's going to say it because it's true," said Ferra, "and because he wants the man who burned his barn hanged more than he wants to stay angry with you." She folded the sheet along its creases. "That's not forgiving you. It's doing his sums."

She put it in the third box, on top of the four lines, flat.



Imani brought the kit up to the gate passage on the evening of the twelfth, while Ward and Rylan were roping the boxes into the chaise for the morning, and did not give it to Rylan or to Ward. She gave it to Emeric.

It was a roll of oiled canvas tied with tape, the size of a loaf. He knew what was in it without opening it, because he had watched her pack the same roll for the garrison's wagon in the war: linen, boiled and dried and folded small, a flask of spirit, needles in a paper, silk in a twist, the little pot of honey she put on burns and would not explain, and a razor, not her father's, a plain one.

"It's not for you," Imani said. "It's for whoever you're with when somebody does something stupid. Ward knows how to use it. So does Rylan." She held it out. "You'll carry it, because they'll be busy."

He took it. Her fingers came off the canvas as his came onto it.

She did not go. She stood in the gate passage in the last of the light with her sleeves still pinned from the ward, and her hair tied up hard, and looked at him the way she looked at a man she was about to send home from her ward with a bandage, checking him over once, all of him, for anything she had missed.

Then she put her hand flat on his chest.

Not on his coat. Inside it, where it was open at the throat, on his shirt, over the left side, flat. He knew what it was. He had watched her do it to a hundred men on the ward, the healer's quick hand on the heart to feel how it went, before she let them out of the door. She did not count. She only held it there, and looked at the gate behind him, not at him.

"Fast," she said.

"I came down the hill."

"You came down the hill," Imani agreed. She did not take her hand away. "You came down it at a walk, with Ward. I watched you from the ward's window." She was quiet. Then she took her hand back, and folded both her arms, and looked at him at last, straight, with something at the corner of her mouth that was not quite a smile and was not quite anything else. "Bring it back," she said. "The kit. I want to know what you used out of it, and on whom, and why."

"I'll write it down."

"You'll tell me," said Imani. "Writing it down is Ferra's." And she turned and went back up the high street toward the ward, at her own pace, without looking round, and he stood in the gate passage with the canvas roll under his arm and the place on his chest where her hand had been, and did not move until Ward said his name twice.



He went to Armand on the last night before they left.

The old man was in his chair in the room off the hall with the board across his knees and a sheet of the barony's paper on the board, covered in his small cramped hand: a list. Emeric could read it upside down. The garrison's rota, as Ward had left it. The board's second holders, Declan and Tess. The fire stones in the tower, Orrin, weekly. The keys. Hale's days. It was the list of a man who had been given a thing to hold and meant to hold all of it, and he was going through it for the fourth time.

"You'll be all right," Emeric said.

"I'll be an old man in a chair with a list." Armand did not look up. "Imani will be all right. Imani will run the town and send a girl to tell me what she's done afterward, out of courtesy, and I'll approve all of it, out of good sense." He put the list down. "Sit."

Emeric sat on the stool across the fire.

Armand looked at him. He did not say anything for a while. He looked the way he had looked in December, by this fire, when Emeric first went out thin as breath and found the room and the hall and the corridor, and Armand had sat in his chair and listened to him go: not at Emeric's face, but at something round him, a little to one side of him, the way a man listens for a draught in a house.

"You've been doing something," Armand said.

Emeric kept his face still.

"Your note's changed." Armand folded his hands on the board. "Not much. A man's note doesn't change much. I've felt yours since you were seven, and it's always been the same, more or less. Fire, and under the fire something very heavy and very quiet that you've never once shown me and I've never asked about." He was watching that place to one side of Emeric. "Since the spring there's something else in it. Low down. Close in." He was quiet. "It sounds like a man who's been in a cold yard swinging a sword. Warm, and tired, and close in. Not going out. Staying."

The fire ticked.

"I've been drilling with Ward," said Emeric. "Every morning."

"You've been drilling with Ward every morning for four years," said Armand, "and your note never sounded like that until April." He looked at Emeric's face then, directly, for the first time. "I'm not asking. I've told you, I'm too old to start asking you things. But I'll tell you what I'd tell any student of mine going to the capital with something new in him that he hasn't learned to hide yet." He leaned forward a little in the chair. "There are people in that city who can hear a man's note as well as I can. Better. Some of them sit in the Circle. Some of them sit in the cathedral." He held Emeric's eyes. "Don't do it there. Whatever it is. Not in the lodging, not in the street, not on the road a day either side of the walls. Leave it at home with me."

Emeric looked at him.

"And keep away from the cathedral," Armand said. "Its people. Its streets. If a man in white comes into a room you're in, find a reason to leave it." He sat back. "I'd like you to come back the same shape you went."

"I'll keep to the court," Emeric said. "The chancery. Keller's room. The posting houses. The lodging."

"Good." Armand picked up his list again, and looked at it, and put it down. "Then go to bed. You have a long way to ride, and I have a town to be an old man in."

At the door Emeric turned round.

"What does it sound like?" he said. "Under the fire. The heavy thing."

Armand did not look up from his list.

"Like a stone falling down a well," he said, "a very long way, in the dark, a long time ago." He turned the list over. "Goodnight, Emeric."
