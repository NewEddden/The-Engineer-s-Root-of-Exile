Chapter 3 - Anything Whispered Is True

It came the way everything else had come that day, all at once and from underneath, and it started at the exact place his memory stopped.

He had the aftermath. He had always had the aftermath. Ward's arms, and the noise the castle made around him, and a word being said quietly by people who had not been given permission to say it.

What arrived now was the fourteen months that came after, and it arrived whole, and it arrived from the height of a seven-year-old.

He had not been afraid. That was the first strange thing about it, standing in a cold gallery at twenty-two with the whole of it unspooling. Children are supposed to be afraid. He had not been, because nobody told him anything, and you cannot be frightened of a thing that has never been given a shape.

What he had instead was logistics.

New rooms. Twice in one week, the second time at night, and he had been annoyed about it, because the second set had a worse window and he had liked being able to see the yard.

New faces on the door, a rotation he learned by sight and never once by name, because they changed too often for names to be worth the effort and because at seven he had assumed that guards were weather.

A man who tasted his food in front of him. He never learned the name. The man came with the food, the way the plates did, and he apologized every time, quietly, before every meal, as though the tasting were a small rudeness he was committing at the table and hoped to be forgiven for. Emeric had believed for the better part of a year that the apology was about the food.

He counted it now, and he could only count it now, because a child cannot count a thing he has not been told is a series.

Four. Four attempts across fourteen months, after the carriage. He could put a shape to each of them out of what had changed the next morning, which was all he had ever been given: a corridor closed off, a cook gone, a tutor replaced between one week and the next with no explanation and no goodbye. The fourth one had moved his rooms twice inside seven days and put two men on a door that had never had one, and after the fourth one nothing happened for a month, and then his father came to see him.

He was eight by then. Nobody had marked the birthday in any way he could locate.

Harrold came in the evening, alone, which by itself had no precedent. He sat down on the edge of the bed, which had none either. And he wore, for the entire conversation, the face of a man holding a pleasant expression stretched over something enormous and furious, and doing it the way a man holds a door shut from the inside.

He explained nothing about the carriage. He explained nothing about the fourteen months. Twenty-two-year-old Emeric, watching this from the far end of fourteen years, understood that the omission had been complete and deliberate and had never once been repaired.

What he said instead was this.

There were powers in the world that wanted his son dead. Not a man. Not a house. Something funded, and patient, and old enough to be patient. Harrold had turned the kingdom over looking for it. He had men standing in rooms he had no business having men in. He had spent a year and he had not come out of it with one name he could use.

Emeric, in the gallery, at twenty-two, added an entry.

It had no face on it and no name attached and nothing that could be done with it, and the list took it anyway and did not appear to mind.

"A threat gets removed," his father said. "A joke gets ignored."

Then he told his son what he wanted.

From that evening, and until Harrold told him it was finished, Emeric was to be reclusive. He was to be lackluster. He was to be, in every room that had a door on it, a disappointment of the particular kind that people stop discussing because there is nothing interesting left to say. He would not be forbidden anything. He would simply stop being worth the cost of killing, and men who are patient and funded do not spend on a boy who is not going anywhere.

An eight-year-old heard all of that, and what he understood was that his father had just handed him the best game anyone had ever invented.

He remembered the exact quality of the excitement, which was the worst part of having the memory back. Not dread. Not grief for a childhood. Delight. There were rules and there was a way to win, and winning meant nobody put anything in his food, and there was a man in the room who would be keeping score.

He had been extremely good at it almost immediately, and the memory of being praised for it, once, in this room, by this man, was the only warm thing in the whole fourteen months.

Then his father leaned in.

Close enough that Emeric, eight years old, had thought for a second he was going to be embraced, and had adjusted his shoulders for it, and had been wrong.

What came instead was a voice that died at arm's length.

Anything that is true, Harrold whispered, I will say to you like this. Anything I say at a volume that reaches that door is for whoever is standing on the other side of it. It may be shaped like the truth. It will not be the truth. Do not correct me, do not look confused, and do not ever repeat back to me at ordinary volume a thing I gave you in this one.

Emeric had asked why he did not simply put up a ward.

He remembered the answer arriving with a pause in front of it, and he remembered the pause better than the words, because at eight it had been the first time he had seen his father consider whether a question deserved an answer and decide that it did.

A ward can be felt from two rooms away, Harrold said. A man who feels a ward learns that there is something under it worth warding. A whisper is not a secret. A whisper is two men standing close together, and men stand close together all day long.

He was eight. He filed the whole apparatus under the same heading as everything else that evening, which was that his father had given him an excellent game with an extra rule in it, and he had gone to sleep happy.

Fourteen years later, in a gallery, on the night of his exile, he stood with the entire architecture of his own life laid out in front of him and found that he could read it end to end and could not find a single place where anyone had told him it was over.

He did not follow that thought any further. There was a banquet tomorrow and a road after it. He put it where he had been putting things all evening, and noted, with some interest, that the drawer was getting full.

"Prince Emeric," Ward said again, from the dark, half a step back.

"Right," he said. "Yes. Bed."



He did not sleep.

He lay in a bed his body knew and his mind was meeting for the first time, and the candle on the side table burned down a finger's width, and somewhere around the second hour he got up and sat on the floor in front of it with his back against the frame.

There was one thing left over from before the drinking, one of the first things anyone with the gift is taught, so early that it sat in the memory with the taste of a particular fruit and the crack in a particular column. You put out a finger, and you reach, and the flame leans to meet it.

At seven, apparently, he had done it without thinking. At seven, people had said words like genius in front of his father.

He put out a finger and reached.

Something stirred behind his breastbone, stiff as a hinge nobody had oiled in fourteen years. A shape began in the air above the wick, a ring of faint light no bigger than a coin with a mark at its center, and it was ugly, lopsided, drawn by a hand that had forgotten how, and it came apart before it closed. The flame flickered once. It did not lean.

The headache arrived a second later, sharp and stupid, right behind the eyes, as though he had stood up into a low beam.

He sat with that for a while. It did not frighten him and it did not particularly disappoint him. What it reminded him of, more than anything, was a gearbox on a test stand at Denholm that somebody had installed eight years earlier and never once run, and that had seized solid the first time anybody put a load through it. Nothing in it had been broken. It had simply sat.

Fourteen years of nothing. That was the number. It would be slow.

He pinched the candle out and went back to bed, and at some point toward morning he slept.



They packed him in the morning, which is to say that four people packed him and he stood in the middle of it getting in the way.

There was a glass over the washstand, and he had not looked into it yet. He did now, while a servant behind him folded shirts into a trunk with the speed of a man who wanted to be elsewhere.

A stranger looked back. Young, and puffy around the eyes with wine, the skin a warm brown like polished copper, the hair cut by somebody who was paid to cut it. And in the middle of it his father's eyes, gold, the clear flat gold of a coin held up to a window.

The knowledge came with it, the way everything came. The royal marker. Every child of the Randillia line had the gold, back as far as anyone had written it down. Even the ones born with odd-colored eyes had one eye gold. No other family in the world had it at all, which meant that anywhere in the kingdom, before he had opened his mouth, people would know exactly whose son he was.

Marcus had worn one face his whole life and had never once seen where it came from. There had been nobody in his parents' house to check it against. He stood at the washstand looking at this one for longer than a man looks at a mirror, and then a servant needed the basin, and he got out of the way.

He did it on autopilot after that, both worlds turning over behind his eyes at once, hands doing the kind of work that does not require anybody to be home. Twelve days south. A barony he could not picture. A father he could not run a load through. Twenty-six numbers in a press release on a world that no longer had him in it.

His hand closed on something at the back of a drawer he had not decided to open.

A stone. Smooth, grey, dull as a riverbed pebble, carved all over in a fine repeating pattern that had gone soft at the edges the way stone goes soft when it has been handled for years by somebody. It weighed slightly more than it looked like it should.

It was, by any measure available to him, worthless. He knew that the way he knew the rest of it, without being able to say where the knowledge came from. Nobody in the household would have crossed a room for it.

He stood there with it in his palm for longer than a man stands holding a rock, and something in him pulled toward it that had no argument attached and produced no reason when he asked it for one, and he was tired, and there were people in the room folding his shirts.

He put it in his pocket.

Somebody had already laid the ornate etching stylus in the bottom of a trunk, silver chased along the grip, because it was expensive and expensive things went in trunks. It was the kind of present a child is given by adults who expect great things of him. He knew it secondhand, like a fact about a stranger.

He took it out. Then, after last night's candle, he put it back himself, near the top, under one shirt and not six, where he could find it without unpacking anything. He did not know yet what he meant to do with it. It seemed enough to know where it was.



His father sent for him in the afternoon.

The way to the office ran along the long gallery above the kitchens, where the air smelled of bread and lye and the windows looked down onto a yard full of barrels. Halfway along it he felt looked at.

It was plain and physical, the pressure between the shoulder blades that comes from a stare across a room before you have found it. He turned. Two maids with an armful of linen between them, arguing under their breath about whose turn it was. A guard at the far end, more asleep than not. A window full of grey sky. Nobody was looking at him. He walked on, and the feeling walked with him to the end of the gallery, and at the door it was gone.

The office was not large, which had always struck Emeric as a deliberate piece of theatre and which he now suspected was not theatre at all. Two men on the outer door. A third at the end of the corridor, positioned where a man is positioned when somebody wants footsteps announced rather than stopped.

Harrold did not sit. He came around the desk, and he stood close, close enough to touch, and he spoke in a voice that died at arm's length, and did not look at his son for most of it.

It came out in pieces, with pauses between them, in the order a man gives instructions rather than the order a man explains himself.

The exile was real. Real in every mechanism that mattered, on paper, in the rolls, in the treasury, in every place a thing has to be real to hold. To every man in this kingdom it was a fall, and it would go on being a fall, and Emeric was to do nothing anywhere at any time to make any man think otherwise.

Pause.

It was his. Not Corbin's. His brother had won nothing today and would be permitted to believe he had. Harrold had been waiting three years for a pretext ugly enough that the court would read ruin instead of strategy, and had not been able to manufacture one, because a manufactured scandal has a manufacturer. Then his son had stood up at a banquet and insulted a foreign king's daughter over her father's business, at Harrold's own table, in front of two hundred witnesses.

Emeric did not say anything to that.

There was a place in the conversation where a question belonged. Whether he had done it on purpose. Whether a drunk man with fourteen years of practice at being underestimated had looked at a twenty-year fixed price and decided, somewhere under the wine, that this was the one worth spending. Neither of them went near it. They stood a foot apart in a small room and left the space entirely empty, and both of them knew the shape of what was not in it.

Pause.

Fourteen years of the mask had bought time. That was all it had ever bought and Harrold said so without softening it. They had not lost interest. They had lost the target. And the capital was where they had always had reach, every single year, without interruption, which meant that the only variable left on the board was distance.

South, then. Kaldo.

A walled town on a big river, a barony four hundred years old, named for a knight called Kal who had taken land instead of a captaincy and then could not produce an heir to put on it. He had left the whole of it to his steward's family rather than to a cousin. The Wexlers had held it since. They had kept their own name.

Take the south road. Twelve days by carriage. Past Wayford the forest runs most of a day and the road through it is poor and has been poor for thirty years. Rest the horses before that stretch and not after it.

The gift tonight would include a chest of uncut crystals. No crest on them, no seal, nothing entered anywhere. A manasmith will buy them in any town in the kingdom and ask nothing.

Write nothing down. Trust no courier Harrold had not sent himself. Eat what the escort eats, out of the same pot, and never first. Do not come back until sent for.

It was the taster's rule. The taster, at least, had apologized.

That was the whole of it. Eleven or twelve minutes, standing up, in a room with a door somebody was listening at.

Emeric had one question. He had been carrying it since the hall and had turned it over for most of a night, and it was not about the road or the barony or the men who wanted him dead.

"How much of what I said to her was wrong?"

His father stepped back.

It was not a large movement. Half a pace, no more, the kind of adjustment a man makes when he is about to address a room rather than a person, and Emeric saw it happen and understood what it meant a quarter of a second before the sound arrived.

"It is done."

It went to the walls. It went past the walls. It went under the outer door and down the corridor to the man standing at the far end of it, and it was delivered in the round, carrying, unhurried voice of a king closing a subject, and every syllable of it was pitched for somebody who was not his son.

Emeric stood there with it.

He could not get the thing open. That was the trouble. He knew it for a lie, immediately and completely, by the only code the two of them had ever shared, and knowing that got him no closer to anything, because there were two lies available and the volume did not tell him which. Either he had been wrong about the deal, and his father wanted the corridor to hear that the boy had been wrong. Or he had been right, and his father wanted the corridor to hear that the matter was closed when it was not closed at all, and would not be closed for years.

He waited for the correction.

He gave it four seconds, which in a room that small is a long time, and he was conscious of counting, and conscious that his father would know he was counting.

It did not come.

Harrold did not whisper a word of it, and that was an answer, and it was worse than either of the lies, because a man who will not whisper the truth to you in a room built for whispering has made a decision about what you are permitted to carry.

There were three things his father would not say in that office, and Emeric noticed all three as they went past.

Who had tried to kill him at seven. What he was actually for, since the amount of machinery being spent on him made no sense at all for a spare third son. And how long. Not one date anywhere in eleven minutes. Not a season, not a condition, not a description of what finished would look like if it ever arrived.

Harrold moved past each of them the way water moves past a stone it has been going around for fifteen years, without turbulence, without appearing to notice, in a channel worn so deep that a man watching would call it the shape of the river.

And here was the thing Emeric could not do.

He had never once doubted that he was being protected. That was not in question and had never been in question, not for an hour of the whole day. The man had turned a kingdom over for him. The man had built him a mask at eight years old and held it in place for fourteen years and burned a night of his own court to get him out of the city alive.

He had also never in his life received a sentence from his father that was not operational. Not one. Every warm thing in the entire arrangement was a thing arranged rather than a thing said, and Emeric had no instrument that separated a father from a king running an asset well, and had stopped some years ago believing that such an instrument existed.

On Earth he had traced a force down through a stack of parts to the ground, member by member, and found in four minutes a flaw that three engineers had put their names to. That was the whole of what he was good for. Take the thing apart, run the load through every member, find the one that will not carry it.

He had every part of Harrold Randillia laid out on the table in front of him, labeled, in order, and he could not make himself run the load through that member.

He did not decide not to. He simply arrived at it, and turned, and worked on something else.

Then his father raised his voice.

He did it without any transition at all, which was its own small piece of craftsmanship, and he did it facing the doors.

"You will go south. You will be quiet. You will cost this house nothing further, and that is the entire of what I require of you, which is less than I have required of any man in my service in thirty years." A beat, perfectly measured. "I expect you will find some way to disappoint me in it regardless."

It reached the corridor. It reached the pages. It would reach, by tomorrow evening, every servant in this castle with a cousin who sold stories in a tavern, and by the end of the month it would reach three other kingdoms, and it was a good line, economical and cruel and eminently repeatable, and it had been built to travel.

Emeric bowed to precisely the depth the room required and not a finger further, because that was what the corridor needed to hear about, and let himself out.

The banquet was in two hours. He walked down through the castle toward it carrying a stone in his pocket he could not explain, a road he had been given in a whisper, and one sentence delivered at the top of a king's voice with the whole of the truth hidden somewhere inside it.

Either he had been wrong, and it was finished.

Or he had been right, and it was not.
