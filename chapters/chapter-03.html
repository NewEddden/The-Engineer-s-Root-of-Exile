Chapter 3 - Downwind

They made the rest of the road in four days and nobody said much on any of them.

Four guards now, and Petram besides, bound and silent in the back of the carriage where nobody had to look at him directly to know he was there. Rylan drove, quiet in a way that hadn't been in him before the fire. Declan and Osric rode close flank instead of their usual loose formation, eyes on the treeline more than the road. Ward rode nearest the carriage door with his forearm wrapped tight under his sleeve and his jaw set the way it got when he wasn't going to mention the pain and wasn't going to be talked into resting it either.

Emeric hadn't slept right since. He kept catching his mind running the same loop, the one an engineer's brain runs on a problem it hasn't closed. Input, failure point, root cause. Every pass came back to the same place and stopped.

He could not separate the drug from himself. That was the whole difficulty. There was no instrument for it and no way to build one, and the question would sit there unanswered for the rest of his life unless something similar happened again, which was a hell of a way to have to run an experiment.

What he did know was that something in him still worked exactly the way it had at seven years old, and he hadn't known that until it nearly cost a man his life. The same man now riding one-armed a few feet from the carriage door.

Kaldo announced itself before he saw it.

The smell arrived first and there was nothing faint about it. Woodsmoke on top, and under the woodsmoke something slickly sweet and worse, rot that got into the back of the throat and stayed. A burial ground too close to a well. Latrines that had never been dug anywhere sensible.

He'd read about this exact failure once. Medieval sanitation, disease vectors, always in the past tense, always something solved centuries before he was born. Standing downwind of it as a lived condition rather than a paragraph was a different experience entirely.

Then the walls. Low and old, patched here and there with stone that didn't match, repair work done by people who couldn't afford to do it properly and had stopped waiting for anyone else to. The gate guard who waved them through looked less like a soldier and more like a man who had been handed a spear and told where to stand.

Nobody had ridden out to meet them. Emeric had never wanted a parade of arrival in his life, but the absence of one raised a question the local baron had no way of answering correctly. Every noble in the kingdom knew the etiquette for a man higher up the ladder coming through. So either he had chosen not to come, or nobody had told him a prince was coming at all.

Emeric couldn't decide which of those was worse.

Small was generous for the castle. It had been built for a barony twice this size, back when Kaldo still had a working port and men who weren't dead or conscripted, and the gap between what it was built to hold and what it held now sat in every corner.

The drawbridge chain had been replaced with rope at some point, thick enough to do the job and ugly enough that nobody had planned on it lasting. A section of the outer wall sagged where water had gotten in behind the stone and never dried out. Same patched-not-fixed logic as the town wall, just higher up and better hidden from anyone who wasn't looking.

Inside wasn't better. The entry hall had been sized for a household staff that no longer existed, half the torch brackets empty, footsteps carrying in a way that told him more about the payroll than any account book would have. A steward met them, thin and tired, doing the work of what should have been three people. He didn't apologize for the state of the place. It was written into the walls already.

A corridor that had clearly held art on both sides at some point led to the office of the current baron.

"Baron Kaldo, I presume," Emeric said, with the doubt already sitting in it.

Kal had been a knight. Generations down the line that discipline should still have shown somewhere in the posture. Knights raised the way Kal was raised took it home with them, and it usually stuck for a few generations at minimum.

This wasn't one of his.

The man behind the desk didn't stand, and didn't correct him quickly enough for it to read as anything but deliberate. He let the pause do its work first, leaving a prince standing there being wrong a beat longer than manners allowed.

"Wexler," he said finally. He still didn't rise. He didn't offer a hand. "Baron Wexler. Kaldo's the land, Your Highness. Not my name." Another pause, just long enough to land. "The line hasn't run blood-to-blood since old Kal himself. No children of his own, so he handed it to the man he trusted most instead of a son he never had. My family kept the name we came with. Kaldo kept ours."

He had corrected other people the same way. It was in the shape of the sentences, worn smooth by use, and in the fact that he had clearly never once minded doing it. The disdain wasn't in his face. It was in how little effort he spent hiding it, the specific laziness of a man who had priced this visitor and decided he wasn't worth the performance of respect, only the minimum required not to be called insolent to his face.

Emeric didn't take offense, which surprised him a little. Wexler was senior, and for most of a life Emeric only half remembered, he had been known as a waste of a prince. It was fair enough that people treated him like less. Making an issue of it would have bought him friction and nothing else, and if he ever did decide this baron had to go, he'd want the town on his side when it happened.

He'd been here an hour. Blood had already been spilled on the road. He had no read at all yet on how this town would take any of it, and he made himself write that down where he could see it, because a man with a theory and no data is just a man with an opinion and a title.

They exchanged the pleasantries the situation required and wasted a quarter hour doing it. Then the steward showed him up to a room that was nothing special and would do, and everything came up from the carriage, and Emeric went to sleep with plans for the morning.

Morning came through a window that didn't quite fit its frame anymore.

"We're really not in the castle anymore," he said, and didn't waste any more of the day on it than that. He ate standing, whatever the steward's kitchen had managed on short notice, and went looking for Rylan before he'd finished the last of it.

"You said you hadn't seen your family in four months," Emeric said. "Take me with you."

Rylan blinked at him. "Your Highness, I can go alone, there's no need-"

"There's every need. I haven't seen the town. You have a reason to walk through it that isn't me inspecting anything."

It wasn't entirely honest, and Rylan's face said he knew it wasn't, but he didn't push. Ward did. He said so with a look first, then with actual words when the look didn't land.

"You're not walking through an unmapped town with one guard and a title nobody here respects."

"Rylan's enough."

"Rylan's a father going home to his family. He's not watching the street behind you while he does it, and he shouldn't have to be." Ward's jaw was doing the thing it did when he'd already decided and was explaining as a courtesy. "I'm not asking permission. I'm telling you how it's going to work."

"And how is it going to work?"

"You won't see me."

"Fine. Then before you vanish, Rylan says there's a healer in this town. Imani. Have her look at the arm."

"The arm's fine."

"The arm's five days old and you've been sleeping on it."

"You won't see me," Ward said again, which was the whole of his answer to that too.

He came anyway, just not at Emeric's side. By the gatehouse he had already dropped back and gone quiet in a way that had nothing to do with the wound, and the second time Emeric looked for him he wasn't anywhere a guard was supposed to be.

The walk did more work than any report could have.

The smell that had announced Kaldo from a mile out only got worse inside it, and Emeric started tracing it backward from the symptom the same way he'd trace any bad system. The burial ground sat too close to the water. That much needed no local's help. The latrines sat too close to the wells.

He couldn't say yet exactly what was wrong, not off one walk through streets he didn't know, but he could already feel the scale of it under the smell, a shape he recognized from a textbook instead of a street.

The people he passed had eyes caught somewhere between hope and resignation.

Somewhere past the second corner he felt it and had nothing to call it. Not a sound. Not a sensation he could point to anywhere on his body. A pull, low and without direction, a weight settling behind his eyes that hadn't been there a breath earlier. He stopped walking without deciding to.

"Your Highness?"

"Nothing." It wasn't nothing. It nagged at him the way a machine running one gear tooth off true nagged at him, a wrongness too small to name and too specific to be imagination. He looked back down the street they'd come up. A woman airing bedding out a window. A dog asleep in a doorway.

He kept walking, and the weight faded into the background without disappearing, the way a smell fades once you stop attending to it.

He was a mage now. He still hadn't gotten used to the word applying to him even inside his own head. He knew, distantly, that mages were supposed to have some feel for nearby mana, heat off a stove sensed without touching it. Nobody had ever told him what that was supposed to be like from the inside, and he had nothing to calibrate against. A decent-sized personal pool handed him capacity, not experience.

Input observed, no working theory, revisit if it repeats. He had no framework for it yet and he knew better than to force one on that little data.

Rylan's house was small and clean in the specific way poor houses stayed clean, cleanliness being one of the few things left that didn't cost money. Nessa answered the door and went still for half a second too long before remembering to bow, and Emeric got down to her level fast enough to stop it, which he suspected was the wrong custom and did anyway.

Her son Finn hid behind her leg. Her daughter Wren didn't, and stared at Emeric with the flat unimpressed curiosity of a child who hadn't yet learned that princes were supposed to be interesting.

He stepped back and let the reunion happen without hovering over it, let Rylan be a husband and a father for a few minutes instead of a guard. It cost him nothing and told him plenty. Neighbors came out for a guard's homecoming in a way they would never have come out for a prince's arrival, and even then there was a wariness underneath it, the caution people keep in reserve for nobility that shows up expecting gratitude.

When the reunion had had its time, he asked what he'd come to ask.

"What was the town like before? Before the war."

Nessa considered it longer than the question seemed to need.

"Busier. My parents talked about it more than I remember it myself. I was young when the men left, ten, maybe. One week the harbor had hands on every dock, the next it didn't, and nobody explained where they'd gone past 'the crown needs them.'"

"There was a shipwright then. Fletcher. Built half of what floated out of that harbor, and my father swore you could tell one of his hulls from shore by the way it sat the water. He went where the rest of the men went. Nobody's built a real boat here since."

"Nobody said which war."

"Would it have mattered? We weren't the ones deciding to fight it." No heat in it, just fact. "Then the plague came a couple of years after, once some of the men who came back were already trying to put things right. Took the ones the war didn't. Men, women, children, animals. It didn't ask who'd already paid."

"How long did that last?"

"The bad part? A year. Maybe not even." She said it the way people say numbers they have gone over often. "It came, it took what it took, and then it stopped taking them by the dozen."

"But it didn't stop."

"Nothing stops here." She shrugged, and it wasn't bitterness, it was arithmetic. "People kept dying after. They still do. Slower. Fewer at a time. Nobody bothers separating the two, if that's what you're asking. Dead's dead, and it all came out of the same ground."

Emeric wrote that down in his head twice.

"The census had this place at over four thousand once."

"It did. Ask anyone over fifty what it used to be and they'll tell you like it happened last spring." She glanced toward the window, toward the street. "Half that now. Less, maybe. Feels cursed some days, if you let yourself think that way. The old folks talk about it like the town is still that place underneath, just resting. The rest of us don't have anywhere else to go, so I suppose we agree with them whether we believe it or not."

Four thousand once. Half of it now. A war that was already over couldn't be helped. The rest of it might be, and he was going to need to be careful about what he meant by the rest of it.

Because there was a thing wrong with the sentence everyone in this town was using. They said the plague the way you say the name of one event. Nessa had just described two shapes and given them one name. A year that emptied the place, and a slow steady bleed for five years after. Diseases did not generally behave like both of those at once, and if they weren't the same thing, then finding a fix for the second one would tell him nothing about the first, and everyone including him would happily believe otherwise.

He also had no idea how to explain any of it to a person here. Creatures too small to see, living in the water, some of them helping and some of them killing. Said out loud to a room of people who had been cheated by better liars than him, that sentence got a man burned or bled or quietly declared mad, prince or not. Field research, then, and time to think.

When Rylan had finished with the last of the pleasantries, he came along, and the two of them walked the whole town.

By the end of it Emeric was sure of one thing, which was less than he wanted and more than he'd had that morning. Whatever was still killing people here was coming out of the water.

The same pattern kept repeating wherever he looked. Latrines dug downhill and too close to the bank. Burial grounds sitting just upriver of the wells the town drank from. And no sign anywhere that a single person had ever connected those two facts to each other.

It wasn't ignorance in the way he'd have mocked back on Earth. Nobody here had access to a framework that made the connection obvious. It was bad geometry, decades old, never questioned because nobody had ever been handed a reason to question it.

It also wasn't enough. Bad water killed people steadily, in ones and twos, for years, which described the last five years perfectly and described the year before them not at all. He kept that where he could see it and didn't let himself pretend otherwise, which took actual effort, because the tidy version was right there and it would have felt very good to believe.

The pull found him once more on that circuit, in an alley narrow enough that he had to turn sideways past a stacked cart. Sharper this time. Sharp enough that he looked up and found empty tile and a gap of grey sky.

Nothing there. It had a direction, though. One he couldn't have described to anyone, sitting closer to his spine than his ears and thinning out the second he tried to look straight at it, the way a faint star does when you stop watching it sideways.

He didn't mention it to Rylan. He wasn't sure yet it was worth mentioning.

Near the well closest to the burial ground, a woman filling a bucket saw Rylan first and started to nod a greeting before she registered who was walking beside him. The nod died halfway through. She finished filling and left faster than a full bucket should have allowed, water sloshing over the rim with every hurried step.

"Was it me?" Emeric asked once she was out of earshot.

"Wasn't you specifically." Rylan didn't slow down. "Her boy didn't come back from conscription. Crown took him, Crown never said what happened to him, Crown's the reason she's hauling her own water instead of having someone else do it. You're the Crown today, whatever else you are on other days."

"That's not something I can fix by being right about the water."

"No," Rylan agreed. "That one you don't fix. You just don't make it worse."
