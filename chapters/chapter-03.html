Chapter 3 - Gifts

They had set the hall for two hundred and eleven. Emeric counted on the way in, because counting was something to do with his eyes, and because it seemed worth knowing how many people would watch him leave.

Two hundred and eleven guests. Two of them were related to him.

It was the custom. Any prince or princess leaving the capital got a banquet and gifts on the last night, whether they were going to a marriage or a governorship or a sickbed in the country. Nobody could call it favor, because everyone got one. It meant the king could give his disgraced son a send-off without anyone reading a word of it as forgiveness. The hall knew that. They had come to see how he'd take it.

So he gave them the drunk.

It took some judging. Fourteen years of practice had made the act easy, though it was several acts, each fitted to whoever was looking. For Lord Aldane, who sat near the dais with a face like a closed ledger, Emeric gave the full performance: the loose shoulders, the cup held too high, a laugh at something nobody had said. Aldane's house was said to be as old as the royal line itself, and Aldane behaved as if the royal line ought to remember that. He looked at Emeric the way a man looks at a stain.

For Varek, he gave almost nothing. Varek had been a soldier for thirty years. Soldiers read how a man stands before they hear a word he says, and Varek would see through a slouch in a heartbeat. So Emeric stood more or less straight near Varek and kept his cup at his side, and let the old man draw what conclusions he liked.

The chair to his father's left, where Waylon should have been, was empty.

A page came down the length of the table with a message delivered in a careful voice: Prince Waylon sent his regrets. He'd been out hunting since yesterday morning and was not expected back tonight.

Emeric thanked him. The alibi had been prepared well before anyone needed it, which meant somebody had known Waylon wouldn't come. Probably Waylon.

His younger brother had been three when their mother died, and four the year Emeric started drinking. He would have no memory of a brother who'd been anything else. From where Waylon stood, his big brother had turned into a joke the moment he was old enough to notice, and a small boy draws the obvious conclusion: whatever Emeric had been, Waylon hadn't been worth staying sober for. He'd spent the rest of his growing up pretending not to care. It was a good pretense. Emeric had believed it for years.

He set that aside. It would keep.

Maeve's chair was empty too, and that one he could account for. His sister was in Emberfell with her mother, away in the south-west under the smoking mountain, and his sentence was a day old. Nobody had told her; there hadn't been time. By the time a letter reached her the story would have passed through a hundred mouths, all of them speaking at the volume of a room. She would hear it the way everyone in this palace heard everything: secondhand, and shaped by whoever told it first.

He minded that more than the empty chair. Maeve was the one person in the family whose warmth had never needed a witness.

Zale's absence was older and colder. His second brother had left Randillia seven years ago, the year he turned twenty, and not by accident. Zale had looked at Corbin early, earlier than anyone, and seen a man who would one day want the throne with nobody standing near it, and who would count a capable younger brother as standing too near. So Zale had gone abroad before anyone had to decide what to do about him. Nobody at court admitted to knowing where he was now, and Emeric suspected nobody did. Nobody had ever needed to decide anything about Emeric. Looking at the place where Zale would have sat, he saw that his father's rule had covered them both. A threat gets removed. Zale had simply removed himself first.

The queens' places told their own story. Queen Sabina, his father's first wife, was somewhere in the palace and had not come down. Queen Rowena, the second, mother of Maeve and Zale, had come to Randillia as part of a treaty, and gone home to Emberfell a year or two after Amara died, when her brother died and his seat passed to her. Emeric had been nine and already practicing at being nobody. He remembered her carriage leaving the yard, and nothing anyone said about why. And the third queen was Amara, and she was dead, and her place was not set at all.



The gifts came after the meat.

A steward stood at the foot of the dais and announced each one in a voice built for halls, and the givers came forward in order of rank.

His father's came first. A leather pouch, heavy enough that the steward held it with both hands, and he announced it as fifty Gold Rand, the crown's entitlement to the holder of the title of Kaldo. Not a father's gift. A sum the law owed to a place-name, delivered once. Behind it two servants set down a chest of dark wood, and when they lifted the lid the hall made a soft sound. Uncut mana crystals of every color the elements came in, packed close in straw.

Around him the room read it as custom done generously. A few faces read it as a father's soft heart, and he marked those. Emeric read it as a delivery. No crest on the chest. No record of where the crystals came from. Exactly what had been whispered to him that afternoon, carried out in front of two hundred witnesses as though it were nothing at all.

He thanked his father at a speaking voice. His father nodded at the same volume. Nothing passed between them that anyone could have repeated.

Corbin came second.

His elder brother carried a flat case of polished wood and opened it himself, at the edge of the dais, tilted so that the nearest tables could see. Nine grey stones sat in nine velvet hollows. They were dull, about the size of a hen's egg, each carved all over in a fine lattice. Everyone at the near tables knew what they were. Hollow Crystals. Pretty, old, and useless: in all of recorded history nobody had made one do anything at all. People kept them for the carving.

"For you," Corbin said pleasantly. "Unchanging. Just like you."

A little laughter, from the right tables.

Then Corbin leaned in to hand him the case, and said something lower, though not low enough to stay private. Three or four people heard it. Emeric watched them hear it.

"Your mother gave you one of these once. Do you remember?"

Emeric's right hand was in his pocket. It had gone there on its own. His fingers were resting on a smooth grey stone, carved in a fine repeating pattern, worn soft.

He didn't remember. But he knew, now, what it was.

"I don't," he said, and smiled the drunk's smile, and took the case.

Corbin was never cruel in private. Emeric could find no memory of a single unkind word between them with a door closed. His brother saved it for rooms like this, with at least three witnesses, and kept it small and regular, a little maintenance on the story of who Emeric was. It was, Emeric thought with some admiration, very good statecraft. You didn't let a story like that go stale.

How Corbin knew about the stone was another question. He set it beside Waylon and Maeve.

Aldane gave him a box of carved wooden soldiers.

It was a child's toy. Painted, well made, a full company with a little drummer. Aldane presented it without a word and went back to his seat, and the meaning was so plain that nobody bothered to laugh. Emeric thanked him with real enthusiasm and noted the name again.

Varek came up with something folded over his arm. It was a coat, heavy wool with an oiled outer layer and a lining quilted thick. A soldier's coat for bad weather and long roads.

"You'll need this more than a crystal where you're going, Your Highness," Varek said.

He met Emeric's eyes when he said it, and Emeric understood that the old soldier had been watching him stand all evening. He held the coat and thanked Varek, and for once the thanks came out a little wrong for the drunk. Varek's mouth moved, very slightly, and he went back to his place.

Armand came next, slowly, an old man who had once been the best crystal theorist the tower had and who still walked as if the floor might not hold. He'd been Emeric's tutor, years ago, before the drinking. He carried a notebook, fat and soft at the corners, its spine broken and mended twice.

"My notes," Armand said. "Thirty years of them. On crystals. Theory, mostly." He held it out. "I never finished teaching you properly. I should have."

He didn't meet Emeric's eyes. He looked at the notebook while Emeric took it, as if it were the notebook he was saying goodbye to, and then he went back to his seat. Emeric watched him sit, and watched him not look up, and filed it without knowing what it was. Guilt, he thought. Over what, he had no idea.



The last of them was Augustine.

The churchman of the Light didn't bring a gift. He rose from his place near the king, which was a place of honor, and stood on the step of the dais with his hands folded, and the hall went quiet for him without being asked. He was one of the oldest voices at court. Everyone said so. He had been at the king's side since before the king was king.

Emeric looked at him and something didn't fit.

It took him a moment to find it. He had memories of Augustine from every year after seven, at his father's elbow, at feast days, at the edge of every important room. In all of them it was the same face, unchanged in fifteen years. And the face in front of him now was a man of perhaps fifty, with lines at the eyes and a grey that hadn't finished coming in.

On Earth he had sat in enough hospital rooms to know what a man of eighty looked like. This wasn't it.

Nobody else in the hall seemed to find anything to notice. People here aged slowly, and they thought it was simply how people aged. Still, there was slow and there was this. Emeric took it as a measurement that didn't fit the rest of the readings. He didn't know what to do with it, so he left it where it lay.

"May the Light find you where you are going," Augustine said, in a warm, carrying voice, "and make of you something better than you have managed to be."

It was a blessing in form. The hall heard it as one, and murmured approval. Emeric heard a verdict that had dressed itself as mercy so it could be said out loud at dinner. He bowed his head and thanked the Light.



The banquet went on for another two hours.

Nobody mentioned Alina. Nobody mentioned the crystal price, or the yield reports, or the fact that a prince had stood up in this same hall the night before and asked an ugly question that nobody had answered. Two hundred and eleven people had agreed, without speaking, to forget it.

And nobody whispered to him. Not once, all night. He went back over the whole evening to be sure. Every word spoken to him had been said at the volume of the room, for whoever might be listening.

By his father's rule, then, he hadn't heard a true sentence since he left the office that afternoon.

When it was finally over he got up, with the coat over his arm and the notebook in his hand and two servants behind him carrying the chest and the case. He crossed the floor toward the great doors at the far end, toward Ward, waiting there like part of the wall.

Halfway across, someone passed close behind him.

He didn't see who. He felt it, a brush across a sense he didn't have a name for, like a draft against skin that wasn't there. It came and went.

He turned. There was nobody behind him. Only the long hall, the tables being cleared, the banners hanging green over grey, a few late guests talking in a knot by the far pillar, none of them looking his way.

Nerves, he decided. Too much wine last night and too little sleep, in a body he'd had for a day.

He turned back toward the doors and let it go.
