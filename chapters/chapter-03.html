Chapter 3 - Downwind

Kaldo announced itself two miles out, and it announced itself by smell.

Woodsmoke on top, which was fine. Under the woodsmoke something slickly sweet that got into the back of the throat and stayed there. Emeric had read about this. He had read about it in the past tense, in a language nobody here spoke, in books that treated it as a problem solved several centuries before he was born. Standing downwind of it as a lived condition was a different experience from reading the paragraph.

A burial ground too close to a well. Latrines dug wherever the digging was easiest. He had the diagnosis before he had the town, which he would later decide was the worst possible order to get it in.

Four guards now, and Petram besides, bound in the back of the carriage where nobody had to look at him directly to know he was there. Rylan drove, quiet in a way that hadn't been in him before the fire. Declan and Osric rode close flank instead of their usual loose spread, eyes on the treeline more than the road. Ward rode nearest the carriage door with his forearm wrapped tight under his sleeve and his jaw set the way it got when he wasn't going to mention the pain and wasn't going to be talked into resting it either.

Nobody had said much since the fire burned down. Emeric hadn't slept right. His mind kept running the same loop, the one that used to run on a bad assembly. Input, failure point, root cause. Every pass came back to the same answer, and the answer was not that he'd frozen. The freeze was a symptom. The finding was that something in him still worked exactly the way it had at seven years old, and he hadn't known that until it cost a man the use of his arm.

He had spent one of the sleepless nights on the other problem, which was who to tell.

The arithmetic was ugly and it was short. Two of six had been bought. The six had been assigned to him out of the palace, by a captain, from a list, and he had watched the list get made in the sense that he had not watched it get made and had never once thought to ask who did. Whoever bought Petram and Garrin had either bought them after the assignment, which meant they knew the roster, or had arranged the assignment, which meant they wrote it. Both roads ran back through the same building.

So a letter to the capital was a letter delivered to the problem. That was the first reason and it was sound.

The second reason he liked less. A prince who reports a survived assassination is a prince announcing two facts at once: that he lived, and that he noticed. The second was worth more than the first. Somebody in Randillia had spent real coin on the theory that Emeric Stelan Randillia was a soft target who would not see it coming, and that theory had very nearly been right, and there was no version of the next year where he was better off correcting it.

Let them wonder. Let the news arrive in whatever shape it arrived in, from Garrin, from a rumor, from nothing at all. A man they'd already priced as harmless was the cheapest armor available and he happened to be wearing it already.

It took him most of a day's riding to build that argument and about four seconds to be satisfied with it, and at no point in either did it occur to him that he had just reinvented, from first principles, the exact doctrine his father had handed him when he was eight.

Nobody came out to meet them at the gate, which told him more than a parade would have.

Every noble in the kingdom knew the etiquette when someone higher up the ladder came through your walls. You met the road. So either the baron of this place had chosen not to, or nobody had bothered to tell him. Emeric turned both over and couldn't decide which was worse, and then decided that not being able to tell them apart was itself the useful piece of information.

The walls were low and old, patched with stone that didn't match, repair work done by people who couldn't afford to do it properly and had stopped waiting for anyone who could. The gate guard who waved them through looked less like a soldier than like a man who had been handed a spear and told where to stand. Ward said who they were and who they were there to see. The guard's face did something complicated and did not resolve into anything, and he pointed them up the hill.

Small was generous. The castle had been built for a barony twice this size, back when Kaldo still had a working port and men who weren't dead or conscripted, and the gap between what it was built to hold and what it held now sat in every corner of it. The drawbridge chain had been replaced with rope, thick enough to do the job and ugly enough that nobody had planned on it lasting this long. A section of outer wall sagged where water had gotten in behind the stone and never dried out. Patched, not fixed. Same logic as the town wall, further up the hill and better hidden from anyone who wasn't looking.

Inside was worse in the quiet way. The entry hall had been sized for a household that no longer existed, half the torch brackets empty, footsteps carrying in a manner that told him more about the payroll than an account book would have. A steward met them, thin and tired, doing the work of three people and not apologizing for any of it, because the apology was already written into the walls. A corridor that had plainly held art on both sides at some point led to the baron's office.

"Baron Kaldo, I presume," Emeric said.

The man behind the desk didn't stand, and didn't correct him quickly enough for it to read as anything but deliberate. He let the pause do a piece of work first, leaving a prince standing in a doorway being wrong a beat longer than manners allowed.

Emeric watched him do it and watched him watch it land, and understood that he had just been tested and had failed, and that the failure had been the point of the greeting. A prince who did not know the name of the family holding the land he had been sent to was a prince without a single person in the kingdom feeding him anything. No secretary. No steward of his own. No letters. Nobody's ear. Wexler had spent one silence and bought a complete inventory of Emeric's resources, and the inventory was empty.

It was, in its way, very good work. Emeric filed the technique.

"Wexler," the man said at last. He still didn't rise. He didn't offer a hand. "Baron Wexler. Kaldo's the land, Your Highness. Not my name." Another pause, measured out. "The line hasn't run blood-to-blood since old Kal himself. No children of his own, so he handed it to the man he trusted most instead of a son he never had. My family kept the name we came with. Kaldo kept ours."

He had made that correction before. Many times, by the sound of it, and had never once minded making it.

"Four generations," Emeric said, because the number had come up out of Emeric's memory unasked and he had learned to trust the things that did that.

"Four."

"Through the war."

"Through the war and the plague after it."

"And eleven days ago somebody rode in from the capital and told you the crown was giving it to me."

Wexler's expression did not change, which was the entire answer, delivered faster and more completely than any words would have managed.

Emeric had assembled it on the walk down the corridor, out of the empty torch brackets and the rope on the drawbridge and a gate guard who hadn't known whether to bow. It was not contempt for a disgraced prince. Or it was, but the contempt was the surface of it, and underneath sat a man of sixty-odd years watching the only thing his family had ever had get handed to a royal embarrassment as a convenient place to put him. Wexler had been told to keep the barony running for the man taking it off him. He had not been asked.

The old Emeric would have taken the insolence personally, because the old Emeric had taken everything personally and had considered that a form of self-respect. He found that he did take it personally. He noted the flush of it going up the back of his neck, catalogued it, set it down, and left it where it was.

Because the other half of the problem was that he could not remove this man and both of them knew it. The steward was Wexler's. The gate guards were Wexler's. Four generations of every debt, marriage, grudge and favor in a town of two thousand people ran through the chair Wexler had not gotten out of. Emeric had a piece of paper, three healthy guards, one of them injured, and a prisoner. Removing Wexler was affordable in exactly the sense that setting fire to your own house is affordable.

So he did the only thing that cost nothing, which was to be pleasant, and they spent a quarter of an hour on the pleasantries the situation required. Emeric gave him nothing to sharpen and took nothing away, and by the end of it Wexler had adjusted his estimate very slightly, which Emeric could see happening and regarded as a mistake on his own part.

He waited until the pleasantries were finished to spend his one coin.

"I've a prisoner in the carriage. He'll need holding."

"We've cells."

"He'll be held in whatever room is nearest mine, with my men on the door."

Wexler looked at him then, properly, for the first time since the doorway.

It was a small thing and an unmistakable one. A man who does not trust the house he is standing in says so out loud, or he says nothing and quietly keeps his own hands on the keys, and the second is worth noticing because it means he has thought about it. Emeric had just told him, in one sentence and without a word of accusation, that he had counted the ways a prisoner can die in a cell and did not intend to find out which one Kaldo used.

"As you like, Your Highness," Wexler said, and did not take offense, and that was worse. He had not been insulted. He had been informed.

The steward showed Emeric up to a room that was nothing special and would do. By dark everything was up from the carriage, and Petram was two doors down with Declan sitting outside it, and Emeric lay in the cold and made his list for the morning.

Petram was not on it.

He had the reasons ready, and the reasons were sound, which was the trouble with them. A man left alone in an unfamiliar room for a week talks better than a man questioned the night he arrives, because fear needs time to work and because silence tells a prisoner nothing about what you already know. Rush him now and he learns the shape of your ignorance. Wait, and he starts filling the quiet with guesses about what you have.

All of that was true. He had checked it twice and it held up.

It was also true that he did not want to be in a room with Petram, and that the not-wanting had arrived first, and the argument second, and he had noticed the order and then gone ahead and used the argument anyway.

He slept badly and got up early.

Morning came through a window that no longer quite fit its frame.

"Right," he said to the window. "We're not in the castle anymore."

He ate standing, whatever the kitchen had managed on short notice, and went looking for Rylan before he'd finished it.

"You said you hadn't seen your family in four months. Take me with you."

Rylan blinked. "Your Highness, I can go alone, there's no need to-"

"There's every need. I haven't seen the town, and you have a reason to walk through it that isn't a prince inspecting anything."

It wasn't entirely honest and Rylan's face said he knew it, but he let it go.

Ward did not.

"You're not walking an unmapped town with one guard and a title nobody here respects."

"Rylan's enough."

"Rylan's a father going home to his children. He won't be watching the street behind you, and he shouldn't have to be." Ward's jaw was doing the thing it did when he had already decided and was explaining as a courtesy. "I'm not asking. I'm telling you how it's going to work."

"And how is it going to work?"

"You won't see me."

"Fine. Then before you vanish, Rylan says there's a healer in the town. Imani. Have her look at the arm."

"The arm's fine."

"The arm's six days old and you've been sleeping on it."

"You won't see me," Ward said, which was the whole of his answer to that too.

He came. Just not at Emeric's side. By the gatehouse he had already dropped back, and the second time Emeric looked for him he was not anywhere a guard was supposed to be, which Emeric decided to find reassuring rather than the other thing.

The smell that had announced Kaldo from two miles out only got worse inside it, and Emeric started tracing it backward from the symptom the way he'd trace any bad system. The burial ground sat above the water. That much needed no local's help. The latrines sat near the wells, or near enough. He couldn't say yet how bad, not off one walk through streets he didn't know, but he could feel the scale of it underneath the smell, a shape he recognized from a textbook and was now standing in.

The people he passed had eyes caught somewhere between hope and resignation.

Somewhere past the second corner he felt it, and had nothing to call it.

Not a sound. Not a sensation he could locate anywhere on his body. A pull, low and without direction, a weight settling behind the eyes that had not been there a breath earlier. He stopped walking without deciding to.

"Your Highness?"

"Nothing."

It wasn't nothing. It nagged the way a machine running one gear tooth off true nagged, a wrongness too small to name and too specific to be imagination. He looked back down the street. A woman airing bedding out a window. A dog asleep in a doorway. He kept walking and the weight faded into the background without going away, the way a smell fades once you stop attending to it.

It came back before they reached Rylan's street, in an alley narrow enough that he had to turn sideways past a stacked cart. Sharper. Sharp enough that he looked up and found empty tile and a strip of grey sky.

This one had a direction. He couldn't have described it to anyone. It sat closer to his spine than his ears and thinned out the moment he looked straight at it, the way a faint star does when you stop watching it sideways.

Three, he thought.

The banquet hall, when a man passed close behind him and every hair on his neck stood up. The treeline past the horses, the night the tea went round. Now an alley in a town four days east of both.

He fitted the line the way he had fitted a thousand others, because that was the one thing he was still reliably good for, and the line came out clean and he did not like where it went. Three points, one following the other along the east road, arriving where he arrived.

Then he asked the next question, which was the question that had made him useful on Earth and was about to make him wrong here.

By what mechanism?

Because that was the test. A correlation with no mechanism behind it was a coincidence wearing a pattern's coat, and he had spent a professional lifetime watching men fall in love with three data points and build a whole theory on top of them. Something that had stood in a sealed banquet hall in the capital could not also stand on a roof in Kaldo four days later without crossing four days of open road, unseen, past four guards, one of whom did not sleep on the job even wounded. He had no mechanism for that. He couldn't even sketch one.

So he threw the line out.

What he kept instead was the explanation lying conveniently to hand, which was that he was a nobleman with an untrained mana pool and no idea what one was supposed to feel like from the inside.

That much was real. Most of the old houses carried it and his was better than most, because his mother had been a mage of some standing and that sort of thing ran downhill. He had a capacity. He had been born with it, and offered instruction in it, and had spent his fourteenth summer telling a tutor he would look at the book later. What he owned was a piano nobody had ever taught him to play, standing in a room he had stopped going into.

Mages were supposed to have some feel for nearby mana. Heat off a stove, sensed without touching it. Nobody had ever told him what that was like and he had nothing to calibrate against, so a stray sensation in an unfamiliar town was exactly the sort of thing an untrained pool might throw off in a place he had never been before.

It was a reasonable theory. It accounted for the data. It required only that he ignore the fact that it had first happened in a room he had stood in a thousand times.

Input observed, no working theory, revisit if it repeats. He put it in the same drawer as the other two and walked on, and did not mention it to Rylan, and above him on the tile the grey strip of sky stayed empty.

Rylan's house was small and clean in the specific way poor houses stay clean, cleanliness being one of the few things left that doesn't cost money. Nessa opened the door and went still half a second too long before remembering to bow. Emeric got down to her level to stop her doing it and felt, as he did, how new he still was to the customs of a world he had twenty-two years of memories in. Finn hid behind her leg. Wren didn't. Wren stared at him with the flat, unimpressed curiosity of a child who had not yet learned that princes were supposed to be interesting.

He stepped back and let the reunion happen without standing in the middle of it. It cost him nothing and told him plenty. Neighbors came out for a guard's homecoming in a way they would never have come out for a prince's arrival, and even then there was a wariness under it, the particular caution people keep for nobility that turns up expecting gratitude.

When they'd had their time, he asked what he'd come to ask.

"What was the town like before? Before the war."

Nessa considered it longer than the question seemed to need.

"Busier. My parents talked about it more than I remember it myself. I was young when the men left. Ten, maybe. One week the harbor had hands on every dock, the next it didn't, and nobody explained where they'd gone past 'the crown needs them.'"

She wiped her hands on her apron, out of habit rather than need. "There was a shipwright here then. Fletcher. Built half the boats that left that harbor. My father used to swear you could spot one of his hulls from shore just by the way it sat in the water."

"And Abloh?" she continued. "He wasn't just a sailmaker, either. He had a real gift for cloth—sails, clothes, anything you put fabric to. Could've done well working for the nobles, but nobles didn't exactly come looking for talent in Kaldo. Fletcher and Abloh came together. Wherever Fletcher went, Abloh went with him. They were a package deal. Then they went where the rest of the men went. Nobody's built a real boat here since."

"Nobody said which war."

"Would it have mattered? We weren't the ones deciding to fight it." No heat in it. Fact. "Then the plague came a couple of years after, when some of the men who did come back were still trying to put things right. Took the ones the war hadn't. Men, women, children, animals. It didn't ask who'd already paid."

"The census had this place at over four thousand once."

"It did. Ask anyone over fifty and they'll tell you like it was last spring." She glanced toward the window, toward the street. "Half that now. Less, maybe. Feels cursed some days, if you let yourself think that way. The old folks talk about the town like it's still that place underneath, just resting. The rest of us don't have anywhere else to go, so I suppose we agree with them whether we believe it or not."

Four thousand once. Half of that now. The war was over and could not be helped. The plague was a different category of problem entirely, and he had walked into its cause on the way through the gate.

He and Rylan walked the whole town after that, and by the end of it he was sure. Latrines dug downhill and close to the bank. Burial grounds sitting upriver of the wells the town drank from. The same geometry repeating in every quarter, and no sign anywhere that one single person had ever put those two facts in the same sentence.

It was not ignorance in the way he would have mocked on Earth. Nobody here had ever been handed a framework that made the connection obvious. It was bad geometry, decades old, never questioned, because questioning it required a reason nobody had ever been given.

Which left him with the actual problem, and the actual problem was not the water.

There are creatures too small to see. They live in the water you drink. Some of them are fine and some of them killed half this town, and the reason they are in your water is that you bury your dead uphill of your wells.

He tried the sentence out in his head and heard it land. Two thousand people who had been taken from by the crown twice inside a decade, being told by the crown's newest prince that their dead were poisoning them and the fix was to dig up the graveyard.

On Earth that speech got you laughed at. Here he was fairly confident it got you a bonfire, or at minimum a physician and a very restful year.

He was going to have to make it true in front of them instead of saying it to them, and he had no idea yet how, and that was going to take field work rather than a lecture. It was the first genuinely hard problem anyone had handed him since the machine started working, and he noticed, with some discomfort, that he was enjoying it.

Near the well closest to the burial ground, a woman filling a bucket saw Rylan first and started a nod of greeting before she registered who was walking next to him. The nod died halfway. She finished filling and left faster than a full bucket should have allowed, water going over the rim with every hurried step.

"Was that me?" Emeric asked when she was out of earshot.

"Wasn't you specifically." Rylan didn't slow. "Her boy didn't come back from conscription. Crown took him, Crown never said what happened to him, Crown's the reason she's hauling her own water at that age instead of somebody hauling it for her. You're the Crown today, whatever else you are on other days."

"That's not something I fix by being right about the water."

"No," Rylan agreed. "That one you don't fix. You just don't make it worse."

Ward was in the corridor when he got back, which meant Ward had been ahead of him the whole way and had gotten there first, and the arm was still wrapped in the same linen it had been wrapped in on the road.

"You didn't see the healer."

"There was no need."

"I gave you her street."

"You did."

Emeric considered several arguments, discarded all of them on the grounds that he had watched this man refuse water in a drought, and got as far as his own door.

"You haven't been down to see him," Ward said.

"Tomorrow, maybe. A man talks better after a week alone than after a night."

"That's true."

"It is true."

"Yes," Ward said. "Which is why it works."

He went to take up his post two doors down, where Declan was sitting outside a room with a traitor in it, and Emeric stood in the corridor a while longer working out what exactly had just been said to him, and arrived, eventually, at all of it.
