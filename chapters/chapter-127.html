Chapter 126 - Rising

He wrote the count stone in the night, sitting on the edge of the bed in the small room at the top of the harbour house with the window open on the mountain and the stone in his closed hand.

It was the same small grey stone that had counted for the bags and the dummy and for Ward. It had come to Emberfell in the bottom of his satchel, because it had never seemed like a thing to leave in a drawer with rivets. He took it into the delve and read what was in it, which was two counts and a cut and thirty counts and a return, and then he stood over it in the room for a long time before he changed anything, because there was a thing wrong with it for her that had not been wrong for Ward.

Ward had gone off the rail of a ship that was already a hundred and eighty fathoms up. His toggle had thrown itself to armed as his boots left the rail, and the stone had begun its count at that moment, which was the moment they wanted. She would be going up off the ground. The deciding stone in her suit would throw her toggle to armed the moment her heels left the shingle, and if the stone counted from that, she would be dark at two, a man's height off the beach, with a crowd watching.

So he wrote one more rule into it, ahead of the others. The first time the toggle goes to armed, do nothing. Count from the second.

He read it through three times in the room. Then he came out of the delve and lay down, and slept, because Imani had told him on the stair that if he did not she would put something in his cup that would make certain of it.

Lorcan and Hesta brought the suit down to the harbour at first light.

They brought it on a frame of poles between them, the way a church carries something on a feast day, and her knights walked on either side of it down the slope, and people came out of their doors to see what it was and followed it down to the quay. By the time it reached the black stone there was a crowd.

It was not bare any more.

There was a plume at the helm, white, and small flowers worked in silk at its root. There were skirts of lace at the back, over the backplate and down, edged with small white feathers that moved in the air off the water. The cloak had been worked along its whole length with snowflakes in white silk thread, and trimmed along its edge with down, so that it looked in the grey light like a cloak of snow; and there was a scarf, pale blue, tied at the gorget and falling over one shoulder. All of it was laced on. All of it was tied. There was not a new hole anywhere in the steel.

Hesta set her end of the frame down on the quay and straightened her back.

"We dressed it," she said. "We didn't open it."

That was the whole of their night, and she said it in one sentence, and Emeric looked at the down along the cloak's edge and saw that every stitch of it stopped a finger short of the path he had drawn for her, and started again a finger past it.

Lorcan was looking at the breastplate.

"I'd like to be there," he said. "One day. When somebody does."

Rowena came down from the hall in her shirt and breeches and a padded jacket, with her tall knight behind her, and looked at the suit on its frame for a long moment before she said anything. Then she said, "Well," and nothing else, and Hesta went pink to the ears.

They put it on her on the quay.

Orrin came up from the ship while they were doing it, with the count stone in his hand, and seated it behind her breastplate in the small housing Emeric showed him, and closed it, and said, as he said about everything he seated, that it was in. He did not look at her face while he did it. He was a mage from Kaldo seating a stone in the harness of a queen on a quay full of knights, and he did it the way he filled a cell, and went back to the ship.

Hesta settled the cloak at the shoulders last, and closed the clasp over the ripcord at the left breast. Rowena looked down at it.

She had worn a cloak over plate her whole life. Emeric knew it without being told; every knight did, for rank and for the weather, and she had worn a half-cloak of blue on the quay the day they came in. She put her hand on this one, on the snow-worked silk over the battens, and felt the battens under it, and the cords that ran from them down her arms to her wrists and down to her heels.

"This isn't a cloak," she said.

"No."

"What is it for?"

He had thought about how to tell her in the night, and he told her the way he had decided to, in terms she had already.

"You know what a dead zone is," he said.

"Every mage-knight knows what a dead zone is." She said it as a thing too obvious to be asked. "Inside one, for as long as it lasts, nothing works. Your casting dies in your hands. You fight with your arms."

"On the ground," said Emeric. "Two miles up, it's the same. Every stone in that suit dies at once. No warning, and no noise. And you're a woman in plate in the air with nothing holding you there."

Rowena did not say anything.

"And your own gift's no help," he said. "The second ring runs on you. In a dead zone the medium goes, all of it, yours with the rest. On the ground, if every stone you carry dies, you've still got your arms and your blade. Up there, you've got that." He put his hand on the edge of the cloak. "On the ground your gift is what you fall back on. In the air, it's the cloak."

He watched that land. It landed harder than anything else he said. She had been a caster since she was a girl, and a knight nearly as long, and the idea of a place where it simply was not there had never once been a thing she had to plan for, because on the ground there was always the sword.

So he told her how the cloak worked, plainly, from the breast.

There was a small stone at her breast, behind the clasp, outside everything else in the suit, on a cell of its own, and all it did was breathe. It kept a leather bellows full, and the full bellows held a catch shut against a set of springs. While that small stone had power and the suit's deciding stone still held, it breathed, and the cloak stayed a cloak. If the deciding stone went dark, the small stone stopped breathing. If its own cell went dry, it stopped. And in a dead zone it went dark with everything else, which was the whole of the reason it worked: it did not have to wake up and do anything. It only had to stop.

"Three counts, near enough, for the bellows to go slack," he said. "Then the springs throw the battens out, and the cords at your wrists and your heels pull tight, and you've got a wing. You hold your arms out and you glide."

"Where to?"

"Out. A dead zone has edges. You glide clear of the dead air, and past the edge of it your hearts might wake again, because they ride in iron, and iron keeps a great deal out." He did not pretend. "I think they will. I haven't proved it. I've written it down as a thing I don't know."

"And if it's too wide to glide out of?"

He turned her by the shoulder, gently, and showed her with his finger the long flat pack down her spine under the heart in the yoke.

"There's a second wing in there that isn't a wing. A canopy of silk, folded. When the cloak opens, a line in the pack throws it out after, below the cloak's edge, and it opens over you and lets you down slowly. If it doesn't throw, the cord under your clasp throws it by hand." He let go of her shoulder. "There's no stone in it. There's no stone anywhere near it. Everything else on you can die at once, and that won't know there was ever anything to die."

"And on the ground?" said Rowena. "In a fight. A dead zone on a field, and this bursts open on my back."

It was Ward's question, near enough word for word, and Emeric heard Ward, a pace behind him, not say anything.

"There's a toggle behind the catch," Emeric said. "The suit throws it armed when you leave the ground, and safe when you land heavy. On the ground nothing opens that cloak but your own hand. You can throw it yourself, either way." He looked at her. "One rule. You say it back to me."

She waited.

"You never shed the cloak in the air," he said. "Not to fight, not because it's heavy, not for any reason. It comes off at two catches at the shoulders, and it's easy to take off, because on the ground you'll want to. In the air, a knight who's shed her cloak has shed her wing."

"A knight who's shed her cloak," said Rowena, "has shed her wing."

She said it the way a knight repeats a watchword, flatly, to be sure of it, and then she looked out over the harbour, at the grey water with the boats on it and the black quays round three sides of it, and he saw her decide something.

"Then kill it," she said. "Once. Over water. I want to see what it does."

Ward, behind him, said, "Yes."

It was the first thing he had said all morning, and he said it to her, not to Emeric. Emeric had known he would. It was the drill Ward had insisted on for himself, in the winter, off the lower water, and he had gone off a rail to do it.

"Fly it first," said Emeric. "You can't drop from a height you can't reach."

Ward and Osric had gone down to the ship while the suit was going on. They came up her ladder onto the black quay now, and the quay saw them.

They came up without the cloaks drawn round them. It was the first time. The capes hung open from the two catches at their shoulders, down their backs, with the battens along the edges, and under them was the plate: black, finished blue in the forge and oiled, with the dark violet glass over every pane, and at each shoulder a lion's head over the cape's catch with glass for eyes and nothing behind the glass. The fur at their collars. Helms on, the winged helms, with the visors up. Their swords at their hips.

The crowd on the quay made a sound. It was not loud. It was the sound a crowd makes when it has been told a story for a month and not believed it and has just seen the thing the story was about come up a ladder. Her knights did not make it. Her knights stood very still, and looked at the two black suits, and at the lions, and at each other.

Rowena looked at the lions for rather a long time.

"Those are new," she said.

"Everything's new," said Osric cheerfully, and put his visor down.

They went up off the black quay together, the three of them, and the crowd on the quay went back a step all at once as though somebody had pushed it.

Ward and Osric went first, slowly, straight up, the air stones breathing under their heels and a ring of dust going out across the stone from under them. They had not flown in a crowd before. They went up to the height of the harbour wall and held there, side by side, and turned to face her, and waited.

Rowena stood on the quay with her hands on the levers.

She did not do what Emeric had done the first time, or what Ward had done. She did not go up all at once. She put the lift lever forward a notch and felt the weight come off her heels, and stopped there, with her heels a finger off the stone, and stood in the air a finger high for a long moment. Then she went up a hand. Then she stopped again. She went up the whole height of the harbour wall that way, a hand and a stop and a hand, the way a knight comes onto a strange yard, one foot at a time, feeling what is under her before she trusts it.

At the top she took hold of the push.

After that it went very fast.

She flew out over the harbour between the two black suits, bright steel under the plume, with the snow-worked cloak down her back and the scarf going out behind her in the wind, over the water and the boats on it, and every boat on the harbour stopped whatever it was doing. She went slowly at first, as Ward had shown her with his hands in the air beside her. Then she went less slowly. Then she did the thing Emeric had been watching for, and put her own hands to it.

He saw it from the quay. He saw the second ring wake.

It showed as nothing, because it was behind blue glass like everything else on her. But he saw what it did. She had been flying on the levers, the way Ward flew, a notch and a notch, clean and careful. And then she was not. She was flying the way she had fought on the yard, without seeming to have been anywhere in between, turning on a line that no lever could have drawn, rising at the end of a turn as though the turn had lifted her. She poured into the second ring with her own hands, and it ran on her, and she flew the base on the levers and put the rest of it into the air herself, and she was finer inside the hour than Emeric had been after a week.

He was not surprised and he was not jealous. It was capacity, and thirty years of knowing where her body was in the air when she jumped, and a lifetime of being the best on every yard. The suit only gave her somewhere new to be the best.

She came down at the head of the harbour, on the black shingle below the boatyard, and she came down heavy.

Nobody had shown her how. She had found it. She came in low over the water with the black suits behind her, and put her heels down on the shingle, and went heavy, all at once, so that she did not skid or step or stagger but stopped where she was, dead, ankle-deep in the black stones, with the shingle thrown up in a ring round her boots.

Ward came down beside her, lightly, and looked at her boots in the shingle, and said nothing at all. From Ward it was approval, and Emeric saw her understand it as approval, though she had known him for a day.

"Now," said Rowena. "Kill it."

He told her the rest standing on the shingle.

"Put the lift at Light before you throw anything," he said. "When the stone gives you back, the suit reads your lever and does what it says, and you want it to say Light." He touched her breastplate over the small housing. "The stone behind here does nothing when you go up. It lets your toggle arm itself and counts nothing. When you're high enough, throw the toggle to safe, by hand, and back to armed. That's when it starts. Two counts and you're dark. Three more and you've a wing. Thirty, and you're given back."

"How high?"

"Higher than you think."

"Where he went," said Ward, "and further." He said it to her. "I went off the rail at a hundred and eighty fathoms by the lead, and I woke a hundred feet off the sea. You'll have no lead. I'll take you up and tell you where I think it is, and then we'll go a good way further, because a man who wakes too high only has further to float."

She nodded.

"And one more thing," said Emeric. "Don't catch yourself."

She looked at him.

"When it goes dark," he said. "Your second ring runs on you. The stone kills the hearts. It doesn't touch you. When you're falling, your hands will want to put something into the ring and catch you, and they'll be able to, because it's only a drill." He held her eyes. "A real dead zone would take your gift with the rest. If you catch yourself, you've learned nothing, and the day it happens for true you'll reach for a thing that isn't there."

That was the hardest thing he said to her all morning. He watched her take it. She took it slowly, the way she had come up off the quay, one hand at a time, and when she had it she nodded once.

Ward took her up.

They went very high. They went up over the middle of the harbour until they were two small shapes against the grey, one black and one bright, and then higher, and the crowd on the quays stood with their heads back and their hands over their eyes. Osric stayed low, over the water, a little off to one side, where she would come down. A harbour boat pulled out under him on its own, without being told, and lay on its oars.

Emeric counted.

He could not see her throw it. She was too high. But he saw the bright shape come away from the black one, and fall.

It fell like a stone for two. It fell for three more while the bellows emptied, and it was a very long way, and he had known exactly how long it would be since the winter and it was still very long. Then the cloak went out. He heard it. He heard it from the quay, a long way below, the crack of it filling, like a sail in a squall, and the bright shape that had been falling was not falling any more. It was going out across the sky over her harbour on a long flat sliding line, with a wing on it, white, with snow on it.

Below the cloak, a moment after, the chute went out, pale against the grey, and opened.

She did not catch herself. He watched for it, the whole way down, the sudden checked line of a caster putting her hands to it, and it did not come. She came down on the cloak and the chute, slowly, over the middle of her harbour, turning a little, a woman in bright steel under a snow-white wing, and the whole of Emberfell stood on its quays and watched her come.

At thirty she was well up. The count gave her back. He saw it happen, the dead hang of her change, the legs come up, and she went Light, and came the rest of the way down as slowly as a leaf, and settled onto the grey water of the harbour thirty yards from the harbour boat, and lay on it.

She floated. She lay on the water in her plate, in her plume and her lace and her snow, Light, with the chute collapsing on the water beside her, and she did not sink.

And she was laughing.

He could hear it from the quay. Everybody could. It came across the water, and her knights on the quay heard it, and some of them began to laugh too, without knowing why, and Hesta, standing beside Emeric with her hands pressed flat together in front of her mouth, looked at her lace floating on the harbour and made a small sound that was not laughing.

The harbour boat took the chute in. Rowena gathered it off her own arms and handed it up to the boatmen like washing, and then she put the push on and came in across the last hundred yards of her harbour a foot off the water, dripping, with the cloak furled again behind her by her own hand, and set down on the shingle heavy, beside Ward, who had come down before her.

She was still laughing, a little. She stopped when she saw Emeric's face.

"I wanted to," she said. "Catch it. The whole way down, for the first five."

"I know."

"I didn't."

"I know," said Emeric. "I watched."

She never needed telling again. He could see that she never would.

The chute had to be packed again on dry ground, and there was nobody in Emberfell who could pack it. Osric came down onto the shingle and put his visor up and looked at the wet silk in the bottom of the harbour boat.

"There's a spare in the drawers," he said. "Mine. Take it." He shrugged in his plate. "I don't mean to fall off anything this week."

Tobe came up from the ship with the spare chute in its oilcloth and opened the seams of her pack on the shingle and seated it, carefully, with Emeric standing over him and Rowena standing very still in her dripping lace, and closed the seams again. Tobe said nothing to her at all, the whole time. It was the first time Tobe had worked on a suit with anybody inside it, let alone a queen, and he did not seem to know which of the two was worse.

When he had finished, Rowena turned round to Emeric, still standing in the shingle to her ankles.

"Where does your ship go next?" she said.

He told her the truth. He had told her the truth at the pot, and he did not see any reason to stop.

"Duvrenne," he said. "The north coast. Where your black stuff comes out of the ground. It's a night's flight from your harbour, near enough, and nobody lives on that coast in winter." He looked at her. "I mean to buy more of it. From the men who dig it. Quietly."

Rowena looked at him for a long moment, and then at the ship lying at the black quay behind him, with nothing coming out of her stack.

"Then show me," she said.

It was not the pitch she wanted to see. He knew it before she said anything else. She had stood on a slip beside a pot of it and not cared about it at all. It was the night's flight. It was a coast three kingdoms off, reached between supper and breakfast.

Ward spoke before Emeric could.

"Your Grace," he said. He did not use it often, and he used it now like a hand put flat on a table. "A queen of Randillia inside Duvrenne, unannounced, in a foreign ship. If anybody saw you, it would be the worst thing that's happened between those two crowns in years."

"Yes."

"Then you stay aboard. You don't set a foot ashore. Not on the coast, not on the ice, not for a moment."

"Yes," said Rowena, at once.

She did not argue it. She did not even look as though she had thought of arguing it. She turned to her tall knight, who had come down onto the shingle behind her, and said that she would be gone two days, and that the table would sit by office, and that she was not going to say where she was going, and the knight nodded as though she had been told the weather. Her law was not needed for two days. The table sat by office anyway. It always had.

Ward caught Emeric's eye then, and walked a little way along the shingle, and Emeric went with him.

"Four tons," Ward said, when they were far enough off.

"I know."

"Along this coast there's never two days from a harbour. You made that rule on the beach, and it's a good rule. Up there, in the winter, off a coast with nobody on it?" He looked at the ship. "If every stone in her died off Duvrenne, four tons reaches a foreign harbour. And nothing friendlier than that. And we'd sit in it burning their wood, with a queen of Randillia aboard, and the whole of the secret out."

"I know," said Emeric. "It goes in Ferra's book."

Ward looked at him a moment longer and nodded, once, and went back along the shingle to Rowena.

Ferra stayed.

She had the fair copies to make, two of every page of the terms in her best hand, with the Treasurer across the table checking every line against the other, and it would take two days to make them properly and she was not going to make them improperly. And she held one half of the relay key. With her in Emberfell and Emeric in the air, the two halves would not be in one hull, which was what the key had been cut in two halves for, and she said so, and wrote it in the book.

Imani stayed for the drains. They were hers. She had already been down to the harbour office and asked for the town's oldest map and been given it, and she had the harbour-mistress's promise of forty men with shovels on the first dry day, and she was not going to be a night's flight away the morning they turned up.

Vantic asked to go.

She did not ask Emeric. She asked Ferra, on the harbour house stair, and gave her reason to Ferra, and Ferra wrote it in the book and Emeric read it there afterward in Ferra's even hand. Kaldo's Rands were the House's security. The House would rather she saw what they bought than read about it later. That was all. It was a true reason, and it was in the same shape as her reason for coming at all, and Ferra had written it down in the same words, as she had the first. Whether it was all of it, the book did not say.

Ward's condition held for Vantic as for the queen. She would stay aboard.

So it was ten of them for Duvrenne: Emeric, and Ward and Osric, and Saban, and Vantic, and Rowena; and Fletcher and Orrin and Tobe and Maren, who went where the ship went. Two of the six, Ward and Saban, which was well inside the ceiling Ferra had written on the eve of midwinter. Rowena would have the first cabin to port, which was Ferra's and Imani's, and was empty.

Ferra wrote the conditions in the margin of the page, before she would let anybody aboard, and read them out in the long room as figures.

"Over water the whole way," she said. "Never over the Core. Not once, not for an hour to save an hour." She did not look up. "Nobody ashore but four. Back in this harbour inside three days. If you're not, I open the sealed instructions. I tell her table, and I write to Armand, in that order." She turned her pen. "And if every stone in her dies up there, she steams for the nearest harbour, and burns whatever it sells, and nobody says a word about where she came from."

She wrote the fourth one knowing what it meant. Emeric watched her know it. If the fourth condition were ever used, the secret was out, whatever anybody said or did not say in that harbour, because a black iron ship with no smoke from her stack would come into it burning wood, with a queen aboard, from nowhere. She wrote it anyway, because it was the true condition. Under it she wrote the date.

Then she weighed them.

She did it on Emberfell's own scale at the head of the quay, the harbour's great beam scale, with the harbour-mistress's clerk at the weights and a crowd watching. Two people off the ship, herself and Imani, with their things. One queen on, and her suit, which had been aboard all along in a crate. Rowena's gift was in the forepeak. The ten tons of coal that had been left in a heap under a sail by Fletcher's cottage were still ten tons of room.

"There is room," said Ferra, "for whatever they sell you, and the gift, and home." She closed the book. "All of it."

They left in the open.

It was the middle of the afternoon, and the whole of Emberfell was on its quays, because the whole of Emberfell had watched its sovereign fall out of the sky that morning and had not gone back indoors since. The ship steamed out from the black quay on the boiler's stones with nothing from her stack, into the middle of the harbour, where Rowena had floated, and stopped there.

Ward had the levers. Emeric was not in the day room. He was on the open deck aft, beside the hatch, bare-headed in the wind, because somebody had to be.

She lightened. The screw raced and came up. The harbour went away under her.

The sound the quays made was louder than the sound the crowd had made for the black suits. It went round the three sides of the harbour and up the tiers of the town and came back off the mountain, and under it, very faint, the bells on the harbour wall began again, one and then two more, as they had the day the ship came in. Not in alarm. Somebody had simply thought it was a thing that wanted bells.

Rowena came up alongside at the height of the rail.

She had gone up off the shingle as the ship lifted, and she rode beside the open deck now, level with it, a pace off, in her bright steel and her plume and her snow, with the cloak furled and her hands on the levers. She was looking at the ship. She was looking at the ship the way she had looked at his feet on the quay: at where it put its weight, and how, and what was holding it.

Ward held the ship low and slow, at a walk, a hundred feet over her harbour.

Emeric held out his hand.

She looked at it. Then she put the push back to nothing and let the lift down a notch, and came in over the rail, and he took her by the wrist, not the hand, the way you take hold of a knight in plate, and she set her heels down on the open deck heavy, and stood.

His fingers were round her wrist over the steel. She looked down at them for a moment. He could not tell what she was looking at.

"Below," he said. "Before we climb. Nobody stays on this deck when she goes fast."

She went down the aft companion into the saloon without a word, and he dogged it shut behind them.

Tobe took the suit off her in the hold, piece by piece, and hung it on the fourth stand, where the crate had stood strapped to the floor since midwinter. The fourth stand had no hose to it and no shelf. The plume went on the bench, and Tobe looked at it there for some time, as though it might be a spare part he ought to know about.

She came up through the doors to the day room in her shirt and her padded jacket, with her hair still wet from the harbour, and went to a port on the starboard side, and stood there.

They were climbing. Ward had taken her up over the sea, straight up, the way they always went now, toward the band, to fill before they went anywhere. Below the port, her coast was falling away.

She had never seen it. Not like this. Emeric watched her not have seen it. The inlet went down into a long grey line, and the town round the harbour at its end into a smudge of dark stone, and the mountain into a cone with a thread of smoke going off it sideways in the wind, and then all of it into a map, the whole of Emberfell at once, the way nobody in Emberfell had ever seen it, the way nobody in the world had seen anything until this autumn.

She stood at the port with her hand flat on its frame and did not take hold of the rail.

"How long?" she said, without looking round.

"Nine hours," said Emeric. "Near enough."

She turned her head and looked at him then. And she laughed, the same laugh she had laughed on the floor of her hall at the giant, from somewhere under everything, with her head back.
