Chapter 127 - Steam on the Water

Fletcher planked her to the rail in eighteen days, with the four levy men he had trained on the black hull and two of Bram's boys who could hold a plank where they were told, and decked her in four more, and on the twenty-third day she went down the ways into the river under the north wall with the engine in her and the comb of tubes standing in its brick box amidships under a little iron hood, and her black funnel, and her screw.

She did not look like the black hull. She looked like what she had always been meant to be: a coaster, broad and solid and plain, sixty feet of oak with a blunt bow, the kind of boat every coast in the kingdom had a hundred of. Except that she had no mast. And she had a funnel. And when they lit her fire on the evening of the launch and let the steam come up, she sat in the river at the foot of the slip with a thin grey thread of smoke going up out of the funnel into the still air, and a sound coming out of her that no boat on that river had ever made: a low steady breathing, and under it, every few breaths, a soft deep cough.

The dock quarter came out on its piles and listened to her breathe.

Fletcher stood at her wheel. Not a tiller; a wheel, on a post, a thing he had made himself in the last week because he said a boat that went without wind deserved to be steered like a cart, and he could not have said why. He did not touch it that evening. He stood behind it with his hands at his sides and listened to his half hull breathing, and nobody went near him.

She had no name. Fletcher said she would have one when she had earned one, and not before.



They took her up the river to Grayrock on the second of October.

It had been meant for the end of September. The ankle had put it off a week. Emeric had walked on the ankle for three days as if nothing were wrong, because a man who could not say how he had turned it on the roof of a stable at two in the morning could not very well limp, and on the fourth day Imani had come up to the keep with her basket, without being sent for, and looked at him on the stair, once, and made him sit down on it and take his boot off. She had not asked how. She had looked at the ankle, and pressed it, and bound it properly, and told him that it was a sprain and not a break and that if he walked on it for another three days it would be a break, and that she did not want to know how a man sprained an ankle that badly between his bed and his table. Then she had looked at him, still holding his bare foot in her lap on the keep stair, and said, quite quietly, that she would very much like to know, one day. And gone back down the hill.

So the second of October. The ankle was sound. The river was low, and slow, and brown, at the end of the dry month.

They went at first light. Fletcher at the wheel. Bram at the engine, in the little iron-floored pit aft of the boiler where the engine stood upright on its frame with the crank going round below it, and Haddon with him at the firebox door, his hands healed now and scarred across both palms. Tess in her chair, amidships, beside the boiler she had drawn, lashed to two ringbolts in the deck that Bram had put in without telling anybody, with a slate in her lap and the steam gauge where she could see it, because she had said that if the boiler was going to do anything interesting she wanted to be the first to know.

Ward in the bow. Vantic at the starboard rail, aft, in her grey coat, with her book, because a new boat bought out of the fifth and an engine built out of the fifth on a run to a barony the security now included was a matter for the House, she said, and Emeric had not argued. Ferra.

And Maren.

Maren had come down to the slip at dawn with two baskets and a sack and a small iron stove on a barrow pushed by one of the kitchen boys, and stood at the foot of the gangplank and looked at the boat.

"Two days up," she said. "Two days back. A night at Grayrock and a night on the water each way." She looked at Emeric. "Who's feeding them?"

Emeric opened his mouth.

"Bread and cheese," said Maren. "That's what you were going to say. Bread and cheese and whatever the inn at Grayrock's got, which is mutton, which is what every inn upriver has got, which has been dead since the plague." She jerked her head at the barrow. "I'm coming. You can't feed seven people on a boat for four days without somebody who knows what they're doing, and Ferra says the barony's kitchen does well enough without me for four days because the girl I trained can do the noon bowl with her eyes shut." She looked at him. "Ferra says I'm wasted on the noon bowl anyway."

"Ferra said that?"

"Ferra said that a prince who eats whatever's put in front of him at whatever hour because he's forgotten to eat at all is a prince who'll fall over one day in front of Keller." Maren picked up the first basket. "So I'm to go where you go. From now on. She's written it down."

She went up the gangplank with the basket. The kitchen boy followed with the stove. Maren set it up on the foredeck in the lee of the deckhouse, on a sheet of iron Bram had not known he was giving her, and by the time the boat was in mid-river there was smoke coming out of its little chimney as well as out of the funnel, and the smell of bacon going back down the deck over all of them.

Ferra, at the rail, did not look at Emeric when Maren's stove was lit. She looked at the river.



Fletcher took her out into the stream and turned her bow upriver and rang the bell to the engine pit, once, the way he had arranged with Bram, and Bram opened the throttle.

The breathing went up into a steady beat. Under the stern the screw bit, and Emeric felt it through the deck, a long shove, and the boat leaned a little against the current and began to move. Up the river. Away from the town and the north wall and the shed and the black hull on her cradle. Against the current, with no sail and no oars and no horse on the bank, at a steady walking pace, with the brown water talking along her sides and her wake going out behind her in a long smooth V toward both banks, and her smoke going up straight into the morning.

The dock quarter, on its piles, cheered. It was the first time Emeric had ever heard the dock quarter cheer for anything.

Fletcher stood at the wheel and steered her up the middle of the river and did not look back.



Ferra had not been on the river since she was a child.

He found that out an hour above the town. She was standing at the port rail, amidships, near Tess, with her hands on the rail and her book under her arm, and she had been standing there since they left, not reading the book, which he had never seen her do. She was watching the bank go by. The willows. The fields behind them, Farris's, with the stubble going pale. A heron. A boy on the bank with a dog who stopped and stared at the boat and then began to run along the bank beside it, with the dog, shouting, and kept up with it for most of a mile before he gave up and stood on the bank and watched it go.

"My mother took me," Ferra said, without looking round, when he came to stand beside her. "Once. On the salt boat. Down to the coast and back, when I was six. My father was mending the boatman's anchor and the boatman said he'd take the smith's wife and girl down and back for it." She watched the bank. "I was sick the whole way down and I don't remember the coast at all. I remember the way back. Against the current. The boatman's two men poling, all day, and the boat hardly moving, and my mother holding me on her knee at the stern, telling me the names of the birds." She was quiet. "It took three days to come back up. From the coast. I thought it would never end."

He did not say anything. She did not say anything else about her mother. He had never once heard her speak of her before, in four years, and he did not move, and after a while she let her breath out and looked down at the water going past the hull.

"How much coal," she said, "is he putting on?"

It was so exactly what she would say that he nearly laughed.

"Ask Haddon."

"Haddon will tell me a shovel." She turned round and looked aft at the engine pit, where Haddon was bent at the firebox door with his scarred hands on the shovel. "I don't want a shovel. I want a weight." She took her book out from under her arm and opened it on the rail. "The barge brings it down from Grayrock. Four tons a fortnight. Bram says the mill engine burned a ton in two days at full pressure." She found a page. "This one's half the size. And the tubes. Tess says the tubes take less coal for the same steam, because the fire's all round them." She looked at Tess.

"A third less," said Tess, from her chair, without looking up from the gauge. "Near enough."

"A third less." Ferra wrote. "Half the size, a third less. And we're going up against the current." She looked at the bank going by. "Which is slower. Which is more coal for the same distance. How far is it?"

"Forty miles by the river."

"Forty miles." She wrote that too. And then she began to do it aloud.

She did it the way she had done the wheel on the downs, in a low steady voice, under her breath, to the book on the rail and the river going by, line by line. So many shovels to the hour, by Haddon's arm, which she had watched for a quarter of an hour and counted. So much to a shovel, by the sack she had had weighed at the slip. So many hours to Grayrock, at this pace, against this current, which she worked out by watching a particular willow come up on the bank and go past and counting until the next one. The coal for the run. The coal for the run back, less, with the current. The coal at the price Bryda's barge charged. The coal at the price the same coal would cost if Kaldo owned the pit it came out of, which, since August, it did.

He did not watch the bank.

He knew he did not. He stood at the rail beside her for most of the morning and watched Ferra doing the coal sums against the bank going by, her lips moving and her pen moving and her eyes going from the book to the willows and back, and the wind off the river pulling one strand of red hair out of its pins and across her cheek, where she pushed it back with the end of her pen without noticing she had, and it came out again. He watched the river light come up off the water onto her face. He watched her get to the end of a line and frown, and go back up, and find it, and go on. He saw the willows go by, because they were in the corner of his eye, and the heron, and the fields, and none of them stayed.

At the end of the morning she got to the bottom of the page and drew a line under it and looked at the figure under the line, and he saw her mouth move at the corner.

"It's cheaper than horses," she said.

"Is it?"

"Up the river. With a load. It's cheaper than a barge with horses on the towpath, if the coal's ours." She looked up from the book at last, and found him looking at her and not at the river, and stopped.

She did not look away. Neither did he.

"You weren't watching the bank," Ferra said.

"No."

"You came up here to watch the bank. You've been talking about this boat since April. Up the river. Against the current." She held his eyes. "And you've been watching me add."

"Yes."

Ferra looked at him a little longer. Then she looked down at the book, at the figure under the line, and closed the book on her pen, and put it under her arm, and turned back to the rail, and looked at the bank. And did not move away from him. He was standing at the rail with his hand on it, and her hand was on the rail beside his, and he felt her hand move, very slightly, along the wet wood, until the side of her little finger was against the side of his, and stop there, and stay.

Neither of them looked down.

They stood like that and watched the bank go by, the willows and the fields and Farris's stubble going pale in the October sun, for a long while, with Maren's bacon smoke going back down the deck over them, and Tess six feet away in her chair with her eyes on the steam gauge and, Emeric was nearly sure, a smile she was keeping very carefully off her face.

At the starboard rail aft, across the width of the deck, Vantic was reading her book. She turned a page. She turned it, he noticed afterward, without having read the one before it.



They lay that night at a bend twenty miles up, with a stake driven into the bank and a line to it, and Maren cooked on the foredeck in the dusk a stew of mutton that had been alive on Tuesday and was, she said, an insult to the word, and everyone ate two bowls of it.

The boiler sat banked in its box under the hood, ticking as it cooled, and Tess sat beside it in her chair after everyone else had gone below and listened to it with her head on one side, the way Fletcher listened to a hull, until Bram came up out of the pit and wheeled her to the hatch and lifted her down.

They went on at first light, and the river narrowed, and the land on either side of it rose, slowly, into the low brown hills that were Grayrock's. Ore country. The banks turned to grey shingle and rock, and there were old spoil heaps above the river here and there, overgrown, and the shafts of old workings in the hillsides like the doors of houses nobody lived in.

A little before noon Fletcher brought her into a bend where the river went round a spur of hill and the far bank was a cliff of grey rock with birch on top, and nothing on either side to see them but sheep, and rang the bell to the engine pit twice, and Bram shut the steam off. She drifted. Ward went over the bow with a line and made it fast to a birch on the near bank, and she swung on it and lay against the shingle with her funnel ticking and her smoke thinning to nothing.

"Three miles," said Fletcher, from the wheel. "Round the spur. Grayrock's landing. Her barges and her ore carts and every man of hers on the quay." He looked at Emeric. "You said nobody upriver sees her."

"Nobody upriver sees her."

Fletcher looked at his boat, lying on the shingle with no mast and a funnel, in a bend where nobody would come.

"She came up forty miles against the current," he said, "on coal, with nobody pulling her, in a day and a half." He took his hands off the wheel. "And she has to hide round a corner like a man come to a wedding in the wrong coat."

"For now," said Emeric.

"For now," said Fletcher. He did not sound as if he believed it. Then he looked at the funnel, and the boiler hood, and Tess in her chair, and Bram coming up out of the pit wiping his hands, and something in his beard moved. "She's earned one," he said. "A name. Coming up that river." He thought about it. "I'll think on it. Go and see your Bryda."

They went the last three miles on foot, by the road over the spur, Emeric and Ward and Ferra and Vantic, with Maren left on the boat with her stove and Bram and Haddon and Tess and Fletcher and a stew she said she would make for when they came back that would be, she promised, a great deal better than the mutton.

From the top of the spur Emeric looked back once. The boat lay in her bend below them, small, dark, on the grey shingle, with the birches over her. You would not have known, from here, that she had anything in her at all.

Then the road went down the other side of the spur, and there was Grayrock: a grey town on a grey hill above the river, with its quay and its barges and its spoil heaps and its pitheads, and at the top of it, on the hill, the hall where a woman who had held it in trust for her daughter for two years and been told by a clerk in the capital that she was now resident in the crown's interest was waiting to see what the new holder would do to her.
