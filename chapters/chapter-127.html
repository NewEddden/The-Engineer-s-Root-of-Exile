Chapter 127 - Burning Water

They filled in the band first, as they always did now, two miles over Emberfell's sea with the probe stone glowing behind its pane and the cells in the rack thickening in the medium, and then Ward took the push off its stop and turned her north.

The route was Ferra's, in the margin, and Emeric had drawn it on the chart in the day room under her eye before she would sign the page. Over water the whole way. North up the long channel between Randillia's western shore and the Core, with the Core off to port and never under her. Through the strait by the Isle of Shards. Up between Duvrenne and Kesrath, keeping to the middle of the water. Then round the top of Duvrenne, well out, to its northern coast, where nobody lived in winter. It was longer than a straight line by a good deal. A straight line would have gone over the Core for an hour, and Ferra had written *not for an hour to save an hour*, and that was the end of it.

Rowena stood at a port for most of it.

She stood at the one she had taken on the climb, on the starboard side, with her hand on its frame, and watched her own coast go by under the ship in the last of the afternoon, and then go away behind. After that it was the channel, and the western shore of the kingdom she was a queen of, going by below in the dusk, long and brown and white along its tops with snow, with the lights of towns coming out along it one by one as the light went. She had lived in that kingdom for twenty years. She had never seen it. Nobody had. She watched it the way Ferra had watched the coast on the way down, reading it, and twice Emeric heard her say the name of a place under her breath as it went by, not to anybody.

She said very little to anybody else. When Maren brought round something hot in mugs at the top of the channel, Rowena took one and thanked her and held it without drinking it, and it went cold in her hand at the port.

It was dark by the strait. They went through it high, in the dark, with the Isle of Shards a black shape to port with a few lights on it and the strait itself only a place where the stars stopped being reflected, and then they were in the northern water with a kingdom on either hand.

Vantic had come to a port on the starboard side, two along from the queen.

She had been at the chart table with her book since the band, writing nothing in it that Emeric could see, and now she stood with the book held flat against her coat and looked out at the dark. There was a coast out there, a long way off, with a line of lights low along it and one cluster of them brighter than the rest.

"Kesrath," she said.

Emeric was at the next port. He looked where she was looking.

"How do you know? It's dark."

"The lights. That's the south harbour, and the bright ones are the tallow works. They run all night in winter." She said it the way she said a figure. "The House keeps accounts on the rendering trade on that coast. And on who lends to it. And on the"

She stopped.

It was not a long stop. It was the width of a breath, and then she was looking at the lights again with her face exactly where she kept it, as though she had come to the end of the sentence she meant to say and not to the middle of a longer one. But he had heard where it stopped, and she heard him hear it, and for a moment neither of them said anything.

"It's a very dull coast," said Vantic, "in the daytime."

"I'll take your word for it."

"You'd be well advised to," she said, and he could not tell whether she was smiling, because she had turned her head to the port and there was only the lamp behind her.

Kesrath went by to starboard in the dark, and after it the trade posts, a string of lights along a far shore, and after the posts there were other lights, fewer, low and orange and scattered, along the eastern coast of Duvrenne where the ground came down to the sea. Saban came and stood at the port and looked at them for some time.

"Seeps," he said. "Camps at the seeps. Those'll be the ones the posts buy from."

"Then we go round them," said Emeric.

They went round them. Ward took her out to sea, well out, so far that the orange lights sank to a line and then went, and brought her north along nothing, and then west, across the top of the world, over a black sea with ice at its edges showing grey under the stars. It was very cold outside the ports. Inside, the dial on the bulkhead stood at its mark, and the floor was warm, and Rowena had taken the blanket Maren brought her and put it round her shoulders without appearing to notice she had done it.

It was a little past nine hours from her harbour when Fletcher said they were there.

He said it from the wheel, under the dome, looking forward. There was a coast ahead in the starlight, low and white, with nothing on it. No lights at all. A long shore of snow going east and west, and behind it, very faint, the darker lines of hills. And up one valley, a long way inland, a string of small fires, red, spaced along what must be a river.

"Diggers," said Saban, at the next port. "There's nobody else would be up there in this."

Emeric looked at the fires for a long time and fixed them.

They came down the way they always came down, a notch at a time on the levers, well out to sea, with every lamp aboard her shuttered and the ports dark, running down onto the water in a long slant so that she would touch the sea moving and not drop onto it. Nobody spoke. The sea came up black. At the height of a man Fletcher said, "Now," quite quietly, and Ward let her down, and she sat on the water.

The screw went down. Tobe woke the boiler's stones, below, and she steamed in toward the land a little way with nothing coming out of her stack, which on this coast in the dark was the only way a ship could come in and not be seen at all, and Fletcher stopped her two miles out, and she lay there on the black water, dark, and waited for morning.

The sea was open. The land was not. Emeric could see it from the port when his eyes had got used to the dark: the white of the shore, and in front of it a band of grey where the ice came out from the land over the shallows, and then the black of open water where they lay. It was as cold a place as he had ever been.

They went in before dawn.

He went aft to the armoury first, through the doors, with Ward's key and Osric's, and took the brooch off the court coat where the coat hung in the stern cabin on its peg, and opened the catch Bram had made, and took the grey stone out. He opened the small iron door in the breast of his own suit and set it in its seat and closed the door on it, and felt the deciding stone come up against it in the iron. The coat stayed on its peg. The silver star went into the drawer under his bunk.

They dressed each other in the plate, the three of them, the way they had in the pattern store and on the first day in this hold. The capes went on drawn, as cloaks, closed at the throat, with the hoods up. The helms stayed on their shelves. On the quay at Emberfell a hood over a helm would have been a man with something to hide; here there was nobody to hide it from, and it would still have been a thing a man remembered. Saban came down in the plainest coat he had and two others over it, with his chain of beads left on the saloon table, as he had left it in the long room at Emberfell the night he went down to the taverns, and a purse of Rands inside the innermost coat.

Rowena was in the saloon when they came through it. She was sitting at the table with the blanket still round her, and she looked at the three hooded men and at Saban, and did not get up.

"Four," she said.

"Four."

"Then I'll sit here," said Rowena, "and keep my word." And she did not move from the table, and did not go up to the day room to watch the boat go, which Emeric understood was a thing she had decided on purpose.

The boat went off the davits in the dark, on the lee side, with Fletcher at the falls and Tobe at the other, and the four of them went down the side into it and took the oars, and pulled for the land.

He kept the sense shut.

It would have been the easiest thing in the world to open it. The shore ahead was black and white and nothing else, and somewhere on it there were men, and the sense would have found every one of them a mile off and told him how they stood. But Rowena had felt it go over her on a quay, from twenty paces, and put her hand to her sword. He did not know who in Duvrenne could feel it. He did not know who was at those fires. A digger with the gift, or a digger's wife, or a ground-house's man sent up to count the casks, and any of them might turn his head on a winter night toward a draught from nowhere. So he rowed with his eyes, the way men had always rowed toward a strange shore, and he found it harder than rowing.

The river came out of the land in a wide mouth, and the mouth was ice.

They ran the boat up onto the shelf where the sea ice met the river's, and pulled it up out of the water, and turned it over, and left it under the lee of a bank with an oar standing up beside it in the snow. Then they walked.

The river was the road. He had thought on the chart that there would be a boat to buy on it, a river boat, and there was no boat on it anywhere because there was no water on it anywhere. It was ice from bank to bank, grey and white and swept clean in long stretches by the wind, and in the grey light coming up behind them it went away inland between low snowy hills as flat and straight as a paved street. There were runners' marks on it. Sledges had been up and down it all winter.

"A frozen river's the winter road," said Saban. "In any cold country." He looked up it. "Nobody builds a road where the river'll do it for nothing."

Ferra made the fair copies alone.

She made them at the Treasurer's table in the long room of the Treasurer's house in the upper town, because that was where the terms had to be kept, and the Treasurer sat across from her with the other copy and read every line she made against the line in the draft, and did not speak except to point. It was the best work she had done in a year. She knew it while she was doing it. There was nobody at the table who did not know the work, and nothing to explain, and when a line was right the Treasurer's finger went on to the next one and when it was not the finger stopped, and it was very seldom not right.

The room was quiet. It was quieter than she had expected it to be.

She noticed that on the second morning, with her pen in the ink, and she did not know at first what she was noticing. Then she knew. There was no pen going at the far end of the table. There was no second book open, at an angle to hers, with a woman's hand moving in it and not in Ferra's, copying something only its owner read. She had sat at a table with that pen going for six years, four times a year, and she had never once thought of it as a sound.

She dipped her pen and went on.

At noon she went down to the harbour office, because the harbour dues for the ship had to be settled against the terms and the terms made them different, and the clerk who had written out the berthing warrant on the first day wrote out the new one. He did it quickly. He did it the way he had done the first. At the foot of it he signed his own name, and under his name, in the same hand, the same form of words he had used on the first day, with the office changed.

*for the Sovereign, in her absence.*

Ferra looked at it for a long moment.

The sovereign of Emberfell was nowhere in Emberfell. She was a night's flight away, in an iron ship on a foreign sea, and she had told nobody where. And her harbour had opened that morning as it opened every morning, and her dues had been taken, and her table had sat at noon by office, as it would have sat if she were in the room, and a clerk had signed for her, as he would have signed for anybody whose office was his to sign for. Nothing had stopped. Nothing in Emberfell had so much as slowed.

That was what the terms had sold her. She was looking at it on a berthing warrant, in its first week, working. Ferra understood that she was very probably the only person in the whole of Emberfell who could see it, because nobody who lived here had ever seen it not work.

She thought of the room at the end of the passage in Kaldo, with the window that faced south, and the three of them in it at their tables.

She thanked the clerk and folded the warrant into her book. On the way up the slope she passed a street where forty men with shovels had begun, that morning, to dig out an open channel along its whole length, under the eye of a woman in a physician's coat with a map in one hand and a canvas roll under her arm, who did not look up. Ferra did not stop. She went back to the Treasurer's table and made the rest of the fair copies, and the room was quiet, and she did not write down anything about any of it.

The camps were half a day up the river.

They came to the first of them a little before noon, by the smoke, which went up straight in the still cold from a place where the river bent under a bluff. The ground at the foot of the bluff was black. It was black through the snow, in a wide stain that ran down to the river's edge, and where it met the ice the ice was black too, and there was a smell on the air that Emeric had last smelled on a slip in Emberfell, over a pot, only colder and thinner and sharper, and stronger.

There were four huts, of turf and timber, and a fire, and men.

They had pits dug into the black ground at the foot of the bluff, roofed over with brush against the snow, and they were working in them with picks and shovels, breaking the stuff out where it had come up through the ground and set hard in the cold, and carrying it out in baskets and stacking it like peat. And in a lean-to by the biggest hut, under a roof of turf, there were casks. A long row of them, iron-hooped, bunged, with frost on the hoops.

Saban went forward alone, the last hundred yards, with his hands where the diggers could see them.

That was the arrangement. Saban talked. Nobody this far north had ever seen Emeric's face, and nobody ever would if he kept his hood up and his mouth shut, and Ward and Osric were two big men in cloaks, which in any cold country was the commonest thing in the world. He stood with them at the edge of the black ground and watched Saban walk up to a fire at midwinter at the top of the world and warm his hands at it and say good day in a trader's voice, and begin.

It took most of an hour. Emeric could not hear much of it. He did not need to. He had watched Saban buy things for years, and he knew the shape of it by now from twenty paces: the long easy start, about the cold and the river and the roads, and the questions that were not about anything, and then the first sight of the casks, not looked at, and the long slow walk round to them.

The diggers were not surprised to be asked. They were surprised to be asked now. They broke the pitch out in the winter, when the ground held it and nothing else could be done, and they kept the liquid in the casks in the cold, where it kept, and in spring when the river went out they took it down to the posts and sold it there. Nobody came up the river at midwinter to buy. Four strangers with Rands at the top of the world, in the dead of the year, were found money, and found money does not ask very much about where it came from.

And they paid in Rands. Nobody at the fire looked twice at a Rand. Duvrenne had traded with Randillia for as long as either had had a coin, and a Rand was a Rand this far north the same as on the quay at Milford.

Saban called him over at the end, when the price was nearly settled, because Emeric had told him on the boat that he would want to smell it first.

He went over with his hood forward and his head down, like a servant sent for, and a digger with a beard frozen white knocked the bung out of one of the casks for him with the back of a hatchet, and he bent over it.

It was not Rowena's.

He knew it at once, and it took him a moment to know why. Hers had been thick and dark and heavy, a smell like a road in summer. This was thinner. It had something over the top of the road smell, sharp, light, a smell that went up the back of his nose and made his eyes water in the cold, and it was that he had come a night's flight for, and he had not known it until he smelled it.

Hers had come thousands of miles. It had come down a river in spring and sat at a post and gone into a ship's hold and round through the inner water and down through the strait, in warm holds, for months, with its bungs working in the heat, and everything light in it had gone off into the air on the way, a little every day, the way the light part of anything goes first when you leave it open. By the time it reached a pot on a slip in Emberfell there was nothing left in it but the heavy end.

This had come up out of the ground this winter and gone into a cask and sat in the cold ever since, with nothing to drive anything off it. The light part was still in it. And the light part was the part he could put through a still.

He straightened up and nodded at Saban, once, and went back to the edge of the black ground with his head down.

Saban bought five casks.

It was Ferra's figure. She had given it to Emeric on the quay at Emberfell, off the scale, as the most they should carry back down a frozen river on their own backs if it came to that, and it had not come to that, but he kept to it. The diggers asked five Silver a cask, and Saban said three, and they settled at four, which Saban said afterward was a Silver a cask more than it should have been and exactly the right amount to keep anybody from remembering the haggle. Twenty Silver for the five.

Then Saban bought the sledge.

It was the diggers' own, a long low thing of ash with iron on the runners, that they would have hitched a horse to on a road. They had another. Two Silver for it and a coil of rope, and the digger with the frozen beard threw in a pair of old sacks to keep the casks from rubbing, because he had been paid well for a morning when he had expected to be paid nothing.

Loaded, the sledge was a horse's load. On a road it would have wanted a horse, and on a road with any hill in it, two.

They were not on a road. Ward and Osric took the traces, one to either side of the pole, and leaned into them, and the sledge went. It went the way things go on ice, which is to say that it did not want to start and then did not want to stop. Two big men in plate pulling on a flat frozen river moved what would have stalled a cart in mud, and moved it at a walk, and kept it moving.

Emeric thought, for the length of about ten steps, about making it lighter.

He knew how. He had lifted two tons of iron off the water at the Branch in the thaw on four gravity stones, and held it for less than a count of ten, and it had cost him the rest of the day. Stones set light under five casks and a sledge, on a cell of their own, would have been a good deal less than that, and would not have needed him at all. But there was no spare gravity stone aboard that ship that did not belong to her, in her frames, in the reserve set under the deck beams; he would have had to strip her to do it, on the one night in her life when she needed every stone she had. And the ice was already doing most of what a stone would have done. And more than either of those, a sledge going downriver at dusk in a strange country with a light under it, lit from underneath by stones that glowed, would be the one thing a man standing on the bank would remember for the rest of his life, and tell his sons, and his sons would tell theirs.

He did not do it. Nor did they fly the casks out, which he had thought of on the chart in the day room and put away there. Air stones roared. Stone light showed for miles on a clear night in open country, and the capes and the chutes were built to bring a man down alive, not a man and a cask. They went down the river on foot, the old way.

He pushed. Where the banks came in and the ice ran rough under a bluff, or where snow had drifted across the river and the runners dragged, he and Saban went behind and put their shoulders to the back of the sledge while Ward and Osric pulled, and the sledge went over. He was not a knight. He had a knight's plate on him, thin, and none of what a knight had under it, and nothing he had done in the delve in ten years had ever put a pound on him. By the second bluff his shoulders were burning. By the fourth he was pushing with his teeth shut and saying nothing, and Ward, in the traces ahead of him, did not look round, and Emeric knew that Ward knew exactly how he was doing and would not mention it.

At dusk they passed the last of the camps, the one nearest the sea, and a man was standing on the bank.

He was the digger who had sold them the sledge. He had come down along the bank by some path of his own, faster than they had come down the ice, and he stood at the top of the bank with his hands in his armpits in the last grey light and watched them go by below him: two big cloaked men in the traces, pulling what he would have hitched a horse to, at a pace that never slowed, downriver toward a coast where there was no port and no village and nothing at all, in the dark.

He did not call out. He did not follow. He stood on the bank and watched until they were round the next bend, and they never saw him again, and he never saw his sledge again either.

At the river mouth the boat was where they had left it, turned over under the bank with the oar beside it and snow on its keel.

It took most of the night. The boat would take two casks and two men, and no more with any safety in the dark, and it was two miles out to where the ship lay with her lamps shuttered and nothing to show where she was but a single hooded lantern Fletcher had hung low over her lee side. They rowed the casks out two at a time, and then the last one alone, and each time the ship's side came up out of the dark at them black and huge and silent, and Fletcher and Tobe and Orrin were at the rail to take the lines. Between the trips Emeric and Saban sat on the shelf of ice at the river mouth with their backs against the sledge and said nothing, and Emeric found that he had stopped being able to feel his feet some time ago and could not remember when.

When the last cask was gone they broke up the sledge.

They did it with the hatchet the digger had knocked the bung out with, which Saban had bought off him for another Silver at the last moment, because he said a sledge you could not break up was a sledge you could not get rid of. They broke it into its runners and its bed and its pole, and lashed the pieces into the bottom of the boat with stones off the shore, and rowed out halfway to the ship, and put it over the side. It went down with no sound at all. The hatchet went after it.

The casks went into the forepeak.

Fletcher had made room for them against the first iron wall, beside Rowena's two and her crate of cakes, with the cable between them and the bow, as far from any fire stone as a thing could be put in her. He lashed them himself, by a covered lamp, and stood in the scuttle afterward looking down at seven casks and a crate, and said that it was the first cargo she had ever carried that was worth more than she was, and then said he did not know that, and went up.

They took the plate off in the armoury. Emeric took the grey stone out of the breast of his suit and carried it aft in his closed hand to the stern cabin, and put it back in the silver star, and put the star on the coat. His hands were shaking with the cold and he did it very carefully.

She lifted before dawn.

The land was grey behind them in the first of the light, the long white shore and the hills behind it, and up one valley a thread of smoke, and nothing else, and then there was no land at all. Ward took her up into the band to fill, over a sea nobody had ever flown before, and turned her south.

Rowena was at the port on the starboard side, where she had been when they left. She had watched the boat come and go all night. Emeric had seen her at the port every time the boat came alongside out of the dark, standing back from it where the hooded lantern would not show her face, and she had not come down to the rail once.

She waited until the last of the coast had gone. Then she turned from the port.

"Was it worth it?" she said. "A night in somebody else's kingdom."

He thought about it honestly, because she had asked it honestly.

"It was worth a great deal more than I paid for it," he said.

Rowena looked at him for a long moment, in the grey light coming in at the port, with the blanket still round her shoulders.

"That," she said, "is how a smuggler talks."

He did not argue. She turned back to the port, and he went aft to sleep.
