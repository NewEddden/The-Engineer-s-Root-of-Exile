Chapter 2 - Anything Whispered Is True

The man on the raised seat was saying twenty-two years.

Marcus heard it with ears that weren't his. They were younger, and they rang a little, like the morning after too much wine. The banners hanging behind the throne were green over grey, threaded with gold, and he knew those colors. He knew the shape of this hall, the two doors at his back and the one at the far end, and how many people it held when it was full, which was two hundred and some. Tonight it was not full. Sixty or seventy of them stood in a ring around the floor, close enough to watch his face.

He knew all of that before he knew his own name. Then the name came too.

Emeric Stelan Randillia. Third prince. Twenty-two.

The memories didn't arrive in order. They came feelings first and facts after, like water reaching a dry field before anyone can see where it's going. A sour stomach. Shame with something hot under it. And under that, older and harder to look at, a dread so worn in that it felt like part of the furniture.

The facts followed. Last night there had been a banquet. He'd had eleven cups, he could count them now, and somewhere after the ninth he'd stood up in front of half the court and the whole crystal delegation from Duvrenne and told the king's guests what he thought of the price they'd just agreed.

Twenty years, fixed. The crown would buy crystals at one price for twenty years.

You only fix a price that long, he'd said, too loudly, to Princess Alina of Duvrenne, who had come as her father's envoy, and whose father owned the mines, if you know where it's going. Or if you don't mean to honor it in year eleven. And then, because he was drunk and because he was right, he'd asked about the yield reports from her father's mines, the ones that had stopped arriving.

She hadn't denied it. She'd gone very quiet, which was worse.

On the raised seat, King Harrold Randillia, his father, was finishing.

"A prince who has produced nothing in twenty-two years," the king said, "will produce nothing more. You will go south. You will hold a title that is a place and not an office, and you will learn there what this court could not teach you."

Exile. The hall took the word and passed it around in murmurs.

Marcus stood very still in a body he had owned for perhaps a minute and understood that he had just been sentenced for something he hadn't done, and that he agreed with every word of it.



At the king's right hand stood the eldest son, Corbin, and Corbin was wearing a face.

It was a good face. Grave, regretful, a brother's sorrow held in check out of respect for the crown. He held it toward the room, and then, for the length of one breath, he turned it toward Emeric, so that Emeric would know it was being worn for him.

The list started on its own.

Two men by the second pillar, who had laughed at nothing more and then covered it with their hands. A woman in blue who had looked at Corbin before she looked at the king. An older man near the far door, whose contempt had been settled for years, as finished as a verdict.

Marcus didn't decide to keep the list. He found it already in progress, like waking halfway down a staircase in the dark.

They let him walk out. Nobody stopped him and nobody spoke to him, and the doors closed behind him with a sound he would remember.

In the corridor a very large man fell in at his shoulder. Six and a half feet of him, in a knight's plain coat, moving with the particular quiet of someone who had been walking at this exact distance from Emeric for fifteen years. Ward. The name came with something warm attached, the first warm thing in all of it.

They walked a while. The corridor was long and cold and lit by stones set into the wall at intervals, each giving off a steady white light.

"How do you think I did tonight?" Emeric said.

Ward didn't break stride. "Spectacular, Prince Emeric."

Emeric laughed.

It came up out of him without permission, too loud for the corridor, and he knew it for his own as he heard it. The prince hadn't laughed like that in years. The engineer had never once laughed in a corridor. It belonged to whoever was walking here now, and it was the first thing the two of them had done together.

The laugh shook something loose.

He reached back for his childhood the way you reach for a railing, and the railing wasn't there. He had memories of being eight, nine, twelve, plenty of them. Before seven there was almost nothing: a smell of candle wax, a blue that might have been a ceiling, no faces. It was a wall, with a few scraps caught at the foot of it.

One image sat against the near side of it. He was small and being carried, fast, by a younger Ward with no grey in his beard, and Emeric was looking back past Ward's shoulder at something he couldn't see. After that came the noise of the castle in a panic. The queen. A carriage. An attack.

His mother was dead. Amara. He had the name. He did not have a face to put to it.

And he had never looked into it. Fifteen years, and he'd never asked a servant, opened a record or put a single question to his father. That was what stopped him in the corridor, more than the wall. Children forget. A grown man who never asks, for fifteen years, about the day his mother died is doing something. Someone had to keep that door shut every day, and if it was him, he couldn't remember deciding to.

Ward had stopped too, and waited, as he always did.

Emeric filed it. Then he filed the bigger question under it, the one about who exactly was standing in this corridor, and he did it deliberately: one entry, no date. The dread belonged to this body. The counting belonged to the man who'd died in the street. The laugh was his own. That would have to do for now.

"I'm fine," he said, and started walking again.



That night the fourteen months came back.

They came in no particular order, but they came whole, and he lay on his back in a bed he knew and let them come.

After the carriage, his rooms had been moved twice in one week. The guards on his door had changed every few days, never the same man twice running. There'd been a food taster, a thin young man who apologized before every meal, as though the poison might be his fault. A tutor had stopped coming one morning and nobody said why. So had a cook. There had been four more attempts on him in those fourteen months. He remembered three of them clearly and one only as a smell.

Then, the winter he was eight, his father had come to his room alone.

No guards inside the door. No candle but the one by the bed. The king of Randillia had sat down on the edge of the mattress, and the mattress had dipped under him, and Emeric remembered that more clearly than the words. A king's weight is the same as any man's.

There were people, his father said, who wanted him dead. Not a man. Not a house. Something older than a house, with money and patience, and his father had spent a year looking for a name and found nothing he could use. He couldn't fight what he couldn't find.

"So we'll change what they're looking at," the king had said. "A threat gets removed. A joke gets ignored."

From then on Emeric was to be the joke. Reclusive. Lackluster. A boy nobody would bother killing because he would never matter. He was to keep it up until his father told him it was over.

And there was the code. His father had taught it to him that same night, sitting on the bed, in a voice so low it died before it reached the door. Anything I say at this volume is true. Anything said at the volume of a room was for whoever might be listening. It might look like the truth. It might even be partly true. It was never the whole of it. And whatever was whispered was never to be repeated at a speaking voice. Not to Ward. Not to anyone.

Why not just use a ward? Emeric had asked. A silence on the room.

Because a ward can be felt two rooms away, his father said. A ward tells everyone in the building that a secret is being kept. A whisper tells nobody anything.

Eight years old, and he had loved it. It had been the best game anyone had ever given him.

Lying in the dark at twenty-two, he went back through fourteen years of memory looking for the night his father came to tell him the game was over. It wasn't there. Nobody had ever told him. He'd kept playing, and kept playing, until the boy pretending to be nothing had become a man who did nothing, and the two were hard to tell apart.

He didn't sleep. Eventually he got up, lit the candle by the bed, and sat on the floor in front of it.

There was one thing he could remember learning before the drinking started, one of the first things anyone with the gift is taught. You reach out, and the flame leans toward your finger.

He reached.

Something stirred behind his breastbone, stiff as a hinge nobody had oiled. A shape began in the air above the wick, a ring of faint light no bigger than a coin with a mark at its center, and it was ugly, uneven, and it came apart before it closed. The flame flickered once. It didn't lean.

A headache bloomed behind his eyes, sharp and stupid.

Fourteen years of nothing. He noted it the way he'd have noted a seized bearing on a machine nobody had run since it was installed. Nothing in it was broken. It needed work, and the work would be slow.

He blew out the candle and lay down, and at some point he slept.



In the morning they packed him.

Servants he didn't know moved through his rooms folding things into trunks with the speed of people who wanted to be elsewhere. He stood out of their way and let them.

There was a glass over the washstand. He hadn't looked into it yet, and now he did.

A stranger's face looked back, young and puffy with drink, his skin the warm brown of polished copper, and in it his father's eyes. Gold, the clear flat gold of a coin held up to a window. The royal marker. Every child of the Randillia line had it, back four hundred years; even the ones born with odd-colored eyes had one eye gold. No other family had it at all. It meant that anywhere in the kingdom, before he'd said his name, people knew exactly whose son he was.

He studied it longer than he meant to. Then he turned away and let the servants work. His hand, resting on a shelf he'd stopped seeing years ago, closed on something small and cool.

It was a stone. Grey and smooth, about the size of a hen's egg but flatter, carved all over in a fine repeating pattern that had been worn soft at the edges by handling. It was slightly heavier than it should have been. By any measure he knew, it was worth nothing. It wasn't a mana stone. Nothing in it answered when he reached.

He put it in his pocket without deciding to.

At the bottom of a trunk, under a drawer's worth of things the servants had already emptied into it, he saw an etching stylus, ornate, silver chased along the grip, the kind of thing a child is given by people who expect great things. He knew it secondhand, like a fact about a stranger: when he was seven people had called him a born runesmith, a genius in the making, and had said so in front of his father more than once. He couldn't remember a single one of them saying it. He took the stylus out of the trunk and put it back in himself, near the top, where he could find it. He didn't know yet what he meant to do with it. It was enough, after last night's candle, to know where it was.

The summons came in the afternoon.

On the way, in the long gallery above the kitchens, he felt looked at. Plain and physical, a pressure between the shoulder blades, like a stare across a room before you find it. He turned. Two maids with an armful of linen between them. A guard at the far end, more asleep than not. A window full of grey sky. Nobody was looking at him. He walked on, and the feeling walked with him to the end of the gallery, and at the door it was gone.

His father's office had two men on the outer door and a third at the far end of the corridor, positioned to hear footsteps before they arrived. Emeric noted the third man and understood him. Inside there was a desk and a fire and the king, standing, with his back to the window.

His father didn't greet him. He waited until the door was shut and then came close, closer than a king stands to anyone, and spoke at a volume that died at arm's length.

"The exile is real," he said. "On paper it's real. In the rolls, in the treasury, it's real. Nobody in this building will find a line that says otherwise." A pause. "It isn't your brother's doing. It's mine."

Emeric said nothing.

"I've waited three years for a reason ugly enough that nobody would ask why I sent you away. You gave me one last night. A foreign king's daughter, insulted at my table, in front of two hundred witnesses." Something moved at the corner of the king's mouth and went away. "I couldn't have arranged it better."

The mask had bought time, his father said. It had worked. Whatever had come for him at seven had lost the target. It hadn't lost interest. And the capital was where they had reach, where they could put a man in a kitchen or on a door. Distance was the one thing left to change.

"Kaldo," his father said. "It's a barony in the south. Four hundred years old. A walled town on a big river." The knight who founded it, Kal, had been offered a captaincy and taken land instead. He'd had no heir and left it to his steward's family, the Wexlers, and they had held it ever since and kept their own name. "Twelve days by carriage on the south road. Past Wayford the forest runs most of a day and the road is bad. Rest the horses before that stretch. Not after."

Emeric kept that, word for word.

"Tonight, at the banquet, you'll be given gifts. Among them, a chest of uncut crystals. No crest. No record of where they came from. Any manasmith will buy them." The king's eyes held his. "Write nothing down. Trust no courier I didn't send you. Don't come back until I send for you."

Three rules. Emeric could feel them settling into the place where the code had lived for fourteen years, as if there'd always been room.

He had one question, and he'd had it since he woke up in the hall.

"How much of what I said to her was wrong?"

His father stepped back.

It was a single pace, but it put him out of whispering distance, and when he spoke it was at the volume of the room, flat and final.

"It is done."

Emeric waited. There was always a whisper after, when there was one. A word at the door, a hand on the arm, a sentence that died before it reached the guards.

Nothing came.

He turned it over and got two answers, and he couldn't tell them apart. Either he had been wrong about Alina and the price, and his father was telling him the matter was finished. Or he had been right, and it was not finished at all, and his father would not say so even here, even at a whisper.

And there it was. He saw it as clearly as he'd seen the stack-up on the drawing: the one test he would not run. He'd taken every part of this apart in a night. The court, the code, the exile, the forest road. The one member he wouldn't put a load through was the man in front of him. Whether this was a father hiding his son, or a king moving a piece he'd kept on the board for fifteen years because it might be useful later. He could feel himself choosing not to find out. He let the choice stand, and he knew he was letting it stand.

There were other things his father did not say, and Emeric counted them on the way to the door. Who had tried to kill him at seven. What Emeric was actually for, because the protection was far too expensive for a third son nobody needed. And how long.

At the door, the king raised his voice so it would carry down the corridor to the man posted at the far end.

"You will go south. You will be quiet. You will cost this house nothing further." The guards straightened, as men do when they're given something to repeat. "I expect you will find some way to disappoint me in it regardless."

The words went down the corridor ahead of him. By the time he reached the stairs, Emeric could hear them being repeated somewhere below, already on their way to the kitchens and the stables and the rooms where people kept lists of their own.

The banquet was in two hours.
