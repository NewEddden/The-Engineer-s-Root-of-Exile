Chapter 2 - Hollow Gifts

Any time a prince or princess left the capital, the kingdom held a banquet in their honor. Well wishes, gifts, a full room of people who'd rather be elsewhere. A going-away party with better silverware.

They held his two nights later, in the same room where the exile had been decided, which Emeric assumed was somebody's idea of efficiency and not a joke. His father had called him in privately that afternoon to say where he was being sent. A barony on the far edge of the kingdom's reach, called Kaldo, named for the knight who founded it. Kal had earned enough merit in service that the crown gave him a choice, stay or go, and he'd gone and built something of his own instead. That was the whole briefing. Then the doors opened and the local nobility filed through with their wishes and their gifts.

Being the third prince meant nothing of real weight was ever going to cross that table. Emeric sat too many steps removed from anything worth buying, and most of the capital's nobles were the quiet-plotting sort who reserved the expensive gifts for people who could return favors later.

His father went first. A purse, pressed into his hands with more formality than warmth, the kind of gift a king gives when he can't say the thing he means. The real money had already been counted out in his chambers by a steward with a carrying voice, and everyone in this room knew the figure, so the purse was theater and both of them knew it.

Alongside it came a modest heap of mana crystals, uncut and unremarkable, the sort of thing an affluent tutor hands a promising student. Emeric read the shape of that without needing help. Harrold had never quite given up on the boy who used to be enthralled by crystals, who read every book he could get his hands on just to see how they worked, and who might resurface given enough idle hours in a quiet barony.

They'd also pawn well. Manasmiths loved the things.

Corbin came next and made a show of it, the way the crown prince made a show of most things. "Something to bring back the old passion." He held out a set of Hollow Crystals, smiling the sort of smile that read as nostalgia to anyone not paying attention, and turned one over in his palm before handing it across. "Unchanging. Just like you. Keep staying true to you, little brother."

The word sat there, pleasant enough on the surface.

Emeric took it apart anyway, out of old habit, the way he'd have parsed a clause that compiled clean and ran wrong. Unchanging isn't a compliment for a gift. It's a diagnosis. They were called Hollow because nobody in recorded history had ever gotten one to do anything at all, dead weight carved to look purposeful, worthless enough that handing them out by the fistful cost Corbin nothing.

So the word wasn't nostalgia. It was a verdict wearing nostalgia's coat. Third son, legitimate by blood and by absolutely nothing else, and never going to be more or less than that no matter what came next. Corbin hadn't handed him a hobby. He'd handed him a sentence, wrapped warm enough that the room would hear the wrong one.

"Your mother gave you one of these once." Softer now, almost gentle. "Thought it might mean something, you having some others."

Something in Emeric's chest went quiet and heavy. No fear in it, nothing sharp, just the specific ache of a memory he'd spent years keeping in a drawer. He couldn't say why the stone itself pulled on that ache the way it did, only that it did, some old grief surfacing without any paperwork attached to explain itself.

In his coat pocket, a dull grey-white lump he had not decided to bring sat against his ribs and did nothing at all.

Corbin was already moving on to the next pleasantry. Whatever his smile was doing by then, it wasn't sincerity.

General Varek brought a soldier's gift for a soldier's road. A heavy oilskin cloak, collar reinforced, stitched the way gear gets stitched for months in the field rather than one season at court. No speech came with it. A short nod, and, "You'll need this more than a crystal where you're going, Your Highness."

First gift all evening that assumed he'd actually be cold.

Armand found him a few gifts later, unassuming as ever, holding out a slim leather-bound notebook with both hands as if it might bruise. "My own notes." His old tutor wasn't quite meeting his eyes. "On crystal theory. I never did finish teaching you properly. Maybe you'll get further with it than I did."

It wasn't an apology. It sat close enough to one that Emeric didn't know where to put it, so he thanked the man and let the line move before either of them had to stand in the space neither of them had filled.

Then a duke he barely knew. Old blood, from a house said to run as far back as the main Randillia line itself. His gift was a set of carved wooden soldiers, the kind you give a boy playing war on his bedroom floor. The kind you give a boy half Emeric's age.

Duke Aldane offered them plainly, with a smirk so small most of the room would have missed it. He'd come out of courtesy, the way most of the capital had come out of courtesy, and he hadn't bothered dressing the insult as anything else. He didn't think Emeric was worth the effort of a decent lie.

The rest of the room moved through the same motions. Minor nobles, foreign envoys, generic luxuries handed over because attendance demanded it. Nobody in that hall expected him to matter again.

Cardinal Augustine came with nothing in his hands, which from a man of the church was its own kind of statement. He was old in the way that had stopped showing on his face a long while back, and he moved through the room like someone who had watched a great many princes come and go and fully expected to watch a great many more.

He stopped, considered Emeric a moment the way a physician considers a case already decided, and offered a blessing in place of a gift.

"May the Light find you where you are going, and make of you something better than you have managed to be."

To the room it was gentle. A holy man wishing a wayward prince well on his road. Emeric heard the second half of it land first, the failure named up front so the grace could be made to sound like generosity, and understood that he had just been reduced to a soul in want of correction in front of four hundred people who would only remember the mercy.

Augustine did not smile. He had no need to.

The line let him go near the end of the evening, and he crossed the hall toward the doors through the last of the crowd. Somewhere in the middle of it a man passed close behind him, near enough that the small hairs on his neck stood up without being told to, and by the time he turned there was nothing but backs and shoulders and the same faces that had spent the night measuring him and finding him wanting.

Something had brushed the edge of a sense he did not yet have a name for. A wrongness with no shape to it, there and gone before he could put a wall around it.

He put it down to the wine and the nerves and the particular airless dread of a room full of people who had all quietly agreed he was finished. It wasn't a reading. It sat beside nothing and belonged to no column, and a single stray figure with nothing to enter it against is a figure a careful man sweeps off the page.

He swept it off and went to find his carriage.

The carriage waiting in the courtyard the next morning was plain, built for distance rather than display, and Emeric's eye caught on the undercarriage before he reached the door.

Four black stones, flecked with small points of light like chips of night sky, sat fitted into the axle housings. They'd been carved in a dense repeating lattice, and nobody cuts a pattern that consistent for decoration. Consistency like that means the pattern is the point.

He crouched for a closer look. A stable hand misread the interest as concern and rocked the carriage to demonstrate, and the whole frame settled and came back in a slow cushioned wobble no cart axle had any business producing on its own.

A stone carved to resist and release force at a fixed threshold instead of passing it straight up through the frame. No moving parts. No linkage. Nothing to lubricate or replace. Somebody had made a rock behave like a spring, and done it without a single spring anywhere in the build.

It shouldn't have surprised him as much as it did. He'd spent years in carriages exactly like this one, crossing the capital at a leisurely pace, thinking about nothing. He wondered when he'd stopped caring how any of it worked, and then remembered that the answer had a date on it, and got in without saying anything.

From the window the city read as a single fortified spiral. Outer wall first, hugging the river's curve, then a band of packed rooftops, then a second wall, then more rooftops, narrower now, until the pattern ran out of room and the castle took over.

The river did most of the work at the bottom, looping nearly the whole way around the base of the hill before doubling back on itself, so that from high enough the city would sit inside a coil of its own moat. Bridges crossed at a handful of fixed points and no more, each narrow enough for a squad to hold and almost certainly meant to be held that way.

He counted the rings without deciding to, the same reflex that used to count load paths, and the count came out clean. Nothing about the layout was accidental. Someone had designed it like a filter.

It dawned on him somewhere past the second wall that the capital didn't smell.

The him from this world had never known it to. The him from Earth had read plenty about what a medieval city was supposed to do to a nose. Back when he was more studious, he'd worked through the books Armand kept giving him, and every one of them said the same thing about this place. The capital was one of the best places in the world to live, and the reason was mages. Not battle mages. Utility. Farming, waste, water, a dozen systems every kingdom needed and this one had made sure were staffed properly.

The mage tower was the loudest example of it.

It rose past the rooftops well before the carriage reached the bridges, and he found himself watching it through the glass without meaning to. Too far off to make out anything. No gallery ring, no mages, just the shape of it against the sky and the fact, sitting in his head with no picture attached, that his tutor had taken him up there once as a boy.

He didn't remember the day. No stairs, no sound of Armand's voice, no sense of what the air was like that high up. What he had was the outcome, flat and factual, the way a fact survives when everything around it has rotted off.

He'd stood at the crown of that tower and watched a full council session hold a floating gallery up by hand.

That was the part nobody in the capital found strange. A full ring of stone hanging clear of the tower's spine with nothing beneath it but air and rune-light, and the thing keeping it there was men. Ten at any hour, each gripping a crystal the size of a melon, eyes half closed, jaws set with the concentration of someone holding a weight he can't put down. Behind each of the ten stood another, watching the way a spotter watches a lift, ready for the instant the first man's grip slipped. Twenty on the ring, always. Every so often one would step back with his shoulders dropping and his relief would move into the gap mid-stride, the handoff practiced so smooth it barely read as a handoff at all.

He'd asked an acolyte why nobody had simply built the thing to hold itself up. The answer had stuck word for word. The gallery held the Circle's council, and it floated because the Circle answered to no staircase. The men holding it weren't laborers. They were the order's best initiates, competing for the honor.

Somewhere in that same conversation a boy who hadn't yet lost interest in how things worked decided the whole arrangement was the finest thing he'd ever seen. That verdict hadn't traveled with him. Only the record of having reached it.

Looking at the same tower now, with nothing behind his eyes but an engineer's read on the problem, Emeric couldn't find the boy's answer anywhere in himself. Twenty men on that ring today. Twenty yesterday. Twenty the year before he was born, and twenty the year before that, generations of them passing an unsolved engineering problem hand to hand because somebody a long time ago had decided the cost was the point rather than the flaw.

It wasn't impressive. It was the most spectacular waste of skilled labor he'd seen since he got here, propped up by a story good enough that nobody currently carrying that gallery had ever been handed a reason to ask why they still had to.

He caught himself sorting again as the tower slid past the window. That reaction was the boy's. This one is mine. The two of them in separate rooms, comparing notes.

Above the rooftops, where the tower had been the only thing tall enough to bother with, the sky had traffic. Ships. Small ones, hulls no longer than a market barge, hanging over the inner rings the way the gallery hung over its spine and by the same trick, gravity stones fed and bled by hand.

He watched one settle toward a landing tier in a series of small corrections, each one a man's whole attention, and had the shape of it before he could talk himself out of the read. The stones didn't hold. They were held. Someone stood in every one of those hulls pouring himself into the lift a breath at a time, in and out, in and out, because the moment the pouring stopped the ship remembered it was a rock and behaved like one.

No setting it and letting it sit. A carriage spring did its job whether anyone believed in it or not. These did theirs exactly as long as a living man kept deciding they would, and not one second past the point his mana ran dry, and every acolyte in the city knew what running a stone dry did to the man doing the running.

That was why you only saw them here. Not the rarity of the stones, though they were rare. Because only the capital could afford to spend people this way, and only the capital had a story polished enough to make the spending read as an honor instead of a fare paid in years off a mage's life.

Down in the poorer rings there'd be men flying the worse routes for the worse coin, the ones who took the work because the work was the only thing standing between them and nothing. The him from Earth knew that arithmetic from the inside. He'd signed the same contract once. Sold the last thing he had left to people who called it a trial and watched his vitals right up until the day it killed him.

He looked away from the ships before the thought could finish itself.

The roads inside the walls were smooth, and that was before you accounted for whatever the axle stones were doing. Just past the gates they went to cobble. Not terrible. Not what he'd gotten used to either. After that they stopped pretending to be roads at all, and the suspension started earning its keep.

Kaldo was the better part of a week out by carriage, slower than a horse would need, but princes didn't ride the whole way through country like this if there was another option. Six guards rode with him. Ward at the lead. Rylan, Declan, Osric, Petram, and a lean, quiet man named Garrin who had said maybe ten words since the capital.

Two days out, three at most, they'd hit the bad stretch, the forest road past Wayford, and the horses would need real rest before it. Not a water break. Rest.

Petram raised it first, reins loose, voice pitched in the easy reasonable register guards used when they weren't trying to be noticed. "Horses won't make the forest road fresh, Your Highness. Better we camp here and push through in daylight tomorrow."

It was sound advice. Nobody argues with sound advice.

By the time the stories started, the fire had burned down to a low orange bed. Osric went first, some half-true business about a bandit camp outside Grayrock that grew in the telling, three men becoming five, five becoming a small army by the end of it. Declan called him on the arithmetic without much heat, and the two of them argued the number back down while Petram laughed into his cup.

"You're all soft," Ward said, not looking up from the blade he was working. "Try a real posting sometime. You'd stop counting bandits and start counting hours until you're relieved."

It wasn't much of a story and he clearly meant it to end there. Nobody pushed. Something in the flatness of it told them not to.

In the lull the fire threw its small ring of light and the dark stood close around it the way dark does past the last town on a road, and Emeric, half-listening, felt it again. The same brush at the edge of that unnamed sense, out past the horses where the treeline started.

He looked and found the ordinary nothing a man always finds looking into the dark. A fox, he told himself, or a marten, or one of the patient night things that drift to the edge of a fire's reach to weigh what the light is worth and then think better of it. He was sitting inside a ring of six armed men. Whatever stood out there in the country wanted the horses or wanted nothing at all, and either way it was a guard's problem.

He filed it where he'd filed the thing in the hall, which was to say nowhere.

Rylan filled the quiet instead, gentler about it.

"Kaldo used to be good, before all that." He poked at the fire with a stick, talking more to himself than the circle. "My wife's family's been there three generations. When I was a boy the harbor still had real trade coming through, not just fishing boats patching their own nets. My mother used to walk me down to watch the ships unload." He shook his head. "War took the men. Plague took the rest. My family stayed through both. I don't know if that was courage or just nobody having anywhere else to go."

"Your wife still there?" Petram asked, easy, like it was nothing.

"Nessa, yeah. And our boy Finn. Little Wren too." His voice warmed on the names. "Haven't seen them in four months. Figured I'd get a real homecoming out of this posting, at least."

Garrin didn't say anything. He listened, the way he'd been listening the whole trip.

Petram dug tea out of the carriage stores and steeped it over the fire, and the cup came around, and Emeric took it without a thought in his head. Bitter, herbal, the kind of thing men drank on the road all the time.

Ward waved his off.

"Don't drink what I didn't watch poured," he said, to nobody, in the tone of a man who had made the same joke at his own expense for fifteen years and had long ago stopped expecting it to be funny.

Osric said something about that being a fine way to die of thirst. Declan laughed. Emeric finished the cup.

He noticed the fire first. It went soft at the edges, the orange spreading past where the wood was, and he had a moment of thinking he'd simply gotten tired faster than usual.

Then Osric tipped sideways off the log he was sitting on and did not put a hand out to catch himself, and Emeric understood the whole thing at once, in one clean piece, with total clarity and absolutely no time to spend on it.

He tried to say the word poison. His jaw came open. What came out had no shape in it, a sound rather than a warning, and even as it happened some part of him was already noting the failure like a line of test data.

Then his body stopped answering him at all. Not sleep. Sleep would have been a mercy and this was nothing like it. He was entirely awake behind his own face and his arms had simply stopped being his, gone heavy and far away and belonging to somebody else's inventory.

Declan went down. Rylan got half upright and then folded over his own knees with a slowness that looked almost polite.

Petram stood up.

He did it without hurry, brushing off the back of his trousers the way a man does when he's been sitting on a log a while, and he drew, and he started walking toward Ward's back.

Emeric watched him do it. He watched the whole distance get crossed. Every instruction he had was firing and not one of them was arriving anywhere, and the last of the tea went out of his hand and the cup hit the stones by the fire ring with a small flat sound that was the only thing he managed to contribute to any of it.

Ward turned on the sound.

After that it was quick. He got his forearm up where his spine had been and took the blade across it, and then he put Petram into the dirt hard enough that the man wasn't going to be running anywhere for a while. Emeric was surprised he was still breathing. Bleeding and one-armed, Ward had kept enough sense to remember that a dead traitor tells you nothing.

Garrin was already gone. Boots into the treeline before anyone thought to stop him.

Ward stood over Petram a moment with his good hand flexing at his side, and Emeric watched him look at the trees and decide against them. The bracer on his forearm sat dead against the skin. Whatever the Kinetic Rune in it needed to pull the crystal's charge through, a clean line of focus, an unsplit head, he didn't have it, and chasing a healthy man through dark woods on legs that had already been asked for one miracle was a good way to end the night face down in a creek.

He let him go.

It was a long time before Emeric got his hands back, and when he did they shook. He sat in the dirt in the grey part of the night with the fire down to coals, watching Ward bind his own wound with his teeth and one working hand, and he did the arithmetic he had been putting off for an hour.

He had been awake for all of it. Everyone else had gone down and he had gone down differently, and he could construct a version where the drug held him and a version where the drug had loosened by the time Petram stood and he simply had not moved, and there was no measurement anywhere in the world that would ever tell him which one had happened.

He knew which one he believed.

"I froze." His voice came out smaller than he meant it to. "Just like then."

Ward didn't ask what he meant.

"You were seven," he said.

Emeric was already elsewhere. A carriage that smelled like his mother's perfume. Her arm coming across him, putting him behind her body half a second before the world ended. He hadn't moved then either. He hadn't moved, and she'd died, and somewhere in the years after, a small silent part of him had decided that not moving was safer than moving wrong.

He dragged the back of a filthy hand across his face. From now on, he thought, there's no Emeric and Marcus. There's just me. I don't get to hide behind either name anymore, not when standing there quietly nearly got a man killed three feet in front of me.
