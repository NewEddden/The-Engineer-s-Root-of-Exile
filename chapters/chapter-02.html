Chapter 2 - Hollow Gifts

His father wouldn't look at him, which was normal, and stood the whole time, which was not.

The king took audiences sitting. Emeric had twenty-two years of memory on the subject and every one of them had Harrold in a chair, because a man who stands has somewhere to be and a king is the place everyone else has to be. Today he stood at the window with his back to the room and his hands behind him, and he laid out the terms of his son's exile to a pane of glass.

"Kaldo," he said. "A barony east of here. Coastal. You'll hold it in the crown's name."

Emeric's memory produced the rest before he asked for it, the way it produced everything now, unpacked and waiting. Kaldo, named for the knight who founded it. Kal had earned enough merit in service that the crown offered him a choice, stay or go, and he had gone and built something of his own out of nothing but the offer. Four generations back. There was a song about it that Emeric could reproduce in full and had never once enjoyed.

"A reward, historically," Emeric said. "Kaldo."

"Historically."

"So I'm being given the same thing Kal was given, minus the part where anyone thinks I earned it."

Harrold's shoulders did something small. Not a flinch. The muscular equivalent of a man deciding not to answer.

"You'll take the east road. Past Wayford there's forest for the better part of a day, and the road through it is poor. Rest the horses before it, not after."

That landed oddly and Emeric turned it over while his father kept talking. Wayford was four days out. The condition of a stretch of provincial road was the kind of thing a quartermaster knew, or a captain, or a man who had recently and specifically looked it up. It was not something a king carried in his head about a place he was throwing a son into.

He filed it under kings know things, which was a heading he had been using for years to avoid thinking too hard about his father, and which, in fairness to the version of him that had invented it, had never once been wrong before.

"Father. About Lady Alina."

"It is done."

"I'm not arguing it. I want to know how much of what I said was wrong."

He had spent a day and a half hoping the evening would come back in pieces, the merciful way a bad night sometimes does. It came back whole and in order and he could have recited it from the soup.

The delegation had put a generous number on the table. A fixed price on Randillian crystal, held twenty years, the first three paid up front. The hall had liked it. His father had liked it. Emeric had been six cups into liking absolutely everything.

And then something in him that had been asleep since he was twelve sat up and did arithmetic.

Nobody fixes a price for twenty years unless he already knows which way it's going to move. Either you have found a deposit you haven't declared, or you do not intend to be honoring this in year eleven, and I would put money on the deposit, because you have been generous, and generous is what men are with something they have already got.

He had said it at a volume that reached the musicians. He had said it to a woman who had traveled three weeks and negotiated in good faith as far as anyone in that room knew. Then, because he was drunk and because his mouth had been running unsupervised for the better part of a decade, he had added a remark about her father's mines that he was not going to repeat to anyone ever again, including himself.

Alina's face had gone cold and flat. The room turned at once, like a field of wheat under a gust.

And here was the part that had kept him awake for two nights. She hadn't denied it. A drunk third prince nobody had ever had cause to listen to told her delegation's business to a banquet hall, and she had gone quiet, and quiet is not what innocence does.

"So that's what I'm asking," he said. "How much of it was wrong."

"It is done," Harrold said again, and this time Emeric heard the size of the voice.

It was pitched wrong. Too large for a room with two men in it. His father was speaking at a volume that reached the doors, past the doors, out into the corridor where the guards stood and the pages waited and every servant in the palace had a cousin who told stories for coin.

Performing, Emeric thought, with a bitterness that surprised him by how well aged it was. Even now. He can't even throw me out without an audience.

At the door, Harrold said one more thing, still to the window. "Kaldo is far from here."

Emeric waited for the rest of it. There wasn't any.

"Yes," he said. "That does seem to be the point," and left, and did not see his father's hands come apart behind his back.

Any time a prince or princess left the capital, the kingdom held a banquet in their honor. Well wishes, gifts, a full room of people who would rather be elsewhere. A going-away party with better silverware. They held his in the same room where the exile had been decided, which he assumed was scheduling and not cruelty, and then thought about for another second and revised.

Being the third prince meant nothing of real weight was ever going to cross that table. Emeric sat too many steps removed from anything worth buying, and most of the capital's nobles were the quiet-plotting sort who reserved their expensive gifts for people who could return favors later. He counted the room the way he now counted everything. Two hundred and change. Two of them related to him.

There should have been six. He kept coming back to that.

Zale had gone north two years ago, but not to serve at some border post or hold together some treaty. He had left the kingdom entirely.

He had taken a couple of ships, a small crew, and enough supplies to make the journey worth the risk, then sailed north without telling anyone where he intended to go. Since then, word had come only in fragments. A portmaster claiming to have seen one of his ships. A merchant swearing Zale had passed through some northern harbor. A sailor insisting he had heard his name in a settlement beyond the kingdom's reach.

None of it was certain.

That was the point.

Zale had realized what his eldest brother would do if he remained close to the capital. He had watched the court long enough to understand that being second in line was not an honor when the man ahead of you wanted the crown badly enough. If Zale stayed within reach, eventually his brother would have to decide whether to remove him.

So Zale had made himself impossible to find.

Emeric was no threat to the succession. Not yet. The assassination attempt had left him too traumatized to reach for power, and everyone at court knew it. The fourth prince was still too young to matter. Their sister was a lady, and no woman had ever sat the throne as King.

That left Zale.

So he disappeared.

Somewhere far to the north, beyond the kingdom's borders, he was apparently still alive and apparently still moving. Adventuring, if the stories were to be believed. Exploring places no one in the capital had any reason to care about. He had made certain that even those who knew him best could not say where he truly was.

Emeric understood why.

You couldn't resent a man for disappearing when the alternative was giving his brother the chance to kill him.

At least, Emeric couldn't.

Waylon was a morning's ride and had gone hunting. A page delivered that with the particular brightness servants use when they've been given the answer in advance, which meant somebody had anticipated the question and prepared for it, which made it less a hunt than an alibi with antlers.

Emeric was fairly sure the alibi hadn't been necessary. Waylon had been four years old the summer his brother became a drunk, and nobody had ever explained to a four-year-old why the brother who used to build things with him now had somewhere else to be, every time, for fifteen years. Waylon had drawn the obvious conclusion, which was that he was not worth staying for, and had spent most of his adolescence proving he no longer cared, which was how Emeric knew he still did.

He had never once been able to say a word about it. That was the toll, and it came due at intervals, and he had gotten very good at paying it without moving his face.

Maeve got no explanation at all. He asked twice. The first time he got a bow. The second time he got a different servant.

He turned it over between the third and fourth minor noble. His sister had taught him to swim by pushing him into a river. She had lied to their father on his behalf so many times that she had once done it by reflex about something he hadn't actually done. Maeve doted on all of them, including Corbin, which Emeric had privately downgraded from a virtue to a blind spot. If she had decided he was finished, she would have come and told him so to his face, at length, with examples. Absence wasn't her.

Which left two readings. Either his sister had stopped caring in a way that broke pattern hard enough to be its own event, or somebody had made sure she wasn't in this room tonight. He couldn't tell the two apart from where he was sitting, and he made himself stop trying, and failed, and kept picking at it through most of the gift line like a man worrying a tooth.

Their mother had been gone six years by then, back east to the holding she had been born to hold, and Emeric had watched that departure at sixteen and filed it under the same heading as everything else his father did to people. Rowena had come to Randillia as a treaty and had left when the treaty stopped needing her in a room. Maeve wrote to her. Waylon didn't.

Sabina was in the palace and had not come down. Nobody found that remarkable. The first queen did not attend the disgrace of the third queen's son, and no page had been briefed with a bright little answer about it, because nobody in two hundred people had thought to ask.

His father went first, because protocol said so. Fifty gold Rand in a pouch, pressed into his hands with more formality than warmth. Alongside it a modest heap of mana crystals, uncut and unremarkable, the sort of thing an affluent tutor hands a promising student.

Emeric read the shape of it without needing help. Harrold had never quite given up on the boy who used to be enthralled by crystals, who read every book on them he could get his hands on just to see how they worked, and who might resurface given enough idle hours in a quiet barony. A gift for a child who no longer existed, from a man who hadn't checked in a decade.

They would also pawn well. Manasmiths loved the things, and uncut crystals carried no crest, no seal, no record, and no way to trace who had spent them. Fifty gold and a purse of untraceable value handed to a man being sent to the edge of the map.

He noticed that. He noticed it the way he'd noticed the road past Wayford, as a clean fact with no home, and then the line moved and he lost it.

Corbin came next and made a show of it, the way the crown prince made a show of most things.

Emeric had known for years that his brother didn't like him, and he had spent an embarrassing amount of his adolescence trying to locate the reason. He had eventually stopped looking for one, because the shape of the dislike was more informative than its cause. Corbin had never been cruel to him in private. Not once. Fifteen years of small precise humiliations and every single one of them had been performed in front of an audience of at least three.

That wasn't hatred. Hatred leaks. Hatred gets you in a corridor when nobody's looking. What Corbin did was maintenance, regular and public, the way you paint a fence before it rots. He wasn't wounding his brother. He was reminding a room where his brother stood, at intervals, so the room never had to work it out for itself.

It was, Emeric admitted, extremely good statecraft, and he wished it made him feel better to understand it.

"Something to bring back the old passion." Corbin held out a set of Hollow Crystals, smiling the sort of smile that read as nostalgia to anyone not paying attention. He turned one over in his palm before handing it across. "Unchanging. Just like you. Keep staying true to you, little brother."

The word sat there, pleasant enough on the surface.

Emeric took it apart anyway, out of old habit, the way he would once have parsed a clause that compiled clean and ran wrong. Unchanging is not a compliment for a gift. It is a diagnosis. They were called Hollow because nobody in recorded history had ever gotten one to do anything at all, dead weight carved to look purposeful, worthless enough that giving them away by the handful cost Corbin exactly nothing.

So the word was not nostalgia. It was a verdict wearing nostalgia's coat. Just like you. Third son, legitimate by blood and by absolutely nothing else, never going to be more or less than that no matter what came next.

Corbin hadn't handed him a hobby. He'd handed him a sentence, wrapped warm enough that the room would hear the wrong one.

"Your mother gave you one of these once." Softer now, almost gentle. "Thought it might mean something, you having some others."

Emeric's hand went to his pocket before he told it to, and closed on the dull greyish stone he had pulled out of the back of a drawer the night before for no reason he'd been able to name.

There it was. Not a rock a boy had liked. A Hollow Crystal his mother had put in his hands, kept through fifteen years of not remembering why, carried out of a room he was being thrown out of because his fingers had refused to let go of it.

Something in his chest went quiet and heavy. No fear in it, nothing sharp, just the specific ache of a grief he had filed somewhere he couldn't get to on purpose. Corbin was already moving on to the next pleasantry. Whatever his smile was doing by then, it wasn't sincerity.

General Varek brought a soldier's gift for a soldier's road. A heavy oilskin cloak, collar reinforced, stitched the way gear gets stitched for months in the field rather than one season at court. No speech came with it. A short nod, and then: "You'll need this more than a crystal where you're going, Your Highness."

First gift all evening that assumed he'd actually be cold.

Armand found him a few gifts later, unassuming as ever, holding out a slim leather-bound notebook with both hands as if it might bruise. "My own notes." His old tutor wasn't quite meeting his eyes. "On crystal theory. I never did finish teaching you properly. Maybe you'll get further with it than I did."

It wasn't an apology. It sat close enough to one that Emeric didn't know where to put it, so he thanked the man and let the line move before either of them had to stand in the space neither of them had filled.

Then a duke he barely knew. Old blood, from a house said to be as old as the main Randillia line itself. His gift was a set of carved wooden soldiers, the kind you give a boy playing war on his bedroom floor. The kind you give a boy half Emeric's age. Duke Aldane offered them plainly, with a smirk so small most of the room would have missed it. He had come out of courtesy, the way most of the capital had come out of courtesy, and he hadn't bothered dressing the insult as anything else.

He didn't think Emeric was worth the effort of a decent lie. Emeric found he respected that more than he'd expected to, and made a note of the name, and was mildly interested to discover that the note felt less like wounded pride than like bookkeeping.

Cardinal Augustine came with nothing in his hands, which from a man of the church was its own kind of statement. He was old and did not look it, and he moved through the room like a man who had watched a great many princes come and go and fully expected to watch a great many more. He stopped before Emeric, considered him a moment the way a physician considers a case already decided, and offered a blessing in place of a gift.

"May the Light find you where you are going," he said, "and make of you something better than you have managed to be."

To the room it was gentle. A holy man wishing a wayward prince well on his road. Emeric took it apart the way he took everything apart now. Where you are going was not comfort, it was placement, a man being filed somewhere out of sight and told it was providence. Better than you have managed to be was not encouragement, it was assessment, a verdict on the whole of him handed over as prayer, the failure named first so the grace could be made to sound like generosity.

Augustine did not smile. He had no need to. He had reduced a prince to a soul in want of correction and called it mercy, and the room heard only the mercy.

Two hundred people, and not one of them said her name all night. Not a joke, not a barb, not one of the sly little condolences the capital specialized in. Aldane had handed him toys rather than say it. Augustine had blessed him rather than say it.

He had come into the hall braced to spend the evening being reminded. Instead the subject went around him like water around a stone, and it took him until the doors to work out why that bothered him. A scandal everyone is willing to discuss is a scandal. A scandal nobody will say out loud in a room this size is something else, and he did not yet have a word for the something else.

He crossed the hall toward the doors through the last of the crowd, and somewhere in the middle of it a man passed close behind him, near enough that the small hairs on his neck stood without being told to. By the time he turned there was nothing there but backs and shoulders and the same faces that had spent the evening measuring him and finding him wanting.

Something had brushed the edge of a sense he did not yet have a name for. A wrongness with no shape to it, there and gone before he could put a wall around it. He put it down to the wine, and the nerves, and the particular airless dread of a room full of people who had all quietly agreed he was finished. It was not a reading. It sat beside nothing and belonged to no column, and a single stray number with nothing to enter it against is a number a careful man sweeps off the page.

He swept it off and went to find his carriage.

The carriage in the courtyard was plain, built for distance rather than display, and his eye caught on the undercarriage before he reached the door.

Four black stones, flecked with small points of light like chips of night sky, fitted into the axle housings. Carved in a dense repeating lattice, and nobody cuts a pattern that consistent for decoration. Consistency like that means the pattern is the point. He crouched for a closer look, and a stable hand misread the interest as concern and rocked the carriage to reassure him, and the whole frame settled and came back in a slow cushioned wobble that no cart axle had any business producing on its own.

He had seen that motion before. On a different world, under a different car.

A stone carved to resist and release force at a fixed threshold instead of passing it straight up through the frame. No moving parts. No linkage. Nothing to lubricate or replace. Somebody had made a rock behave like a spring and done it without a single spring anywhere in the build.

It shouldn't have surprised him as much as it did. He had spent years in carriages exactly like this one, crossing the capital at a leisurely pace, thinking about nothing at all.

Today it bothered him. He wondered when he had stopped caring how any of it worked.

He would find out on the road, and it would be worse than he expected.

The city went past the window and he let it. Wall, rooftops, wall, rooftops, narrowing all the way up until the pattern ran out of room and the castle took over. He counted the rings without deciding to, the same reflex that used to count load paths, and somewhere past the second wall it dawned on him that the capital didn't smell.

The him from this world had never known it to. The him from Earth had read enough to know what a city this size was supposed to do to a nose. The books Armand kept giving him, back when he still opened them, had all said the same thing about this place, and the reason was mages. Not battle mages. Utility. Water, waste, farming, a dozen systems every kingdom needed and this one had bothered to staff properly.

The mage tower rose past the rooftops well before the bridges, and he found himself watching it without meaning to. Too far to make out anything. What he had instead was a fact with no picture attached: Armand had taken him up there once, as a boy, and he had stood at the crown of it and watched the Circle's floating gallery hang clear of the tower's spine with nothing under it but air. Ten men at any hour, each gripping a crystal the size of a melon, eyes half closed, and behind each of the ten another man waiting to step into the gap the instant his grip went. He had asked an acolyte why nobody built the thing to hold itself up, and the answer had stuck word for word. The gallery held the Circle's council, and it floated because the Circle answered to no staircase.

A boy who hadn't yet lost interest in how things worked had decided on the spot it was the finest thing he'd ever seen. That verdict hadn't traveled with him. Only the record of having reached it. Twenty men a day, and twenty before them, and twenty before those, generations passing an unsolved engineering problem hand to hand because somebody a long time ago decided the cost was the point rather than the flaw.

Above the rooftops the sky had traffic. Ships, small ones, hulls no longer than a market barge, hanging over the inner rings by the same trick. He watched one settle toward a landing tier in a series of small corrections, each one a man's whole attention, and had the shape of it before he could talk himself out of the read. The stones didn't hold. They were held. Someone stood in every one of those hulls pouring himself into the lift a breath at a time, because the moment the pouring stopped the ship remembered it was a rock and behaved like one.

A carriage spring did its job whether anyone believed in it or not. These did theirs exactly as long as a living man kept deciding they would, and not one second past the point his mana ran dry, and every acolyte in the city knew what running a stone dry did to the man doing the running.

That was why you only saw them here. Not the rarity of the stones, though they were rare. Because only the capital could afford to spend people this way, and only the capital had a story polished enough to make the spending read as an honor instead of a fare paid in years off a man's life.

He had signed that contract himself once, in a waiting room that smelled like new carpet, and they had called it a trial and watched his vitals right up until the day it killed him.

He looked away from the ships before the thought could finish.

"Goodbye, sweet Randillia City," he said, and heard the sadness in it.

The roads inside the walls were smooth, and that was before you accounted for whatever the axle stones were doing. Past the gates they went to cobble. Not terrible. After that they stopped pretending to be roads at all, and the suspension started earning its keep.

Kaldo was the better part of a week out by carriage, slower than a horse would need, but princes didn't ride the whole way through country like this if there was another option. Six guards rode with him. Ward at the lead. Rylan, Declan, Osric, Petram, and a lean quiet man named Garrin who had said perhaps ten words since the capital.

On the second day Emeric got bored enough to ride up alongside Ward, which was a thing he had done roughly four thousand times in his life and which Ward had never once acknowledged as an event.

"Rank the gifts," Emeric said.

"Your Highness."

"Best to worst. Go."

Ward considered the road ahead. "The cloak was good."

"And?"

"That is the ranking."

"You've given me a list of one item and called it a ranking. That's not a ranking, that's a survivor."

Ward said nothing, which was Ward's version of a laugh.

"All right." Emeric shifted in the saddle. "Fifteen years. Say one untrue thing about me. Anything. A compliment. Lie to me, Ward, I'm asking as your prince."

Ward thought about it for long enough that a lesser man would have felt insulted.

"You are not the worst prince I have guarded."

"How many princes have you guarded?"

"One."

Emeric laughed. It came out of him sideways and surprised them both, the first real one in two days, and it hurt somewhere under the ribs the way laughing does when you've been holding a thing very carefully in that exact spot.

"Sorry you got dragged into this," he said, when it had run out. "Whole career and they send you east with the family embarrassment. You'd think fifteen years buys better."

"I requested it."

Emeric looked over. "Did you."

"It was granted the same hour."

He turned that over, and something in it snagged, and he almost had it. A request like that goes to a captain, and the captain goes to a quartermaster, and the quartermaster loses it under a pile for a week, and that is the fastest the palace has ever moved on anything in the history of the building. Same hour meant somebody with authority had been waiting for the request. Or had arranged for it to be made.

The thought reached the edge of his father and stopped there, the way water stops at a wall it has stopped at for so long it no longer registers as a wall.

"Fine," he said. "Then you're an idiot and I'm glad."

He told him the rest that evening, at the fire, before the stories started, because it was going to come out somewhere and Ward was the only person alive it would not be news to.

"Do you remember what my father told me to be?"

Ward's hands went on working the blade. "Yes."

"I was eight." Emeric watched the fire instead of the man. "Four attempts in fourteen months, and the fourth one got close enough that they moved my rooms twice in a week. And he sat me down and told me a threat gets killed and a joke gets ignored, and he was going to make me a joke, and I was going to help."

He remembered the exact posture of it. Harrold in a chair, that time, and looking straight at him, which was the strange part, because it was the last clear memory Emeric had of his father's eyes.

"So I did. I was good at it. I'd get caught drunk in the right places by the right people. Fail things loudly. Ask stupid questions in front of ambassadors so it would get repeated at dinner in three other kingdoms by winter. I thought it was the best game anyone had ever given me, because I was eight, and it was a game where the prize was nobody trying to poison me."

He picked at the dirt with a stick and did not look up.

"And it worked. That's the thing. It worked."

"Yes," Ward said.

"So I couldn't stop. That's the part nobody tells you about a good plan. It doesn't end. There's no year where somebody comes and says the danger's finished, take the mask off, well done. It just keeps working, so you keep doing it, and then one day you're nineteen and you haven't opened a book since you were twelve and you can't remember deciding not to."

He had been circling this for two days, since the carriage, since a rock behaving like a spring had made him feel something he hadn't felt in a decade and he had not been able to name it until an hour ago. The name was curiosity. He had been a boy who took things apart to see how they worked and he had put that boy away as a costume choice and never gone back for him.

"I stopped pretending to be an idle, useless, uninteresting man," he said, "at some point I can't locate, because I became one. The act ate the actor. There's no seam. I've been looking for the seam for two days and there isn't one."

He finally looked over. Ward was watching him with an expression Emeric had spent fifteen years failing to read.

"When?" Emeric said. "You were there for all of it. When did it stop being an act?"

Ward set the blade down, which he never did, and that alone told Emeric the answer was going to cost something.

"Your fourteenth summer," he said. "You had a tutor come with a book on crystal lattices. You told him to leave it and you would look at it later." A pause. "You did not."

"You remember the book."

"I carried it up."

Emeric sat with that. Eight years. Eight years he had been playing a part in front of a man who had watched him stop playing it and had not said a word.

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"I am your guard," Ward said. "I keep you breathing. I was not given the rest of you to keep."

It was, Emeric thought, the single most brutal sentence anyone had said to him all week, and the man had delivered it with the tone of someone reporting a fence in need of repair.

What he did not say, because it was too close to the bone and the fire was too small, was the rest of the arithmetic. His father had built a hollow man on purpose, for the best reason available, and it had worked, and then somewhere in the years after, Harrold had stopped directing the performance and started believing it. That was the only reading that fit the last decade. A king who looked at his son and saw the thing he himself had commissioned and forgot he had ordered it. Forgot, and was disappointed, and finally, over a diplomat's cold face at a party, ran out of patience with a character he had written.

It was a clean explanation. It accounted for everything Emeric had. It required only one assumption, which was that his father did not love him, and he had been making that assumption so long that it no longer looked like an assumption at all. It looked like the floor.

On Earth he had traced a force down through a stack of components, member to member, all the way to the ground, and found the flaw three engineers had missed. He could not do it with his own father. He had all the parts on the table. He simply could not make himself run the load through that one member and see what it held.

By the time the stories started the fire had burned down to a low orange bed. Osric went first, some half-true business about a bandit camp outside Grayrock that grew in the telling, three men becoming five, five becoming a small army by the end of it. Declan called him on the arithmetic without much heat and the two of them argued the number back down while Petram laughed into his cup.

"You're all soft," Ward said, not looking up. "Try a real posting sometime. You'd stop counting bandits and start counting hours until you're relieved."

It wasn't much of a story and he clearly meant it to end there. Nobody pushed. Something in the flatness of it told them not to.

In the lull the fire threw its small ring of light and the dark stood close around it the way dark does past the last town on a road, and Emeric, half-listening, felt it again. The same brush at the edge of that unnamed sense, out past the horses where the treeline started.

He looked and found the ordinary nothing a man always finds looking into the dark. A fox, he told himself. A marten. One of the patient night things that drift to the edge of a fire's reach to weigh what the light is worth and then think better of it. He was surrounded by six armed men. Whatever stood out there could want the horses or want nothing at all, and either way it was a guard's problem.

He filed it where he had filed the thing in the hall, which was to say nowhere, under no heading. A second stray number with no column to keep it in, and no reason yet to think the two were the same figure written twice.

It would occur to him later, with great precision and no use whatsoever, that two data points make a line.

Rylan filled the quiet instead, gentler about it.

"Kaldo used to be good, before all that." He poked at the fire with a stick, talking more to himself than the circle. "My wife's family's been there three generations. When I was a boy the harbor still had real trade coming through, not just fishing boats patching their own nets. My mother used to walk me down to watch the ships unload." He shook his head. "War took the men. Plague took the rest. My family stayed through both. I don't know if that was courage or just nobody having anywhere else to go."

"Your wife still there?" Petram asked, easy, like it was nothing.

"Nessa, yeah. And our boy Finn. Little Wren too." His voice warmed on the names. "Haven't seen them in four months. Figured I'd get a real homecoming out of this posting, at least."

Garrin didn't say anything. He listened, the way he had been listening the whole trip.

Petram had dug tea out of the carriage stores and steeped it over the fire, and Emeric didn't think twice about the cup that came around. Bitter, herbal, the kind of thing men drank on the road all the time.

There was nothing to think about.

Not until the world went soft at the edges and every guard but one slumped where he sat.

"Fuck." He got the word out through a drowsiness that was already taking his jaw. "We've been poisoned."

Ward didn't go down. He swayed, fought it, planted himself between the fire and the carriage like a wall refusing the concept of falling. Whatever dose had been measured to drop a normal man barely made a dent in a frame built like his.

Emeric didn't go down either. His vision blurred and his limbs turned to lead, and then something in him that had always run a little hotter and cleaner than it should have burned the fog off before it finished the job.

He came back to himself in time to see Petram's knife already drawn and already moving toward Ward's back.

And here was the thing he would take with him for a long time afterward: he saw all of it.

He saw the angle of the blade and where it was going. He saw that a shout would reach Ward faster than his body could. He saw the fire iron eighteen inches from his right hand, and the exact arc it would need, and he saw Garrin already rising in his peripheral vision with the unhurried economy of a man who had known this was coming since the capital. He had never in his life been more certain of what to do.

He did none of it. He sat there.

Ward turned faster than a drugged man had any right to and took the blade across his forearm instead of his spine, then put Petram into the dirt hard enough that he wasn't going to be running anywhere. Emeric was surprised the man was still breathing. Half-fogged and bleeding, Ward had kept enough sense to remember that a dead traitor tells you nothing.

Garrin was already gone. Boots into the treeline before anyone thought to stop him. Ward's bracer sat dead against his skin. The Kinetic Rune in it needed a clean line of focus to pull the crystal's charge through, and there was no clean anything in his head right then. He could probably have run the man down on muscle and instinct alone. Chasing a healthy man through dark woods without the boost, on legs he couldn't fully trust, was also a good way to end the night face down in a creek.

He let him go. Somebody would have to find Garrin later, if Garrin was going to be found.

Emeric sat in the dirt afterward with his hands shaking, watching Ward bind his own wound with his teeth and one working hand.

He made himself go back through the evening, because that was the one thing he was still good for. Petram had raised the camp. Petram had brewed the tea. Petram had asked Rylan, easy, like it was nothing, whether his wife was still in Kaldo, and Emeric had heard the question and heard the tone and filed neither, because he had been busy feeling sorry for himself about a fire and a father.

He had all of it. He had had all of it an hour before it happened. The gift only ever worked when he pointed it at something, and he had spent the entire evening pointing it at his own chest.

"I froze." His voice came out smaller than he meant. "Just like then."

Ward didn't ask what he meant.

"You were seven," he said.

Emeric was already elsewhere. A carriage that smelled like his mother's perfume. Her arm coming across him, pushing him behind her body half a second before the world ended. He hadn't moved then either. He hadn't moved, and she'd died, and somewhere in the years after a small silent part of him had decided that not moving was safer than moving wrong.

His hand found the crystal in his pocket. Fifteen years of carrying a dead stone he couldn't explain, given to him by a woman he couldn't picture, and the first honest thought he'd had about himself in a decade arrived on the back of it.

No more Emeric and Marcus. He dragged the back of a filthy hand across his face. There's just me. I don't get to hide behind either name anymore. Not when standing there quietly nearly got my men killed.
