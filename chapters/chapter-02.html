Chapter 2 - Twenty-Two Years in Four Seconds

The room arrived before the man did, and it arrived whole.

Not as description. He did not look up at the vault of the ceiling and work out that it was a vault, or count the banners and deduce whose colors hung on them. He walked into the knowing the way you walk into a kitchen in the dark and put your hand on the drawer you have not thought about in twenty years. The audience hall. Green over grey, hung down the long wall because the short wall took the afternoon and had faded two sets of them to nothing inside a decade. Two hundred and some could stand in here without touching, and he knew that because he had been made to stand in here and be counted among them for most of his life.

Then the people, and the people came with feelings attached and facts trailing behind, which was backward and which he could do nothing about.

Dread first. Then father. Then the rest of it, arriving in the wrong order like a drawer pulled out too fast: the crown, the shoulders under it, a voice that had never once in twenty-two years been raised at him in private. Harrold Randillia. King, and the word came with no ceremony in it at all, because a boy does not learn that word from a herald. He learns it the way he learns which stair creaks.

Pleasure next, and it was somebody else's. Corbin, at the king's right, eldest, and the pleasure arrived before the name did, unmistakable, warm, and aimed.

Then absence with a reason behind it. Zale, second, and the fact came stapled to it: out of the kingdom, and out of it because Corbin wanted the chair and Zale had worked out the arithmetic earlier than anyone.

Then something that stung, and he could not immediately find the wound, and the name was Waylon.

Then warmth, clean and uncomplicated, and Maeve with nothing in her hands and no move to make here.

And then himself, which was the strange one. Emeric Stelan Randillia. Third prince, twenty-two, and the file came out thick and unflattering and entirely familiar, which was its own problem, because he recognized the man in it the way you recognize a photograph of yourself you do not remember being taken. Nineteen years of doing very little at considerable expense.

Underneath that, out of sequence and badly filed, sat one fact that did not match any of the others. At seven he had been considered a potential genius runesmith. Not promising. Not gifted. A word people had used on purpose, in front of his father, more than once.

He looked at that fact for a quarter of a second and could not make it sit anywhere near the rest of the drawer.

Marcus went first, because Marcus always went first. Count the room. Two doors behind, one at the far end, three men on the near wall who were standing in the particular way of men who are on duty and pretending not to be. Sixty, seventy nobles, ringed rather than seated, which meant this was not a session anyone had convened. This was a thing that had happened and people had come to watch. He got the exits and the numbers before he got a single word of what was being said, and he did it with the flat competence of a man reading a fixture drawing.

Emeric came second, and Emeric brought dread, and it came in without asking and did not knock.

That was the part that decided it, if anything did. He could hold the identity question at a distance. He could not hold the dread at a distance, because the dread was not a conclusion he had reached about the room. It was older than the room. It was a thing this body had been carrying up these stairs for two decades and it had simply resumed carrying it the moment there was someone inside to notice.

Then last night arrived, whole and in order, the way the medicine had arrived at a backyard party.

The banquet. The crystal delegation. A woman with her hair off her neck and a delegation's business in her hands, Alina, and a price schedule his father had agreed to that morning and had been pleased about for most of the evening. Twenty years at a fixed rate.

He had been eleven cups into a night with no floor in it, and he had heard the number, and the number had walked up and stood on his chest.

Nobody fixes a price for twenty years. Not for a commodity that has moved in living memory. You fix a price for twenty years when you already know which way it is going to go, or when you do not intend to be honoring it in year eleven, and there is no third door on that corridor. He remembered saying exactly that. He remembered the music stopping in pieces rather than all at once, one instrument at a time, which is how musicians stop when the room has turned and they have not been told why.

Then he had said more. About her father's mines, and about what the yield reports out of them would have to look like for the schedule to be honest, and about what it meant that nobody had shown anyone the yield reports.

He did not take that part out and look at it. He filed it, sealed, in a drawer he had every intention of never opening, and the sealing was itself the most Emeric thing he had done since arriving.

What he kept instead was the thing underneath, and it was not embarrassment.

She had not denied it.

He turned that over while a king was ruining him in front of seventy people, and he turned it over carefully, because it was the only piece of the evening that was worth anything. A drunk third prince with no standing, no office and no record of ever being listened to had said her delegation's business out loud into a room full of musicians, and Alina had gone quiet, and quiet is not what innocence does. Innocence is loud. Innocence is offended, immediately and at length, and then goes home and writes a letter about it.

He had been a drunk. He had also been right, and being right in a room like that had cost him something he had not been carrying when he walked in.

"- have made your choice for you," Harrold was saying, "since you have not made any for yourself in twenty-two years."

Exile. Stripped of the courtesy of a posting, given a place instead, south, and a title that was a location rather than an office. The stated reason was that a prince who had produced nothing in twenty-two years would produce nothing in twenty-two more, and the kingdom would stop paying for the demonstration.

It landed on the room the way a verdict lands. He watched it land. That was the useful thing he did in that hall, the only one: he watched seventy faces take delivery of a sentence, and he catalogued which of them had already known.

Corbin, at his father's right, did not smile. What he did instead took a quarter of a second and was worse: he arranged his face into something grave and respectful, and held it, and made certain that Emeric saw him holding it.

Noted, Emeric thought, without heat, and was mildly surprised to discover that the noting had a place to go.

Because he was, he realized, keeping a list.

Not deciding to keep one. It was simply being kept, somewhere behind the part of him that was busy being sentenced, and the entries were going in cleanly and without his supervision. The two men by the second pillar who had laughed and then remembered to arrange themselves. The woman in blue who had looked at Corbin before she looked at the king, which told him who she believed was running the room. An older man near the front who had not bothered arranging anything at all, and whose contempt had at least the decency to be finished before it arrived.

He did not know what the list was for. He wanted nothing from any of them and had no instrument in the world to use on them. He noticed, with the mild professional interest of a man reading his own gauges, that the list did not contain a single person who had merely been present.

Then he was dismissed to his rooms, and the hall did the thing rooms do, which is close over the space where a person was.

The corridor was cold in the particular way of old stone in the evening, and he walked it without being led, and that was the problem.

His feet knew the turns. Third door, then left where the passage narrowed at the old wall, then the long gallery where the floor changed from cut stone to boards and the sound of a man walking changed with it. He had never been here. He had been here ten thousand times. There was a crack running down one column that he knew the exact feel of under a fingernail, because he had traced it at nine, waiting for something, bored, and the fingernail had been smaller.

So which was it.

Had he taken a body, or had a body taken a mind? He put it as plainly as that, because he had spent eleven years learning not to dress a question up before answering it. If he was Marcus, then Marcus was wearing twenty-two years of another man's reflexes and had not noticed putting them on. If he was Emeric, then Emeric had just inherited three months of a stranger's judgment and a death, and was walking to his rooms with them.

He looked for a test. Something with a result. A measurement that would come back one way if he was the first thing and another way if he was the second, and he could not construct one, and after a few paces he understood that he was not going to be able to, because both men would answer every question he could pose in exactly the same voice.

So he filed it under tomorrow, which was not courage and was not denial. It was triage. There was a problem in front of him with a date on it and this one did not have a date on it, and a man who works the undated problem first does not get to the dated one.

He noticed himself doing it. He noticed that it had been easy. He put that on the list too, though the list did not have a column for himself, and made a small adjustment to the list.

The knight walking half a step behind him had been doing that for fifteen years.

Six foot six and built like a wall that had been given a grudge, hands loose at his sides in the way of a man who has spent his whole adult life keeping them close to something. He had not spoken in the hall. He had not spoken in the corridor. The name surfaced without being sent for.

Ward.

Fifteen years. Emeric had the whole shape of it and none of the texture: a hundred training yards, a thousand doorways, a man standing at the edge of every stupid thing this body had ever done and declining to comment on any of it.

"How do you think I did tonight?" he asked.

He asked it mostly to hear what his own voice did in this throat, and partly because he wanted to know whether the man behind him could be provoked into an opinion.

"Spectacular, Prince Emeric."

Flat. Immediate. No inflection anywhere in it, delivered with the exact weight of a man reporting the depth of a river.

Emeric laughed.

It came out of him before he had decided anything about it, loud in a stone corridor, and it went on slightly longer than the joke justified, and it was the first thing since the street that had felt like it belonged to him rather than to either of the men whose memories he was carrying. He did not explain it. Ward did not ask. They kept walking, and the sound faded out of the gallery ahead of them, and neither of them said anything about the fact that a man who had just been exiled had laughed in a corridor.

And then, because that is how it works, the laugh knocked something loose.

He was reaching for it the way he had been reaching for everything all evening, casually, expecting the drawer to open, and the drawer was not there.

Nothing before his seventh birthday.

He stopped walking. Ward stopped a half step later, without being told, and said nothing.

Not thin memory. Not the ordinary blur that sits at the bottom of every life, where a man has three photographs and a smell and builds the rest from what he has been told. He had that for the years after. He had the crack in the column at nine, and a tutor's name at ten, and the precise taste of a fruit he had not eaten since he was eleven.

Before seven there was a face he could not see and a wall where the rest of it should be, and the wall was smooth.

What he had, and it was all he had, was being carried. Ward's arms, and Ward younger, and himself small enough to be carried, and his own eyes fixed on something a long way past the man holding him. He could not have said what he had been looking at. He did not think he had been looking at anything.

And after it, noise moving through the castle. Not words. The shape a house makes when something has happened in it. The queen. A carriage. Guards, more of them than usual and in the wrong places. An attack, said quietly and often, in the way servants say a thing that is not theirs to say.

He knew his mother was dead. He had a name for her, Amara, and the name came with no face attached and no resistance at all, the way a fact comes when it has been sitting in a drawer so long the drawer has stopped being a drawer and become part of the wall.

Fifteen years.

Fifteen years in this body, with a hole in it that size, and he had never once gone looking. Not as a boy. Not as a young man with nothing else to do and a palace archive two floors below him. Never.

He stood in the gallery with the cold coming up through the boards and turned that over, and it would not come apart the way things came apart for him now. A man does not simply happen to not investigate the death of his own mother for fifteen years. That is not an absence of action. That is a decision, maintained daily, at some cost, and it had been made and kept and renewed a few thousand times.

He could not remember making it.

Somebody had. And whoever it was had done it well enough that it had held for fifteen years without once coming up for review, and had gone on holding right up until a knight told a joke in a corridor and knocked the whole thing sideways.

"Prince Emeric."

"A moment," he said.

Ward gave him the moment. He gave it the way he gave everything, without comment, standing a half step back in the dark with his hands loose at his sides, and he did not ask what it was for.
