Chapter 358 - Beyond the Map

Ferra wanted to buy the window.

She said so on the fourth of April at the mess table on South Sentinel, with the island's supper cleared to one end and her book open at the other, and Fletcher and Zale and Noll Finch on the bench across from her, and Emeric beside her with his coffee going cold because he had been about to say the opposite.

"We wait for a whale," he said.

"We haven't got a whale." She turned the book round. Two columns. One headed Window, with Full, 15 Mar in it in her own hand from Zale's note. The other headed Day, and empty. "The last one came on the fourth of February. The one before that was autumn, by the castaways' book. One a year, if we're lucky, and we don't know when." She put her finger on the empty column. "Zale's ship is sitting on four pegs behind the dunes with her stones charged. Every day she sits there is a crew on wages and a good ship doing nothing. I can price that. I can't price waiting for a fish."

"It's not a fish," said Noll. "Whales aren't fish."

"I can't price waiting for a whale either."

The island's plan had the answer in it already, in Emeric's own hand from two years back, one line under the Catch's description that he had written and then forgotten: or spend it first on a decoy raft of charged stones. Ferra had copied it into her book in October, with a price beside it.

"How many stones," said Fletcher.

"You tell me. Enough to be the densest thing on its line, by a long way, so it doesn't think twice."

Fletcher looked at Noll. Noll counted something on his fingers under the table.

"The reject bin in the yard's got thirty-one," he said. "Cracked ones and short ones, out of the Challenger's first racks and the Hod's old ones. They'll hold charge, just not evenly. I can fill them off the island's canisters in four days."

"Thirty-one cracked stones," said Fletcher. "That's a lot of stone to throw at a rock."

"It's thirty-one stones that were going in the dust mill," said Ferra. "You don't waste a whale. You can waste a reject bin."

Fletcher opened his mouth, and shut it, and laughed, which he didn't do often enough for anybody at the table to have got used to it.

Zale had his big hands round a cup of the island's tea and was looking at the empty column. When Emeric looked at him he said, "Six weeks from the day it fires. Out past the islands and back. It's enough, if nothing breaks."

"And if something breaks?"

"Then I'm a sailor with a broken ship and a full Catch between me and my bed." He drank. "I've been that before. It's not the worst."

"Four days," said Emeric.

Ferra wrote it. Not in the Day column. Above it, small, in pencil: raft, 4 days, Noll. She didn't believe in ink until a thing was done.

The guest had taken her supper at the far end of the bench since the middle of March, with a bucket of sea under the table for her feet and her hood up, and the cook sent the board down to that end first without being asked. It was the pressed fish tonight. The cook made it twice a week now. Some time in January she had decided, without telling anybody, that she could do it better than Emeric, and she had been proving it ever since.

Zale hadn't eaten one since December. The first time the board went round he'd said that fish was for cooking, and that he'd eaten enough of it raw in bad years to last him. Tonight the board had come back up the table with four left on it, and he'd taken one without looking, talking to Noll about the reject bin, and then another.

The guest watched him take the third. She had a good deal more Kaldo than she let on by April, and spent it the way she spent everything on land, sparingly. She used three words now, slowly, the way you set stones across a stream.

"You," she said to Zale. "Like. Fish."

Zale looked at the piece in his fingers as if it had got there on its own. Then he looked at her, quite seriously, the way he looked at a sky he was making his mind up about.

"I love everything about the ocean," he said, and ate it.

Emeric laughed into his coffee before he could stop it. Ferra's hand came down flat on his thigh under the table, hard, and she didn't look up from the book.

"Ow."

"Drink your coffee."

He drank his coffee. Across the table Noll had found something urgent at the bottom of his cup, and his ears had gone red to the tips. Fletcher was studying the beam over the door. At the hatch the cook stood with her cloth on her shoulder and her eyebrows somewhere up near her hair.

The guest didn't say anything else. She took the last piece off the board, dipped it, ate it, and looked at the far wall. At her throat, under the edge of the hood, a little colour came and stayed. Not the slate, and not the pearl. Blue. Deep-water blue.

Zale had already gone back to the bin. "If half of them are cracked through, you'll not get four days out of them," he told Noll, who said yes without having heard a word of it.

Ferra, still not looking up, wrote something small in the margin beside the raft. Emeric read it upside down. Zale, and after it, a question mark. Pencil, like the raft.



Fletcher built it out of the old slip's decking, two layers crossed and spiked, the size of a farm cart's bed, with a frame of oak in the middle like a hen coop where the stones sat in a rack of their own, lashed down with tarred line. He built it in an afternoon, complaining that it was the ugliest thing his yard had ever put in the water. Nobody had asked him to paint it. He painted it anyway, red, so that he could see it go.

Noll filled the stones in the old castaways' hut behind the dunes, because it had stone walls and stood away from everything, and by the second day Emeric could feel the rack from the Cradle's deck without trying: thirty-one voices that didn't agree, like a choir of men each given a different hymn. It was meant to be loud. It was.

They took it round to the reef's north-west face on the morning of the sixth in the island's rowing launch, which had no stone in her anywhere, eight of Fletcher's men at the oars and the raft on a long line astern. The Pull took them as soon as they were past the point. The water looked flat and the land slid. Half a mile up the track from the Catch's line, by Emeric's sense and Zale's eye, Fletcher cut the raft loose.

Then they rowed, hard, across the Pull and out of it, into the slack water under the cliff, while the raft went on alone.

It went slowly at first, turning on the current, red against the grey. Emeric had his sense down on the reef, where the Catch lay in its broken pen with the whale's grey ribs across the wall beside it, quiet and full, a stone the size of a house holding its breath.

The raft came into the line.

He felt the stone notice. There was no other word for it. Something in the reef's bedrock turned toward the raft the way a sleeping dog's ear turns. Then the water between the raft and the pen went smooth, and the raft stopped turning and began to move against the current, straight, the way nothing on that sea ever moved on its own. It went faster, and it hit the pen's broken wall beside the whale with a sound that came back across the water like a barn door slammed, and the oak coop burst, and red planks went up in the air and came down in the lagoon.

The men in the launch cheered. Fletcher didn't. He stood in the stern sheets with his hands on his hips looking at what was left of his paint.

Emeric waited until he was sure. The stone's note went down the way water goes out of a bath, until there was nothing left of it but the grain of the rock.

"Dry," he said.

Ferra was on the Cradle's deck when they came round the headland, with the book. She didn't ask. She looked at his face and then wrote, in ink, in the empty column: 6 Apr. Raft. And under it, because she put every cost where she could see it: 31 stones, rejects. Window bought.



The guest had been etching the shell for three evenings.

She did it at the slab's seaward edge, sitting on her heels, with her feet in the wet and the grey cloak pushed back off her arms. The shell was a flat ribbed thing from the bay's floor, a little larger than her palm, pearl on the inside. Bram had made her a steel scriber, a good one, and she had looked at it politely and put it aside, and gone down onto the pen wall at the Catch once it was spent and broken a sliver off one of the whale's ribs. She worked with that.

Emeric sat with her on the last evening, a little way off, because she let him.

The lines weren't letters. Letters went along. These went round, and in, and crossed each other at angles that looked chosen, some cut deep and some barely scratched, and every so often she stopped and looked out at the bay mouth, or up at the first stars, and then cut again. When he asked, through Zale, what it said, Zale listened to her answer and said, "It's where we are."

"A map?"

Zale asked her. She answered at some length, in a low voice with a click at the back of it that no human throat made.

"Not a map," said Zale. "It's how they say where a place is. Which water runs by it, what's overhead at a season, how far from something they all know. The island's place, the way her people count places." He thought. "The way a man from the Port Town would tell you he lives two streets up from the fish market. You'd have to know the fish market."

When she had finished, she held the shell in both hands for a while without doing anything to it, and Emeric felt her put something into the cut lines. Not much; her note had come back a long way since Imani's knife and the doses, but she spent it carefully. What she put in the shell was small and settled, and it stayed there after she took her hands away, a faint steady sound in the grooves like a struck glass that wouldn't stop ringing.

He would have given a good deal to have Bram look at it, and asked.

She said no. Zale didn't need to translate it.

She wrapped it in a square of oiled cloth from the galley and tied it with a strip of the same, and then she held it out, not to Emeric but to Zale, and said something short.

"By hand," said Zale. "In the thick water. Not thrown. Put it in by hand and wait, and something will come for it, and I'm not to be in its road when it does."

"What will come?"

Zale asked. She looked at Emeric with the eyes that had been afraid of his on the day of the memory read and weren't now, quite, and gave him a word he didn't know.

"She says I'll know it," said Zale. "She says it's polite."

That night at the mess table Ferra wrote down the plan as it stood. It was short. The shell went west with Zale, to tell her people she was alive. When she was at her own density, which Imani said would be the end of summer if the doses held and not before, and when the Challenger had done her long trials, Kaldo would carry her in the Challenger to the wall's edge, where the thick water began, and open the lock, and let her swim home.

Nobody had planned past the lock. It was a long way to the wall's edge, and further than that was somebody else's sea.

Ferra underlined it once and closed the book.



Zale lifted on the eighth, at first light, with the Catch dry and the wind off the land, and went west low and slow on quarter veins, a hundred miles a day and no more, because the rack was no use read in a hurry and Noll wanted the air at every mile of it.

His ship still had no name. She had a stone-keeper, Noll, who had asked for it, and two of Fletcher's riggers: an older one who had sailed with Zale on the coasters, and the yard boy who had crewed her across the bay the August before last. In her hold, in a frame Tess had sent out, she had the charge rack: six matched test stones drained to the same mark, each on a dial, so that Noll could read every watch how fast the air filled them. In her cabin, wrapped in a sock, she had a heavy glass jar with a ground stopper and an iron sleeve over it that Bram had made in a temper because nobody would tell him what it was for.

"Bring me some of it," Emeric said, at the foot of the ladder. "The water."

"Aye." Zale had the shell inside his coat, against his chest. He looked down at Emeric from the third rung. "You'll want to know how fast it goes thin in the jar."

"Like the tooth."

"Like the tooth." Zale climbed. At the top he turned and said, "Tell Ferra I'll be back inside her six weeks," and then he was gone over the rail, and somebody hauled the ladder up after him.

The guest was on the Cradle's deck with her hood back, which she didn't do when the yard was working. She watched the ship come off its pegs, and turn, and gather way along the dunes with the vein louvres open down her flanks, and then she heard Zale whistle, two notes, low, rising, and the ship went forward on it like a horse let off a lead and climbed away west into the grey.

She watched it until there was nothing to watch. Then she put her hood up and went back to the tank.



Tess had pinned the tape's plot on the long wall of the Motion Shop, where the census strip had been for a week, and she had done it in three colours, which meant she was pleased with it.

It was the tenth of April. They had read the raw tape the day after Lantern Night, a long ribbon of strokes that meant nothing to anybody but Tess. Turning it into numbers had taken her and two logic-line clerks ten days. Turning the numbers into a picture had taken her one evening.

Height along the bottom, worked from the barometer. Three lines climbing out of the left-hand side: cold in blue, the stone's charge in black, and in red a line Tess had worked out from the black, because nothing in the canister could read the air's mana directly: how much the air must hold, at each height, to make the stone leak at the rate it did.

"There's the Band," said Tess, and put her stick on it.

The red line went up in a long hump where the station ran. Then, past it, it came down, lower than where it had started, in a slow slope that kept going, and at the far right of the sheet it was lying almost on the bottom of the paper, a thread.

"It doesn't come back," said Armand.

He was in the shop's one good chair, with the cane across his knees that he hadn't needed in three years and carried to point with.

"No," said Tess. "It goes toward nothing. It doesn't get there. The stone gave out first."

That was the black line. In the Band it had held nearly level, costing about what Emeric had said it would. Past the Band the black line fell faster than the work should have cost it. Much faster. It fell like a bucket with a hole in it.

"It's leaking," Emeric said. "Not working. Leaking. The air out there's so thin the stone's bleeding into it."

"Densest bleed fastest," said Armand. "We knew that on the ground."

"We guessed it on the ground. Now it's on a sheet."

The blue line went the other way. At the end Tess had written a figure beside it that made Dov, at his bench, put down his file and come over to look.

"That's not a temperature," said Dov. "That's a grudge."

"And it stopped here," said Tess. "Twenty to one. The stone hit empty, the heater went cold, the clockwork froze. That's the height, from the last pressure it sent. It'll come down in the sea, I should think. I put my name on the canister in case."

Nobody said the thing for a while. Then Tess said it, because she always did.

"So when."

She meant the next one. Not a canister. She had never once said so aloud. She had drawings, though, folded small at the back of her desk, that she had never shown him and he had seen anyway.

"Not this year," he said.

"I know not this year. I've got eyes." She jabbed the black line. "Why."

He gave her the honest answer, and it was the same one he had given himself. Lift was the easy part. That wasn't the trouble. The trouble was Kaldo's own first rule: a failed stone must never kill anybody. On the ground there was always something under the magic, a drop weight, a propeller, a parachute, a mundane floor that caught you when the stone let go. Past the Band there was no floor. There was nothing to push on and no air for weeks, and every stone would bleed itself white faster than it worked, and the only measurements anybody had of the place were on this wall in three colours.

"The floor first," he said. "Then a hull that holds its stones in. Then people. In that order."

Tess looked at the sheet for a long time.

"The floor first," she said, and took a pencil from behind her ear and wrote it on the bottom of the plot, small, in her own hand, under the blue line. Then she wrote, under that, Then the hull. Then us. She didn't write people. Emeric noticed and didn't say anything.

Armand had been looking at the plot's other edge, where the sonde's track had been worked out from the wind readings. It had gone up drifting. The high wind had carried it north-west for most of the climb.

"It read a shade kinder on that leg," said Armand. "The charge. Before the Band. You see it?"

Emeric saw it. A hair. He wouldn't have bet a coin on it.

"My register says the same thing," said Armand, "and has said it for four hundred years, and nobody has ever asked it why. The mages in the north-west towers live longest. Ten years, fifteen, on the rest of us. We always said it was the air." He leaned back. "Perhaps it is the air."

Tess wrote that on the sheet too, in the margin, with a question mark. It was the only question mark on it.

On his way out Emeric put his head into the ward. Imani had Abel Dunning in the clean bed at the end, the one Danner had had, with his good hand on the blanket and the other arm in its sterile sleeve on a frame beside him, and a bowl of liver on the tray that he was regarding the way a man regards a debt. Imani didn't look up from her sheet.

"Out," she said. "You're not sterile and you're not interesting."

He went.



He had written the page in the nights after Tess pinned her plot, and kept it in his pocket, and on the fourteenth he put it on the long table in the castle's long room after supper, when the six were there and the plates were gone and Ferra had the month's post in a stack at her elbow.

She did the post first. Most of it was ordinary, and some of it looked ordinary: a packet among the House's packets for the factors abroad, sealed in Ducatis wax with a merchant's mark, which Saban had made up and Ferra had checked and would go out on Vantic's next ship with all the others. It said nothing much. It was meant to say nothing much. It went every month, and every month the Church said nothing back, and that was what it was for. The Church had been quiet since the winter. Ferra put the packet on the outgoing stack and initialled the list, and that was the heartbeat for April.

Emeric had the big map of the kingdoms on the wall behind him, as it always was. Before he sat down he put his finger on Kaldo and drew it down the road to Ostrel, the whole long way, and took it off at the gate. He did that sometimes. Ward had never asked. Ferra and Imani had seen him do it and knew why. Nobody said anything, and he sat down.

Then the page.

It was a mess of pencil and arrows. At the top, in the clearest hand, he'd written: Mana pools where gravity holds it. That was the Band's reason, and the same reason the ground was thicker than the sky. Under that: Between the pools, a trace. Drifts. Then Tess's red line, copied out, the thread going toward nothing. Then a column down the right side that said ship's heart at the top and under it, mana ice, sealed. Liquid, canisters. A tree. And then, circled, by itself: a tree in a ship's hold.

Bram read that one out loud and looked at him.

"It's for later," said Emeric.

"It's for never, in my hold," said Bram. "You know what roots do to a deck."

Under the circle, smaller, was the line he had come back to most often that week, and which had no answer under it: trees in the Band. Tuned for thin air? Nobody asked him what it meant. Armand read it twice and made a small noise and passed on.

At the bottom of the page, underlined: The wall is the sea, not the sky.

"That's the part I want," said Emeric. "The rest is for later." He turned the page round. "We've been thinking about the drought as a thing the land has. It's not. It's a thing we have, here, because we're a long way from anything thick. The far sea's thick. The Band's thick. And the Band's ours. We've got a station in it with racks growing stones."

"And?" said Ward.

"And seed. Grove seed." He had the rest of it on the back of the page. "You soak the pods in the Band, in the racks, the way the stones soak. A season. Then you crack the coat, which is what a bird's gut does when it eats one, or a dragon's, if the stories are right, only we do it with a mill. Then you drop the pellets over the hills where the groves were before the Church burned them, from the hulls, on the way back from the Band, every run."

"You want to replant the old groves," said Imani.

"Not replant. Re-seed. With seed that grew up thick." He looked at the page. "Every tree that comes up from that is a stone well. Not much of one. Enough, over a hundred years, that a valley's a little denser than it was. And then the next valley."

Saban had not said anything yet. He had his hands folded on the table in front of him and was looking at the line about the trees, and when he did speak it was in the reasonable voice he used when he had already decided he was right.

"You're handing the Church stronger mages."

Nobody at the table pretended not to have thought it.

"Every acre you thicken," said Saban, "is an acre thicker for their towers too. You can't seed the hills for Kaldo and not for Ostrel. The wind doesn't take orders. In a hundred years there'll be a tower mage in every valley you've touched who's twice what his grandfather was. You've fought them. You know what he'll do with it."

"I know that."

"Then why give it them?"

Emeric took a while.

"Because thin mana's a cage," he said. "For everybody. For them as much as us. The towers have been in it for four hundred years, and the Church has burned every grove it ever found. The drought didn't make anybody gentle. It made them hungry." He tapped the page. "Kaldo's not ahead because we've got more mana. We haven't. We're ahead because of the method. Stones in a register. Racks. The dose by the room, not the number. The mundane floor under everything. They can have thick air. They can't have that, not unless they ask for it, and if they ask for it they've stopped being what they are."

Saban looked at him.

"I'm still against it," he said.

"I know."

"I want that in the book."

Ferra wrote it in the book. Saban against. And the date. Saban seemed satisfied by that, the way he usually was, and unfolded his hands.

"It's the land," said Ward, after a bit. He said it to the page, not to anybody. "Putting the land back. That's what you're saying."

"That's one thing."

"What's the other?"

"Making peace with the people it was taken from." Emeric didn't look at Imani or Ferra when he said it. "The ones Vey wrote about. The ones the guest's people are, maybe. If any of them are left and want to talk to us. There's a debt both ways, and none of us know the size of it yet."

Ward nodded slowly. Armand said nothing at all. Bram said that if anybody wanted to make peace with the people who'd built the cave golems he would like to be in the room, and to bring a notebook.

The page went round the table once more. When it came back to Emeric it had something on it that hadn't been there before: in the bottom corner, below the line about the wall, in Ferra's quick small hand, a circle, a smaller one a long way off, and two dots, tiny. The moon, and the far moon, and the two small ones. No words. She had drawn them while Saban was talking.

He looked at her. She was writing the minutes.

"That's not on the agenda," he said.

"It's on your page," said Ferra, without looking up. "I'm putting it on the agenda."



The charge rack read faster every watch from the last islands on.

Noll had the log. He sat with it in the hold by Tess's frame with his knees up, reading the six dials at the turn of each watch with a lamp and writing them down in a column that, by the ninth day out, had stopped looking like a column and started looking like a staircase. Zale had him read it out at the change. He liked to hear it.

"Twice the island," said Noll, on the morning of the ninth day. "Bit more. Fills twice as fast as it did at the island, near enough."

"And yesterday?"

"Half again."

Zale nodded and looked west.

He had stood off the last islands before, a long time ago, in a coaster with a hold full of needles and salt and a captain who wouldn't go further, and seen what he was seeing now: nothing. Open sea, grey-green, under a high thin sky. The charts stopped at the last islands. His own charts stopped there, twenty years of them. West of them the paper was blank except for one word in his own hand from long ago, which was thick, with a question mark.

The question mark could go. He'd known that for two days.

It wasn't in the colour. The sea was the same colour. It was in the way it moved. The swells out here were long, longer than any he knew, and slow, and they came under the ship with a sort of heaviness, as if the water had thought about it first. The birds were big. There had been a gull on the fore rail yesterday the size of a goose, and it had looked at him as if it knew he was a stranger.

And the air. That was the thing he felt first and liked least.

The air did what he asked out here. It had always done what he asked, a little, more than it did for most men; it was why he had a ship. Out here it did more than he asked. On the tenth day he'd whistled for a little more way off the veins, the two notes he'd been whistling since he was a boy on his mother's step, and the ship had jumped forward under him like a horse that had been stung and thrown the older rigger into the mast. Nobody hurt. The older rigger had said something about Zale's mother that Zale had decided to let go. After that Zale whistled smaller, and wrote in his own log: Thick air. Ask it less.

They had all felt the other thing too: a dull ache behind the eyes after a watch on deck, that went off after an hour in the cabin, where Noll ran the scrubber and kept the mark. On the tenth night Noll's nose bled on the chart, and he was very apologetic about the chart. Zale had all four of them sleep inside the mark after that, and nobody complained, which told him more than the bleeding had.

At noon on the eleventh day, Noll came up out of the hold and said, "Three times," and Zale said, "That'll do," and took her down.

He brought her low and slow over the long swells into the wind, so she'd hold steady, the veins at a quarter and the louvres half shut. Thirty feet off the water. Twenty. It smelt like the sea had when he was eleven and everything smelt stronger. The younger rigger was at the rail looking down at it with his mouth a little open.

The jar first. Noll let a bucket down on a line, a fresh one, and brought it up full and slopping, and they stood round it on the deck. It was a bucket of clear sea water with the light in it. Zale put his hand over it, not in it, and felt nothing; he had never had much of the sense. Noll put his hand over it and took it away fast and said, "That's a lot," and filled the jar to the shoulder, and stoppered it, and put Bram's sleeve over it, and took it below in its sock like a baby.

Then the shell.

By hand, she'd said. He could have let it down in a net and cut the line; he had thought of that on the second day and put it away, because she had spent her own thin strength on that shell and meant what she said. So he had the rope ladder over the lee side, weighted at the foot, and he went down it in his shirt and trousers with the shell in his teeth in its oiled cloth, like a dog with a letter, while the older rigger stood at the top with a line round Zale's chest and the other end round the mast.

Near the bottom he hooked his arm through the ladder, took the shell out of his teeth, unwrapped it one-handed, and let the cloth go on the wind.

He could feel it now, a little, even he could: a faint ringing in the cut lines under his thumb.

The swell came up. He put his hand down into it, to the wrist, and opened his fingers.

The water was warm. That surprised him. And it was thick, it was really thick, he felt it close on his wrist like something alive and curious, and the shell went down out of his hand slowly, not sinking so much as being taken, turning once, pale, and then grey, and then gone. His wrist burned. Not like heat. Like a nettle.

He pulled the hand out and looked at it. Little red points were coming up on the skin, dozens of them, as if he'd been pricked all over with a pin.

"Up," said the older rigger, at the top. Not loud. "Zale. Up. Now."

Zale looked where he was looking.

A quarter of a mile off, a patch of sea longer than the ship had gone smooth and dark, the swells going flat across it as if over a reef, in water a mile deep if it was a foot. The patch was coming toward them, and it wasn't hurrying.

Not in its road, she'd said.

He climbed. He was three rungs up when the sea rose under the ship. It didn't break. It rose, the whole of it, in a long hump, the way a blanket rises when a dog gets up under it, and the ship's lee side went down into it and the ladder went down with it and Zale with the ladder, to the waist, into water that was warm and thick and burning, and something enormous went past under his feet.

He didn't see it. He saw a darkness the length of a street, and in the dark something pale going by and by and by, and he felt the water pull at him as it passed, gently, the way a crowd pulls at you, and then the ship came up out of the hump with a lurch that threw him against the hull and slammed the breath out of him.

Something hit her from below. One blow, glancing, aft. The whole ship rang like a bell, and from up on deck he heard the younger rigger shout and then stop shouting.

The line round his chest came tight and the older rigger hauled, and Zale climbed and was hauled at the same time, and came over the rail on his stomach and lay on the deck with the sea running out of his trousers.

He whistled. He didn't decide to; he'd done it in storms on worse ships than this, and the air out here heard him far too well. The ship went up as if she'd been kicked. The deck tilted, and the charge rack in the hold banged against its lashing, and Noll somewhere below said a word Zale had never heard him use, and they went up out of the thick air over the water, a hundred feet, three hundred, five, before Zale got his mouth shut and the veins stopped roaring.

He lay there and breathed.

When he could, he sat up. Below them the sea had gone back to long slow swells. Far off to the west something broke the surface once in a white plume, and the plume hung in the air and drifted down, and then there was nothing there at all.

The younger rigger was sitting against the mast holding his left arm across his chest with his right, and he was a bad colour, and when Zale looked at him he said, "Collarbone, I think," in a voice that was trying hard. He'd been at the rail when she hit. The rail had come up to meet him.

Zale's legs were burning. He looked down. Under the wet cloth, from the knees to the hips, the skin was coming up red in points and patches, and where the patches met they were already darkening. His left wrist, where the hand had gone in, was purple to the forearm.

"Inside," said Noll, coming up out of the hatch. He looked at Zale's legs and his face went still. "Inside the mark. Now. Both of you."

"The vein," said the older rigger, at the stern rail, looking over.

Zale went and looked before he went inside, because the vein was his. The aft starboard vein had taken the blow. Its louvres were stove in, the plating bent like a tin cup somebody had stood on, and Noll said the air stone in its throat had been shaken off its seat and wasn't drawing.

"She'll fly on five," said Zale.

"She'll fly on five crooked."

"Then she'll fly crooked." He went inside.

Inside the mark, the burning in his legs went down to an ache in the time it took to drink a cup of water, and the red points stopped spreading, and the dark patches stayed dark. Noll strapped the boy's arm with a scarf and a belt. Then he wrote the last reading of the six dials in the log, very neatly, and the time, and under that, because Zale told him to, Token in, by hand. Something came.

"What was it?" said the boy, from his bunk.

Zale thought about the darkness going by under his feet, and the gentle pull of it, like a crowd.

"Polite," he said.



Sorrel's propeller went round on the twenty-third of April at the top of the west hill, and the cart didn't move.

She had built it over the winter from three laminated strips of ash, shaved and glued and shaved again, as long as she was tall. Tess had sent up a motor from the Motion Shop, one of the small fast ones off the logic line's test bench, and a cell bank in a crate, and Sorrel had bolted the lot to the front of a handcart with the propeller out in front on its shaft like a windmill that had lost its way. There were six people on the hill to watch, and a dog, and Emeric had been told to stand well back.

"It's not meant to go far," Sorrel said, to everybody and nobody, while she checked the wires for the third time. "It's meant to go."

She threw the switch.

The propeller turned, slowly and then not slowly, and then it was a disc, a pale blur in front of the cart with a noise like a very large bee, and the grass in front of it laid down flat, and the dog left. The cart sat on its wheels. It rocked once on its springs, as if it had thought about going and decided against.

Sorrel stood with her hand on the switch and watched the cart not move for a long time.

"Well," she said. She didn't turn it off. She knelt by the front wheel, closer to the blur than Emeric liked, and held up a strip of rag in the wind behind it and watched how hard it went back. Then she wrote in a little notebook of her own and turned the switch off.

"It's pushing," she said. "It's pushing. It's just not pushing enough to beat the cart. The cart's too heavy and the motor's too small and the blade's wrong at the root." She looked at it. "Too flat at the root. I knew that. I made it flat because I didn't know how much to twist it. Now I know it's not this much."

"That's a result," said Emeric.

"That's half a result. A result's when the cart moves." She was already looking at the blade again, at the root, with her thumb. "Next one, I'll twist it more. And a lighter cart."

He didn't tell her anything. It was harder than he'd thought, with the right twist in his head and fifty years of other men's crashing behind it. She'd work it out in two. She had the rag and the notebook. That was the method.

On the way down, at the fold of the hill where nobody could see him, he stopped and took out his own notebook and turned to a page near the back that he had drawn in February and shown to nobody. A wing, swept back, long and thin. No propeller anywhere on it. Behind it, under the tail, a pipe with fire coming out of the back of it.

He looked at it a while and put it away again. That one could wait for the dragons, if there were any. If they turned out not to need it, nobody would ever know it had been drawn.



The ruin was at the foot of the hill, where it had always been.

It was a low jumble of squared stone grown over with bramble and dock at the bottom of the west slope, where the old road had once come in before the circle was built, too square to be rock and too broken to be anything anybody wanted. Children played on it. Ward had looked at it once, years ago, and said nothing, which Emeric had remembered for no reason he could name.

He had reread Vey's second half the week before, at night, in the castle, with Imani asleep against his shoulder and Ferra asleep against hers. The old races had been here. Not over the sea. Here. They'd had roads and stations on this coast, and something had killed them so well that nobody in the kingdoms knew they'd existed. Reading it again, he had kept thinking of the ruin.

So he walked it in the late afternoon with his sense open, along what had been the outer wall and in through what had been a door. Under the bramble the blocks were cut true, and laid without mortar, and fitted so close he couldn't have got a knife blade between them, and they were old, so old the stone had a skin on it like an apple's.

He went down on his knees at the north-east corner and pulled the dock away from the bottom course with his hands, where the footing met the earth.

There was something in the stone. Not a stone. A pattern, in the footing block itself, cut into the inside face where it met the course above, where nobody would ever have seen it once it was laid: a fine branching of lines, keyed, with no mana left in it at all, as dead as old bone. He knew it. He had seen it before, under the Isle, in the drowned city, in the joints of the door golems. The same hand. The same strange angles at the joins. He'd have known it in the dark.

Then he put the dock back, roughly, the way it had been, and stood up and brushed off his knees. He thought about telling Bram, who would want to dig, and Tess, who had a page with one word on it and a question mark. He didn't. A dig was months and the whole town asking, and he hadn't got the months this year.

In his notebook, on the walk back, he wrote one line: West hill ruin. Footings keyed, same hand as the Isle golems. Waystation? Dig later.

Ward had looked at it and said nothing. He had always wondered what Ward knew.



The telescope stood on the castle roof under a canvas hood, on a mount Bram had made, pointed at nothing in particular, and on the night of the third of May, with the sky clear to the hills, Emeric took the hood off.

He had ground the mirror himself the year before, after hours in the Motion Shop's back room, a glass disc as wide as a dinner plate and the grit going from coarse to fine over weeks, walking round the bench as he worked. Ivo had silvered it. Ivo had wanted to grind it too, and had been offended for a week when Emeric said no.

Before he went up he sat in the long room and went through the back pages of the notebook, where the list was.

It had grown over the winter. One line each. Answer Towers. Rider Frames. Missiles, with a line under it, because he didn't like the word. The Dragon Net. The Sand Room. A gyro stabilizer, for the hulls, when Tess had the flywheels. The jet: dragons decide. The float field's next step. A fusion research reactor, which he'd written very small. The stone press, restarted. Lithium cells. Kaldo cotton for denim. The Jacquard loom, the one that read cards. Light in glass, for when Orrin's glass was clean enough. And at the bottom, last, the floating train, with two question marks, because he had promised himself he'd take it seriously one day and didn't yet. He read them all, and added nothing, and shut the book. They'd keep. None of them was this year.

Ferra and Imani were already on the roof. Ferra had a blanket. Imani had her feet in it.

"It's cold," said Imani. "You said it wouldn't be cold."

"I said it would be clear."

"You said it like it meant warm."

He put his eye to the tube and found the moon first, because it was easy and he wanted to check the focus, and it came up out of the dark at him so huge and so sharp, every crater rimmed in its own black shadow, that he took his eye away and laughed. Ferra wanted to look. She said, "Oh," quietly, and gave it back.

Then the small ones.

It took him most of an hour. The far moon was easy, a pale half-disc over the eastern hills. The two tiny ones were harder; he went back and forth along their strip of sky a hair at a time, from Armand's old tables, while Imani fell asleep against Ferra and Ferra didn't. Then one of them came into the field. Not a point. A shape. A small lumpy shape like a potato, grey, with one side lit and the other dark, and a smudge on the lit side that might have been a crater and might have been his eye watering.

He took the notebook out of his coat and drew it, by the light of the lamp under his hand, as well as he could: the lumpy shape, the lit side, the smudge. He didn't write anything under it. There wasn't anything to write that the drawing didn't say better.

"What is it?" said Ferra.

"A rock."

"A big one?"

"Not very. Bigger than Kaldo." He gave her the eyepiece. "A long way off."

She looked. She looked for a long time, longer than she'd looked at the moon, and when she gave it back she didn't say anything, and then she said, "How long to get there," the way she'd have asked how long a road was.

"I don't know."

"Then find out." She pulled the blanket further round Imani. "I'm not in a hurry. I'm only asking."

He put his eye back to the glass. There were other places he could point it, and he knew exactly where, and what he'd be looking for: things far enough off that they never moved, a smudge, two small clouds, a dark split down the length of the sky's band of stars, the things that would tell him whether he was home and only very far from it. He didn't, not tonight. Tonight it was a rock, and Ferra wanted to know how far it was, and that was enough.

He put the hood back on at one, and they went down.



Zale came in on the twelfth of May, with the Catch three parts full and a vein dark, flying crooked.

They'd had his set on the wireless for the last four nights, faint and then less faint, Noll's hand on the key: positions, the charge log in figures, and on the second night, Token in. Two hurt. Vein out. Coming. Ferra had read that one off Tess's pad and taken the next packet to the island without a word. She stood on the Cradle's deck now with the book under her arm and Fletcher's notes in her pocket, kept in Zale's place and in Zale's style: Catch a quarter, 1 May. Half, 8th. Three parts, this morning.

"Three days more and he'd have been sitting out there waiting for a whale," she said.

"He's not."

"I know he's not. I'm saying it so I can stop thinking it."

The ship came over the headland low, with a list to starboard, and wallowed down onto her pegs behind the dunes like a tired cow lying down, and the ladder came over the side.

Zale came down it slowly. He was walking stiff, and when he turned round at the bottom Emeric saw why: his trousers were cut off at the knee, and the skin was mottled purple and yellow and green to the hips, like a man who'd been beaten with boards. His left forearm was wrapped. Behind him the older rigger helped the boy down, one-armed, his left arm strapped to his chest.

Imani was there. She'd come out with Ferra on the packet and had said nothing about why. She went to the boy first, because he was the one who couldn't use his arm, and had his shirt open and her hand on his collarbone before he'd got both feet on the sand, and said, "Clean. It'll knit. You'll hate the sling." Then she turned to Zale and looked at his legs without touching them, for a while, with her sight open, and her face went very still in a way Emeric knew.

"How long were you in it?"

"To the waist? Ten breaths. Maybe fewer." He held up the wrapped wrist. "That one longer. I put my hand in."

"Of course you did." She knelt and touched the purple at his knee with two fingers, very lightly, and Zale's face didn't move at all, which was how you knew it hurt. "It's the bleeding sickness. The vessels let go under the skin. Ten breaths, and this." She stood up. "You're going in a bed. Not a ship, a bed. And I'm going to want to look at the inside of your eyes."

"Aye," said Zale, which meant nothing in particular, and went where she pointed.

Noll came down last with the log and the jar. He handed the log to Ferra, who opened it on the spot and stopped at the eleventh day and stood looking at it. He handed the jar to Emeric. It was still in its sock.

Emeric took the sock off. He took Bram's iron sleeve off, and held the jar up in his hands in the sun.

It was water. A jar of clear sea water, with a little grit at the bottom and the light going through it. He could feel it through the glass as soon as the iron was off: a dense, close note, denser than anything on this coast but a full stone. And under it, already, very faint, the other thing. A thinning. The slow leak of it into the thin air, the way the tooth had leaked on his bench years ago. Three weeks home, and it was already less than it had been when Noll put the stopper in.

He put the sleeve back on. He wanted to measure it going.

The guest was on the Cradle's deck when they went back round. Somebody had told her. She was standing at the slab's edge, hood back, and when Zale came along the deck toward her on Imani's arm, stiff-legged and purple and walking like an old man, she looked at his legs, and then at his face, and said something.

Zale answered her. Emeric caught a little of it, a word here and there from the memory read's leftovers: hand, and thick, and a word that he thought meant came.

She asked him something else. Zale thought about it, and then he did a thing with his hand, flat, sliding, long and slow, and lifted his eyebrows. She looked at the hand. Then she laughed, not loudly this time, a short low sound with the click under it, and put her own hand over her mouth, and said a word.

"What did she say?" said Ferra.

"She says I was very rude to stand in its road," said Zale. "And that it was very polite not to eat me." He considered that. "She's glad I'm not eaten."

"Is she," said Imani.

"It's what she said." Zale's face was perfectly flat. "Put me in my bed."

That night Ferra wrote it down at the mess table, under the plan she had written a month before. 12 May. Token delivered by hand, west of the last islands, in the thick water. Received. She hesitated over Received, and looked at Emeric, and he nodded, and she left it. Under that: Charge three times the island's, 11th day. Under that: Two hurt. One vein. Under that, after a while: Plan unchanged. The wall's edge, in the Challenger, when she's ready.

She closed the book and sat with her hand on it.

Across the bay, on the slab's edge, the guest stood with her feet in the water a long time after dark, facing the way Zale had gone, where there was nothing at all to see.
