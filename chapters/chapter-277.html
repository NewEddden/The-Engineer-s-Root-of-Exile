Chapter 277 - Keller Counts Faces

Keller had been told the engine was green, and it was not green. It was the colour of a bottle held up to a window: a deep dark green that was nearly black in the shadow of Wayford's new shed and went to green only where the dawn came in sideways along its flank and caught it. And it was not the shape of an engine at all.

He stood on the platform with his board under his arm and his collar up against the September morning, and looked at it for a long time, because he was a man who looked at a thing for a long time before he said anything about it, and had been for forty years.

He had seen engines. He had seen Wick, five years ago, in the yard below the works at Kaldo, black and square and honest, a boiler on wheels with a chimney at the front and a box at the back for the men. This was not that. This was long and low and smooth all over, its boiler and its chimney and its domes all hidden inside one long curved casing that came forward from the cab and swept down at the front into a nose like a wedge, like the prow of a ship turned on its side, like the head of a pike. There was nothing on it to catch the wind. Even its wheels were half hidden behind a skirt of the same green plate. On its flank, below the cab window, painted in gold, small, a single rose on a stem; and under the rose, in plain gold letters a hand high, GOLD ROSE.

"She's for the air," said a voice beside him. "Not the rails. The rails are easy. It's the air that's in the way."

It was a woman of about thirty in a leather coat, with wind-burned cheeks and her hands in her pockets, looking at the engine's nose with an expression Keller had seen on the faces of men looking at their first child.

"Sorrel Amesbury," said the woman. "I did the nose. I jump off towers." She said it as though it explained everything, and went off down the platform without waiting to see whether it did.

"She does," said another voice, on his other side; and Keller turned, and it was Livia Ducatis, in a grey travelling coat with the House's falcon on its clasp, holding out her hand.

He took it. He had met her twice: at the investiture, and in his own chambers at the capital in May, when she had come with the papers for her bank in Kaldo and stood at his table for an hour while he read every one of them, and not sat down, and not said a word that was not an answer to a question. He had liked her very much. He had not said so.

"Magistrate."

"Madam Ducatis." He let go of her hand. "Your registration is in my bag. I'll sign it at Kaldo, in front of your notes, with your watermark under my glass, as the act requires."

"I know." She looked at the engine. "And this?"

"This is a favour," said Keller. "To a duke who asked me in writing whether I would be the witness to a number. I told him in writing that I'd be the witness to nothing I hadn't checked myself." He shifted his board. "He wrote back and said that was why he'd asked."



The duke was on the footplate.

Keller saw him there as he came along the platform to the cab: in his shirtsleeves, with a smut on his cheek, leaning out of the cab's side and talking down to a short square man on the platform below who was arguing with him about sand. He had not seen Emeric since the investiture, two years ago. He saw him now, and stopped walking for a moment, and then went on.

"Magistrate," said Emeric, and came down off the footplate.

"Your Grace." Keller looked at him. Then he looked at the engine. "You told me the record would be the engine's alone. I've come to make sure of it." He took a stick of red wax and a seal out of his coat pocket, his own seal, the plain one he used on the crown's evidence. "Your stones. All of them. Off the engine and out of the coaches, and anything else with a stone in it within a yard of the rail. I'll seal them in a box and carry the box myself."

The short square man on the platform said, "Now look here..."

"Odo," said Emeric.

"He wants the sanders off. The sanders! On a September morning, with dew on the rail at the top of the bank, he wants me to start a three-hundred-ton train with no sand down?"

"There's a sandbox," said Keller.

Odo looked at him.

"On the engine. Under the running plate." Keller pointed with his seal. "A sandbox with a pipe to the rail in front of the driving wheels, worked by the driver from the cab with a lever. I saw it in the drawings in May. Sand, gravity, a lever. No stone." He put the seal back in his pocket. "Keep that. I want the other sanders off. The ones that press down. And the things under the coaches that make them light on the hills."

Odo opened his mouth, and shut it, and looked at Emeric.

"You heard the magistrate," said Emeric.

It took them forty minutes. The bogies came out from under the coaches on jacks, eight of them, and Keller looked at each and had the gravity stone in each lifted out of its housing in the tongs and laid in the iron box Bram's men had brought, on a bed of felt; and then the pressing sanders off the engine, two of them, and the stones out of those; and then he went down the train on his own, all eleven coaches, with his board, and looked under every seat and into every lamp. The lamps were electric. He looked at them for some while. He found one stone in the guard's van in a little brass cage by the door, which the guard said was for warming his hands, and had it out.

Then he shut the box, and dripped his wax on the hasp, and pressed his seal into it, and wrote on his board, and had Emeric sign the board, and then had Livia Ducatis sign it under Emeric; and carried the box himself to the recorder car at the train's head, behind the tender, and put it on the floor between his feet.

"Now," he said. "Show me your number."



The recorder car was a guard's van with its insides taken out and a long table down one side, and on the table, bolted down, a machine.

A woman sat at it. She was small and grey and neat, with ink on her fingers and her sleeves held back with garters, and she did not get up when Keller came in; she looked at him over a pair of half-spectacles and said, "Ottoline. Mind the roll."

The roll was paper, a hand wide, coming off a drum at one end of the machine and going across a flat brass bed and on to another drum at the other end; and a clock drove it, a big plain clock in a glass case that you could hear ticking from the door, so that the paper went past at a pace you could set your watch by, so many inches to the minute. Over the bed, two pens. One drew a short mark on the paper's edge every second, by the clock. The other was on an arm; and the arm went down through the car's floor to a little wheel, Ottoline said, that ran on the rail itself beside the tender's wheels, its own wheel, nothing to do with the engine, with a governor on it like a mill's, two balls on arms that flew out as it spun; and the faster the wheel turned, the further the balls flew out, and the further the pen moved across the paper.

"So you can read the speed off the paper," said Keller. "Against the clock's marks."

"Off the paper. Not off anybody's word." Ottoline looked at him over her spectacles. "Nobody touches the pen. Nobody touches the wheel. You can sit there and watch me not touch it." She pointed at a stool by the bed. "There's a rule on the wall, for the miles. And I've ruled the paper for the speeds. Every ten. You'll see."

Keller sat on the stool.

Livia Ducatis sat on the other stool, at the end of the bed, with her own board; because Keller's rule was that a thing was witnessed by two who did not answer to each other, and she answered to nobody in Randillia but her own House. He had said so, in May, and she had said that she would like to be the second, if the duke asked; and the duke had asked.

"Paired," said Livia, quietly, ruling a line.

"And attested," said Keller.

They looked at each other, once, the two people in the kingdom who had never in their lives put a figure in a book that was not the figure; and neither of them smiled, and both of them very nearly did.

Up at the front, the whistle went.



It was eight hours to Kaldo, the timetable said. The first two were the plain: river flats, hedges, Wayford's farms, the halfway villages, the engine running easy at sixty with the paper creeping past under the pen and Ottoline doing a sum in pencil in the margin every ten minutes and Keller watching her do it. Then the ridge country. The line went up into it on a long climb out of the Wayford side, slowly, the speed pen falling back across the paper to forty and then thirty-five as the engine leaned into the grade without her stones and the beat of her exhaust came back along the train hard and even, and the duke, Keller was told, was on the footplate with the fireman, shovelling.

And then the top. And the line came over the ridge's shoulder and went down the other side, on the long grade the crews called Fallow Bank: eleven miles of falling ground from the ridge to the river flats on Lord Lisle's side, straight, or near it, surveyed from the air two years ago by an old man with a level and a weather book and a K Discovery to stand in; ballasted by Odo's gangs, every chair and every key of it gone over that summer by Odo himself on his knees.

The engine put her nose over the top, and the driver opened her out.

Keller felt it in the car first. The note of the wheels on the joints, which had been a slow clack and clack, went to a run of them, and then to a hum, and the car began to sway, not much, a long easy swing that the springs took. Out of the window the hedges went from things into lines. The pen moved.

Seventy. Ottoline ruled it, without looking up. Eighty. The car's lamps swung on their hooks. Ninety. Keller had never in his life gone ninety miles in an hour; he had not known anybody went ninety miles in an hour; and he sat on his stool with his board on his knee and watched the pen go past the line for ninety and on, and found that his hand was holding the edge of the bed, and took it off.

A hundred.

Livia Ducatis said nothing. She had her board on her knee and her pencil in her hand and her eyes on the pen, and her lips moved, very slightly; she was counting the clock's marks against the rule. Out of the window the country had become a smear of green and gold, and the car was singing, a high thin note out of every joint in it, and the engine's exhaust had gone from a beat to a roar to something else, a long tearing sound like cloth.

A hundred and ten.

"She's not done," said Ottoline, to the pen.

A hundred and fifteen. A hundred and twenty. The pen was near the edge of the paper now, near the line Ottoline had ruled across the top of it in red the night before, with nothing written beside it. Keller watched it. Everybody in the car watched it. The car swayed and sang and the hedges were not lines any more but a colour.

The pen touched the red line.

It went a hair over. It stood there, trembling, a hair over the red, for the count of four of the clock's marks; Keller counted them himself, aloud, under his breath, and heard Livia count them beside him in exactly the same time.

And then the smell came.

It came down the train on the wind from the front, sweet and thick and absolutely out of place: aniseed. The smell of a child's sweet. It filled the car in a breath. Ottoline lifted her head from the pen and said, "Ah," quite calmly, as if somebody had told her it might rain.

"What is that?" said Keller.

"Bram's capsule." Ottoline was already ruling a line across the roll at the pen's place, very neat, and writing the time beside it. "There's a little glass bead of aniseed oil in the middle bearing. On the middle crank. If it runs too hot the wax round the bead melts and the bead breaks, and you smell it on the footplate, and you know." She looked up. "She's run her middle big end hot. They'll shut off."

They shut off. Keller felt it: the roar of the exhaust stopped all at once, like a door shut on a storm, and the train ran on in a long rushing quiet down the bank, and the pen came back from the red line, slowly, slowly, across the paper; and the brakes went on, gently, and the hedges became hedges again.

Ottoline took the roll off the drum, carefully, with the clock still ticking, and cut it across with a knife, and unrolled the last two yards of it along the table in front of Keller, and laid her rule across it at the place where the pen had touched the red, and counted the clock's marks with the point of her pencil.

"One hundred and twenty-six miles in the hour," she said. "And a little. I'll not say the little. Call it a hundred and twenty-six."

Nobody in the car said anything.

Keller looked at the paper. He looked at it for a long time, as he looked at everything; at the clock's marks, steady, one a second, all the way down the paper's edge; at the pen's line coming up across it from the left and up and up, to the red, and the hair over, and the four marks, and down. He looked at the iron box on the floor between his feet, with his wax on the hasp and his seal in the wax, unbroken.

Then he took the pen out of his pocket, his own pen, and wrote on the roll in the margin beside the red line, in his own small square hand: Witnessed. The stones under my seal throughout. K. And the date, and the hour.

He held the pen out to Livia Ducatis.

She took it, and wrote under his: Witnessed. L. Ducatis, for the House. And gave it back.

They limped the last forty miles at forty, with the middle bearing cooling and a man on the running plate every quarter of an hour with an oil can and his sleeves rolled, and the aniseed smell blowing back along the train all the way to Kaldo, so that for a week afterward every man who had been on it smelled of sweets; and the duke came back down the train from the footplate at the first water stop, black to the eyes, and stood in the door of the recorder car looking at the paper on the table, and did not say anything at all.

"A hundred and twenty-six," said Keller.

"I heard."

"It's the engine's," said Keller. "The stones were in my box the whole way. Nobody can say otherwise. I'll say so in any court in the kingdom." He looked at Emeric. "Is it a good number?"

"It's the fastest any engine's ever gone," said Emeric. "Anywhere." He said it oddly, Keller thought; as if he were saying something else, under it, to somebody who was not there. "It'll be the fastest any engine goes for a long time."

"And the bearing?"

"The bearing will be fixed by Thursday." Emeric looked at the roll. "Bram will want to know why. Bram will spend a month on why." He almost smiled. "And then it'll run hot again one day, on some other run, because that's what a middle big end on a three-cylinder engine does if you thrash it." He looked up. "We'll run her at eighty for the timetable. Nobody needs a hundred and twenty-six to get to Wayford."

"Then why did you do it?"

"So that you could write that on the paper."

Keller looked at him for a while.

"Hm," he said, and put his pen away.



Kaldo had come down to the station.

Keller had heard that it would; he had not believed it. He stood in the recorder car's door as the train came in under the long shed's roof, limping, with the aniseed on the air, and saw the platform from end to end packed with people, and the fence along the yard, and the spoil bank behind the fence, and the roofs of the wagon sheds; people in their work clothes and their good clothes, women with children on their shoulders, men from the works in their aprons, nurses in white from the hospital, and over all of them, as the engine came to a stand, a noise he had heard once in his life before, on the day the old king was crowned.

He got down onto the platform with his box.

And looked at the faces.

He had not been in Kaldo for years. He had been here before, years ago, on the crown's business, for a week; and before that, in the first year of the exile, a bookkeeper's daughter had ridden north to his own chambers with the barony's books and sat at his table reading them to him faster than he could follow. He had a magistrate's memory for faces. It was half of what a magistrate was. And he stood on the platform with his box in his hands and the crowd pressing and the noise going over him, and looked, and looked.

The bookkeeper's daughter was there, at the front of the crowd, by the engine's cab, with a book in her hand. Ferra. She was older; she was the Chancellor of a duchy. She had a pen in the knot of her hair. And her face was the face he had sat across his own table from when she was twenty-two, without a line on it that had not been there then; the same skin, the same freckles, the same steady grey-green look at him across the platform. She nodded to him. He nodded back.

Behind her, her father: the smith, Bram, in his apron, enormous, with his arms folded, looking at the engine's middle cylinder as though it had insulted his mother. Keller remembered Bram from his week here. A big man then, already grey in the beard. He was grey in the beard now. He was not one hair greyer. He stood there by the engine as if he had stepped off the day Keller left and onto this one, with the years between taken out.

And at the platform's end, keeping the crowd back from the line with his spear held crossways, a gate guard. Keller knew him. He did not know his name. He had come in at the South Gate on his first day in Kaldo, on a hired horse, and a guard had taken the horse's bridle at the gate and asked his business; a young man then, broad-faced, with a broken nose, who had said "Magistrate, is it?" in a voice that did not care for magistrates. This was the man. The broken nose. The broad face. He was not young. He was not old. He was exactly as he had been.

Keller stood on the platform with his box in his hands.

Then he turned and found the duke beside him, black to the eyes from the footplate, and looked at him too: at the face of a man who had been twenty-two at the exile and was, by Keller's arithmetic, thirty-three, and might have been twenty-five.

"Nobody here has aged a day," said Keller.

He did not say it loudly. He said it under the noise, only to Emeric, in the voice he used to read a finding.

Emeric did not answer.

"Not a day," said Keller. "Not your Chancellor. Not her father. Not that man at the gate. I've a good memory for faces, Your Grace. It's my trade." He looked along the platform again. "I sat across a table from that young woman ten years ago. I'd have said then she was twenty. I'd say now she was twenty-two." He was quiet. "Later. Not here. You'll give me an hour."

"Yes," said Emeric.



He gave him the hour that evening, in the small room off the hall where the duke did his letters, with the shutters closed and the lamp lit and a bottle of Kaldo's spirit on the table between them that neither of them touched.

Keller signed the House's registration first. He did it at the table with Livia Ducatis standing at his elbow and the bank's notes spread under his glass: the Kaldo paper, the engraved border, the falcon in the watermark when he held a note to the lamp. He read every word of the charter again, though he had read it in May, and signed, and sealed, and gave it to her; and she thanked him, and went; and he was left with the duke and the lamp.

"Evidence," he said.

Emeric waited.

"You know what I mean. I can see on you that you know." Keller laid his hands flat on the table either side of his board. "In the capital the old do not look young. Not like that. A mage looks young, a little; a great house looks young, a little; the king looks well for his years and so did his father. Everybody knows why. They hold more. That is what nobility is, Your Grace, in the end, when you take the robes off it and the land and the law: the ones who hold more, live longer and look better and can't be made to do anything they don't want to by anybody who holds less." He did not raise his voice. "And I have just walked down a railway platform past a smith, and a bookkeeper's daughter, and a gate guard with a broken nose, who hold more."

The lamp hissed.

"I don't know how," said Keller. "I don't want to know how. If I knew how I'd have to write it down. But I'm not a fool, and there will be other men who aren't fools, who will walk down that platform. The cleric who came through here in the fifth year saw it, and went home and said so, and nobody listened to him because he was a cleric and odd. They'll listen to the next one." He looked at Emeric. "What I'm telling you is that it's evidence. Not a rumour. Evidence. A face is evidence. You can't burn it or file it under the wrong letter. It walks about. And if anybody ever brings a case against this barony, and I think somebody will, the first thing they'll do is bring a witness who was here ten years ago, and stand him on a platform, and have him look at faces."

"And what will you do?" said Emeric.

"What I always do. Tell the truth." Keller picked up his board. "If I'm asked whether the people of Kaldo look younger than their years, I'll say yes. If I'm asked whether I know why, I'll say no. Both will be true." He stood up. "I'd sooner you didn't make me a liar by telling me."

"I won't."

"Good." Keller went to the door. He stopped there with his hand on it. "A hundred and twenty-six," he said, without turning round. "I'll put that in front of the king myself. It's a good number. It's a clean one. It's nice, for once, to bring the king a clean number from Kaldo." He opened the door. "Good night, Your Grace."



The new hospital opened on the Monday.

It had been building since the spring, out past the north-east gate on the Port Town side: a long block of Kaldo brick, three storeys, with tall windows on the south side for the light and a roof of the new grey slate, and over its door, cut in the lintel by the old ward-three mason in letters a foot high, nothing but HOSPITAL, because Imani had said that anybody who needed to be told whose it was had not been in it. It had six wards and two theatres and a laundry with its own boiler, and pipes in every room for the steam heat that was to come off the station's main before winter, and a lift: a cage on a cable in a shaft, worked by an electric motor, for the beds. It had a room at the end of the ground floor with a door that locked and a smell of bread gone wrong, where Hester Mull's mould grew on broth in flat glass dishes in rows on racks, and a nurse in a mask did nothing all day but watch it and write down what it did.

And it had, at the end of the ground floor's other wing, beside the tank, a dark room.

The tank had its own room, as it had always had: the long bath of warm water and the brass frame with its sensor stones, and the print head over its paper; and the atlas in Ottoline's drawers along the wall, five years of scans of every volunteer who would lie still. That was the tank, and it worked, and everybody in the four baronies who had been through the hospital had been through it.

But the tank read mana. It read the way a body bent its field. And a body full of mana bent it so hard, all over, that the tank saw nothing but fog: knights, mages, anybody whose tracery lit, anybody Kaldo had been feeding its water to for long enough. Ward in the tank was a grey cloud the shape of a man. Armand was a white one. Emeric, since the pod, they had stopped putting in it at all.

So beside the tank's room there was another, with no window, and walls lined with sheet lead behind their plaster, and a door lined too; and in it a table, and over the table, on a wooden arm, a glass bulb the size of a man's head.

The forge had made the hard part of it on the Sunday before: a disc of tungsten set in a block of copper, so that the copper would carry the heat away from the tungsten when it was hit; and a little cup of nickel, shaped, to gather what came off the cathode and aim it at the disc. Ivo had built the bulb round them, and pumped it as hard as he pumped the valves, harder; and the spark set's big induction coil from the wireless, the one that had sent the first crackling sparks across the river years ago, drove it.

And on the wall opposite, in a frame, a sheet of card a yard square, painted all over with a fine white powder: calcium tungstate, out of the same tungsten, ground and sieved and mixed with gum. In the dark, when the tube was running, the card glowed green wherever what came out of the tube struck it; and where something was in the way between the tube and the card, less got through, and the card glowed less.

Ward was the first.

He had not wanted to be. Imani had asked him on the Saturday, in front of the six, and he had said he had nothing wrong with him; and she had said his left hand had been broken twice and set badly once, and he had ached in it every winter for as long as she had known him, and the tank showed her a cloud; and he had looked at his left hand for a while and said, "All right."

So on the Monday, with the new hospital full of the town walking through it in their good clothes looking at the lift and the slate and the mould, Ward sat on a stool in the dark room with his left hand flat on a board between the tube and the card, and Imani beside him, and Emeric at the switch, and Keller, who had stayed over for the opening because Imani had asked him to cut the ribbon and he had said that a magistrate did not cut ribbons but would stand near one, at the door, in the dark.

"Don't move," said Imani.

Emeric closed the switch.

The tube lit. It lit a pale strange green inside, all through its glass, and the coil buzzed like a wasp in a jar; and on the wall opposite, the card glowed, green, all over, a square of faint green light in the dark room.

And on it, black against the green, a hand.

Not the hand. The bones of it. Long, fine, the four fingers and the thumb, every joint, every little bone in the wrist like pebbles in a bag, there on the wall in front of them all, enormous, in shadow: Ward's left hand, from the inside. The flesh was a faint grey ghost round them. The bones were black.

Nobody said anything.

Ward looked at it. He looked at it for a long time, holding very still, as Imani had told him, with his hand flat on the board; and the hand on the wall held very still too.

"That's mine," he said at last.

"That's yours," said Imani.

She had gone close to the card, as close as she could, and was looking at it with her face a foot from the glow. "There," she said. "The second bone of the third finger. There. See where it's thick?" She put her finger near it, not touching. "That's where it knit. It knit crooked. And there: the long bone behind the knuckle. Two lines across it. Two old breaks. One of them never quite joined." She was quiet. "You've been holding a sword with that for twenty years."

"Longer," said Ward.

Imani looked at the bones on the card a while longer.

"I can't mend that," she said. "Not now. It's too old." Her voice did not change. "But I can see it. I've never seen it. The tank never showed me anything in you but a cloud. And there it is." She turned to Emeric in the dark. "It doesn't care, does it. It doesn't care what he holds. It goes straight through."

"It doesn't care about mana at all," said Emeric. "Knight, mage, child. Dead zone or no dead zone. It's made of glass and copper and a spark. It'll show you anybody."

Keller, by the door, in the dark, had not moved at all.

"Switch it off," said Imani; and Emeric did, and the green went out of the card and the room was black, and Ward took his hand off the board and flexed it in the dark, slowly, and nobody saw his face.



They ran it all that week. Every knight in the garrison who would come, and every mage, and Armand, who sat in the dark with his old hands flat on the board and looked at his own bones on the wall for a long time and said only, "Thin," and got up and went out.

On the Friday Bettany's hand went red.

Bettany was the ward sister; she had held the card's frame steady for every look all week, and the board, and arranged every hand and every foot and every knee on it, with her own hand in the beam a hundred times. And on the Friday morning the back of her right hand was red, as if she had sat too long in front of a fire, and by the afternoon it was sore, and the skin on it had begun to peel.

Imani looked at it in the light of the window for a long time.

Then she went into the dark room and locked the door, and stayed in there alone for an hour; and when she came out she had a sheet of paper in her hand, and pinned it to the dark room's door with four of Ottoline's brass pins, at the height of a woman's eyes.

It said:

THE TUBE.

Nobody is looked at twice in one month without my say.

Nobody holds the card, or the board, or the patient, while it is running. Use the stand.

Whoever runs the switch stands behind the lead screen.

Nobody looks at the tube itself.

No child, and no woman who may be carrying, without my say.

Every look is written in this book: who, when, how long.

It burns. You can't feel it burning. That is the trouble with it. I.

Bettany read it with her red hand in a wet cloth, and said nothing, and went and fetched the book.

Emeric read it that evening. He stood in front of it in the corridor for a long time, with his hands in his pockets.

"You knew," said Imani, behind him. "Didn't you."

"Yes."

"And you didn't tell me."

"I told you to keep the time short." He did not turn round. "I said every look should be as short as you could make it. On Monday. Twice."

"You said it as if it were a matter of tidiness." She came and stood beside him and looked at her own paper on her own door. "You should have said it burns."

"Yes," he said. "I should have."

She looked at him sideways for a moment, and then back at the paper; and then she took the pencil out of her hair and added one more line at the bottom, small, under her initial.

He knew. Ask him what else he knows.

And went back to her ward.



Saban brought the other news on the Saturday, at the long table, with his cup in his hand.

"Wayford," he said. "The station. The day after the record." He drank. "There were four men on the platform when she went back up the line on Thursday with her bearing mended, and I know three of them. One's a factor for the lord at Coldwell. One's a factor for the duke in the north. One is a gentleman who says he buys timber, and does, a little, when he isn't buying other things for a house in the capital I'd rather not name at supper." He set his cup down. "They all wanted to know what she'd cost."

"She's not for sale," said Ferra.

"I told them so. They said everything's for sale." Saban smiled. "The timber gentleman had a man with him with a measuring rod. He measured her. Her wheels, her nose, the length of her, her chimney's height, while she stood at the platform, as cool as you like. Odo nearly put him under her."

"Let him measure," said Emeric.

Everybody looked at him.

"Let him." He turned his cup. "He can have her shape. Sorrel's nose, the casing, the wheels. Anybody can have a shape. What's inside her came off masters the forge made and Bram copied, out of steel from our furnace on our rolls, in a boiler our shop tested at half again what it'll ever bear, by men Bram's been training for nine years." He drank. "He can build her shape in Coldwell's yard. It'll go eighty, if he's lucky. Then it'll throw its rods off on the first long bank and kill his crew." He was quiet. "And we'll be sorry about the crew."

Saban looked at him for a while, and then nodded slowly, and wrote something in his own small book, which he did not show anybody.

"And the fare," said Ferra, without looking up. "Three Silver Rand to Wayford on the express. The people's train stays at what it is, twice a day, and the express runs once each way, and it isn't instead of anything." She ruled a line. "The merchants will pay it. The crown's men will pay it, and charge it to the crown." She looked up at last. "And the man who sweeps the station at Coldwell will ride the people's train for a tenth of it, and get there four hours later, and I want it printed in the Almanac in the same size type."

"The same size," said Emeric.

"The same size," said Ferra, and wrote it down.
