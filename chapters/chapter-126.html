Chapter 126 - The Unsold Hull

The half hull came up the river on the eighth of September behind the eel boat, on the flood, with Fletcher standing in her bow.

She had sat on her blocks in the reeds for six years. Fletcher had laid her on his own money in the summer the port died, a coaster by her lines, broad in the bow and full in the quarter, because a coaster was the one thing every coast always wanted and somebody would come. Nobody had come. She had her keel and her stem and her sternpost and every frame, and planking up to the turn of the bilge, and above that nothing, so that her ribs stood up bare against the sky like the fingers of two hands held up together. There had been moss on her blocks when Emeric first saw her. A gull on her stem.

The barony bought her out of the fifth. Ferra wrote her into the book at the price Fletcher named, which was what he had paid for her timber six years ago and not a copper more, and Vantic checked the line and initialled it and said nothing, and Fletcher sat in the reeds for two days after the money came with the barony's paper in his hands, not doing anything, and then got up and put her on rollers.

They floated her off the blocks on a spring tide with empty casks lashed in her and towed her up behind the eel boat over three days. She came up the river slowly, with her bare ribs standing up out of the brown water, and the dock quarter came out on its piles to watch her go by, because it was the strangest-looking thing that had come up the river since the black hull went down it, and then they saw who was standing in her bow, and somebody on the piles called his name.

Fletcher did not wave. He stood in the bow of his half hull with one hand on her stem, where the gull had sat, and looked up the river toward the shed, and did not look at the piles at all.

They brought her in to the foot of the slip on the high water, beside the black hull in her cradle, and warped her up onto the ways, and blocked her. Fletcher went over her side onto the slip and walked round her once, slowly, with his head on one side, the way he walked round everything. Then he went to the shed's tool rack and took down his black-handled adze, and climbed back aboard her, and stood in her in the middle of the bare frames and looked up at the slate roof of the shed over her, and the light coming down through the open end.

"She's under a roof," he said.

Nobody answered him. He did not seem to need it.



The engine was Bram's and Tess's.

Emeric had said so in the lean-to, at the slate, with the big circle wiped off it and the little one left. He kept to it. It was harder than he had expected. He went down to the works on the Monday after the commencement and found them both at the long bench in the lean-to with a fresh slate between them, Bram standing and Tess in her chair, and on the slate a drawing that was not his, and he stood in the doorway and looked at it and felt his hands want to take the chalk.

He put them in his pockets.

It was the little circle, many times over. A box of brick, the firebox, and running through it from end to end, slanting, side by side like the pipes of an organ, a great many thin iron tubes, each the width of Tess's thumb, with the fire all round them. Water in the tubes. Fire outside them. At the top, where the tubes came out of the box and met, a small drum, a fraction of the size of the mill engine's, where the steam gathered off the water and went to the engine. And beside the drawing, in Tess's hand, small and very neat, a column of figures: the width of each tube, the thickness of its wall, the pressure it would hold, and beside each figure the figure for the old drum at the same pressure, and a line under each pair, and under the lines, in Bram's square chalk, one word.

Tubes.

"Water-tube boiler," Emeric said, from the doorway, before he could stop himself.

Bram looked round.

"Is that what it's called," he said. It was not a question.

"That's what it's called where I read about it."

"Then that's what it's called." Bram turned back to the slate. "I'd been calling it the organ. Tess calls it the comb." He picked up the chalk. "Water-tube boiler. Write that down, Tess, or he'll sulk."

Tess wrote it down. She did not look at Emeric. She was smiling at the slate.

He did not go into the lean-to. He stood in the doorway and watched them, for most of that morning and a part of every morning after, and did not say anything that was not asked for. They asked him two things. Bram asked how a man joined a tube to a tube so that it held steam, and Emeric said the tubes in the book had been rolled from strip and their edges lapped over each other and welded in the fire, hammered while they were white, the way Bram welded a chain link, only long; and that the book had warned in capitals that it must be done in the fire and not any other way, which Bram took as an insult to any other way he might have thought of, and went to the forge. And Tess asked how many tubes. Emeric said he did not know. Tess said that was a stupid answer, and worked it out herself from the coal Haddon could put on the grate in an hour and the steam the engine would want, and got a figure, and it was the figure Emeric would have got, and he did not tell her so, and she knew.

It took them three weeks. The tubes were the worst of it. Bram rolled them from strip on the bending rolls and lapped them and welded them at the forge, one a day at first and then two and then four, each the length of a man, and tested every one of them with Tess's hand pump to twice the pressure it would ever see, in the yard, with a bucket over the end. Nine split. Bram cut them up and made nails of them, swearing. Sixty held. Sixty tubes, in the brick box, slanting, with a little drum on top that Bram riveted by hand, every rivet driven hot by himself and nobody else, while the heater boy who had driven the cold rivets two years ago stood by the fire with the tongs and watched every one go in, white, and did not say a word, and Bram did not either.

The engine itself was smaller than the mill engine by half. It stood upright, not beam, a cylinder on a frame with the crank under it, so that it would go in a hull. Bram bored the cylinder on the big lathe in the lean-to with the bar Emeric had made Vesk teach him to bore with, in the spring. It took him two days. When he had done he put his hand into the bore and ran his fingers round the inside of it, slowly, the way he had run them round the inside of the mill engine's cylinder two years before, and took them out, and looked at Emeric in the doorway.

"Better," he said.

"Better," Emeric agreed.



The shaft gave Bram a week of language.

The engine turned a shaft, and the shaft went aft, along the bottom of the hull, to the stern, and out through the stern below the water to the screw. And the hole the shaft went out through was a hole in the bottom of a boat. The river wanted to come in through it. The shaft had to turn in it, a hundred times a minute, all day, without letting the river in, and without being gripped so tight it could not turn.

A stuffing box. A collar round the shaft where it went through the stern, a little iron cup, and in the cup, packed round the shaft, rope soaked in tallow, and a gland on top of the cup that screwed down on the rope and squeezed it tight round the turning shaft. Not tight enough and it leaked. Too tight and it seized, and smoked, and burned. In between there was a place where it let in a drop or two, enough to keep the rope wet and cool, and no more.

Bram found the place on the eighth day. He found it by sitting on the bottom of the half hull in the shed in three inches of river water with a spanner, with the engine turning the shaft above him on steam from the yard boiler run down to the slip in a hose, turning the gland a quarter turn and waiting and watching the drips and swearing and turning it back an eighth, for most of a day and a half. Fletcher sat on the frames above him the whole time with his arms folded and offered no advice, which was so unlike him that Emeric wondered whether he was ill.

"Three drops," Bram said at last, on the evening of the eighth day, from the bottom of the hull. "Three a minute. And she's warm, not hot." He sat back in the water. "That's the place."

"That's the place," said Fletcher, from above. He had been counting too.

The screw went on the end of the shaft on the ninth day. Bram cast it in brass in the foundry: four blades, broad, twisted, like the sails of a windmill seen end on, each one bent in the mould to the same twist so that the water would come off all four alike. It was the one part of the whole thing Emeric had drawn. He had drawn it because neither Bram nor Tess had ever seen a screw, and there was no way to arrive at one by looking at a paddle wheel, and he had said so; and Bram had said that was fair, and let him.

When it was on, Fletcher climbed down off the frames and went aft along the slip under the stern and stood looking up at it, the brass screw with its four twisted blades on the end of the shaft, in the shadow under his own hull.

"That pushes her," he said.

"It screws her through the water," said Emeric. "The way a screw goes into wood. Every turn, it pulls her forward the length of its twist, or near it."

Fletcher put out his hand and touched one of the blades. He ran his thumb along the edge of it, from the hub to the tip, following the twist.

"Forty years," he said. "Oars and sail. And the wheel on the old mill boats that splashed half the river into the boat behind." He took his hand away. "And it was this. A thing like a sycamore seed, under the water, where nobody'd see it." He stood looking at it a while longer. "She'll go without any wind at all."

"Against the current."

"Against the current," Fletcher said, as if trying the words out to see whether they would hold his weight. "Up to Grayrock. Against the current." He shook his head. Then he went back up the slip to the shed's tool rack for his adze, because the half hull still had no planking above the turn of her bilge, and he meant to do something about that.



He asked Rylan the other thing in the last week of September, on the wall, at night.

Rylan was on the river stretch and Emeric had come up to walk it with him, as he walked it with whichever knight was on it two or three nights in seven, because Ward had said a holder who walked his own wall at night was a holder whose wall did not go to sleep. They walked the stretch from the water gate to the north tower and back with the river going by black below them and the lamps on their posts above, and did not talk much, and at the north tower Rylan stopped and leaned on the parapet and looked at the shed, dark under the north wall, where the two hulls lay.

"How do you do it?" Emeric said.

Rylan looked round.

"The strength. The jump. When you call it." Emeric leaned on the parapet beside him. "In the passage. On the river, with Finn. I've seen you call it a hundred times. I've never asked you what it's like."

Rylan thought about it. He thought about it the way he had thought about the card in the line room, a word at a time, seriously, with his big hands folded on the parapet.

"It's mine," he said. "That's the first thing. My own. It's in me. In the lines." He turned his wrist over on the stone, and in the lamp light Emeric saw the silver under the skin, ring over ring, from the wrist up into the sleeve. "It's not like a stone. A stone's outside you. You pull on it. This is just there."

"And when you call it?"

"You think it." Rylan frowned, as if the words were the wrong shape. "You think it, and you see it. In your head. The jump, say. You see the jump. You see yourself up on the wall, before you're there. And there's a thing you see with it." He touched his own forehead with one finger. "Like a sign. A shape. The rite puts it in you, the mages say. The same one for every knight. You see the shape and you see the jump, both at once, and it comes." He took his finger away. "That's all. Think it, see it, it comes. The first year after the rite you can't do it without screwing your eyes shut. After that you don't notice you're doing it."

"A shape," said Emeric. "In your head."

"A sign. Like on a stone." Rylan shrugged. "I don't know. I can't draw it. Nobody can. It's not a thing you can draw. It's a thing you see."

Emeric was quiet.

A sign in the head, put there by the rite, the same for every knight. Drawing on the knight's own mana, in the silver lines, because it was the only mana a knight had. Not on a stone outside him.

But a sign was a sign. He had cut a hundred of them, inside stones, from the room. A sign told mana what to be and do. It did not care where the mana came from. If a knight could see a sign in his head and draw on his own mana with it, there was no reason in anything Emeric knew why a sign could not be put somewhere else, on a knight, in him, that drew from a stone instead of from his own lines. A battery. Worn on the body. So that a knight who had spent everything he had could keep going on the stone's.

Or so that a man who had never had the rite at all, and had no lines, could.

He did not say any of it. He wrote it down that night, at his table, in the back of the notebook under Vantic's tally of the frames: a glyph could draw from a battery instead. And under it, smaller: on the skin? And then he looked at that until the candle guttered, and did not write anything under it.



He tried Rylan's way three nights later, in the yard behind the keep, at two in the morning, with nobody awake but the man on the board, whose window did not look on the yard.

He had been doing it wrong. He saw that the moment Rylan said it. Behind the shutters since the spring he had been letting the mana go down into himself and telling it what a fire was, or telling it to close a hand, the way a mage told mana what to be outside him. Pushing it. Giving it orders. And it had worked, slowly, at a cost, the way a thing works when you do it the hard way. Rylan did not push. Rylan saw it. He saw himself up on the wall before he was there.

So Emeric stood in the yard in the dark in his shirt and looked at the wall of the stable, which was twice his height, with a flat top of old slates, and did not tell the mana anything.

He saw himself on top of it.

He saw it the way he saw a drawing before he drew it: whole, finished, there. His own feet on the slates at the top of the wall. And he did not see a sign, because he did not have one; he had not had the rite. But he saw the jump, the shape of it, the line of it from the ground to the top of the wall, and he let the mana go down into his legs and did not tell it anything at all but that.

He jumped.

He went up the face of the stable wall like a thrown stone. Too fast, much too fast, so fast that the wall went past his face in a grey blur, and the top of it went past his feet, and he was above it, a body's length above it, in the dark, in the air, with the whole of the keep yard spread out below him and the lamps of the wall walk going round the town beyond it, and for an instant he hung there, at the top of the jump, before it remembered to come down.

It came down.

He landed on the slates of the stable roof, not the top of the wall. He landed badly, on one foot, on a slate that cracked under him, and his ankle turned on it and went over, and he sat down hard on the roof in the dark with his ankle in both hands and his teeth shut on the noise he would otherwise have made, while a cracked slate slid away down the roof beside him and off the edge and broke in the yard below with a sound like a dropped plate.

The board window, round the corner, did not open.

He sat on the stable roof with his ankle swelling in his hands and his heart going like a hammer and did not know whether he was laughing. He thought he might be. He had seen the top of the wall and the mana had taken him to the top of the wall and then kept going, because he had not seen where it should stop. Rylan saw himself on the wall. Rylan had never once thought to see himself stop.

He got down off the roof the slow way, by the water butt, and limped up the keep stair to his room, and bound the ankle himself, badly, with a strip of shirt, and lay on his bed with his foot up on the bolster and looked at the ceiling.

In the morning Ward looked at the ankle at the dawn drill, and then at the stable roof, where there was a gap in the slates, and then at Emeric, and said nothing whatever, and put him on the ground four times, and the fourth time he did it gently.
