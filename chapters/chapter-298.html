Chapter 298 - Lantern Night, Year Twelve

The Hod went up from the secret dock at a quarter to five on the morning of the sixth of March, with the lake black and the reeds stiff with frost, and an old man on her ladder who was not old any more and did not yet know what to do with his knees.

He came up it two rungs at a time. Then he stopped at the hatch with one hand on the coaming and looked down at the ladder he had just climbed, as if it had said something to him he had not expected.

"I used to be carried," said Armand. "Up that. In a chair, on a rope. Cade swore the whole way."

"I still swear," said Cade, from the racks. "I've just got nothing to swear at."

Armand looked at him, and at the ladder, and came in. He had to stoop in the hatch. He was not used to that either; he put his head into the frame with a crack that the whole house heard, and stood rubbing it with an expression of deep scholarly interest.

"An inch and a half," said Imani, behind him, with her bag. "I measured you on the fourth. You've an inch and a half you didn't have at Long Table, and you keep putting it into door frames."

"I'm told I had it at thirty."

"Then you'd have known where to put it at thirty."

He laughed. It was the same laugh, the dry short one out of the nose that Emeric had heard across a tower table at ten years old; it had only lost the cough on the end of it. He went and sat down on the forward bench under the cupola in a borrowed coat of Ward's, because none of his own reached his wrists now, and his robes, which he had refused to give up, showed a hand's width of stocking at the ankle above his boots. His hair was cut short. Maren had done it on the second, in the kitchen, with the big scissors, and swept up a heap of white off the flags; what was left was white at the ends and black from the roots out to the length of a finger joint, so that he looked, in the lamp, like a man who had walked through a snowstorm and come in before it settled.

Ned put the bank's lever over and she came up off the lake without a sound.



Imani had made the rule in the long room on the night of the fourth, and had written it on a card, and the card was in her coat.

The Band could not hurt him now. That was not her saying so; she had his sheet, every hour of ten weeks, and the dome had sat on him at the tree's line all that time and torn him and mended him and finished, and a body that had been through that had nothing left in it for dense mana to tear. Emeric had looked through him on the third with his hand on the new chest and found the channels wide and clean as a mill race, no tracery, no scar, the heart's wall whole where the dead place had been, the size of a thumb's end, for forty years. So the Band was nothing to him. She had written that down and underlined it.

The air was another thing. The air at four miles was the air at four miles, thin and cold enough to stop a man's thinking long before it stopped his heart, and no stone in the world had changed that. A changed body held its breath longer than an ordinary one. How much longer she did not know, and would not guess, and so the card said two minutes, mask off, on the open deck, harnessed, with her watch in her hand, and not one breath over.



They came out of the cloud at three miles into a sky going grey in the east, with the stars still hard overhead and the sea under the cloud's edge a long way down, dark, and the frost coming up white on the cupola's glass from its rim.

Emeric felt the Band before the probe showed it, the way he always did: the thin air outside the iron going thick, all at once, from one side of the sky to the other, warm to his sense the way a bath is warm. The probe on its pole brightened. The racks opened their throats along both sides of the hold and drank. And on the forward bench, inside the iron, behind the scrubber, where nobody in the house should have felt anything but a faint ease in the joints, Armand lifted his head.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, that's rude of it."

"What is?"

"It's been there all along." He was still looking up. "It's been there all along, and I've been sitting under it with a cup."

Ned held her at the Band's foot. Ward unclipped the deck door's dogs and went out first, as he went first through every door, and clipped on, and stood with his back to the rail; and Imani went next with her watch, and Armand after her with Arthur's hands on his harness from behind, which Armand bore with patience for the length of the doorway and then, outside, very politely, removed.

Armand walked to the rail. He walked, in his borrowed coat and his robes and his harness, on the iron of the deck, with no stick, in a wind that leaned on all of them, and put his two gloved hands on the rail, and looked out.

No silver came up at his wrists. Imani was watching for it. Emeric saw her look, and look again, and then not look.

"Mask," said Imani. "When you're ready. Two minutes. I start when it's off."

He unbuckled it. His hands were steady on the buckle; that was the thing Emeric would remember. Ten weeks ago the old man could not undo his own collar stud without three tries and a curse. He took the mask off and held it at his side by its strap and breathed the air at four miles, straight in, cold enough to make Emeric's teeth hurt through his own.

He coughed once. Then he breathed again, slower, and again, and turned his face up to the gold coming along the cloud's edge, and grinned.

It was not a smile. Emeric had seen Armand smile ten thousand times, thinly, at a sum, at a student, at the corner of his own mouth when he had won. This was a grin. It took up his whole new face, under the white-and-black hair, and showed his teeth to the wind.

"Thirty," said Imani.

He breathed. His chest went up and down under the harness, hard, faster than it should, and he did not seem to care.

At seventy he lifted one boot and set it against the rail's upright, sole flat to the iron, and put his weight into it.

Emeric felt it go: a thread of the old man's mana going down through the sole into the steel, too much, far too much, the way a boy pours a whole jug into a cup to see what happens. The rail rang under it. Rime came off the upright in a sheet. Armand's other boot came off the deck, and for one breath he stood out from the rail at an angle no living man should have been standing at four miles up, in a harness, with his face going a pleased dark red.

"Stop it," said Imani.

"I've been practising."

"You've been practising on the keep tower, where it's brick and the ground's forty feet off. Stop it." She did not raise her voice. It was her ward voice, the one that told a man with a broken leg he was not going to walk to the privy. "You're short of breath and you're pouring mana into a rail at four miles because you've just found out you can. Get your foot down. Ninety."

He got his foot down. He stood at the rail with both boots on the deck and both hands on the iron and his chest going like a bellows, and looked, Emeric thought, exactly like the farrier's boy at Long Table when Bram had said his name.

"It's a very good rail," he said.

"It's a rail. A hundred and ten." She stepped in. "Mask."

He held out his hand for it. At a hundred and twenty she buckled it on him herself, because his fingers had gone slow at last, and he let her; and he stood with the mask on his face breathing the cylinder's air in long hard pulls and his eyes over it full of water from the wind and something else, looking at the sun come up over the cloud.

"There," said Imani, at his ear. "That's what two minutes is. Now you know."

"Now I know," he said, through the mask, muffled. And then, after a while, still looking east: "Eighty-seven years. I came up here once on a rope and once in a chair. I never once stood up."



Emeric went off the bow at a quarter to seven.

Not in a suit. The crew had seen it twice now, in the autumn, and Cade had stopped making the sign against it. He had the suit's harness on over his flying coat and nothing else of the suit: the chute on his back, and on his chest the breath bottle in its felt jacket, the small one from the MK2's kit, with its hose to the mask on his face. The bottle was the only reason Imani allowed it. A body that flew itself at four miles still had to breathe at four miles, and he was not changed; he had only eaten one stone, and an unbonded one, and it had done nothing for his lungs.

He stepped up onto the rail with the line unclipped and let go of his weight.

It came off him like a coat. That was the first thing. On the ground, lightening himself was a cast he did a hundred times a day without thinking and paid for a little each time, the way a man pays for walking. Here he cast it, and the Band came into the place it spent before it was spent; he felt the cost go out of him and the Band pour in behind it, so fast that he was fuller at the end of the cast than at the start. He hung beside the Hod's bow on nothing, with the cloud a mile under his boots, and laughed into the mask.

Then he dug the hole.

Old Edrin's way, Sorrel's way: a patch of air taken out ahead of him and the air behind rushing in to fill it, and himself carried with it. Air was not his. On the ground it cost him three times what it cost Sorrel and he felt it in his shoulders by the end of a mile. Here the cost went out of him and came back in again before he knew it was gone. He made the hole bigger than he had ever made one and went into it, and the Hod went away behind him so fast that he heard Bren whoop from the deck and then did not hear anything but the wind.

He flew.

He did not keep going. He turned at the end of a mile, because the bottle on his chest had a needle on it and the needle was where Imani had told him to turn, and because he was cold: not his body, which he held warm from the inside without thinking, but his face round the mask, and his eyes, which were watering and freezing at the lashes. He came back along the Hod's side slowly, a man's length off her rail, and held there beside the deck, still, as easily as he would have stood beside a cart.

Armand was at the rail with his mask on, watching.

Armand stood at the rail with his gloved hands on the rime and looked at Emeric hanging in the sky at four miles, and his eyes over the mask were the eyes of a man who has been told for sixty years that a thing could not be done, and had taught it, and is watching it done.



They were down on the lake by ten. Armand went down the ladder by himself, and stood on the jetty in the reeds with the frost going off them in the sun, and looked back up at the Hod's grey side for a while. Then he went up the hill to Bram's forge on foot, a mile and a half, because he said he could, and Ward walked beside him the whole way and did not offer him an arm once, which was the most respectful thing Emeric had ever seen Ward do for anyone.



The glider went off the west hill at one.

It had been in the long shed at the works since October, under a sheet, and before that it had been in Sorrel's head for six years, and before that, if you asked her, it had been in the air all along, waiting for somebody with the sense to stop pushing it and start asking it what it wanted. It was the Seers' Table's, by Sorrel's own decree. They had drawn every rib of it. Corin had built the frame in the paper mill's back room out of spruce and Grayrock's light metal tube, and shouted at the mill's foreman when the foreman wanted the room back; Pell had walked in from her father's farm every Saturday all winter to sit on the floor and glue. Rue had drawn the wing.

It was long and pale and very simple. Two wings out of one root, covered in Abloh's doped cloth, longer from tip to tip than the long room was wide; a thin body like a canoe with two seats in it, one behind the other, and a tail on a boom. No stone in it anywhere. Sorrel had made a point of that at the table in the autumn, and made Ferra write it down. Nothing in it that a dead zone could stop, she said, and nothing a priest could call anything but cane and cloth and glue. Only the air, and the shape, and the man in the seat.

The launch was a rope. Not a rope; a long cord of Isle rubber braided inside a jacket of sailcloth, made by Tess on her bench in a week, staked to the glider's nose and spread out down the hill in a great V, with Corin and five of the Table on one arm and the north well's boys on the other. You walked, and then you ran, and the cord stretched, and stretched, until it was stretched as far as it would go and a man at the tail was hanging on to it with his heels in the turf; and then he let go.

Half the town had come out to see it go. Sorrel flew it first; nobody had ever thought she would not.

She sat in the front seat in her leather coat with her bad leg out straight along the floor and her cropped head bare, and looked down the hill at the town, and at the marsh beyond it going out to the sea, flat and brown and silver in its channels, and said, "Walk."

They walked. "Run," she said, and they ran, down the hill, twelve of them in two lines leaning into the cord, and the cord went long and thin as a bowstring; and Pell at the tail with her heels dug in looked back at Sorrel, and Sorrel nodded, and Pell let go.

The glider went off the hill like a stone out of a sling.

It went straight out over the heads of the people on the slope, low, so low that a man's hat came off; and the crowd made a sound and ducked all together like a field of wheat; and then the wing found the wind coming up the hill's face and it went up. Not quickly. The way a gull goes up off a cliff: without seeming to do anything, leaning on the air, rising. Up past the terraces. Up past the ruin on the hill's shoulder. Up, until it was a long pale cross against the blue over the whole of Kaldo, turning, slowly, and the people on the hill had their heads back and their mouths open, and nobody said anything.

It made no sound. That was what the town talked about afterward. Everything that had ever flown over Kaldo, the balloon with its fire basket, the cape-wings, the hulls on their stones, had made some kind of sound or showed some kind of light. This made none. It went over the town in a wide slow circle with the sun on its cloth, silent as an owl, and came back along the hill's face, and turned into the wind, and came down on the long flat field at the hill's foot, slowly, its belly skid hissing on the turf, and stopped.



Sorrel flew it three times more, with three of the Table in the back seat, one after another, Corin last; and then she limped up to Emeric on the hill with the cord's end in her hand and held it out.

"You've flown more than I have," she said. "Not like this. Nobody's flown like this. But you know where up is." She looked past him. "And there's two people who've been standing on this hill since half past twelve pretending they came to see the Table."

"I came to see the Table," said Ferra.

"I came to see if anybody broke anything," said Imani.

"Then you'll want to be in it," said Sorrel, "if anybody does." She put the cord's end into Emeric's hand and closed his fingers on it. "Hands light on the stick. She's not a horse. You're asking her, not telling her. If she wants to go somewhere, go with her and then ask again." She looked at him with her head on one side. "And no tricks. I'll know."

Ferra went first. She had decided that on the hill, he thought, without saying so; she had decided it the way she decided which column a figure went in. She got into the back seat in her grey coat with her book buttoned inside it, because she was not going to leave her book on a hill, and sat very straight, and took hold of the two side rails.

"If you drop me," she said, "I'll never speak to you again."

"You said that at Hollow Night."

"I meant it at Hollow Night. I've had no reason to change it."

The Table walked, and ran, and Pell let go.

The ground went. That was all, at first. The ground went away under them, fast, and the people on the hill went past below in a smear of coats and faces, and the wind came round the low screen at the front of the seat and hit him in the face; and then the wing bit, and the glider stopped being a thing thrown and became a thing held, and they went up.

He asked it to turn. It turned. Slowly, wide, out over the town, and he looked down past the wing's edge and saw Kaldo go round under them in the March sun.

Behind him Ferra said, "I'm counting."

"Roofs. The new ones. In ward five." Her voice came forward over the wind, quite level. "I've the Works Office's count in my book. Two hundred and twelve finished by the end of February. I can count them from here. I want to know if the Works Office can count." A pause. "Two hundred and eleven. One of them's got no roof. It's on the book as roofed."

He laughed so hard the glider wobbled, and Sorrel, a long way below on the hill, would have seen it.

"You're in a glider," he said. "The first one there's ever been. Over the whole of Kaldo. And you're counting roofs."

"Somebody has to." But she had let go of the rail with one hand; he felt it, through the frame. And after a while she put that hand forward over the back of his seat, onto his shoulder, and left it there; and they went round the town twice more without either of them saying anything, over the dome's glass going gold in the sun and the six blue cones of the water towers round it and the long straight avenues going out to the walls, with her hand on his shoulder and her chin, he thought, on her own wrist.

"Than me?"



Imani got in at half past two, when the Table had walked the glider back up the hill on its little cart and the cord had been laid out again.

She did not say she would never speak to him again. She sat in the back seat in her ward coat, which she had worn up the hill because she had come straight from the morning round, and buckled the strap across her lap herself and pulled it tight, and looked at the long pale wing beside her with the face she had for a new instrument.

The Table ran. Pell let go. They went up off the hill into the wind.

He took her higher than he had taken Ferra, because the wind had freshened a little and the hill was giving lift all along its face; he found it the way Sorrel had told him, by feeling the seat push up under him and turning to stay in it, back and forth along the hill in long slow beats like a man mowing, and every beat a little higher, until the people on the slope were dots and the town was a map and the marsh went out under them to the sea's grey line and the sea went out to the edge of everything.

Then, over the marsh, a long way out, she said: "Drop me."

He looked back over his shoulder. She was looking down past the wing's edge at the marsh, at the channels catching the sun, two thousand feet below; and her face was the face she had had once before, six years ago, over this same marsh, in the dark, at the end of his arm.

"Not out," she said. "I know there's no other thing, in this. Nobody's holding me but the cloth. Sorrel said." She looked at him. "But it can go down. Can't it. Fast. For a breath. And then come up." She held his eyes. "A breath. I want to know what it's like when there's nobody holding me at all. When it's only the air."

"Imani."

"One breath," said Imani. "You'll bring us up. I know you will. You'll do it with your hands, and not with anything else. That's why I'm asking you here and not off the keep."

He faced front, and put the stick forward.

The nose went down. Not far. But the wing, which had been leaning on the air all the way up the hill, stopped leaning; and for the space of a breath, as the glider went over the top of its curve and down the far side of it, nothing held them. Not the seat, which fell as fast as they did; not the straps, which went slack; not him. The marsh came up. His stomach came up into his chest. A pencil that had been in somebody's pocket since the Seers' Table built the thing floated up out of the floor between his boots and hung in front of his face, turning slowly, in the sun.

Behind him Imani made a sound he had heard once before in his life, over this marsh, in the dark.

He brought the stick back. Gently, the way Sorrel had said, asking. The wing took the air again; the seat came up under him and pressed; the pencil fell; the marsh stopped coming. They went out over it level and slow, the long pale wing humming very faintly with the speed they had picked up in the drop, and Imani was laughing.

Not the short dry laugh. The other one. She was laughing with her head back against the frame, helplessly, the whole of her shaking with it, the sound going away behind them over the marsh in the wind; and the birds down there in the reeds, a long way below, got up in a cloud and went crying away across the water, the same birds, he thought, or their grandchildren.

"That," she said at last, breathless. "That's what it is."

"You said that last time."

"Last time you were holding me." She leaned forward against the strap, and put both arms over the back of his seat and round his neck, and her face against the side of his head, and held on. Her two fingers found the place under his jaw. "This time nobody was. And you brought us up anyway." A breath. "Hundred and four."

"Is that good?"

"It's a hundred and four," said Imani into his ear, "in a glider, over a marsh, after you've dropped a woman two hundred feet on purpose because she asked you to. It's very good. It's the best I've ever had out of you." She did not let go. "Take us down. Slowly. I want to watch the hill come."

He took them down slowly. On the hill, when they had stopped, and the Table came running, and Sorrel limped after them, Imani got out and stood on the turf in her ward coat with her hair half out of its cloth and her face still lit, and walked straight past all of them to Ferra, who was standing at the cart with her book open, and took Ferra's face in both hands and kissed her on the forehead.

"You should have asked him to drop you," she said.

"I was counting roofs," said Ferra.

"I know you were." Imani let go of her. "There's one without a roof in ward five."

"I know there is," said Ferra. "I've written to the Works Office."



The falls dam had been running since noon with nothing to do.

He went out to it at three on the Mere line, the first train of the afternoon, along the aqueduct's arches over the fields with the fields going by thirty feet below the carriage windows; and he thought, as he thought every time, of a room in the third year and a woman with a book asking him what the arches were for, and his own voice saying they would carry nothing. Ferra had written that down. She had brought it out on the day the first train went over, in October, and read it to him on the platform, and not said anything else.

The turbine hall stood at the dam's foot in plain brick, three storeys high and long as a church, with the dam going up behind it to the sky: grey, curved, smooth, the better part of three hundred feet of Kaldo's cement poured in lifts over two years and a half, with the river held behind it in a lake that had drowned the gorge above the falls and lay now flat and green and still under the March sky as far up the valley as he could see. Along its crest the three spillway gates hung on their counterweights, iron as tall as houses. If every stone in the world went dead at once they would still open when the water rose, because nothing held them shut but their own weight. He had drawn them that way on purpose, and Bram had built them that way, and Linus had walked every foot of the cut before there was a cut, with his chain, and had told him the rock would hold; and it had.

Inside, the hall was warm and full of one sound, the machine, the first of the two: the turbine down under the floor on its shaft where the water went through it, and over it the alternator, in a green iron case as big as a cottage, turning at its proper speed and making its proper voltage and sending none of it anywhere, because the big switch on the board between it and the line to Kaldo was open.

Tess had the board.

She sat in front of it in her chair with her hands in her lap and her eyes on two lamps. Ordinary lamps: two plain bulbs in brass holders on the board above the switch, wired across it, one leg to the dam's side and one to the town's. When the two machines were out of step, the dam's here and the coal station's down the river, the lamps lit. When they came into step, the push on each side of the switch was the same, and there was nothing across the lamps to light them, and they went dark.

They were going bright and dark now, slowly, like a man breathing in his sleep.

"Down a touch," said Tess.

At the governor, the turbine man eased his wheel a hair. The breathing slowed. Bright, and dark, and a long dark, and bright again.

Tess did not move. Bright. Dark. A long dark, longer, the lamps hanging black over the switch for a breath, two breaths.

Her hand went out and closed the switch.

Nothing happened. Nothing was supposed to happen. The machine took up its share of the town with a deeper note under the floor, like a horse leaning into a collar, and the needles on the board swung over and stood still.

On the telephone by the board a bell rang. Tess picked up the earpiece and listened.

"They're banking," she said. "Down at the station. Two boilers." She listened again. "Ferra says, what's the hour, exactly."

Emeric looked at the clock over the door, which the exchange corrected every noon and which agreed with every clock in the four baronies.

"Twenty-six minutes past four," he said.

Tess said it into the telephone. Then she listened a while longer, and her face did something, and she put the earpiece down.

"She says that'll do," said Tess. She sat back in her chair. Her hands were in her lap again, and Emeric saw that they were shaking, very slightly, the way they had not shaken when she closed the switch. "And she says Father's on the bench outside the forge, and he's asked Maren for a cup of tea, and Maren says he's never asked for a cup of tea in his life."

Osric put his hand on the back of her chair.

"The cells are in," said Linus, from the window. "At the fertilizer works. Twelve of them. They'll start on tonight's power, after midnight, when the town's down. The man there says he'll have hydrogen off them by morning that never saw a coke oven."

"And the pot room?"

"Doubled its line from tonight," said Tess. "The light metal by the ton, Bram says. Not by the ingot. By the ton." She almost smiled. "He wants to make a saucepan. For Maren. A big one. He says if the town's going to have the light metal in its kitchens it ought to be Maren's kitchen first, and she ought to be able to lift it with one hand, and he's going to stand there and watch her do it."

He did not say anything. Linus, at the window, did not either. After a while Tess turned her chair and wheeled herself to the hall's west door, where the light was coming in low and gold off the drowned gorge, and sat in it, and looked at the dam.



They lit at dusk.

He was on the river wall by then, at the head of the water stair, with the trestles along the inside of the wall and the pots of lamp-black and the school's brushes and the town writing its names; and the light went out of the sky over the west hill, and at the minute of dusk the board kept in its book, the lamps came on.

What came after them was the thing.

It came from inside the houses. It came the way the lamps never had, not all at once, not on the board's minute, but one at a time and then in handfuls and then everywhere, a window and a window and three windows, as people inside them who had come in from the cold to fetch a coat or a child or a lantern put their hands to the switch by the door and pressed it. There was no board for those. Every house had its own. Tess's men had wired the last of ward six in February, the lower town, the old rows below the church where the plague had started and the burial rows had run down the slope to the wall; and so tonight, for the first time, there was not one window in the walls of Kaldo that lit by tallow or by a candle or by anything but a woman's thumb on a brass switch beside her own front door.

And past the wall, it did not stop.

Two years ago from this wall he had counted the windows past the mile and given up on the farms, because there had been nothing out there to count. Now the light went out from the walls on every side and kept going. The Port Town along the river road, white now, not gas, a long bright crescent of its printed streets. The garden suburb on its grid, every street of it, five hundred houses in their young trees. Out along the rails, the lock villages, white. And out on the flats, in every direction, small and steady and very many, the farms: a pole and a grey box at every lane's end, as he had said on the river tower to two women and a smith two years ago, and a bulb in every room that was lived in. He could not count them. That was the point. He tried for a while, from habit, and lost his place at a hundred and something, and was glad.

And in the middle of it all, New Kaldo: the civic ring round the dome with its towers. Six of them now. The House's first, twelve storeys of glass on its steel frame at the ring's south-east corner, and five more round the ring since the summer, the tallest of them twenty storeys, two of them flats, their steel frames showing through their glass in the dusk like the veins in a leaf held up to a lamp; and tonight every one of them lit from its foot to its crown, floor over floor, gold, on the falls dam's power, which had been running since twenty-six minutes past four and which nobody on the wall had seen begin.



Armand was on the wall.

He was standing. He was standing at the parapet by the water stair's head where his chair had been every Lantern Night since the first, with his lantern on the stone in front of him written and folded, and Ward a yard off with his arms folded looking at the river; and he had no rug and no fur and no stick, and the people along the wall walk were looking at him. Some of them had seen him at Long Table, in the chair. None of them had seen him since.

Nobody in Kaldo knew what to say about it. The Almanac had no line for it. Ferra's notice on the records hall door, which had gone up on the fifth of February, said only that the Secretary of the Arcane had been gravely ill over the winter and had come through, and was taking the air; and the town had read it and looked at the man taking the air on the castle stair, tall and straight in robes too short for him with his hair coming in black at the roots, and decided among itself in a week what it thought, which was that the old master had a son nobody had known about, come south from the tower to look after him.

"They think you're your own son," said Emeric, coming up.

"I know. The girl in the kitchen told me." Armand did not look round from the river. "She asked whether my father was very ill. I said he'd been much better lately." He turned his lantern over on the stone. "She then asked me whether I was married. On the stair. With Imani on the landing above us."

"I heard."

Below them the wall was filling. Tess came along the wall walk in her chair from the works' trestle with her lantern on her knees, the same two names on it as every year, and Osric walking beside her; she had come straight from the dam on the last train and her face was still lit with it, and she stopped her chair by the water stair's head, and looked up at Armand standing there.

Then she turned her chair a little, toward Imani, who was at the hospital's trestle with her hand on a lantern and her brush in the pot, and wheeled herself over.

Emeric did not hear what she said. He did not try. He saw Imani look down at her, and stop with the brush in the air, and put the brush down in the pot; and he saw Tess's hand go to her wrist, to the band under the leather where the stone was, the long grey egg she had carried every day and every night since the July before last and gone into every night since August; and he saw her hold the wrist up a little, between them, the way a child holds up a thing it has made.

Then she knelt down on the wall walk beside the chair, in her good coat, on the cold stone, so that her face was level with Tess's, and said something; and Tess nodded. Imani said something more, longer, with her hand on the chair's rim, and Tess nodded again, harder, and then said one word that Emeric could read from where he stood because he had read it on her mouth a hundred times across a bench: Both.

Imani got up. She looked across the wall walk at Emeric, over the heads, a long look; and then at Armand standing at the parapet in robes too short for him; and then she went back to her trestle and picked up her brush and finished her lantern.



The bell went in the church tower.

All along the wall people struck matches, and the wall walk filled with the small hard crack of them and the smell of the heads going off; and he struck one too, from the box on the trestle, and lit his cake with it, like everybody. Ferra saw. She lit her own and shook the match out and did not say anything, and he saw the corner of her mouth.

The wind came round with full dark, as it did one evening in two in March, as Ferra's column had said for twelve years now and the Almanac said in print with A GUESS under it; and the lanterns tugged, and the town let them go.

They went up off the wall in a long slow drift and out over the river, more of them than any year before, and the wind took them away downriver over the water toward the marsh, glowing. Imani's went first, her father's name toward the town. Then Ferra's. Then his. And at the water stair's head, Armand's.

He let it go himself. He held it up in both hands until the paper was tight and full and pulling, the way the children did, and opened his hands; and it went up straight, and did not go crooked, and went out over the water after the others; and he stood at the parapet with his hands still up, open, empty, watching it go, until Ward said something to him very quietly that Emeric did not hear, and he put his hands down.

The lanterns went out over the lit town and the lit farms and on over the marsh, where there were still no lights, and down toward the sea.



Fennick's display went up at nine from the marsh dyke, a long way from anything, because Ward had chosen the place as he had every year.

It had petals. He had promised it two years ago and it had taken him two years. The last shell went up very high over the black marsh and burst in a ring of gold, and inside the ring a second burst a breath later, smaller, and inside that a third, gold on gold on gold, so that for the space of three breaths there hung over the whole of Kaldo's sky a rose with its petals open, the duchy's own; and the wall, which had cheered every rose since the first, went silent for it, and then roared.

He came up the water stair at half past nine, black to the elbows, with no grin at all on his face, which was so rare that people stood aside for him. He went to Ward, and Ward wrote nothing in his book and turned it so Fennick could see the blank line, and Fennick nodded; and then he came to Emeric.

"Petals," said Fennick.

"Petals."

Emeric looked past him at the marsh. The smoke was going away on the wind toward the sea, and the dyke was dark, and the rockets' frames stood on it in a row, small and black against the faint shine of the water. He had watched them go up all evening. They went up off their sticks with nothing to push against but their own fire. No kick. No recoil. A rocket did not shove the frame it stood on; it shoved its own smoke.

The big one, Fennick said when he asked, had gone up a mile, near enough.

"And sideways?" said Emeric. "Not up. Along. If you laid it on a rail and pointed it at the far side of the marsh." He kept his voice ordinary. "Could one carry a stick of dynamite four miles?"

Fennick stopped grinning.

He did not say anything. He stood on the wall walk in the lantern light, black to the elbows, and looked at Emeric; and then out at the marsh, at the dark beyond the dyke where his frames stood, four miles of it to the sea; and then down at his own hand, the one with three fingers, for a long time. His lips moved once, as though he were counting something, and stopped. The people round them on the wall went on talking about the rose.

Beside him, without a word, Ferra took her pencil out of her book's spine and opened the book at the day's page and wrote a line, small; and closed the book on the pencil.

"Not tonight," said Fennick, at last. His voice was quite different. "Ask me in the morning. In the yard. With Ward." He looked at the marsh again. "Not on Lantern Night."

"In the morning."

Fennick nodded, and went off along the wall walk toward the trestles, slowly, not telling anybody about the rose.

"What did you write?" said Emeric.

Ferra did not open the book. "The hour," she said. "And that he was quiet." She put it under her arm. "He's never quiet. I wanted it written down that he was, and when, and what you'd asked him."

"Why?"

"Because one day somebody's going to ask me when it started." She looked out at the marsh where the rockets' frames stood. "And I'd like to be able to tell them it was on Lantern Night, and he didn't want to answer, and it wasn't him who asked."



They went up onto the river tower at ten, the three of them, because it was theirs on Lantern Night; the watch had gone down to the quay for the late train's crowd and Ward was at the stair's foot.

It was cold, and very clear. The town lay round them lit to its edge and past it, and past the edge of it; and the farms went away over the flats in their small white points until the flats rose into the hills and the points thinned and stopped. He took the long glass off its stand and swung it west along the Milford road, and found Milford on its upland thirty miles off: not a smudge now, not a thumbnail, but a street of white lamps on a ridge, and the wind row above it with a red light on every mast; and north, forty miles up the valley, Grayrock's pithead with its rows round it, white, lit by its own powerhouse, and a line of lamps along the rails going down the valley toward Kaldo to meet the lines coming up.

After a while Imani put her arm through his.

"She asked me," she said. "Tess."

"I saw."

"I said after the checks. After I've looked at him again in a month, and he's the same, and his heart's the same, and nothing's come back. I said April, and not before." She was looking at the river. "She said she'd been waiting since the July before last, and April was nothing. She said she'd wait for my yes and not a day past it." A breath. "I said it was the right thing to ask me. I said it was hers to ask and mine to say, and I'd say it as soon as I could. She said both."

Down below them, at the water stair's head, Armand was still at the parapet, standing, alone now. Ward had gone down to the stair's foot. The wall walk had emptied round him, and he stood with his hands on the stone and his face toward the river and the dark marsh where the lanterns had gone, very straight, in his robes too short at the ankle; and as Emeric watched, he lifted one foot, and set it carefully against the parapet's face, sole flat to the stone, and stood there a breath with his weight in it.

A flake of the coping spalled off under his heel and went down the wall's face into the river with a small dry sound.

He took his foot down at once, and looked round, guiltily, at the empty wall walk, and at the stair, and up, at last, at the river tower.

Imani said a word.

Armand, at the parapet, lifted one hand to the three of them on the tower, palm out, like a student caught, and then put both hands back on the stone and looked at the river again with great attention; and from the tower, across the dark, Emeric could see that he was laughing.

"I'll count him at seven," said Imani.

The late train went at eleven, as the Almanac said, its lamps going away along the Milford line across the lit flats; and they watched it go from the river tower, the three of them, until it was one more light among all the others and they could not tell which.
