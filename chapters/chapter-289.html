Chapter 289 - The Crown's Bounty

The Hod came home on the second of August, at six in the evening, to the yard.

Not to the Mere. Ned brought her in over the port and the river's fork and down onto the airship yard's north slip, beside the K Amara's great silver frame, because the Hod's starboard rig wanted a new frame and the yard was where the welders were; and because Emeric had asked him to, on the set, that morning, without saying why, and Ned had looked at him and not asked.

The yard was working. It was always working now. The welders were on the K Amara's frames under their canvas screens with the blue-white light of the arc going in a dozen places at once along her length; the riveters were on the twins, the clocks going, a thousand of them, from the slips either side; there were cranes moving on their rails, and carts, and a gang of boys sweeping the slip's edge, and Fletcher in his hat at the foot of the K Amara's stern with a drawing, arguing with the yard's foreman about a frame; and a hundred people, near enough, who looked up as the Hod came in low over the yard's head and settled on the north slip's cradle beside the K Amara, and watched her come down, as they always watched a ship come down, and went back to what they were doing.

And two who did not.

They were standing on the slip's edge where the cradle met the yard's stone, side by side, in the evening sun. Ferra in her grey, with no book. Imani in her scrubs under her coat, with no bag. Neither of them said anything when the Hod's hatch came open. Neither of them moved when he came down the ladder, in his filthy coat, with the dust of the plains on him and Hester's green dried in his hair and five weeks of the north in his face.

He came across the slip to them. He did not say anything either.

He had thought, on the way in, over the port, that he would say something. That there was something to be said, after five weeks, after Coldwell and Hollis's field and Bren in the stubble and the ten o'clock nights. He had had a sentence ready. He stood on the slip in front of the two of them in the evening sun and found that he did not have it any more, and did not want it; and Ferra looked at him, and Imani looked at him, and nobody said anything at all.

Then Ferra took one step forward and put both her hands flat on his chest and pushed him back against the Hod's cradle, quite hard, and kissed him.

And Imani was there on his other side before Ferra had stopped, with her hand at the back of his neck, pulling his head round; and when Ferra let go of his mouth Imani did not let go of his neck, and it went on, and on, the three of them against the Hod's cradle on the north slip in the middle of the airship yard at six in the evening, with the arc lamps going along the K Amara's frames above them and the riveters' clocks going on the twins, and nobody, any of the three of them, caring in the least.

The clocks stopped.

He did not notice them stop. He noticed afterward, when he thought about it, that at some point the yard had gone very quiet. That the riveters on the twins had stopped riveting, and the welders on the K Amara had pushed their masks up, and the cranes had stopped on their rails, and the gang of boys sweeping the slip's edge had stopped sweeping and were standing in a row with their brooms, and Fletcher at the K Amara's stern had stopped arguing with the foreman, and every one of the hundred people in the airship yard was looking at the north slip.

And then Ward's voice.

It came from the yard's head, where Ward had evidently been standing all along, by the drawing office's door, waiting for the Hod as everybody else had been waiting for her; and it was the voice he used in the castle yard at dawn, flat and not loud and carrying to every corner of a parade ground.

"Yard," said Ward. "About turn."

There was a pause.

Then a hundred people in the airship yard turned round. All of them. The welders on the K Amara's staging turned their backs to the slip and stood facing the wall. The riveters on the twins turned their backs and faced the sea. The boys with their brooms turned round in their row and faced the yard's gate. Fletcher, at the K Amara's stern, took off his hat, turned round, and stood looking at the frame he had been arguing about with every appearance of the deepest interest, and the yard's foreman beside him did the same.

"Carry on," said Ward.

The clocks started again.

Ferra laughed into Emeric's mouth. He felt it before he heard it: a great helpless shake that went all through her and came out of her against his lips, so that she had to stop kissing him, and put her forehead against his chin, and shake; and Imani, on his other side, with her face against his neck, was laughing too, silently, so hard that he could feel her ribs going against his arm.

And then neither of them was laughing, quite. Ferra lifted her head and looked at him from very close in the evening light, and her grey-green eyes were wet.

"Five weeks," she said.

"Five weeks and two days," said Imani, into his neck.

"I know," said Emeric. "I counted."

"You didn't," said Ferra. "You never count anything." And put her face against his chest, and held on to his coat with both hands, and did not say anything else for a long time; and nor did Imani; and nor did he; and behind them, at the yard's head, Ward stood with his arms folded in the drawing office's door and looked at the sea, and did not turn round until they had gone.



He slept for fourteen hours.

He did not mean to. He meant to sleep for four and then go to the table. He went up the keep stair at eight that evening between the two of them, and was put into Ferra's bed by Imani, who took his boots off and his shirt and looked at every inch of him by the lamp with her hands and then with the hollow view, and found him thin and tired and with a fresh burn across the back of one hand that he could not account for and had not noticed, and nothing else; and he lay down, meaning to close his eyes for a moment, and opened them at ten the next morning, with the sun full on the window and Ferra sitting on the end of the bed in her grey with her book in her lap, writing.

"Fourteen hours," she said, without looking up. "I've written it down."

"Where's Imani?"

"At the ward. She sat up with you till two, and counted, and then she went to sleep on the floor, and then she got up at six and went to work." Ferra turned a page. "She said you were to eat before you went anywhere. She said it in her ward voice. Maren's bringing a tray." She looked up then. "I'd eat it."



The second leaflet went out on the tenth.

It was for the egg beds. The swarms had laid, wherever they had come down, all over the north and the middle of the kingdom and as far south as Milford's hills: ten thousand eggs to every patch of ground they had settled on, in little pods in the earth an inch down, to lie there through the winter and hatch in the spring. And if they hatched, it would all begin again, next May, from more of them, in more places. So the ships were spraying the egg beds every day, every patch they knew of; and the second leaflet told everybody else, in every parish in the kingdom, how to find the ones the ships did not know of, and dig them up.

A spade's depth. In the autumn, after the harvest, before the frost. Turn the earth where the swarm sat, and the pods come up, grey, like grains of rice in a little case, and the hens eat them, or the frost does. Every parish. Every field. Ferra wrote it again, in carters' words, and Ivo drew a pod, life-sized, and a spade, and a hen.

And on its back, at the top, in larger letters than anything else on it, Emeric had put the bounty.

He put it in himself. He wrote it at the study table on the morning of the eighth, five days after he woke, with Ferra across the table and her pen in her hand watching him write it; and when he had written it he turned the sheet round and pushed it across to her and she read it, and looked up at him over it, and her eyebrows went up a long way.

It said: THE CROWN'S BOUNTY. Prince Corbin's measure.

And under it: For every pound of hoppers' eggs dug up and brought to your parish church, the crown pays two coppers, by order of His Highness the Prince Corbin, the King's heir, under the great seal, at Midsummer, this year. It is a good measure. It pays you to save next year's harvest. Dig, and be paid for it.

And under that, smaller: The duchy of Kaldo will pay the same, in the four baronies, besides.

Ferra read it twice.

"You're printing his bounty," she said.

"Yes."

"On our leaflet. In our ink. At the top. With his name on it."

"Yes."

"In larger letters than anything else on the sheet."

"Yes."

Ferra put the sheet down on the table and looked at him for a long moment with her pen still in her hand.

"Why," she said.



He told her that evening, in Ferra's room, with Imani there, because he wanted to tell them both at once, and because it was not a thing for the table.

Imani had come up from the ward at nine and sat on the warm floor with her back to the bed and her shoes off and her eyes shut, which she did now at nine every night, Ferra said, as if somebody had wound her down; and Ferra sat at her table by the window with the leaflet's proof in front of her, the first one off the press, still smelling of ink; and Emeric sat on the window seat between them with his back against the frame.

"Three reasons," he said.

"There are always three," said Imani, without opening her eyes.

"One. It's a good measure. It's the best measure anybody in the kingdom thought of this year, including me." He looked at the leaflet on Ferra's table. "I'd never have thought of a bounty. I'd have thought of ditches and spades and mould and trains, and I did, and they were right, and the bounty's better than any of them at one thing, which is making every hungry man in every parish in the kingdom into a man who's being paid to dig. It's his. He thought of it in three nights with clerks, and it's good. I want it done, everywhere. And I want it known whose it was." He was quiet. "That's true. That's the first reason and it's true."

"And the second," said Ferra.

"The second." He looked at the leaflet. "His proclamation's on every market cross in the kingdom. Our leaflet's in every parish church. A hundred thousand of them, at least. It says, in larger letters than anything else on it, that the crown will pay two coppers a pound for eggs at the parish church, under the great seal." He paused. "Every collector in the kingdom will read it. Every one of them will take their eggs to their parish church and ask for their two coppers. The crown's promised it. In print. In our print, with his name on it, everywhere."

Ferra had gone very still.

"So he has to pay it," she said.

"Or the crown breaks its printed word in front of a hundred thousand people. In the year of the swarm. After a day of prayer." He shrugged. "He'll pay it. He's not stupid. Out of a treasury his grain price has already strained, because a crown that buys up the north's grain at a fixed price has to pay for it, and the crown's been paying for it since May." He looked at the window. "And he'll pay it the way the capital's clerks pay things. Which means they won't be able to count it."

"Count what?"

"The sacks." He looked at her. "A man takes a sack of eggs to his parish church. The priest weighs it and pays him two coppers a pound. And the man goes out of the church's back door with his sack, and round to the front, and takes it in again. Or his brother does. Or his neighbour, in a different parish, the next day. The same sack. Paid for three times." He nodded at the leaflet. "The capital's clerks can't stop that. They've never had to count anything that didn't come to them on paper. They'll pay for the same sack of eggs three times over in every parish in the kingdom, and it'll go on the crown's books under the crown's bounty with the Prince Corbin's name at the top."

"And ours," said Ferra slowly.

"Ours, we count." He looked at her. "You count. Every sack that comes into a parish church in the four baronies gets a punch through its neck when it's paid. A clerk with a punch, at every church. A punched sack's been paid. You can't bring it in twice."

Ferra looked at him for a long moment.

Then she picked up her pen, and pulled the leaflet's proof toward her, and wrote on it, under the duchy's line at the bottom, in small exact letters, slowly: In the four baronies, every sack paid at the church is punched by the parish clerk. A punched sack is paid once.

She turned it round and showed him.

"That goes on it," she said.

"That goes on it."

"So that every man in every parish in the kingdom who reads it," said Ferra, "reads, at the bottom of the sheet, that in Kaldo they punch the sacks." She put her pen down. "And wonders why the crown doesn't."

"Yes."

Ferra looked at the line she had written for a while.

"That's the third reason," she said. "Isn't it."



"The third reason," said Emeric, "is that it's his idea."

Imani opened her eyes.

"His one good idea," said Emeric. "The best idea anybody had this year. And it's going to run on our leaflet. In our ink. On our paper, out of our presses, on our trains, to our railheads, beside our grain and our mould and our ducks. Every man in the kingdom who gets paid his two coppers for a sack of eggs this autumn is going to get the bounty's terms off a sheet that says Kaldo at the bottom. And every man in the kingdom who gets his sack paid three times over by a capital clerk is going to read at the bottom of the same sheet that Kaldo punches theirs." He looked at the window. "The idea's his. The bread is ours. The mould is ours. The warning, the paper, the counting, the trains. The only thing in all of it that's his is the one thing that's printed biggest, and it's printed by us." He was quiet a moment. "It's going to look as if his one good idea is a thing his little brother lets him have."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"There's a word for it," said Emeric. "Where I come from."

They both looked at him. He had not said those words to them in that way for a long time. Where I come from. Not here; there. The other side of everything, that only the two of them in all this world knew about.

"Little brothering," he said. "You little-brother somebody. It's when you make a man look small by beating him so completely at his own thing that he looks like the junior. Like the little brother, tagging along. You do his thing better than he does, with his name on it, so that everybody can see." He looked at the leaflet. "And when the actual little brother does it to the actual elder brother, it's the worst kind there is. Because the little brother's made you into the little brother. It's the whole of it. It's complete disrespect, done politely, in public, with a smile."

Ferra was looking at him with an expression he could not read at all.

"You're doing that," she said. "To Corbin. On purpose."

"I'm doing all three. On purpose. I'm doing the first one because it's true and the second because it'll work and the third because..." He stopped.

"Because," said Ferra.

"Because he called me a drunk at our father's table for seven years," said Emeric. "And at my own investiture he stood at the high table and said what he said, in front of the whole court, and I answered it, and it wasn't enough. And because he sat that council every week for fourteen months when he was fourteen while our father hunted for the men who killed my mother, and our father came home and went to my door and not his, and he's never forgiven either of us for it, and I don't blame him, and he's going to come for me anyway, sooner or later. Saban's man says so. Ward says so. I say so." He looked at the leaflet. "And I'd rather he came having paid for the bounty."

The room was very quiet.

Imani sat up.

She sat up off the warm floor and looked at him, on the window seat, with her knees drawn up and her arms round them, steadily, the way she looked at a wound she did not like the shape of.

"Are you sure," she said.

"About what?"

"That you want to do this." She did not look away. "To a man who already wants you gone. You said it yourself. He's going to come. You know he's going to come. You've known since the investiture." She was quiet. "And you're going to stand on a hundred thousand sheets of paper in every parish church in the kingdom and make him look like your little brother, on purpose, in front of everybody, the year he did the one good thing he's ever done for anybody." She looked at the leaflet on Ferra's table. "That's not a measure. That's a slap. With his own hand."

"Yes."

"And you're sure."

He thought about it. He thought about it properly, the way he would have wanted her to think about it if it had been the other way round; on the window seat, in the dark, with the lamp, and the leaflet's proof on Ferra's table smelling of ink.

"He was always going to come," he said at last. "Imani. Whatever I did. From the day our father gave me Kaldo. He worked out what Kaldo's for before anybody in the capital did; Saban says so, and Saban's right. He's watched it grow for twelve years, and he's watched our father shield it. He was always going to come." He looked at her. "This way, when he comes, he'll have paid for the bounty. Out of his own treasury. Three times over in every parish. And the kingdom will know who printed it." He was quiet. "That's what I'm sure of."

"And the rest?"

"The rest," said Emeric, "I'm not sure of at all."

Imani looked at him a long time.

"Good," she said, and lay back down on the warm floor, and shut her eyes again. "As long as you know which is which."

Ferra had not said anything for a while. She was sitting at her table with the leaflet's proof in front of her, and her pen, and she was looking at the line she had written at its bottom, about the punch; and when he looked at her, she was smiling, very slightly, at the corner of her mouth, the way she did when a column came out right that she had not expected to.

"Little brothering," she said, trying it.

"Little brothering."

"I'm going to use that," said Ferra. "Not in a book." She picked up her pen. "In my head. Every time I'm in a room with him."

The proof went to the press at dawn with her line on it.



They replanted in August.

It was late. It was very late; it was the last of the summer, and anything sown now had to come up and be got in before the frost, which on Milford's benches came in October. But Hollis had a list. He had had it since June. He brought it to the table on the twelfth, with his hat, and read it out: turnips, which would come in ten weeks and feed people and cattle both; buckwheat, which would come in twelve and did not care how poor the ground was; and the beans. Hollis's runner beans, the little fast ones he had bred up from a handful bought off a carter at Wayford market three summers ago, that Hollis had grown in his strip ever since and saved the seed of every year, because Hollis saved the seed of everything; which came up in eight weeks from sowing to picking if they had water, and fed a family off a row.

"Will it be enough," said Ferra.

"No," said Hollis. "It'll be a lot."

So the eaten fields of the four baronies went under the plough again in the third week of August, in the heat, with every horse and every truck and every man and woman who could walk behind a seed drill; and Hollis's own square mile on the Milford bench, that the swarm had eaten to the stubble while Linnet Shaw held a mile of sky off it for an hour, went under the plough on the fifteenth, and was sown with his runner beans, every acre, by Hollis himself, with his hat on the back of his head and his lips moving.



Saban brought the songs from the capital on the twentieth.

He brought them the way he brought everything from the capital, in his own head, from his own men, who had their ears in every inn and market and chancery corridor between the river and the palace; and he sat at the table in the wine store with his hands folded on it and told them, in his dry commercial voice, as if he were reading out a price list.

The capital was hungry. Not starving; the crown's grain price had held the worst off, and the swarms had come down thinner on the city's own fields than on the north's. But hungry. The bread was dear in spite of the price, because the bakers could not get the grain the price was for, because the farmers had buried it in their orchards. And the bread that was not dear, in the poorer markets by the river, was Kaldo's.

It had got there by the Wayford line. Ferra had sent it, in July, at cost, to the capital's own railhead, because the capital had people in it too; and it was sold in the river markets off Kaldo's own carts by Kaldo's own carters at the price Ferra had written on the shed's wall, and the people in the river markets had worked out by the end of July which bread was which.

And they had songs about it.

"Three," said Saban. "That my men have heard. There'll be more by now." He looked at his hands. "One of them's about the duke's bread being cheaper than the prince's prayers. One of them is about a prince who counted the hoppers and a duke who counted the sacks." He paused. "And one of them, which is the one the children sing in the river streets, skipping, is about a little brother who fed his big brother's city while the big brother was at church."

Nobody at the table said anything.

"I didn't write them," said Emeric.

"Nobody thinks you did." Saban looked at him. "That's the trouble with songs. Nobody wrote them. Everybody sings them." He unfolded his hands. "And he's heard them. My man in his household says he heard the third one on the twelfth, from his own carriage, going through the river market. He stopped the carriage. He sat in it and listened to the children skipping to it in the street for a long time. Then he told his coachman to drive on."

"Saban," said Ferra.

"Two more things." Saban did not look at her. "One. His clerk, the old one, the one who does his private letters, went to the tower in the second week of July and asked the tower's archivist for the rite's floor numbers. Every season's. For the last ten years. Who went in. Who came out. How many." He looked at Ward. "The tower gave them to him. He's the heir. The tower gives the heir what he asks for."

Ward did not move.

"And two," said Saban. "The same clerk has been in the crown's own rolls. The army's. He's been going through the postings. And he's found what any clerk would find if he looked, which is that since last spring a good part of the crown's rite candidates, the crown's own men, the soldiers who go to the floor through service, have been posted south. To a ducal garrison. Here. Under the king's seal. With no reason given on any paper anywhere." He looked at his hands again. "He's found that the king has been sending his soldiers to Kaldo. And he's found the floor's numbers. And he's laid them side by side on his table, my man says, and looked at them for a long time."

"He doesn't know why," said Ward. Very flat.

"No," said Saban. "He doesn't know why. Not yet." He folded his hands again. "He knows that his father has been doing something with Kaldo for more than a year that his father hasn't told him about. That's all. That's enough."



And on the twenty-sixth, Saban's man in the household sent one more line, by the Wayford express, in his own pencil, on the back of a laundry list; and Saban brought it up to the study himself at ten at night, and put it on the desk in front of Emeric without a word, and stood back.

It said that on the morning of the twenty-fifth a copy of the Kaldo leaflet, the second one, with the crown's bounty at the top of its back in large letters and the Prince's name under it and the line about the punch at the bottom, had been on the Prince's breakfast table, put there by nobody knew who.

That the Prince had read it. Twice. Both sides.

That he had sat with it for a long time, and then turned it face down on the table, and put his hand flat on it.

And that he had not eaten his breakfast; and that at noon he had sent for his private clerk, the old one, and for paper; and that the clerk had been with him until after dark, and that nobody in the household knew what they had written.

Emeric read it three times.

Then he put it down on the desk, face down, and put his hand flat on it, and sat there.

Saban watched him do it. He did not say anything about it.

"A letter," said Saban, at last.

"Yes."

"To you?"

"To me." Emeric did not take his hand off the laundry list. "Or about me. To somebody." He looked at the window, where the dark was, and the lights of the town going away below it to the walls; and beyond the walls, north, the long black line of the Wayford road. "Either way, it's coming."

"When?"

"When he's sure he's right." Emeric looked at the laundry list under his hand. "He's a careful man. He'll want to be sure."

Saban looked at him a moment longer. Then he picked up his hat.

"You wanted him to come having paid for the bounty," he said.

Emeric looked up.

"Ferra told me," said Saban. "This morning. She said you'd say so if I asked." He put his hat on. "He's paid for it. My man in the chancery says the crown's paid out four times what it budgeted for the bounty in July alone, and it's August, and every parish in the kingdom is digging." He went to the door. "You wanted him to come having paid," said Saban. "He's coming. He's paid. I hope it was worth it." He paused in the doorway. "I think it was. I'd have done the same, if I were you, and I'd have regretted it, a little, at about this time of night."

He went out.

Emeric sat at the desk for a long time with his hand on the laundry list.

At midnight Ferra came in from her room, in her shawl, with a candle, and found him there; and did not say anything; and came round the desk and stood behind his chair and put her hands on his shoulders, as he had put his on hers in the shed at the junction in July, with the map; and after a while he let his head go back against her.

"He's writing," he said.

"I know. Saban told me." Her hands did not move. "You knew he would."

"I knew he would."

"Then come to bed," said Ferra. "And be sure of what you're sure of. And not the rest."

He went to bed.

Imani was asleep on the warm floor where she had been at nine, with her shoes off and her arm under her head, and did not wake when they lay down on either side of her; and Ferra was asleep in a breath, between one and the next, with her hand on his arm across Imani's waist. And he lay on his back on the warm oak in the dark with the two of them beside him, and thought about a man in a house in the capital, at a table, with a sheet of Kaldo paper turned face down under his hand and a clerk with a pen, writing until after dark; who had sat that council every week for fourteen months when he was fourteen, while their father hunted, and had never been thanked for it, and had never forgotten.

Little brothering.

He had told Imani he was sure. He was. He was sure Corbin was coming.

He was not sure, lying there, in the dark, with the pipes ticking under the floor, that he liked what he had done to bring him, quite as much as he had thought he would.

He supposed that was what Imani had meant. He supposed that was what she had wanted him to know. He lay awake a long time, knowing it.
