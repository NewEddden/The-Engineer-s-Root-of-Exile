Chapter 357 - Lantern Night, Year Sixteen

The first string burst behind the sleeve.

Emeric saw it before he heard it: four small grey knots opening in the air over the bay in a ragged line, well behind the canvas and below it. The sound came back across the water a breath later, four flat cracks like planks dropped on a yard floor, and a gull that had been sitting on the harbour wall got up and left.

"Short," said the woman at the rangefinder, without lifting her face from it. "Short and low."

"It isn't short," said Ward. "It's late."

The battery had the wall's north-east corner, where the old town had kept a beacon basket and the new one kept four guns. They were long pale-barrelled things on pedestal mounts, each with an iron seat for the man who laid it up and down and another for the man who swung it round, and a rack of brass-nosed shells at its elbow. Bram had built the housings, the turning rings and the shields, and had complained all winter that nobody would let him paint them. Tess's group had fitted the mounts and the sights. Emeric had drawn the guns two years before and then, as far as he could manage it, left them alone. They were Ward's. Ward's gunners had had them since the autumn, firing at empty sky by the clock and the book, and this was the first morning they'd had anything to fire at.

The sleeve was a cone of canvas the length of a hay cart, towed on a wire half a mile behind one of the two white trainers. The trainer flew a slow straight line across the bay mouth at the height of a tall cloud, with a Crestless pilot at her helm and orders in Ward's hand to turn for home if anything burst nearer to her than to the sleeve. Ward had measured the wire himself. Twice.

The rangefinder stood on a tripod at the back of the platform: a brass tube as long as a tall man, with a little window at each end and the eyepieces in the middle, so that the reader looked out of both ends of it at once. Ivo had ground its prisms over the winter and had come up to watch them used, and was standing by the stairhead in his hat with his hands behind his back like a man at his daughter's recital. In the eyepiece the sleeve showed cut across the middle, the top half and the bottom half not lining up, and the reader turned a drum under her right hand until they did, and read the range off the drum, and called it. The fuze setters turned the brass ring on each shell's nose to the number Ottoline's card gave for that range. The shell would burst that many seconds out of the barrel, wherever it had got to.

That was the whole of it. A tube, a drum, a card, a ring. Nothing on the platform held a charge but the gunners' hand-warmers.

"The radar would have had that," said Tess.

She was in her chair at the end of the platform with a blanket over her knees and her stick across it, and she had said so, by Emeric's count, three times since breakfast.

"The radar isn't built," said Ward.

"Because the logic line can't carry it yet. I know. I wrote the logic line." She glared at the bay. "I want it on the record that I'd have had it."

"It's on the record," said Bram, who had come up the stair behind them with a tin of grease. "It's on every record there is. They'll carve it on the rotunda under the Charter."

"Again," said Ward.

The second string went the same way. The reader called, the setters turned their rings, and the four guns went off together with a sound that came up through the stone into Emeric's heels. The bursts opened in a row, tidier this time, at the right height, and well behind.

The reader took her face from the tube. "The range is right," she said. "I'll swear to the range."

"The fuzes are right," said the setter on number two. "Off the card."

"The card's right," said Ottoline, who had come up the stair at some point without anyone noticing, and didn't add to it.

The layer on number three was a holdings boy from somewhere out past the Mere, with big red hands and a fur hat with flaps, and he hadn't said a word all morning. He said one now, mostly to his gun.

"You're shooting where it is."

Ward looked at him.

"It's not there when you get there, sir. It's gone on." The boy went red under the hat. "You don't shoot a duck where it is. You shoot it where it's going."

Emeric had the word for that, and something like the drawings: whole rooms of gears on ships that had never sailed here, turning a target's speed and course into a point ahead of it. He had meant to bring it up after the third string, if the third went like the first two. He shut his mouth.

"How far ahead," said Ward.

The boy squinted at the sleeve. "Three of its lengths. Four, maybe. It's slow, for a duck."

Somebody on number one laughed, and stopped when Ward didn't. Ward stood with his arms folded looking at the sleeve, and then he said, "Four lengths," and the layers wound their wheels, and the four barrels came round after the sleeve and went past it, ahead of it, onto empty sky.

Two of the third string burst in front. Two burst round it, close enough that the canvas jumped on its wire.

"Three and a half," said the boy.

The fourth string took it apart. One shell went off close under it and one very nearly inside it, and the sleeve folded in the middle like a shot goose and came down in pieces, slowly, a long way, onto the bay, while the white trainer far out ahead began to wind in a wire with nothing on the end of it. One of the gunners whooped. Nobody else did. They were mostly watching Ward.

Ward took his book out and wrote in it. Then he looked at the boy on number three.

"Name."

The boy told him. Ward wrote that down too, under the four strings, and put the book away.

"Radar would have done it on the first string," said Tess.

"Radar'd want a housing," said Bram. "Bring me a radar and I'll house it."

"I will."

"Then I'll house it."

Emeric went down the stair with Ivo, who was very pleased about his prisms and said so twice. Under the castle, in the hardened room cut into the keep's old foundations, the Shell's grown core sat in its sphere and took on mana by the ounce. When it was finished it would stand over Kaldo unseen and turn aside most of what came at the city fast. A dead zone forced at the wall would blind a patch of it for most of a day. On that day there would be four guns on the corner and a boy who shot ducks. Emeric's word for guns like these was flak, and the yard had it by noon, because Tess had heard him say it once on the stair: the Flak Battery.



The two stones from the board's grant went into the body on the eleventh of February, one in the head and one in the chest beside the canister, and Emeric wrote her first lines that night at the bench in the Motion Shop with nobody else in the room but Tess.

He wrote them in his own hand, as they had been written on the sheet that hung over Tess's bench for a year and a half. Change nothing not signed by a listed hand. Stop on a listed note. Anything that cuts these lines, lock. And the fourth, which was Tess's: unless she asks. His mother's note went into the list beside his own and the six's. Below the lines, in the chest, the inducer waited by the canister, so that a dead zone would stop her for one breath and no longer. The brakes were Bram's. The drives were Tess's and Dov's, the bearings Ottoline's to the last knuckle, the knit under the skin Abloh's. The thirty bundles of fiber in each forearm were the same stuff Tess and Bram had drawn for Abel's hand years ago, and the stones at every joint sat on top of them for when she wanted to lift a cart.

When he'd finished Tess wheeled over and looked at the stone a long while without touching it.

"She'll want to see what you wrote," she said.

"She's seen it. It was on your wall."

"Not on paper. In there." Tess nodded at the stone under his hand. "I would."

It had taken most of two years, and the pieces had come from all over the town, which was how Kaldo built anything now. The bones that carried her weight were bucket steel, hollow, drawn at Bram's furnace the winter after the war. The ribs, the forearms and the frame of the skull were duralumin, because she would lift her own arms ten thousand times a day and Tess had done the sums on that and gone quiet. Every bone wore a fired coat of enamel with stone dust in it, so that when one flexed its note shifted and the chest stone heard it. That was how she would know her own weight on her own feet. Ottoline called them the gossiping bones. The name stuck in the shop, though not on the drawings.

Under the breastbone sat the heart. It was a pump, nothing cleverer, the size of two fists, and it pushed liquid mana out of the canister through flexible tubing to every stone in her, so that all of them drank from the same blood and none went hungry in a dead field. It had little brass gates that shut off whatever she wasn't using. Imani had asked why a golem needed a heart when the sentries managed on wire. Emeric had said a wire carried an order, and the tube carried the order and the means to obey it, and he wasn't putting his mother in anything that could be starved. Imani had written that down. Underneath it she'd written also because he's sentimental, and shown him.

Over the frame went a shell of dust ceramic in two layers, a shard face over a gravity core with a keystone each, so a blow slowed over its last inch and the plate behind it didn't shatter. Over the shell went the skin.

The skin was the Forge's silicone rubber, laid into Abloh's knit in two layers: a soft gel underneath that moved like flesh, and a thin tough coat over it. The colour went on by hand in more coats than anyone had counted, each thinner than paint. Her hair was her own. It had grown back in the dome faster than anyone could keep it trimmed, and Abloh's two best girls had punched it into the scalp a few strands at a time over the whole of one autumn.

The cast had come from her. Imani and Tess had taken it over three evenings in the dome, from the body on the mattress by the tree, every crease and freckle, and then painted the skin with that body beside the bench so that every vein ran where hers ran. Emeric had not been in the room. He had made the frame of the face, and the hands, and the teeth, and he'd made a very good job of all three. For the rest he'd gone and stood in the Motion Shop yard with his back to the dome and found things to do. There were parts of his mother a man did not build. Tess had told him kindly that nobody had asked him to. Ferra had told him, less kindly, that on the second evening he'd stood in the yard for two hours in the rain, and that the yard hadn't needed him.

Amara stood up in it on the nineteenth.

There was no ceremony, because she wouldn't have one. Bram and Dov brought the body up the hill on a handcart under a tarpaulin, which made it look, Dov said, exactly like what it was, and they stood it on its feet in the ring between the two domes, braked, by the inner glass. Abloh had finished the face on the fifteenth from the good picture, the one Harrold had been made to bring, and had declined to be in the dome when she first saw it. He'd said he had a hem to do. Everybody knew he didn't.

She went into it the way she had gone into the cave golem, from inside the inner dome, with her son's hand on its forearm and her own hand laid over his. Emeric felt her go. Then the face moved. It moved badly at first, too much and then not enough, and the eyes, which were Ivo's glass, went round the ring and found the dome's outer pane, black with the February night behind it, giving back the lamps and her, standing.

She looked at herself for a long time.

What looked back was a woman a little under middle height, straight in the back, in the high-collared jacket, long dark skirt and boots that Abloh had sent up with the face, because he would not have her stand up bare in front of the Motion Shop. Brown skin, the same brown as Imani's; Imani had held her own wrist against the body on the mattress and then against the cast and sworn to it. Her own black hair, straight from the treatment, loose past her shoulders. Light hazel eyes that went amber under the lamps, and behind the glass the etched seeing stones, and behind those nothing anyone in the ring could see. The hands at her sides were her hands. Harry would know them in the dark.

"He's made my nose straighter," she said.

"Harrold took him the good picture."

"Harry hasn't got a good picture. He's got the one where the painter straightened my nose for money." The face tried to do something, and managed most of it. "Tell Abloh it's very good. Tell him the nose is a liberty."

When she spoke again it came out of the throat, where a reed sat with an air stone breathing across it, and it sounded like breath and not like a wire. "Is that my voice?"

"It's close," said Tess.

"It's lower."

"You were lying down for two years," said Tess. "Everybody's voice is lower after that."

She walked the ring twice that night, slowly, with the drives at her hips making a small dry sound that she said was like a woman with bad knees going upstairs; Tess, in her chair by the door, said that was exactly what it was. Then she went out through the outer door onto the gravel. Past the dome's ground her note didn't thin. It went with her, inside the body, a lamp carried out in its own glass. She walked past the place where the cave golem had stopped at twenty paces with its foot in the air, and on, to fifty, to the castle gate and the head of the hill road, and stood there looking down at the town's lights as long as Bram's watch said she ought to on a first night, and walked back.

Harrold found Emeric in the morning at the bench, putting the night's notes into Tess's book, and stood beside him reading them upside down for a while in the way he had, the way a king reads a petition he has already decided to grant.

"She's complete," he said at last. It was nearly a question.

"She walks. She talks. She feels a hand. She'll fly when Tess is happy with the drives." Emeric kept writing. "She doesn't eat. She'll never need to."

"Complete," said Harrold again, more carefully.

Emeric put the pen down and looked at his father. His father looked back, a big gold-eyed man with his hands behind him, entirely serious and a little pink at the ears.

"I built what a son builds," Emeric said. "Face. Hands. Teeth. Feet. Imani and Tess did the rest, and I stood in the yard." He picked the pen up. "If the two of you want anything added, you ask Tess. Or Bram. Not me. Never me."

Harrold considered it. "Bram," he said, doubtfully.

"Bram doesn't talk."

"That's what worries me."

"Then ask Tess," said Emeric, "and go away," and Harrold went. On the step outside he was heard to laugh, once, which was about as often as Ward asked anybody for anything.

On the mattress by the tree her own body lay as it always lay, and didn't stir. Imani went in every evening after that and checked it as she always had, the pulse and the breath and the colour of the nail beds, and wrote it in the ward book. Nobody asked her to stop.

The kit went on in the last week of February, in the ring, with Bram fussing at the buckles. Under it went one of Abloh's close black under-suits, the same cut the Crestless wore, sleeveless, closed down the spine under a lapped edge and thin enough that from across the ring she looked painted. The plate went over that, and the plate was thin. Thinner than any knight's. A knight's suit was half his strength and most of his protection, and hers was neither. Everything she needed was already in her: the stones that moved her and steadied her, the ones she saw with and the ones she'd fly on, all set in the body under the skin, and the shell under the skin would turn a sword without help from anybody.

So the kit was extra, and everybody in the ring knew why. Emeric knew his mother would fight. He'd known it since the day Ward told him about the stair: the queen in her plain grey gown coming along the family passage, and the Duke of Aldane on his back on the floor with his arm across her knee and her hand at his throat, and her letting him up and smoothing her gown. A woman who'd done that in a gown would do it again in whatever she happened to be wearing. He'd rather it was this. He doubted anybody alive could hurt the body while she was awake in it. He'd built the kit anyway.

It was the knights' black, matte as charcoal so it took no light, with violet only where a stone sat under glass: the chest port, the ribbed lines down the sides, the eyes in the shoulder plates, the crystals at the knees and wrists. Those were his, all of them, stones on top of stones she already had. The chest port held her own canister behind a disc of Ivo's violet glass, because liquid mana glows blue and nobody outside the board was to see blue on a woman's chest. That canister was enough to fly on, Tess said. She'd done those sums too. Eighteen minutes flat out, a good deal longer at a sensible speed.

Emeric had put two more on her back.

They stood upright between her shoulder blades in their own sleeves, each a full canister behind its own violet window, and over them he'd fitted a back plate that opened like a cabinet door: a catch-plated face of shard dust, the stasis kind, over a core of gravity dust. Two layers wanted two keystones. He'd used one, a sphere of crystal no bigger than a walnut, clear shading into violet, in a plain brass collar at the top of the plate. It was one of the first small fused stones out of Armand's racks at the Well that the Shell hadn't spoken for, grown the way the Church had grown the stones in its hulls, stasis and gravity in one body with the seams running straight through. It keyed both layers at once and drew on both, so neither could run dry before the other. Tess had written its lines. In the margin beside them she'd written for the castle gate? and underlined it twice.

Bram put the plate on the range and fired a rifle into it with the sphere dead, because that was the rule, and it held on its own steel. Then he fired again with it live, and the round slowed in the last inch and came to rest in the face like a stone dropped into sand.

"She's walking to the castle gate," Tess said. "Not to the Band."

"She might go further."

"You've given her three canisters to walk to the gate."

"I've given her two spares," said Emeric, "that nobody can shoot."

Amara stood in the ring with the back plate open and Bram's head inside it, and said it was like having a son who packed a winter coat for a picnic. She said she'd keep it. She said he was to stop looking at her like that.



February went out wet. The Charter went up on the rotunda's south face a word at a time, under the old ward-three mason who had hated the printer and still did, who had cut the town's doors by hand to prove a point and was now proving it again on scaffolding at an age when he ought not to have been up a ladder. The 32-hour week was in force from the quays to the Milford rows, and the Works' night shift had stopped being called the night shift because there wasn't one. On the falls road the cold brakes held the stone wagons on the long hill without smoking, and on the hill line the new gyro trams leaned into the bends and came upright again, and children rode the end platforms to feel them do it. Out at Milford the green turbines turned on bucket-steel blades that Bram had stopped going out to look at, which was how anyone knew he trusted them.

In the harbour the escorts rode at their moorings with their new shells on, cut from the Church's own shard stone off the hulls taken in the air battle, and the Church's old lines run again in Kaldo steel through their ribs. The two white trainers flew every fair morning with a cadet crew. The pope's stones, the ones that had come out of those hulls with his hand still in them, stayed sealed where Armand kept them, and nobody broke a seal on them without writing in his register first.

The Crestless taught. Arthur had the morning yard at the knights' base, Mira the afternoon, and Bren the flying school three days a week, where the cadets learned the harness on the old frames before anybody let them near a suit. Emeric went out to Ward's yard on a raw morning in the first week of March because Ward had asked him to, which Ward did about once a year, and stood at the rail with Declan watching Mira take a line of twenty through the grappling. She'd walk down the line, stand where the man had read her, on the side her scar was on, and say, "Now. What can't I see?" and wait till he worked it out. Noll Finch was in the line, over from the island for the week with his cough gone, and got thrown twice, and got up both times, and the second time he got up grinning, which Mira said was wrong and was the first thing she'd liked about him.

"She's better than I was," said Declan.

"She's better than you are," said Ward, who had come up behind them.

"That's what I said."

"It's not what you said." Ward leaned on the rail. "You said was."

She went down to the river market on the second Saturday in March, in daylight, without the hat. Emeric went with her, two paces off, because she'd said one pace was hovering and three was spying.

Nobody stared. That was the first thing. A woman in a grey jacket with her hair in one long braid went along the stalls with a basket on her arm, and the stalls looked at the basket. At the onion stall the man weighed her a pound and a half and told her the price, and then he stopped with the scoop in his hand and looked at her face again, longer than a man looks at a customer, as if he'd heard a note in a room with no instrument in it. He couldn't have said what it was. Emeric could. She stood very still between sentences, stiller than a living woman stands, and the air round her was thick with mana that a man who sold onions wouldn't have known was mana, only that something was there. The man's eyes went to Emeric, and to the plain dark coat, and back to her, and he settled it the way Kaldo had learned to settle most things. High mage. Mana did odd things to people. Everyone knew that. He gave her a good onion off the top for nothing, to be on the safe side.

The second thing was at the fish steps.

An old woman was sitting on a crate there with her grandson's dinner in a cloth, a capital woman by her shawl, come down on the train to see the new house her grandson kept writing about. She looked up as Amara went by. Then she stood up off the crate, which took her a while, and put her hand over her mouth.

"Majesty," she said, behind the hand. "You were in the laundry passage. You came down for the boy's things yourself. You said nobody else got the grass out."

Amara stopped. Emeric watched her decide.

"People tell me I look like her," she said gently. "I'm told she was very fond of the laundry."

"She was." The old woman's eyes were wet. "She was. You've her hands."

"I've been told that too."

She went on to the end of the stalls, and bought bread, and didn't look back, and on the hill road home she walked faster than she had all month. Halfway up she said, without turning her head, "I did get the grass out. Nobody else could," and Emeric said he knew, and she said he couldn't possibly know, he'd been small, and he said he remembered now, and she stopped in the road for a moment and then went on.

The letters came in with March. Lights in all three holdings now, the last of them on the first of the month. Grayrock's rows at Standard 2, the prefab blocks up with showers in them, and the teams that put them up mostly holdings-born, which was the line in Dagny's letter she had underlined. From Emberfell, Rowena: the geothermal throat was lit and drawing, the print works running off it, and the school had opened with her name over the door, which she had not asked for and was not going to discuss.

Zale's notes came over from the island on the evening packet in his square careful hand, one line each, the way he wrote everything. Catch a quarter, 1st. Half, 8th. Three parts, 12th. And then, on the sixteenth: Full, 15th. Guest well. Seals back in the bay.

Emeric read it at the long table and passed it to Ferra, who wrote it in her book, in the column she had ruled for the window, and left the column beside it empty, the one headed Day.

Full meant it would fire again on the next big thing that came down the Pull. It also meant the water past the reef was open for as long as nothing came, with the Challenger in her berth under canvas and the guest on the Cradle's deck most afternoons with her feet in the wet, watching the bay mouth. He didn't say any of that, and Ferra didn't either. It was April's argument. It would keep three weeks. He wasn't sure he believed that, and from the way she closed the book, she wasn't.

Corbin's watcher's report came up with the others on the twentieth. Corbin had asked at the works' paper shop for a lantern a week early and been told they weren't selling till the Tuesday, and had gone back on the Tuesday and bought one. His mother's note had come to his door on the Thursday, as it did every week, from four streets away, with a copy of the weather letter she sent the castle, and he had read it on the step. The watcher put that in as well, because he put in everything.



The counting engine finished at twenty past six on the morning of Lantern Night, while Ferra was still on her first cup.

It lived in the long back room of the Motion Shop, where it had been built, because nobody had yet worked out how to move a thing that size without taking it to pieces, and Tess said she would sooner move the shop. Ferra had come down the hill at five in the dark with her book under her coat because she wanted to be there when it ended. It had been running since nine the night before. All night it had made the sound she had come to think of as its own: a dry close rattle like rain on slates, rack after rack of relays opening and closing in rows down the length of the room, and every so often the heavier clack of the reader as Ottoline fed it another brick of cards.

There were a great many cards. One for every household in the four baronies, punched over the winter from the enumerators' sheets by Ottoline and four clerks from Ferra's own office, whom Ottoline had trained and then, by stages, frightened. They stood in tin trays down the wall in the order they'd gone through. Ferra had watched the trays still to go grow shorter by the hour, the way you watch a kettle, with nothing to do about it but watch.

At a quarter past six the last brick went in. The rattle went on a while after, and changed, and then the printer at the end of the room woke and began to type.

Ferra stood over it. She had counted the barony the old way once, a long time ago, with every clerk she had and a ledger and six weeks of walking, and come out with a number she had never entirely trusted. This came out a line at a time on a paper strip in the engine's square blunt letters, each barony and its towns, the Port Town, the rail towns, the holdings, and at the foot of the strip, by themselves:

Inside the walls: 55,108.

Four baronies: 179,634.

She had a column for that too. It had a figure at the top of it in pencil, written years ago at the long table when Emeric had drawn a line up a page through the years and said about here, and she hadn't believed him and had written it in pencil so she could rub it out. She turned back to it. The pencil was very faint. 180,000, yr 16.

"Three hundred and sixty-six short," said Ottoline, reading over her shoulder.

"It said about."

"I know what it said."

Ferra added the barony lines in the margin of the strip in her own pencil, the way she would have added any clerk's, and they came to the bottom line. Then she wrote both figures in her book in ink, and the date, and the hour, and under them, because it was true and because she would want to see it there in ten years: Done before breakfast.

Tess came in at seven in a coat over her nightdress, with her stick, and read the strip without taking it out of Ferra's hand.

"Told you," she said.

"You told me Friday."

"I told you before breakfast. It's before breakfast." She looked down the room at her racks, which had gone quiet, and her face did something Ferra didn't often see it do. "It's never been this quiet in here," she said, and went to make the coffee.

On the way up the hill Ferra stopped at the rotunda. The mason's scaffold was empty at that hour, a tarpaulin over his tools. He'd reached the word heard two days ago, and had cut the h twice, the second time because he hadn't liked the first. Across the square the card bank's shutters were already up, and Vantic was on its steps in a fur collar with a lantern paper rolled under one arm and her ledger under the other, and called across that the House would be on the records hall steps tonight if the king's household didn't mind sharing them, and that she would be counting how many lanterns the bank's clerks bought on bank time. Ferra said she'd mind very much, and that she'd want the figure. Vantic laughed and went in. Keller's notice was pinned on the records hall door: the Monday hearings would sit at nine as usual, lanterns or none.

She found Ward at the magazine at eight, as arranged.

He had the key on its chain round his neck, where it lived, and opened the iron door and the inner door and lit the lamp, and stood back. The tins were on their own shelf at the back, above the rifles' plain rounds, in a row. Each was the size of a snuffbox, numbered on the lid in paint, and each held one round with a chip of stone in its nose that only Emeric could cut, and he wasn't cutting any more. Each was a choice somebody would one day have to make, standing somewhere, with a rifle.

Ferra opened them one at a time, looked, and closed them. She didn't touch the rounds. She knew the numbers; she had the issue column in her book with Ward's initials down it, and the tins that weren't there, and the night each had gone.

"Eight," she said.

"Eight in October," said Ward. "Eight at Christmas."

"Eight now." She took the wax and the little brass seal out of her coat pocket, and warmed the wax at the lamp, and sealed the shelf's box across its lid in two places with her mark and the date. "Why tonight, you're going to ask."

"I wasn't."

"Because the whole town will be in the square and nobody will be in here. And because next year I'd like to count eight." She wrote it, under the issue column: Eight. Sealed. Then, after a while, the other thing, the thing that had been said in the war the last time one of them went, by someone who'd meant it. There won't be a second.

Ward looked at the line, and at her, and locked the inner door.

When she got up to the castle Emeric was at the kitchen table with coffee he had made himself, which was worse than Tess's and better than last month's, and Imani was eating Ferra's toast.

"You went out at five," said Imani.

"I left a note."

"You left a column." Imani held up the scrap: two figures, ruled, one of them blank. "I filled it in for you."

Ferra read it. In the blank, in Imani's hand: Where's my wife.

"That's not a figure," said Ferra.

"It's the only one I wanted," said Imani, and finished the toast.



Tess's cell lit the bulb at three in the afternoon, in the Motion Shop's south window, with the whole shop pretending not to watch.

It was a grey-blue wafer about the size of a coaster, cut from one of the Forge's float crystals and dosed with things Tess had spent the winter adding to it by the grain and writing down. Two fine wires ran from it to a torch bulb on a block of wood. There was no stone anywhere in it. Emeric had asked, and Tess had made him look, and he had looked: no keystone, no grit, no dust, nothing in the whole of it that a dead zone could reach.

The sun came round the corner of the next roof and fell on it.

The filament went dull red, and then orange, and stayed there, a small hot thread in the glass that you would have missed in any room with a lamp lit. Dov, at the far bench, stopped grinding.

"That's it?" he said.

"That's it," said Tess.

"It's not very bright."

"It's sand, Dov. It's sand I lied to." She didn't take her eyes off the bulb. "Solar cell," she said to Emeric. "That's what you called it. Year twelve. I wrote it down."

Above the window, pinned beside the radar drawings she wasn't allowed to build yet, was the sheet for the seam valve. If Tess was right it was a relay with no moving parts; if she wasn't, it was a nice drawing. By dusk Lantern Square was full again from the records hall steps to the fountain, and the streets that opened into it were full back to the corners, and there was no smoke.

Emeric noticed it because he went looking for it. From the top step he could see over the low roofs of the lower wards to the river, and the river to the long sheds of the coal station on its far bank, with its two stacks standing up against the last of the light, cold. He had never seen them cold at dusk. Even in the year they'd stood down, there had always been somebody who lit a test fire. Along the town wall above the north gate a row of men sat on the parapet with their legs hanging over, in their caps, passing something down the row in a bottle: the reserve crew, who kept the station ready and hadn't lit it since autumn and were very proud of both. Past them the sky was clean down to the hills on every side. Wherever he looked, the four baronies weren't burning anything they had to see.

Beyond the north gate the shore line's lamps went away in a double string down to the bay, and at the end of them Saint Edwy's spire stood up white against the dusk, taller than anything in Kaldo but the dome's glass. Somebody out there had set lanterns floating on the long pools, so the church stood on light. Gideon Marl's young trees along its terraces had come into blossom that week for the first time, the whole double row of them, pink and white; they had gone into the ground the autumn of the wedding with their leaves already turning, and Marl had told anybody who asked that they would either flower in the spring or he would hear about it from the cardinal. From the top step, a mile and a half off, they were only a colour at the foot of the white, like a hem. Emeric looked at them for a while.

Imani came up the steps last, from the hospital, with her coat done up to the throat, and stood on his other side.

"Nineteen," she said.

He looked at her.

"Port and the Works. Bad chests. The whole winter." She was looking at the stacks too. She said nothing else about it, and he didn't ask her what it had been the first year. He remembered the first sheet. It had been two pages, both sides.

Then she put her hand in the pocket of his coat, beside his.

"That's not your pulse, by the way," she said. "In case you were wondering. I'm not taking it."

"You never were," said Ferra, on his left, without looking round.

"I was, the first time. A little." Imani's fingers found his and settled. "Not now."

Ferra had her book out, which she didn't usually on Lantern Night, because she'd been writing lantern counts from the paper shop in it all afternoon. Imani reached across Emeric with her free hand, took it from her, and put it in his other pocket. Ferra looked at the pocket. Then she put her own hand in after it, and left it there, and Emeric stood on the top step of the records hall with both his pockets full and the town in front of him and nothing whatever to do with his hands.

Below them the square went on filling. Ivo's shop on the corner, which was the picture house now and showed the train twice nightly to people who no longer ducked, had its doors shut for once and its owner out on the stones in his hat. Maren and Tamsy Brook had a trestle of hot pies against the bank's railings and a queue that went round the fountain, and Hester from the ward kitchen was at the head of it, buying for the night nurses, arguing about the gravy. A nickel-iron cab whined in at the top of the square with a load of Milford people and was turned back by a constable, and went off backwards, slowly, with everybody in it waving. The town's battery vans stood in a row in the Works' yard with their lamps off. Sorrel's glider was pegged on the castle roof under a sheet where no lantern could find it, because she'd made the town promise. Down at the fountain Doran, who was a cardinal now and still came to Kaldo's Lantern Night in his old brown coat, stood by the clerks' table to light the first candle, talking to Abloh about the price of wool. On the way down that evening Emeric had passed the Hollow Night stone at the foot of the lane, the one for the people with no graves, set in February and waiting for its first October, and somebody had already put a turnip lantern on it, unlit, and nobody had been the one to take it off.

The steps filled up round them. Sela beside Ward with her shawl over her head, and Saban beside her, with his hands behind him, not saying what he thought of the crowd. Waylon and Alina, down from the duchy on the afternoon train and standing a careful betrothed hand's width apart, which fooled nobody on the steps. Vantic and the House's people on the step below, as threatened. Ward at the top for a little, and then gone, because he had a suit to get into. Rylan with Finn beside him, and Wren already gone into the crowd. Tess in her chair at the end, with a wireless set on her knees in a wooden box and a pair of earphones round her neck, and Osric standing behind the chair. Armand wasn't there. He had gone up to the dome at four with a book and a lamp and said that somebody ought to sit with her, meaning the one on the mattress, and that he'd seen enough lanterns. Imani had done his yearly check in January, three years on from the stone, and written the same thing as the two years before it: No drift. He had asked her to read it to him twice, and then said that he'd always found the word drift insulting in a man of his age, and gone off pleased.

Bram wasn't there either. Bram was on the hill.



Danner held the lantern in his left hand.

He did it on purpose. He'd decided it on the walk down, and decided it again at the corner of the square when the crowd closed round him, and a third time at the fountain, where the table was covered in pens and candle ends and the clerks sat behind it writing names for people who couldn't. He could. He'd learned at the night school over the winter, three evenings a week in a room full of grown men making their letters, and he wrote his own names on his own lantern with his right hand, slowly, the tongue in the side of his mouth. Four names. Two lines each. The fourth company's, the ones who hadn't come out of the capital with him.

Then he blew on the ink and passed the lantern across to the left, and closed the hand on the wire loop at the top.

It closed. That was the thing people didn't understand when they asked, and they still asked: it did close. It did everything. It just did it like a hand on a man who'd been asleep on his arm. The fingers came in a beat after he told them and went where they went, and he'd learned to look at them when he wanted something done properly, which with the other hand he'd never done in his life. It was paler than the right, too, a sort of indoor colour, as if it had been kept in a drawer, and the hair on it had come in soft and fair when everything else on him was dark. In the cold it went white at the knuckles before the rest of him had noticed it was cold.

He'd been up at the ward that morning for the check, as every week. Imani had had him squeeze the brass grip with the needle on it, both hands, three times each, and written the numbers on his sheet without saying anything, and then said, "Better," which was what she said every week, and every week it was true, by a little.

"Still under the right," he'd said.

"It'll be under the right till midsummer. Then it won't." She'd written something else on the sheet, at the bottom, under his name, and he'd read it upside down because he could now, which was still new enough that he did it to everything. Rehab weekly as before. Grip to match by summer, not before. Don't let him carry coal. And under that, in the same hand, a new line: a name, with the word hand after it, and a date in April.

Abel Dunning.

He'd asked who that was. She'd told him a quarryman, and that it was his turn, and that it would be easier than an arm because there was more of the map left. He hadn't understood the last part and hadn't asked. He'd understood the first. Next on the sheet. Somebody else would lie in the clean bed at the end of the ward and eat liver twice a day and be told it squeaked.

The candle on the table was lit. Danner bent and took a light off it with his own candle end, the right hand, and put it up inside the lantern, and the paper went gold from the inside with the four names showing dark through it, and he straightened up with the whole thing in his left.

The new fingers felt the heat come down the wire before he did. They opened.

Not much. Enough. The loop slid, and the lantern tipped, and the candle flame leaned over inside it toward the paper. He felt it go and couldn't think how to stop it with that hand. The other hand came up from his side all on its own, the way it had been coming up for him his whole life, and he made it stop, an inch short.

He looked at the left. He told it, very clearly, the way Imani had taught him on the first day: shut.

It shut. The lantern swung once on the loop and hung straight, and the flame stood up again.

"Nearly," said somebody beside him.

It was the port lad who'd been writing at the table next to him, a tall one off the lighters, with one small lantern and one name on it that Danner hadn't tried to read. He was holding his own lantern up by its frame in both hands, carefully, like a dish.

"Nearly isn't dropped," said Danner.

"No," said the lad, and seemed to think about it. "Tolliver," he said.

"Danner."

They stood with their lanterns among all the others round the basin, waiting for the word to go. Across the fountain, a heavy, quiet man in a quarryman's coat stood holding his own lantern by its loop in a hand that wasn't one, Tess's work, the fingers in it curled just so; he held it steady as a bracket, and somebody in the crowd beside him called him Abel and asked after the quarry. Danner looked at the hand, and at his own, and at the man's face, which was patient and a little bored, like a man waiting for a tram.

He didn't go over. He would see him in April, in the ward. He'd tell him about the liver then.

The lamps round the square went down. Somebody up on the steps said something, and it went back through the crowd as a murmur, and then there was the push of air he'd been told about and hadn't believed, coming up off the stones round his boots, steady and not cold, as if the whole square had opened a door upward. The lanterns at the basin's edge began to lift on their own strings.

Tolliver let his go with both hands. It went straight up.

Danner held his a little longer. The wind had it already, pulling up on the loop, and the left hand held against the pull, white at the knuckles, and didn't open, because he was looking at it and telling it not to. He thought he could stand there all night if he had to. Then he thought that was a stupid thing to want, with four men in it waiting to go.

He told the hand to open. It took its beat, as it always did. Then it opened, all the fingers together, which they had never once done in two years, and the lantern went up out of it with the four names dark on its side, over the fountain and the clerks' table and the heads of the crowd, among all the others.

Danner put the hand in his coat pocket where it was warm. It was shaking a little. So was the other one, which had no excuse.



Harrold had been walking beside her for twenty minutes before he understood that he was waiting for her to stop.

She didn't. They had come out of the castle gate a little after six, the two of them, and down the hill road in the dusk with the town lit below them and the lanterns not yet up, and she had walked the way she did everything now, a little carefully, with the faint dry sound at her hips that she said was a woman with bad knees going upstairs. At the twenty paces past the dome's ground he had felt himself brace, and she'd gone on. At the gate he'd braced again. At the first corner of the hill line, where the tram came round leaning and righted itself, she had stopped, but only to watch the tram, and had said, "Oh, that's clever," and gone on, and he had walked after her feeling foolish.

Bram was somewhere fifty yards behind, with his hands in his pockets, out for the air. He had been out for the air since the castle gate.

She was in a long dark coat Abloh had made for the body and a hat with a brim, and gloves, and in the lanes, at night, she passed. Emeric had told him she would. He hadn't believed it. People knew him. Since his son's crowning he'd been the old king to Kaldo, which still made the knights laugh, because he looked younger than most of them, and on the hill road people said good evening to him and touched their caps and looked at the woman beside him and did not ask, because in Kaldo you didn't. A little girl on her father's shoulders stared down into the face under the hat brim all the way along one street. His wife smiled up at her, not well. The face still did a smile like someone carrying it in both hands. The girl considered it for a long time and then smiled back, hugely, and was carried off round the corner.

"She knew," said Amara.

"She didn't know anything."

"She knew something was a bit wrong and decided she didn't mind." She walked on. "That's all I want from the whole town, Harry. I'll take it from children first."

At the rotunda she stopped again, under the mason's scaffold, and read the Charter's first article as far as he'd got with it. The cut letters were deep and square and very clean, and they stopped halfway along a line, after heard, with the next word chalked on the stone ready for him in the morning.

"Who wrote it?" she said.

Imani had sent the note. Prudence had answered it with one line, which Harrold had seen: I'll bring the book and I'll bring my own pen, the board's pens are a disgrace.

She stood up when they came in. She looked at Amara for some time, and Amara let her.

Prudence turned the book round on the table, to the page with Imani's case on it and the line in the clerk's writing that Prudence had made him put in word for word: Name to be entered in the patient's own hand, when she has one. They'll not start the lanterns without the king and he's on the steps with his hands in his pockets."

Amara took her right glove off.

Harrold had seen the hand bare before, in the Motion Shop and in the dome, on the bench and in Tess's lap and turned over under Imani's lamp. Imani's brown, the creases over the knuckles where the cast had caught them, the nails a little pale at the moons the way hers had always been. It looked like skin. Under a lamp, very close, it was a shade too even, the way a good copy of a picture is a shade too clean, and that was all; and he only knew to look because he knew. She picked up the pen and held it, and he watched the fingers find their places on it one at a time, the first finger and then the thumb, the way Tess had taught her in February with a pencil, a drawing pin, a cup.

She wrote her name.

All of it, the long way, as it had been written on the day she married him, and it took her a long time. The letters weren't hers. They were careful and upright and a little large, like a child's copybook, where hers had always run uphill and been impossible to read. She knew it. She stopped once in the middle and looked at what she'd done, and he thought she might put the pen down. She didn't. She finished it and underlined it, which she had always done, and the line was straight, which it never had been.

Prudence turned the book round and read it. Harrold waited for her to read it out, because she read everything out; the town said she'd read out a will at a wedding if somebody handed her one. She didn't. She read it twice to herself, and blotted it, and closed the book on it.

"That's a good hand," she said.

"It isn't mine."

"It is now," said Prudence. "It's in the book."



They didn't go to the fountain. Amara said she'd been looked at enough for one evening and would like to do some looking, and so they went down the river steps instead, out of the square's far corner, to the water.

The river's edge was lined with ice. The school's mage masters had set the children to it that afternoon: cups of the river's own water drawn up and frozen into holders along the quay rail, one every few feet for half a mile, each with a lantern standing in it ready, and the children who had made them stood guard over their own with the ferocity of landlords. The ice was clear and blue where the quay lamps caught it. A boy of about eight told Harrold not to touch that one, it was his, and Harrold said he wouldn't dream of it.

They found a space at the rail past the end of the ice, by a mooring post, where the quay went dark and the river ran past black and quick with the lamps of the far bank in it. The coal station's stacks stood over there against what light was left. Behind them the square was a long noise.

She took her other glove off and put both of them in her coat pocket.

He had his left hand on the rail. He had been going to turn it over, the way he had turned it over for her at a hundred doorways when they were young, palm up, so she could put hers in it or not. He'd done it in the dome in year fourteen, for a stone fist the size of a loaf, and she'd turned the fist over and touched the back of his hand with one knuckle, very lightly, because it was the only part of it she trusted. He started to do it now.

She didn't wait. She took it from the side, the way you take a hand when you're walking somewhere together and not standing in a door, and her fingers came round his.

They came one at a time. He felt each of them arrive and then settle, the first and the second together and then the third, slower, and the smallest last of all, as if it had needed to be told twice. The thumb came over the back of his hand and lay there. There was a faint tick somewhere in her wrist, a drive taking up, and then nothing. The knit was cool from the air and then not cool, because his hand was warming it.

She held it. That was all she did.

It wasn't hard. It was about as hard as a hand is held by somebody who has been holding it for years and has stopped thinking about it. He could have taken his away without effort and she'd have let it go. He found he was holding his own very still, like a man with a bird on his finger, and made himself stop, and closed his fingers round hers, and she let him.

He didn't look at it. He looked at the river.

"Is that all right?" she said.

"Yes."

"You'd tell me."

"It's not enough," said Harrold, and his voice did something he had not given it leave to.

She laughed. Not much, but it was her laugh, the real one, and it came out of the made face as if the face had been waiting for it. "Tess says that's as hard as I'm allowed tonight," she said. "She was very firm. She said if I broke the old king's hand on Lantern Night she'd never hear the end of it from Bram."

"Bram's fifty yards back."

"Bram's forty. He's been getting closer since the steps." She didn't look round. "Leave him. He made the elbows."

Across the river, somewhere in the station sheds, a door opened and a man came out with a lantern of his own and walked along the bank under the cold stacks to the water's edge and stood there, a long way off, a small light by itself. The square behind them went quiet in the way a crowd does when the lamps begin to go down, and the quay lamps went down too, one after another, from somebody's board at the substation, until there was only the river and the ice and the lanterns waiting in it.

Up on the hill, the dome had one lamp in it. Armand's. Harrold could see it from here, a small steady yellow under the great glass, beside the tree, where the other one of her lay on the old mattress with an old man reading beside her. He knew she was looking at it too. Neither of them said anything about it.

Her thumb moved once on the back of his hand. Then it was still again.

"There," she said, as if she'd finished putting something away in a drawer, and they stood like that and waited for the lanterns.



They went up on a held wind.

Emeric felt the mages take it before he saw anything: a dozen of them along the river steps and at the four corners of the square, the school's mage masters and two of their best children, drawing the still air up off the stones into one slow standing column and holding it there. It wasn't strong. It needed only to be steady. The first lanterns went up from the fountain into it and rose straight as if they'd been strung on a line, and the square made the sound it made every year, the long breath out, and then they were all going, from the basin and the quay and the ice along the river and the doorsteps and the windows, hundreds of them and then more, gold, going up into the dark over the roofs.

Some of them strayed. They always had. One drifted at the column's edge toward the records hall's roof, and something came down out of the dark above the square and touched it, lightly, the way you'd touch a child's back to start it up a stair, and it righted and went on up. Ward, in the float suit, with its little lamp at his belt, a dark shape turning slowly over the town's lanterns with his arms out. He'd wanted to do it since he'd first had the suit on. He hadn't said so. He hadn't needed to; Saban had said it for him, at the long table, in front of everyone, and Ward had said nothing, and then turned up at the fitting a day early.

Every so often Ward reached and turned another straggler with a touch of gravity off the roofs, and a cheer went up from whatever street it had been about to land on.

The lanterns climbed. At the height of the castle's towers they went into the Shell.

It was lit tonight over the north quarter only, one face of it, held by the first two of the Answer Towers' relay posts on the wall, the towers themselves not yet built, and the core under the castle. Nobody in the square could see it. Emeric could feel it, the way he could feel a stone wall in the dark with his hand an inch off it, a great quiet surface standing in the air over the town and paying attention. The lanterns went into it at the pace of a walking man. It read each one, the warmth and the weight and the nothing of them, and let it through, because that was what it did with slow things, and they came out above it without a waver and went on climbing. He watched it do that a few hundred times. Then he stopped watching it, because it was boring, which was what he had wanted from it all along.

Above it all, very high, where the Band ran, there was a steady white light that wasn't a star. The K Meridian's station, the whole of it built now, with a crew of its own and the growing racks in the lab. You could see it on any clear night from the square, if you knew where to look. Tonight half the square did, because the News had said that something would go up from it.

"There," said Tess, at the end of the step.

She had the earphones on, and her pencil going. Emeric couldn't hear it from where he stood, but he didn't need to; he had built half of it in his head the summer before and handed it to her, and she had built all of it on her bench. A sealed canister with a held stone in it, a barometer and a thermometer and a dial for the charge, and a clockwork that walked a contact across them in turn and tapped what it found out over the radio in Tess's code. A radiosonde, the big sister of the island probe. They had dropped it off the station's rail at seven, and it was going up past the Band on its stone, and talking.

Tess wrote. Osric held the lamp for her. The pencil went along the paper in short strokes, and every few minutes she said a number under her breath, and Emeric didn't ask what any of them were, because he would read the tape tomorrow with her and Armand, all of it, and tonight he wanted to stand on the steps.

The lanterns thinned. The last of them went up off the river, where the ice was. He saw them go up from the far end of the quay, past the place where the quay lamps were dark and two people stood at the rail by a mooring post, too far off to make out. He knew which two they were anyway. They were standing close, and they didn't move, and neither of them was holding a lantern.

Ferra's hand was still in his pocket with her book. Imani's was still in the other, round his. Neither of them had taken them out for the lanterns, and nobody on the steps had said anything about it, except Vantic, who had turned round on the step below, looked at all three of them, and turned back again.

The radiosonde talked until twenty to one. Tess stayed with it, on the steps, under a rug Osric brought, long after the square had gone home, and Emeric stayed with her, because he'd said he would. At twenty to one she lifted the earphones off one ear, then the other, and held them out in front of her and listened to nothing coming out of them.

"Stopped," she said, and wrote the time.
