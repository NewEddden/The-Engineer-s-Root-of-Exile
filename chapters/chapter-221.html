Chapter 221 - Windows

Ward walked the island wall on the last night the garrison slept on the island, because he had walked it on the first.

That was not the reason. He knew it was not the reason while he told it to himself, going up the gatehouse stair in the dark with his hand on the cold rope. The reason was that he had told the prince the passage a week ago, three miles up in the dark over the sea, and he had been walking ever since. He walked the practice yard in the mornings and the wall of the new city in the afternoons and the road between them in the evenings, and he did not sleep much, and he had not been able to stop. It was a thing he had done after the channel too, the first winter, until the walking wore it down. He had not expected it to come back. He had thought, in the band, that saying it would put it down. It had not put it down. It had only moved it from the place where he kept it to a place where he could see it.

So he walked. And tonight the garrison went across the bridge for good, and there was a wall to walk, and he walked it.

He came out on the wall walk above the Western Gate and turned north along it, the way he had turned the first time.

It had been a long time ago. He did not remember how long without counting and he did not count. There had been a town inside this wall then that was too full for itself. The rooms behind the market had been three families deep and the lean-tos on the foundry bank four, and the prince had come to him in the evening and asked him to walk the wall and look, and he had walked it and looked down into every lane and counted the lights in the windows. Three to a house. Four to a house. A house on Tanner's Row with a light in every window on every floor and shadows moving across all of them. He had come down off the wall and told the prince what he had counted, and the prince had not liked the number either, and the city on the flat had come out of that, somewhere, a long time after.

He counted now.

The island lay below him in the dark on his right hand as he walked, lane after lane going down from the wall toward the market and the water, the roofs black against a sky that was only a little less black. It had been a clear cold day and it was a clear cold night, the first hard frost of the winter, and the stars were out over the lake, and he could see his breath. He walked slowly, the way he walked a wall on watch, and looked down into every lane he passed, and counted the windows with a light in them.

Tanner's Row was dark. Every window. There was a lead seal on every door, he knew without seeing it, with the house's number stamped in it, and a clean swept step. The Asters and the Dunnings and the young couple under the roof were four miles away in the new quarter, in three houses side by side, and the tall thin house with the crack down its front stood black against the stars with nothing in it but a broken chair and a picture of a ship. He had heard that from Ilsa. He had also heard about the drawer.

Cooper's Row, dark. Salt Lane, one: old Pardew the thatcher, who would not live in a house nobody built, with a candle in his kitchen and his shadow going across it. The lanes behind the market, dark, one after another, all the way down. The weigh house, dark; Ilsa had gone to the palace in the autumn and her room over it was on Orrin's list for the school's clerks, and nobody had moved in yet.

The ward, lit. It was always lit. The ward stayed open until the last family on the island had gone or died in its bed; the prince had written that in his own hand, in the winter, and signed it, and the ward's night lamp burned behind its long windows as it had burned every night since there had been a ward, with one of Imani's people sitting under it with a book, for the dozen households that were left and the old ones in the end room who could not be moved.

The market street, lit, and that was the strangest thing.

The gas lamps were burning. All of them, on their iron posts down both sides of the market street from the cross to the water, the way they had burned since the year the prince had the gas brought over from the works. Every one of them lit. And nobody under them. The stalls were gone to the new square, all but the little Thursday market that set up by the cross for the families that stayed, and tonight was not Thursday, and the cross stood in the middle of the empty street in a ring of gaslight with nobody at its foot, and the lamps went down the street in two lines, bright, and the cobbles between them shone with the frost, and there was not a soul on them. The promise said the lamps on the market street stayed lit until the last family went. So they were lit. They burned for a street nobody walked, all night, every night, and Ferra paid the gas works for every hour of it and wrote it down.

He stood on the wall and looked at that for a while.

Then he walked on. The bakehouse, lit, at the bottom of the market: Holm the baker, who had four households on his round now instead of four hundred, and whom the barony paid to stay open, and who baked every night anyway, because a baker bakes, and gave away what he could not sell to the ward. The Aggetts', by the water steps, one window, low, where the old woman sat up late. And Tam Brewer's, three doors along, one window, and the two benches by the two doors with the frost on them.

He counted to the end of the wall and back, and it came to eleven. Eleven windows, on the whole island. He had counted over a thousand, the first time.

The watch was at the gate when he came down.

Two men. Dunn and Pollard, off the old garrison, men who had stood on this gate in the war and been too old for the levy since. They had a brazier in the gate arch and their hands over it, and they came to attention when they saw who it was, and he told them to stand easy, and they stood easy and put their hands back over the coals.

"Last night, sir," said Pollard.

"Last night."

"They've all gone over? The garrison?"

"The last company went at dusk. Their kit went in the afternoon. The barracks are warm." Ward held his own hands to the coals. "There's two of you on this gate from tonight, and a man to relieve each of you, and the ward's bell if you want help, and a runner to the new city. That's the island's watch."

Pollard nodded. He did not say anything for a while. Dunn was looking at the fire.

"Is it worth it, sir?" Dunn said.

Ward looked at him.

"I don't mean anything by it." Dunn did not look up. "I'll stand it. I stood it in the war. I'll stand it as long as I'm told. Only there's eleven houses on the island now, and two men on the gate, and the lamps lit on the market street for nobody, and the baker baking for four doors. And the ward. It's a lot, sir. For eleven houses." He moved his hands over the coals. "I only wondered."

Ward thought about it. He thought about it properly, because it was a fair question and Dunn had asked it honestly, and a man who asked an honest question on a cold night was owed an answer that had been thought about.

"The prince wrote it down," he said at last. "In his own hand, in the winter. The well, and the lamps, and the watch, and the ward. Until the last family's gone or died in its bed. He didn't write until there's too few to be worth it. He didn't write a number." He held his hands to the fire. "A promise isn't a thing you size to the crowd. If it was, it'd be a bargain. The eleven houses are the promise. The ones who went are the reason it was made. The ones who stayed are the only ones who can tell whether it was kept."

Dunn looked at the fire for a while longer.

"Yes, sir," he said.

Ward took the gate's key off his belt, the big iron one that had hung on him since the year the prince came, and held it out. Pollard took it. He looked at it in his hand in the firelight as if he had never seen it before, though he had seen it every day for ten years.

"Your gate," said Ward.

"Sir."

He went out under the arch, and down the gate steps, and across the causeway to the head of the bridge, and onto it.

Halfway across the bridge, his hand went to his wrist.

It went there on its own. It had gone there every night for years, the way a man's hand goes to his purse at a crowded fair, and he had stopped noticing it long ago. On his left wrist, under the cuff, there was a band of iron with a stone set in it, one of the six the prince had made for the table, so many winters back that he had forgotten what his wrist had felt like without it. It did nothing that he could feel. It had done nothing he could feel for years. The prince had said it would put something into him slowly, the way the band put something into the crew of the ship, and he had worn it and felt nothing, and gone on wearing it because he had been asked to, and because Bram had gone into his own room with one, and then Ferra, and a man did not take off a thing the table wore.

He put his hand on it on the bridge in the dark, with the frost on the parapet and the lake black on both sides and the lights of the new city a long low scatter four miles ahead of him across the flat, and he was somewhere else.

It was a room.

He knew at once that it was not the prince's. He had been in the prince's. The prince had taken him in, years ago, by the hand, and he had stood in it for a minute among the prince's shelves and come out and not liked it, and gone in again when he was asked to. That room was the prince's. This one was not.

This was a guardroom.

It was the guardroom at the foot of the east tower in the old king's house, the first one he had ever stood a watch in, with a bench along the wall and a rack for the pikes and a brazier and a slit window with the dark outside it. He knew every stone of it. He had not thought about it in thirty years. And there were shelves in it that had never been in the real one, rough plank shelves along the wall where the pike rack should be, and there were things on the shelves.

He did not move. He stood in the middle of his own guardroom, which he had never been in, and looked.

On the nearest shelf, the passage. He knew it before he looked at it properly. The long passage on the east side, with the study door shut, and a woman in grey wool against the wall, and a big man in a good coat with his hands on her, and the far end of the passage getting nearer at a run. It was exactly as he had told it a week ago in the band. Not a word more or less. It sat on the shelf the way a thing sits that has just been taken down and looked at and put back.

Beside it, the channel.

He had not told that to anybody. He had told the king, twenty years ago, in the mews, the way the king took reports, and never again. It was on the shelf beside the passage, the whole of it: the carriages in the road below the pass, and the hooded men coming down both slopes, far more of them than there should have been, and the captain at the narrow place with his back to the rock, holding it, holding it. And the queen with her hands up and light coming off them. And the boy. A boy of seven in the road with his face white, and then in Ward's arms, and the weight of him, and the road going up behind them into the dark, and the stones in Ward's armour going hot against his ribs as he ran, and the running.

And in the middle of it, as he looked, the other thing.

He had felt it that day, at the channel, and never once in twenty years had a word for it. He had put it in a report once, in the prince's cellar at Wayford, when the prince's sense had gone out of him like a door kicked open and something had come off him that filled the cellar, and Imani had asked Ward what he had felt, and he had said in my teeth, because that was the only place he had felt it. It had been the same in the channel. A pressure. Not a sound, not a light, not a thing a man could point at. A pressure in the teeth and the bones of the jaw, from one side of the road, from up the slope in the hooded men, strong, pressing, like a hand on a door from the other side.

In the room it had a shape.

It sat in the memory of the channel like a stone in a stream, and he could see it: not with his eyes, with whatever he was seeing with in here. It had an edge, and a weight, and a way of lying in the air of the memory that was its own and nothing else's, the way a man's hand is his own and a stranger could tell it from any other hand on a page. He could not read it. He did not know what it was. He looked at it and it was a shape he had never been taught the letters of. But it was there, on his shelf, with an edge, and he had felt its edge in his teeth twenty years ago, and he would know it if he felt it again.

He came out fast.

He came out the way a man comes up out of cold water, all at once, gasping, and he was on the bridge in the dark with his hand clamped round his wrist so hard the band had cut into the skin, and the frost on the parapet under his other hand, and the lake black on both sides. He was shaking. He took his hand off his wrist and put both hands flat on the parapet and stood there, and looked at the lights of the new city, and waited for his hands to stop.

It took a long time. He was a patient man. He waited.

When they were still he straightened up, and walked on to the end of the bridge, and at the end of it, on the flat, he stopped and turned round and looked back.

The island lay across the water behind him, a long black shape on the black lake with the wall round it, and the market street's gas lamps in their two bright lines, and the ward's long windows, and the rest of it dark. He counted the windows from here, the way he had counted them from the wall an hour ago, from the bakehouse at the bottom of the market to Tam Brewer's by the water steps.

Twelve.

He counted again. Twelve. There was a light on Dock Street that had not been there when he walked the wall. A low one, in a front window, near the end of the street, in a house that had had a seal on its door since the spring.

He knew whose it was. Ilsa had told him, at the palace, a fortnight ago, when she was putting her books on the shelves in her new room: the widow Eames, who could not sleep where she could not hear the lake. Her son was bringing her home after the first frost, when the harvest was in and the rail let him go, with the cart. It was the first frost tonight. He had come.

Ward stood at the end of the bridge in the cold and looked at the light on Dock Street for a long time. Then he turned round and walked the four miles to the new city, and the barracks, and a bed he had never slept in, and slept in it, all night, for the first time in a week.

The prince was at the door of the long room in the morning, early, coming out as Ward came up the stair, with a sheaf of Ferra's papers under his arm.

Ward stopped on the top step.

"I was in mine," he said.

The prince looked at him. He looked at him for a long moment, at his face and then at his left wrist and then at his face again, and something happened in his own face that Ward had not seen there before and could not have named. Then it was gone, and it was only the prince, with Ferra's papers, at a door, in the morning.

"Third," the prince said. "Bram, Ferra, you."

He did not ask what was on the shelves. Ward had known he would not. He would have been ready to say nothing if asked, and he found, standing on the top step, that he was glad not to have to be.

The prince stood aside to let him through the door. Ward went through it, and to his chair by the door, and sat down in it, and the day began.
