Chapter 221 - Six Articles

The summons came on the twenty-sixth of February, from the chancery, in the gazette and by the post on the same day, so that nobody in the kingdom could say they had not seen it: the Lieutenant, in the king's name, would hear the matters between the house of Aldane and the house of Kaldo in the chancery's long hall on the ninth of March, and the parties were to attend in person or by their advocates.

"In person," said Ferra, at the long table, before anybody else could say it. She had the summons flat under her hand. "He'll send Serle. You'll go yourself."

"You're not coming."

"No." She did not look up. "The capital's going to see us together once. I'd as soon it was a day you'd want to remember." She turned the summons over and ruled a line under the date on the back of it, for no reason he could see. "And somebody has to be here with the book while the Holder's in a room with his brother and his brother's uncle. In case the book's wanted."

On the slip on the morning of the second of March, with the ship's ladder down and Ward already aboard and Saban's hat visible in the hatch, she gave him nothing at all. No paper, no list. She straightened his collar, which did not need it, and stepped back.

"A letter a day," she said.

"You asked that in November."

"I'm not asking. I'm telling you what you're getting." She put her hands in her coat pockets. "One a day. By the ship to the clearing and the post from Wayford. They'll be a few days behind you all the way up, and one every day you're there." She considered him. "You'll know where I am at any hour. That's all a letter's for."



The ship put them down in the beech woods past the twelfth milestone south of Wayford, and they took post horses from Aster's posting house and rode north up the capital road, and it took them three days, which was what it had taken him the first time, nine years ago in August, the other way.

The road had not changed. The posting houses had not changed. The ostlers had, a little: at every one of them a man came out to the horses and looked at Emeric's face and his eyes, and then at Ward, and then went and got the best horses in the stable without being asked, and at one of them, at noon on the second day, an old man in a leather apron stood in the stable door the whole time they were changing and did not say anything, and as they rode out took off his cap.

"He's heard," said Saban, behind them.

"Heard what?"

"One of the things." Saban settled his hat. "There's a choice of them now."

They came into the capital at dusk on the fifth, by the south gate, with the lamps coming on.

He had forgotten the sky. Not forgotten it; put it away. Over the inner rings the sky-ships were coming down for the night toward the landing tiers, five or six of them strung out against the last of the light, small hulls no longer than a market barge, settling in their small corrections with a man in each of them pouring the last of his day into the stone. He watched one come down the whole way, from the moment it turned toward the tier to the moment the hull touched and the lamp at its bow went out, and he knew to the breath when the man inside it stopped pouring, because the hull dropped the last foot onto its cradle with a bump he could see from the street.

"Somebody's tired," said Saban.

"They're all tired. Every one of them, every night." Emeric looked away from it. "That's what they're for."

Saban's man in the capital kept a house on the drapers' street, three storeys and a shop, with a back room over the yard that had a bed in it and a table and a window with a grating, and he gave it up to the Holder of Kaldo without being asked and went to sleep at his sister's. Ward walked the house before Emeric went into it, every room, the yard and the stair and the roof, and then sat down on a chair inside the back room's door and did not move from it all night.

The first letter came on the evening of the sixth.



It was a single sheet, folded twice and sealed with plain wax, and the post boy brought it to the shop door and Saban's man's apprentice brought it up the stair, and Emeric broke it at the table under the window with Ward on his chair and the drapers' street going quiet below.

2 March. Evening. The counting room.

Linus came up at noon with his level and his staff and Ottoline's cards and said he means to walk every lane in the town and level every one of them at one in a thousand, for the plan you've not drawn yet, and wanted to know if he might. I said he'd not need your leave to walk a lane. He said he would for the cellars. I said he could have my leave for the cellars and you could argue it when you were home. He started at the north gate at one. By six he had the first lane and two cellars and a dog bite. Imani has seen to the dog bite.

The roller's boiler is lit. Tess says the ground's soft enough at the works end and they've begun again on the road, our side, toward the border and no further, as you wrote. Rylan says he'll ride on it the first day it's dry and have the Holder's leave afterwards.

Imani says you're to eat. I've told her I'd put it in.

The book's in order. F.

He read it twice. Then he folded it along her folds and put it inside his coat, and went to bed, and did not sleep particularly well, and the second letter came the next evening.



The chancery's long hall had windows set high in its walls so that the men inside it could see the sky and nothing else, and a floor of black and white stone like a board, and at the far end of it a dais with a canopy and a chair under the canopy that was the king's, and nobody was in the king's chair.

His Majesty did not sit on the ninth. The chancery's usher said so, before the hall was opened, to the two parties and their people standing on the board: by the old custom of the hall, the king did not sit in judgment on a matter between two houses that were both his own, one by blood and one by marriage, and the Lieutenant sat for him with the king's leave. Ward, at Emeric's shoulder, made a very small sound that might have been a breath going out.

"Both his own," said Saban, under his voice. "And the Lieutenant is the one man in the room who is both at once."

Corbin came in at ten by the door behind the dais.

Emeric had not seen him in nine years, since the banquet hall in this city where Corbin had sat at the king's right hand and smiled into his cup while Harrold told the court what his third son was. He was heavier now, and held himself better, and he had their father's height and their father's eyes, gold, and none of their father's stillness; and he sat down not in the king's chair but in a lower chair beside it that had been carried in for him, and arranged the folds of his coat over his knees, and looked down the hall at the two parties with an expression of patient, attentive, slightly regretful interest, as a man looks at a dispute between his tenants.

His eyes went over Emeric once. They did not stop.

"The court will hear His Grace of Aldane," said Corbin, "by his advocate."



Aldane was not in the hall either. His advocate was. Serle was a tall old man with silver hair worn long in the old fashion and a voice that had been built for this hall over forty years, so that he hardly seemed to raise it and it went to every wall; and he stood at the barrier with a paper in his hand that he did not look at once, and read the articles of His Grace of Aldane against the house of Kaldo.

There were six.

That the house of Kaldo, by its soldiers, had struck first at Harrow Bridge on the third of November, upon the servants of the house of Lisle at their lord's lawful toll, and slain a man of fifty and a boy of fifteen.

That the house of Kaldo, having accepted the crown's mediation in writing, had marched its levy under arms while the mediation was yet pending, in contempt of the crown's peace.

That on the twenty-sixth of November the house of Kaldo had set commoners of its levy to kill knights of the realm with iron tubes, contrary to every custom of arms, so that a hundred and eighty-eight knights of good family lay dead in a morning at the hands of men who had never held a lance.

That on the same day a thing had flown over His Grace's host, of no kind ever seen in the realm, to the terror of his men, and had hung over the field throughout.

That the Holder of Kaldo was no man, but a thing whose demons came when it called them; that a sword had come down off a peer's wall in his own hall at its word, with no glyph and no hand; that men in black had fallen out of the night sky upon a peer's road and upon the house of Staple at Wayford; and that a thing which could do so could not be held to the law of men.

And that the house of Kaldo had hanged five men of the north at Wayford in August without a crown judge, by the Holder's own order, and so put out the north's lights, and begun the whole.

Serle let the last of it go out to the walls and come back. Then he folded the paper, which he had not read from, and laid it on the barrier.

"His Grace asks the crown's peace," he said, "and the crown's protection, for the north, from that."

He did not point. He did not need to. Two hundred faces along the benches on both sides of the hall turned and looked down the board at the Holder of Kaldo, standing with his hands at his sides and his knight at his shoulder, and Emeric felt them looking; and under the looking, in his sense, which he had let out thin across the hall from habit, something else: a long low tightening of notes along the benches, men's notes, close and wary and drawn in, as notes drew in at the edge of a fight. Not anger. He had felt a great deal of anger in rooms. This was the other thing.

They were afraid of him.

"The court will hear the house of Kaldo," said Corbin, "by its advocate."

"By its Holder," said Emeric.

There was a small stir along the benches. Corbin's eyebrows went up a fraction, politely.

"As the Holder wishes."



He answered them in order, and briefly, because Ferra had taught him that a long answer to a short charge sounds like guilt.

The first. The house of Kaldo did not know who had struck first at Harrow Bridge, and had said so, in writing, to the house of Lisle, on the night of the third of November, by a rider under a white flag, before anybody had died of anything but that bridge. He gave the clerk of the hall a copy of the letter. He gave him also the posting house's own note of the hour Lisle's steward came for the two bodies: two in the morning, washed, laid out, under a lantern. Then he gave him three sheets of a counting house's ledger from the drapers' street in this city, copied and sworn: bills for oats bought in the north in September by His Grace's quartermasters and sent south to the capital to be paid, for two thousand men and nearly a thousand horse, from the middle of October. Three weeks before the bridge.

The clerk took them. Along the benches somebody said something under his breath, and stopped.

The second. The house of Kaldo had accepted the crown's mediation without conditions, in writing, on the day it read the offer, and in the gazette in the first week of November, and had said in the same letter that it would keep the peace of the road and pay Lord Lisle's toll in full under protest until the crown's officer came. It had paid the toll. It had receipts for every Copper. The crown's officer had not come. His Grace had declared war on the eleventh. The house of Kaldo had then marched its levy to the stream ground north of Harrow Bridge, which was in the barony of Wayford, which was Kaldo's, and dug in on its own ground behind an earthwork, and had not crossed Lord Lisle's line or His Grace's at any time, before the battle or after it, except under a white flag with a cure.

The third. There was no law of the realm that named the arms a levy might carry to defend its own field. The crown's own law of the levy made every man of a barony liable to stand for it. The men at the stream ground had stood, on their own ground, behind their own bank, and had not gone over it. The hundred and eighty-eight had ridden at them.

He stopped there for the space of a breath, because the hall had gone very quiet, and then went on.

The fourth. The house of Kaldo kept a ship that flew to Wayford on a public run, and every posting house on the capital road knew it. On the twenty-sixth of November she had hung far to the south of the field, over Kaldo's own road, under a written order that she was not to come nearer for any reason. She had done nothing. She had dropped nothing. That a thing had hung in the air was not, so far as the house of Kaldo knew, an article.

The fifth he answered in one sentence.

"I'm standing here," said Emeric. "His Grace may have me examined by anyone he likes."

Nobody laughed. He had not expected them to. On the benches to his left an old lord in a fur collar looked at him with an expression of perfect, unmoving dislike, and his note under it was a small hard knot of fright, and both were true at once.

The sixth. The five men hanged at Wayford in August had been hanged by his order, not by writ, and he had said so in his own words to the crown's magistrate within a week, and the whole of it, with the names of the dead and the evidence on which he had acted, had lain in the crown's file since September. It was before the crown already. It was not a matter between the house of Aldane and the house of Kaldo. It was a matter between the house of Kaldo and the crown, and the house of Kaldo would answer it there, whenever it was asked.

He stepped back from the barrier.

"The court thanks the Holder," said Corbin, pleasantly, "for his brevity." He looked down at his own hands. "And for his patience with the crown's mediation, which the court regrets was not sooner." He looked up. "The crown's magistrate will give his finding in law on the eleventh. The court rises."



Two letters came that evening, because the post from Wayford had been held a day by a lame horse at a posting house, and the apprentice brought them up together.

4 March. The coke ovens are up to the arch on the first two. Haddon says he'll have the tar off them by May if Bram stops telling him how. Bram says he's not telling him anything. I was there. He was.

Tess dipped the first length of pipe in the zinc bath today, in front of the works, and it came out looking like frost on a window. She made them all put their hands on it. She says it'll not rust in a man's lifetime. I've entered it as a guess.

The book's in order. F.

And the other.

5 March. Linus has done the lower ward, every lane, and wants to do the dock quarter next, and the dock quarter wants to know why a man with a stick and a brass pot is looking in its cellars. I've sent Wenna Hallam down with him. They've stopped asking.

A correction, under the second of March. I wrote that Linus is levelling at one in a thousand. He says it's one in a thousand for the plan and one in five hundred where the lanes are steep, and he'd like it known. It's known.

Come home when you're done. F.

Emeric read the correction three times. Ward, on his chair by the door, watched him do it and said nothing at all.



Keller sent for him on the tenth.

Not to the hall. To his own room in the chancery's north range, up two stairs, at the end of a corridor of shut doors: the same room. Emeric knew it before he reached the door, by the turn of the stair and the smell of the corridor, old paper and lamp oil and stone that never dried. Nine years ago, in August, he had stood in it with Ferra and Ward and a box of Wexler's books, and Keller had sent a baron to his death by the crown's order and told Emeric to his face that the judgment was the crown's and not his. The windows were still set too high to see out of.

Keller was at the table. He had not changed either, as far as Emeric could see; a dry small man in black with his hands folded on a closed file, who looked up when Ward shut the door and did not get up.

"Holder."

"Magistrate."

"Sit, if you wish." Keller did not wait to see whether he did. "I give my finding in the hall tomorrow. It will rebut each of His Grace's articles in law. It will make no difference to anybody in that hall, who decided what they thought of the matter when they read the count of the dead." He unfolded his hands and put one of them flat on the file. "I have a question that is not for the hall."

Emeric waited.

"His Grace has filed six articles," said Keller. "He has a seventh grievance against the house of Kaldo that is older and larger than any of them, and that the whole north has heard him speak of for years. The barony of Grayrock was taken from Renner by attainder and given to you, and with it a stone working that His Grace has said at every table in the north was improperly surveyed and improperly granted." He looked at Emeric without any expression at all. "It is not in his file. Neither is the matter of the crystals he bought in the autumn, which I have from the same counting house as your oat bills. A man does not leave his best charge out of a file by oversight. Not with Serle drawing it." He waited a breath. "Why?"

Emeric had wondered, on the road north, whether Keller would see it. He had thought he would. He had brought it for that.

He took the folded paper out of the inside of his coat, where it had been since Kaldo, and put it on the table in front of the magistrate.

It was two papers, folded together, out of Renner's box. The first was a commission on the heavy paper the chancery used, with a red seal at its foot cracked across by the years, naming three surveyors of the crown's own school and setting them to measure a hill in Grayrock, privately, at the commissioner's cost, with their findings to go to the commissioner and nobody else. The second was the survey: the east hill in section, in a trained hand, the old working and the drowned shaft, and below the shaft a long shape shaded grey and drawn like nothing in any mining book, with three words beside it in the crown surveyors' shorthand. Gravity. Body. Whole. The commission's date was the old date, the one Ferra had found under every crooked page of Wexler's books in the first summer. The seal on it was Aldane's.

Keller read it. He read it twice, slowly, the second time with one finger moving down the margin, and when he reached the foot he lifted the finger and held it there over the name for a breath, without touching the paper.

Then he folded it up again along its folds and pushed it back across the table.

"Keep it," he said. "In a dry place."

"Not the crown's file?"

"The crown's file will be read," said Keller, "by whom it is not mine to say." He folded his hands again on his own file. "A thing in a file can be lost. A thing in a man's coat can be produced when the court is one that will hear it." He looked at the high window. "That is not this court. You know it. That is why you showed it to me in this room and not in that hall."

"Yes."

"Then we are agreed." Keller unfolded his hands and drew a fresh sheet toward him, and dipped his pen. "Good day, Holder."

Emeric was at the door, with Ward's hand on the latch, when Keller spoke again without looking up.

"The patent of the duchy," he said, "has lain unsealed in the chancery since the grant of the baronies. I have read the statute this week. It does not give the chancery a discretion." He wrote a line. "I shall say so tomorrow, after my finding, if the Lieutenant does not."



Keller's finding took him a quarter of an hour on the eleventh and he did not raise his voice once.

He found that there was no evidence on which any court could say who had struck first at Harrow Bridge, and that the war had been declared by His Grace eight days afterward with a host mustered three weeks before it, and that the court might draw from those dates what it saw fit. He found that the house of Kaldo had kept the terms of its acceptance of the crown's mediation, and had fought on its own ground. He found no law of the realm that named the arms of a levy. He found that a ship hanging in the air over its own road was not a cause of action known to the law. He found that this court did not try demons, and that the Holder of Kaldo had stood at the barrier for the whole of the ninth in plain sight of it. He found that the hangings at Wayford were in the crown's file, before the crown's magistrate, which was himself, and that they were not a matter between these parties, and that he would deal with them in their place.

Then he sat down, and folded his hands.

It made no difference, as he had said it would. Emeric felt it make no difference. The notes along the benches did not ease. They had come into the hall frightened of a man who had killed a hundred and eighty-eight knights in a morning with farmers, and they were still frightened of him when Keller sat down, and they had been told in law that he had done nothing wrong, which made it worse.

The settlement was read by the chancery's clerk at noon, under the Lieutenant's hand and seal, in the king's name.

The toll at Harrow Bridge was void. The charter of the house of Lisle gave a toll for the keeping of the bridge at Harrow in repair, and the bridge at Harrow was not the bridge that stood now, which the crown had built and the barony of Wayford kept; and the toll could not in any case be laid upon one barony's goods and not another's on the king's highway. The bar was to come off the road within the month. The relay post on Lord Lisle's land was to be opened at once, on the terms of its written lease, and kept open.

Damages were released both ways. Nobody owed anybody anything for the dead. The ransoms of the knights taken at the stream ground were to be paid on the old terms, by the custom of arms.

The house of Kaldo was not to keep more than three hundred men under arms in time of peace, in all its baronies together.

And powder, which had not been known in the realm in any quantity before the year past, was from this day a thing of the crown's: to be made, kept and carried only under the crown's licence, by weight, at the crown's discretion, with the crown's inspector to see the stores at any hour. The house of Kaldo, which had petitioned for it in its answer, was granted a licence for the powder of its mines and its works, by weight a year, the figure to be set in the instrument; and was to seal whatever powder it held for arms in its armoury, under the inspector's seal, against the day the crown should call for it.

Saban, beside him, wrote the figure on his cuff as the clerk read it, and Emeric watched him do the sum.

"Three hundred men," said Saban, very low. "And about three hundred rifles at the stream ground." He put his pencil away. "They've measured exactly what beat him, and capped it. That's a compliment, Highness, from people who'd never pay you one."

Corbin took custody of the papers. Every one of them: the articles, the answer, the oat bills, the copy of the letter to Lisle, Keller's finding, the settlement in its fair copy; the chancery's clerk gathered them up off the barrier and the table and carried them up the dais steps and put them into the Lieutenant's own hands, and Corbin passed them to a secretary behind his chair, who carried them out by the door behind the dais. Nobody in the hall remarked that the duke whose articles they were was the Lieutenant's mother's brother. Nobody looked as if it had occurred to them.

And then Keller stood up again.

"If the Lieutenant pleases," he said, in exactly the voice he had used for his finding. "There is a matter of the chancery's own that the crown's magistrate is bound to raise while the parties are in the hall."

Corbin looked at him. It was the first time all day that Emeric had seen his brother's face do anything it had not been told to.

"The magistrate may raise it."

So Keller raised it. Four baronies had been granted to the Holder of Kaldo by crown order in the year of the grant, Kaldo and Grayrock and Milford and Wayford, and the patent of the duchy that the statute made of several baronies under one holder had been drawn at the same time and lay in the chancery unsealed. The statute gave the chancery no discretion in the matter. A patent so drawn was to be sealed. If the chancery knew of any cause why it should not be, the crown's magistrate would be glad to hear it, in the hall, now.

He sat down.

The hall waited. Emeric felt it wait: two hundred notes held still, and one, on the dais, gone very smooth and very quiet, the way a man's note went when he was choosing between two things he disliked.

"The chancery knows of none," said Corbin.

He said it lightly. He said it as if it had been his idea. He called for the chancery's keeper and the seal, and they came, and the patent came out of wherever it had lain since the grant, a long sheet with a ribbon, and the keeper sealed it on a table at the foot of the dais in front of the whole hall, and Corbin signed it in the king's name with a pen that did not hesitate.

"His Majesty," said Corbin, putting down the pen, "will name the day of the investiture."

His Majesty named it within the hour. A herald came in by the great doors at the far end of the hall in the king's colours with a white staff, and stood on the board, and read it out so that it went to the walls: His Majesty would invest his son the Holder of Kaldo as Duke of Kaldo, in this city, in the week of the Feast of the Ascent, on the Thursday.

Somebody on the benches drew a breath through his teeth. Along the benches, here and there, men turned and looked at one another; and Saban, beside him, very softly, said, "Oh, well done."

"What?"

"The Feast of the Ascent," said Saban. "The reading of the names. Every cardinal in the realm goes to Ostrel for it, and every bishop who can sit a horse, and they stay a fortnight." He did not look at the dais. "The lords spiritual have voted against Kaldo in every matter that's come before the council since the grant. He's put the girding of his son in the one week of the year when there won't be one of them in the city to object to it."



A page came for Ward that evening, to the house in the drapers' street, in the king's household livery, and asked at the shop door for Sir Ward of the Holder's household, and for nobody else.

Ward looked at Emeric across the back room. Emeric looked back. Then Ward stood up off his chair, and put on his coat, and went.

He was gone an hour and a quarter. Emeric sat at the table under the barred window with Ferra's letters, the sixth and the seventh, which had come together again, and read them both twice, and did not take in a word of either. Down in the street the drapers were putting up their shutters. Saban came up once and looked in and went down again.

Do not come back until sent for.

Nine years. He had not been sent for. Ward had.

When Ward came back he shut the door and stood inside it and took off his coat and hung it on the peg, slowly, and sat down on his chair, and looked at the floor for a while before he spoke.

"His Majesty asked after your health," he said. "I said it was good. He asked after mine. I said the same." He was quiet. "He asked me about the stream ground. I told him. All of it. He listened to all of it without speaking, standing, in the small office. He did not ask about the knights. He asked about the nine."

Emeric did not say anything.

"And then he went to the door," said Ward, "and put his hand on it, and asked me what it cost him to have Aldane in his hall." He looked up. "Loudly. To the door. Not to me."

Emeric thought of a small room and a voice that died at arm's length, and a line built to travel.

"Built for the corridor," he said.

"Yes." Ward was quiet a long breath. "He did not say why it cost him. I think he meant to be heard asking." He looked at Emeric directly, then, which he did not often do at the end of a day. "He did not send for you because he is going to send for you. The Thursday. That is the sending, Highness. In front of the realm, with the sword. He will not spend it in a back room on a Wednesday."

"You don't know that."

"No," said Ward. "I know him." He leaned back on his chair against the door. "Read the Chancellor's letters. You have not read a word of them. I watched you."



The ransoms were paid at the chancery on the twelfth, on a table in the clerks' hall, in purses, by His Grace of Aldane's steward, who counted them out without argument and without once looking at anybody from Kaldo, as a man pays a debt he regards as a misfortune of the weather. Twenty-nine purses. Saban counted them back and wrote a receipt for each, the old terms for a knight of each knight's degree, and Bastian Sallow's among them, paid by his uncle's steward with the rest.

A thirtieth purse had come to the drapers' street the night before, by a rider in a northern lord's livery with a white hart on it and a bar across the hart, who would not come in and would not wait for an answer. It was for Sir Tancred. With it was a note for the Holder of Kaldo, four lines in an old man's hand: that the sum was enclosed, by the custom; that the knight would not be coming back for the court, there being no need; and that the writer had received his son. It was signed by Harlow's father.

"And Lord Sallow's?" said the steward, at the end, still not looking up.

"Lord Sallow was taken on the field by Sir Jabari Mensah," said Saban, "who has his own terms for him. He'll give them at the stream ground." He blotted the last receipt. "Your lord needn't trouble himself about it."

The steward looked up then, for the first and only time, at Saban, with an expression Emeric could not read at all; and then he gathered his empty satchel and went.

On the way out of the chancery yard, among the carts, Saban stopped and looked up at the sky over the inner rings, where a hull was coming down toward the nearest landing tier in its small careful corrections.

"I've bought one of those," he said.

Emeric stopped too.

"This morning. Before the ransoms." Saban kept looking up. "A widow in the third ring. Her husband kept a pleasure hull and a mage on wages to fly it, and the husband's dead and the mage is seventy and wants to go home to his daughter's on the coast, and she can't keep either of them. It's old and it's sound and it's been in a shed since the autumn." He looked down at last. "The old man'll fly it south once, on contract, and go home. It'll take him a fortnight. He'll want to land every night." He put his hat straight. "You'll want a ship the capital's seen before, Highness, to come back in for the Thursday. One that flies the way theirs fly."

"You didn't ask."

"No," said Saban. "I never do. It's quicker." He began to walk. "It was cheap. Everybody in the third ring knows why. Nobody wants a hull without a caster, and nobody can afford a caster any more. They'll have heard about you."



They rode south on the thirteenth and came down the capital road to Wayford on the sixteenth in rain, and Isolde and Jabari were at the stream ground to meet them, off the morning ship, standing in the barn's south door with the rain coming off the slates in a sheet between them and the yard.

The barn had emptied. The company that had kept it all winter had the knights' horses in a line along the earthwork, saddled, and the knights themselves in the yard in the rain in their own coats, with their harness on the carts, ransomed and free and not yet gone, because Ward had asked them to wait for the Holder and they had decided, it seemed, to do Ward that courtesy. Twenty-eight of them stood about in twos and threes and talked low and did not look toward the barn door.

Lord Aurel Sallow stood by himself under the eaves with his limp and his hat in his hand. Bastian stood ten yards from him, by the carts.

Jabari came out into the rain to meet Emeric at the earthwork. He had been told by the ship's crew that his wife's father's ransom had not been paid, and he had come up from Kaldo for that, and he did not waste any time on the rest.

"Highness. He's mine."

"He's yours."

"Then I'll not take anything for him." Jabari looked back at the barn, at the big grey man under the eaves. "Not a Copper. I've told him so already, this morning. He wouldn't hear it from me. He said it wasn't done." He was quiet. "He said a man who'd been put on his back in the mud by a knight in his own household's training wasn't owed courtesies, and he'd pay what was owed like anybody else." He wiped the rain off his face. "I told him a man who'd trained me had paid for it already. He didn't know what to say to that."

They went in under the eaves.

Isolde was standing beside her father. She had a paper in her hand, a sheet of the mill's best, and a small case of ink and a pen; and she did not give them to him, and did not say anything, and he did not look at her.

Sallow looked at Emeric instead. His face was heavier than it had been in his own hall in the coal light; it had been three and a half months in a barn.

"Your knight," he said, "won't take my ransom."

"Sir Jabari's your son-in-law, my lord. He's not my knight. He can take or leave what he likes."

Sallow looked at Jabari. Then at his daughter. Then down at the paper in her hand, for a long breath.

"I expected to die on that field," he said, to nobody in particular. "Or to pay." He was quiet. "I've done neither. I don't know what's owed for it." And then, very abruptly, he held out his hand to Isolde for the paper.

She gave it to him. He went to the carts and put it down on the tail of the nearest, on a dry board under the canvas, and Jabari held the ink, and Sallow wrote. He wrote slowly, a few lines, in a big old hand that went uphill: that he, Aurel, Lord Sallow, gave his consent and blessing to the marriage of his daughter Isolde to Sir Jabari Mensah, made under the seal of Kaldo in the November of the year past, and would hold them his daughter and his son. He signed it. Then he took off the ring with the grey horse's head and pressed it into the wax Jabari held under the candle in the shelter of the canvas, and gave the paper back to her.

She read it. Her mouth moved and she did not say anything.

Sallow turned to Jabari.

"Son," he said.

It came out of him like a stone out of a field, heavily and with a good deal of earth on it, and he plainly had no plan for it once it was out; he stood there with his hand half up as if he had meant to put it on Jabari's arm and then not been sure where the arm was. Jabari looked at the hand. Then he took it, and shook it, once, hard, and let it go.

"My lord."

"Yes. Well." Sallow turned away and put his hat on. "Yes."

Isolde held out the paper to her brother.

"Witness it," she said.

Bastian looked at the paper and did not take it.

"No."

"Bastian..."

"I'll not put my name under that." He said it quite quietly, with the rain coming off the eaves behind him. "He can sign what he likes. I don't have to." He looked past her at Emeric. "I said I'd remember."

Isolde lowered the paper. Then she took a purse out of her coat, a small one, and held it out instead. "I said I'd pay your ransom if he wouldn't. It's paid. Take it for the road. You've nothing."

"I've an uncle," said Bastian. "He paid." And turned, and went to his horse, and mounted it in the rain, and sat there waiting for his father with his back to all of them.

Isolde put the purse away. She looked at the paper in her hand, her father's big uphill lines and the grey horse's head pressed into the wax, and folded it once, and held it against her chest.

"One of two," she said, to nobody. "Ferra would enter that."



The toll-house went at four that afternoon.

Lisle's steward was waiting for them at the south end of Harrow Bridge with two of Lisle's men and a cart, in the rain, beside the bar. The bar was already down off its post and lying in the cart; the steward had seen to that himself that morning, he said, by the settlement's order, within the month, as the crown had required. The board was gone. The timber house stood behind it with its stovepipe cold and its door shut and its new yellow wood gone grey with the winter.

"My lord has no use for it," said the steward. He was the same man who had come for the two bodies at two in the morning, and he looked at Emeric now as he had looked at him then, across the yellow-haired boy, without saying anything he did not have to. "A toll-house that takes no toll is a house on a bridge. He'd sooner it was gone, and he'd sooner it was not his men took it down." He held out a letter. "And he's written."

It was from Lisle. In his own hand, short, sealed with his own seal, the first word from Lisle himself in five months.

It thanked the house of Kaldo for the return of his two people, washed, on the night of the third of November. It said that the boy's name was Toby, and that he had been fifteen, and that he had been his mother's only, a widow's, from Lisle's own village; and that the boy's mother had come to Lisle's steward in February and asked for the sum the house of Kaldo had offered, for herself, because she had heard of it in the market and she had nobody now to keep her, and that Lisle would not stand between her and it. Nye's sister would take Nye's. Lisle himself would take nothing, and had nothing to say about the rest.

Emeric read it twice. Then he gave it to Saban, who would give it to Ferra, who would enter it, under the page at the back of her column book headed Lisle, where she had written in November: the boy, name not known.

There's always somebody who asks.

"Fennick," he said.

Fennick had come up on the morning ship with Jabari, with the tin box under his arm that had gone into the Staple in August on Emeric's belt and come home unopened, and he had been sitting on it on the bridge's parapet in the rain for an hour waiting to be asked. He got up. He took the canvas sausage out of the tin and looked at it with great tenderness, as at a dog that had been left behind on a hunt, and went into the toll-house with it, and was in there some minutes, and came out paying out a fuse behind him and walking backward along the bridge, and the steward and Lisle's two men and Ward and Emeric and Saban and Isolde and Jabari all walked backward along the bridge with him, without anybody telling them to, until they were at the north end and Fennick stopped.

"Ferra will want the weight," said Emeric.

"Two pound and a quarter," said Fennick. "Under the settlement. Mining. Ferra'll want it in her book." He looked at the toll-house. "It's mining. I'm mining a house."

He lit the fuse. It ran down the wet bridge spitting, slowly, and round the door post and in under the door, and Fennick watched it go with his maimed hand held a little out from his side, and just before it reached the end he laughed; a breath of it, short and delighted, as if the house had told him a joke.

The toll-house sat down.

It did not blow apart. Fennick had not meant it to. It went up an inch all at once, every board of it, and the stovepipe jumped off the roof and went into the river, and then the whole yellow-grey timber box sat down into itself on the bridge's end in a heap of planks and shingles with a sound like a man dropping a load of firewood, and lay there smoking a little in the rain.

Lisle's steward looked at it for a while.

"Thank you," he said, to nobody, and went to his cart.



The relay post below the bridge had its padlock off by evening. Lisle's men took it off on their way home, and Tess's line man from Wayford went down at dusk with a lantern and his box and spent an hour inside the brick hut, and came out, and the direct line through Harrow was live again for the first time since October.

The telephone in Sethe's counting room rang at ten that night.

Emeric was sitting with Aster's books, with his boots on and his feet on the floor, because he had been warned. He took the earpiece off its hook. The hum, the hiss, three hundred and fifty miles of wire, and under them, far down it, close.

"Are your feet on the table?" said Imani.

"No."

"Liar." She was quiet a breath, and the line breathed with her. "I heard it came back on. Tess's man rang the hospital to say. I've come down in my nightgown again. I'm standing in the cold again." Another breath. "You've been to court."

"I've been to court."

"Are you a duke?"

"In May."

"Good," said Imani. "Then I've two months to tell you the rest of that sentence before anybody has to call you Your Grace while I'm doing it." He heard her shift the earpiece. "I'm not going to. Not on a line Lisle's steward has had his hands on." And then, lower: "Come home. Ferra's letters are making me jealous and she won't tell me what's in them."

"Nothing. Levels. The coke ovens."

"I know," said Imani. "That's what I'm jealous of." And put the earpiece down before he could answer, as he had once done to her by accident; and this time, he was fairly sure, on purpose.
