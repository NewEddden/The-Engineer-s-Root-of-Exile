Chapter 391 - Root of Exile

He gave Tess the books at half past four in the morning, in the Motion Shop, by the light of one lamp, because it was the only hour of the day there'd be nobody else in it.

They were on the long bench under a tarpaulin, where he'd put them at midnight with Wren's help and the door locked. When he took the tarpaulin off they were just books. Twenty-two of them in two rows, in the plain dark green board covers the Polytechnic used for its own texts, each with a number in white on the spine and nothing else; and beside them, in a lower pile, the seven that weren't numbered, and Wren's three; and beside those, the crate. On top of the crate, tied with string, a stack of blank notebooks a foot and a half high.

Tess stood at the end of the bench in her coat and looked at them.

She'd known. She'd known since the summer, he thought, since Ferra's long division had gone round the six and come back to the Works the way everything came back to the Works, and she'd never once asked. She'd come into the Motion Shop on a hundred mornings and found the little room across the landing's lamp still warm and said nothing. She stood now with both hands in her coat pockets and didn't touch them.

"That's a lot of long division," she said.

"Some of it's the school's. The copyists did the parts you've already had. I marked where." He put his hand on the first row. "The rest is mine. What's past what the school teaches. The fourteen are why things work and how to find out. The eight on the end are the road to the thing I couldn't build you before I went." He moved his hand along to the lower pile. "These are the ones that aren't homework. Light in glass. Logic, and the switching machines. Cells. The loom. Dams, for your dam. Radar, for when the valves let you. And the hand." He paused. "That one's just for you."

Tess didn't say anything.

"Thirteen," he said, "is mostly questions. It's the one about mana and everything else. I wrote what I've measured, and Armand's put his in under his own name, and yours from the seam table under yours, and the ring bursts, and what the note plates do and don't show. And then I left the rest of it empty, because I don't know." He turned it so she could see the spine. "Two thirds of it's blank. That's on purpose. Ferra's got it on her bottom shelf; it never goes outside the delvers."

"And the crate?"

"Things I started and won't finish." He lifted the lid. The first light-keyed blanks from Vesk's floor, in a tray, each in its own felt; a spool of Orrin's purest fibre on a wooden drum, the long run, the one that had carried light a mile in the shed without losing enough to measure; two spare relay boards; a selenium cell bench in a box with its lamp; the first pair of lithium cells, in a tin lined with sand, with a note on the tin in his hand that said slowly; and on top, wrapped in sacking, the Jacquard punch, which she'd been asking to see since October and which he'd kept from her on purpose so she'd have something to unwrap. "Play with them. Break them. Write down how."

Tess took one hand out of her pocket and laid it on the cover of the first book. Volume One. She didn't open it.

"You've written in the front," she said.

"Yes."

"You'll want me to read it."

"I'd like you to read it after I've gone."

"Then you shouldn't have stood here," said Tess, and opened it.

It was a page in his own hand, inside the cover, in the black ink he'd used for all of it, the hand that had got a little smaller and a little faster over the winter, the way a hand does that's written three thousand pages. She read it out. She read it standing at the end of the bench in the one lamp's light, not loudly, in the voice she used for reading a load off a drawing to Bram, steady and flat, so that it would be read and not performed.

"To Tess. These books contain a fraction of what there is to learn. Don't mistake that for an expectation that you must finish them all before I return. There is no examination waiting for you, and there is no failure in taking your time. I want you to understand what you're reading, question what you don't understand, and experiment whenever you can. If you discover something that contradicts what I've written, document it. Don't assume I'm right simply because my name is on the cover."

She stopped. She looked at the next line.

"Teach Little Wren," she read.

From the end of the bench, where she'd been sitting on her stool with her knees drawn up and her scarf over her hair since four, saying nothing, Wren said: "I'm just Wren now."

Tess looked up.

"I'm grown," said Wren. She was looking at the floor. "I'm twenty. I've got two motorcycles and the frames' joints and Sorrel's offsets. He knows. He wrote it to make me say it."

"Did you," said Tess, to Emeric.

"A bit."

Tess looked at the page a moment longer. Then she took the pencil from behind her ear, the stub she'd had there since the day she came to Bram's yard, near enough, and leaned over the book, and drew one line, neat and straight, through the word Little.

It was the first mark anybody had made in any of them that wasn't his.

"There," said Tess. She went on reading, in the same flat voice. "Teach Wren. Teach her to understand, not simply repeat. When she's ready, let her teach the next class. Let them teach the next. I want you to build something that doesn't need me to keep building. When I return, what you've learned will help determine how quickly we can advance. If you've made considerable progress, we'll have considerably more ground to cover together. But don't rush yourselves on my account. Build well. Learn well. And leave the door open for those who come after you."

She shut the book.

She didn't look at him. She stood with her hand flat on the cover and looked at the crate, and then at the long dark windows of the Motion Shop, where the first grey was coming up behind the Works' roofs, and then at Wren on her stool.

"You heard him," she said. "Up. We're walking him down."



She left the stick on its hook by the door.

He saw her do it. He was holding the door for her, and she came to it in her coat with her hands in her pockets, and the stick was on the hook where it lived when she was at the bench, the plain ash stick with the brass ferrule that she'd had since the year she'd stood up; and she looked at it, and walked past it, and went out into the yard.

Nobody said anything.

It was a mile, near enough, from the Motion Shop to the yard. Down the Works road to the gate, along under the town wall in the dark with the lamps still lit on it, down the long slope of the lower ward past the shut shops and the bakers' lit windows to the river's mouth, and along the quay to Cade's yard, where the masts were. Tess walked it. She walked it at her own pace, which was not fast and was not slow, with her hands in her coat pockets and her eyes on the road ahead, and Osric beside her on one side, not touching her, and Wren on the other, not touching her, and Emeric and Ferra and Imani a little behind, and nobody touching her at all.

At the gate she stopped once to let a cart go by. On the slope of the lower ward she stopped once more, halfway down, by the baker's on the corner of Cooper's Lane, and stood a moment, and Emeric saw Osric's hand come an inch out of his side, the way Zale's had come out of his at the wall water, and stop, and go back. Tess stood there for the space of three breaths. Then she went on.

The dynamo house had nearly killed her. She'd been in the chair for years after it. She'd stood up for the first time nearly four years ago, for thirty breaths. A stick, later. The chair for long days. And now, on the morning the fleet went, a mile in the dark on her own two legs, down a hill, with nobody's hand on her, because that was how she wanted to walk him down, and nobody who loved her was going to take that off her by being kind.

They came along the quay to the yard as the sky went from grey to pale.



The yard was full.

The eight of them rode at their masts and moorings over the slips and the quay in the grey light with their lamps still on, and the yard was full of everybody who had a reason to be there and a great many people who hadn't. The K Discovery at the yard's own mast, small and unarmed, with Sorrel on her deck. The K Amara out over the coal quay, huge, grey, with three hundred aboard and her knights' aprons along her flanks. The K Endeavor beside her, her cradles empty under her keel, to go out to South Sentinel and take the Challenger off the Cradle on the way, now the Catch was spent. Hull four behind her with Bram's workshop lamps lit inside. The two escorts in their pair with Ruan's mounts on their quarters and the lane painted on their decks. Zale's ship, and the K Tam on her sister's quarter, green and white.

And below them on the slips, the two keels. Hulls five and six. Cade had laid them in the autumn and had them in ribs by February and was planking them now, the long ribbed shapes of them lying side by side on the slips in the half light with the scaffolds up round them and the steam boxes cold, and Cade's men standing on the scaffolds in their caps to watch the fleet go, the way the town had stood on the wall to watch the first ship. Ferra had their date in her book. If the net went quiet past it, hull five and the Hod would come looking. Cade said five would be ready. He said it every day.

Bram was at the foot of hull four's gangway with his bag.

He saw Tess come into the yard. He saw her come in at the gate between Osric and Wren with her hands in her pockets and nothing in them, and walk across the yard's cobbles to where the crowd was, the last two hundred yards of the mile, at her own pace. He didn't move. He stood at the foot of the gangway with his bag in his hand and watched her come the whole way, and his face didn't do anything at all.

When she stopped in front of him he looked down at her.

Then he turned one hand over, palm up, and back.

"Don't let anybody paint anything," he said, and went up the gangway.



Abloh caught him at the corner of the rope store.

He'd had the coat back since Sunday, for the hem and a last press and a thread at the left cuff that nobody else in Kaldo would ever have seen, and by the look of him he'd sat up with it. He had it over his arm in a long canvas bag, and his own sea bag over his shoulder, and he stood in Emeric's road in the middle of Cade's yard with five hundred people in it and said, "Arms."

"Abloh. The fleet's..."

"The fleet'll wait." He unbuttoned the bag. "Arms."

So Emeric took off the old sea coat and handed it to Imani, who put it over her arm without a word as if she'd been doing it all her life, and stood in his shirt in the grey light in the middle of the yard and was dressed.

The jacket first. Black wool off the mill's last black run, close to the body, double-breasted, two rows of buttons down the front that the Works had cast and gilded with a rose on every one, because Abloh had said the dragon was for the belt and the cap and he'd not have it on the buttons where a man fiddled with it. The collar stood up stiff to the jaw, edged in gold, with a small gold device at each point. Abloh put the red scarf round his throat under it, a long one, silk, the same red as the linings, and knotted it once and pulled the knot a little crooked on purpose and left it. Then the belt, wide and black, with the buckle he'd drawn on Bram's tracing paper in July standing up off the leather in gilt, and the strap across his chest to the left hip, and the gloves.

Then the coat.

It was heavier than it looked and it went on like water. It fell from his shoulders nearly to his boots and hung open, and when he turned to let Abloh at the back of it the skirts swung and the inside showed, red, the whole length of it, and somebody in the crowd by the slips made a noise. A short cape lay over the coat's shoulders to the elbow, gold at its edge. Gold cord, braided, looped from the left shoulder. Gold piping down every edge there was, the fronts and the hem and the cape and the pocket flaps, and three bands of it round each cuff. Abloh tugged the cape a quarter-inch to the left, and looked, and tugged it back.

The cap last. Black, the peak polished like a boot, and over the peak a dragon in gilt on a spray of laurel. Emeric had slid the gold cuffs onto his locs in the dark at four for the morning, out of habit, the way he'd done on his wedding day; the cap sat over them as if it had been measured for them, which, he supposed, it had.

"Turn round," said Abloh.

He turned round, and Abloh looked at his back for a long time, and Emeric couldn't see what he was looking at, and didn't need to. He'd seen the sheet. He'd never seen it finished. Ferra had, he found out later: she'd gone up the outside stair one night in February with a lamp to bring Abloh a cost he'd asked for, and found him at the end of the cutting table at one in the morning with two of the Commencement girls and the coat's back stretched on a frame between them, working gold thread a stitch at a time by three lamps, and had stood in the door and not said anything and gone down again. The dragon between his shoulders, coiled round on itself, inside its ring of leaves.

"Three hundred and forty hours," said Abloh. "The back. After the knights."

"Nine thousand nine hundred yards," said Ferra, beside him, as if she were reading it off the wall. "Thirty-three thousand hours. Sixteen machines. Bram's whole winter on the floor, and the mill's." She looked him up and down. "And that."

"It's a very good coat," said Imani, holding the old one.

"It's a very expensive coat," said Ferra.

"It's paid," said Abloh, and did up the last two buttons for him, which Emeric could have done himself, and stood back.

All along the K Amara's flanks the knights stood on the aprons in theirs. They'd had them since Midwinter, the first coats off the loft, short black jackets buttoned to the throat, the red knotted under the collar and the small gold dragon on the cap and collar points, a hundred and twenty of them across the eight hulls; and down on the quay, under Osric, the hundred and eighty who were staying stood in a block in theirs, which had come out of the rope store in the last week of February. He found the three without looking for them, three notes packed heavier than any around them, at the forward end of the starboard apron where Ward had stood them. Arthur, a head above the knights on either side of him, his face giving the crowd nothing at all. Mira beside him, her cap set dead level and her plait down her back over the black. Bren on the end with his moustache trimmed for the day, grinning at somebody on the quay. And outside the yard, two deep up the road to the gate and along the town wall above it, stood the regulars, in the same jackets as the knights' with the red at their throats and the gold rose where the dragon would have been. Three thousand of them, near enough. The last two hundred had gone out of the loft on the Saturday. Nobody had torn a sail.

At the foot of Zale's ship's ladder, Ilenne was looking at the sword.

It was the one thing on him Abloh hadn't made. The Works had, to the coat's drawing, because Abloh had said the coat was drawn for one and would hang wrong without it: a long blade in a black scabbard with a gilt hilt, the dragon again for a pommel. Ilenne looked at it, at his hip, and then at him, with her hood back and her colour very still.

"Not at my mother's door," she said.

"It goes in the strong box," said Emeric. "With your dress."

"Good." She looked at the rest of him, the whole length of the coat, and the back when the wind turned it, and her colour came up warm at the throat, slowly, all the way to her jaw. "The rest, yes," said Ilenne, and went up the ladder after Zale.



Rowena had come down by the night packet from Emberfell and stood in the middle of the yard in a grey travelling coat with her hands in her muff and her chin up, looking at the K Amara the way she'd have looked at a horse somebody was trying to sell her.

She'd said goodbye to Zale already, at the foot of his ship's ladder. Emeric had seen it from the gate: Zale bending down, the long blue coat, his mother's hand coming out of the muff and going flat on the middle of his chest, the way a woman puts her hand on a door she's shutting for the night to see that it's shut; and then going back in the muff. She'd said goodbye to Maeve on the K Amara's gangway. That had taken longer. Maeve had come down off it again twice.

Now she watched Emeric come across the yard to her with the face of a woman who'd decided in advance exactly how much of this she was going to allow.

"I'm not going to make a scene," she said.

"I didn't think you would."

"Good." She looked at the K Amara, and Zale's ship beyond it. "I'd go, you know. If I could. I'd go tomorrow. I'd go today, in this coat." She didn't look at him. "I've a duchy. I've a port with a new quay and a sulfur works that's gone up a level and a school with my name over the door that I didn't ask for. I'll not leave it to a steward for a year to go and see a queen at the bottom of the sea." She was quiet a moment. "They both hate leaving me. Did you know? Zale and Maeve. They always have. Zale went to sea at twenty and wrote me a letter every month for seven years so I'd not think he'd gone because of me. Maeve's been trying to leave since she was six and never once managed it without coming back down the gangway." Her mouth moved. "They think it hurts me."

"Does it?"

"Of course it does." Rowena looked at him at last. "And they're going. Both of them. Off the edge of the map with the king and a mermaid." She looked back at the ships. Her voice, when it came again, had gone a little different: lighter, and a little far off, as if she were looking at something beyond the K Amara that nobody else in the yard could see. "It's nice to be free," she said. "Isn't it."

She didn't mean herself. He knew that, and she knew he knew.

He took the thing out of his coat pocket and put it in her hand, out of the muff.

It was a set. A small one, Ambrose's best after Amara's, in a leather case the size of a prayer book, with a handset clipped to its lid and a short whip folded down its side, and on the dial, already set, two call signs, scratched into the brass by Ambrose himself: the K Amara's, and Zale's ship's. She had a telephone on her desk at Emberfell. That talked to Kaldo. This talked to the fleet.

"The line room will put you through ahead of anybody but the regent," he said. "Whenever the chain's up. When it's not, it'll hold what you say until it is, and they'll get it the minute it comes back." He closed her fingers on it. "Call whenever you want. Call them. Call me, if you like. Call at three in the morning and tell Maeve she's had her hair cut."

Rowena looked down at the set in her hand for a long moment.

"I shall," she said. "Every day. They'll hate it." And she put it in the muff with her hands, and held it there, and didn't take it out again, and didn't make a scene.



Corbin was at the foot of the K Discovery's ladder with the box.

He'd brought it from the box room under his arm, Ferra's square wooden box with the slot in its lid, as though it might need emptying before the king went; it had four papers in it. He stood at the foot of the ladder in his good grey coat with his hands on it and his boots on the cobbles, and both ankles the same under the cloth, and a bonded stone five weeks settled in him that had put his colour back and his back straight and something into the set of his shoulders that hadn't been there since he was a boy. Behind him, a little way off, Saban, with his hat on, watching everything. Harrold beside Saban.

"Majesty."

"Corbin."

They looked at each other. There was a great deal to say, and none of it was for a yard at dawn with five hundred people listening, and both of them knew it, and Emeric found he was glad of the five hundred people.

"The box has four in it," said Corbin. "Cade, about the steam boxes. Ilsa, about the escrow. The Wayford main. And Lysa Orme, asking whether the chairs can sit in the summer, which I'll answer." He looked at the box. "I'll have it empty by noon. I'll have it empty every noon." He paused. "That's all, Majesty."

"That's all."

Corbin nodded. He didn't hold out his hand. Neither of them would have known what to do with it. He stood back from the ladder with the box under his arm, and as he stood back he said, not loudly, to the cobbles: "Come home. I'll want to give it back."

And turned away before Emeric could answer, and went to stand by his father.

Harrold looked at his eldest son standing beside him with the box, and then at Emeric at the foot of the ladder, and Emeric saw him remember, in the K Discovery's hold, five miles up, a promise; and keep it behind his face, the way he'd promised to; and nod.

"It'll be fine," said Harrold.

Imani, at Emeric's shoulder, put her hand into the pocket of her coat and took out a jam jar. It had eleven coppers in it, and a button. She held it out to the regent of Randillia without a word.

Harrold looked at the jar. He looked at her.

Then he felt in his own coat pocket for a long moment, and found a copper, and put it in.

"Bring them home," he said to Ward, over Imani's head.

"That's the job," said Ward.

Maren had come. Nobody had seen her come. She was at the foot of the K Amara's gangway with a basket on her arm, and she gave the island's cook a tin out of it, and said something in her ear that made the island's cook laugh, short and loud, and then she came across the yard to Emeric with the basket and took a second tin out of it and put it in his hands.

"Biscuit," she said. "Not the ship's. Mine. For when you're working and won't eat what she puts in front of you." She looked up at him. "She'll stand there. I've told her. You'll eat it for her the way you eat it for me or I'll know."

"From here."

"From wherever I am," said Maren, and put her hand flat on his cheek for a moment, a cook's hand, hard and warm and smelling of the morning's bread; and took it away, and went back to the gate with her basket without looking round, and he saw her shoulders go once, at the gate, and then not.



His mother was at the end of the quay.

She'd come down in her kit, with the set in her back. She stood a little apart from the crowd at the quay's end where the yard met the coal quay, where she could see all eight ships at once, with her made hands at her sides and the light coming up behind her off the sea; and he went to her last, because he'd known since February that he would, and had known since then that it would be the worst, and had been right.

She didn't say anything when he came. She looked at him.

He'd lost her when he was seven. He didn't remember the room he'd been told in. He'd grown up, and gone into exile, and built a town, with only the shape of her face; and three years ago he'd put her back, under the tree, with a bridge he'd built out of himself, and she had come back into her own head and known him. Three years. He'd thought, the first night, lying awake between Ferra and Imani with his mother's voice still in his head, that if he had ten years he'd never get used to it. He'd had three. He wasn't used to it. He'd never be used to it.

And he was going, and she was staying. That was right. She was the first dial. She'd hear every heartbeat on the chain from here, and every held message, and she'd be the one person in his family who couldn't ever be told he couldn't reach her, because whatever he said would wait for her in a relay until she could hear it. He'd built it that way. He'd built her that way. He'd built a mother he couldn't leave behind, and then left her behind on a quay.

"You can't tell me you couldn't reach me," said Amara. "You know that. I'll be in the relay."

"I know."

"Good." She looked at him a moment longer. Then she put up one made hand and laid it flat on his chest, on his sternum, the way she'd laid it there in the covered yard in December with him on his back on the boards, not pushing. Not pushing at all. Just there.

"Go on," said his mother.

He went.



They lifted at a quarter past six.

The K Discovery went first, because she was on point, and Emeric was at her rail on the forward deck with Sorrel at the wheel behind him and the fifteen relays in their racks in the hold below his feet, beating to themselves. She went up off the yard's mast slowly, without a sound but the necks, and turned into the west, and the others came up behind her one after another and took their places, the K Amara at the centre, the carriers, the escorts in their pair off the centre line, Zale's ship and the K Tam on the flanks; eight ships, eight hundred and thirty, the way it was written on Ferra's sheets, in the order they'd boarded.

They went out low.

That was the rule, and the wind's: west was under the wind, a few hundred feet up, never high, not on the way out. So Kaldo didn't go small under them all at once, the way it did from the Band. It went past.

The yard first, and the two long keels on the slips with the men standing on the scaffolds, and Cade on the highest of them with his cap off. The quay, and the crowd at the end of it, and a woman in dark plate a little apart from the rest with the morning on her, not waving, standing very still, the way she sat on the gravel under the tree at night. Then the river's mouth, and the coal station's two cold stacks, and the lower ward's roofs going away up the hill, all slate and brick and smoke going straight up from the bakers' chimneys in the windless morning, and in among the roofs, every street of them, the lamps still lit, because the town lit its lamps from the station and the station didn't put them out till seven, and it wasn't seven. Lamps all the way up the hill. Lamps on the wall. Lamps on the trams, going along the river road on their wires, the first of the morning, with their windows lit and nobody in them yet but the drivers. The wall itself, the whole round of it, grey and very long, with the frames standing on it at the gates in the dawn like men who'd been told to wait. The towers on the bay with their glass caps catching the light. The school's long roof and the Polytechnic's dome beside it and the hospital's three blocks with every window lit. The castle on its rock in the middle of all of it, old and small now, among the new.

And the dome, on the hill behind the castle, catching the sun before anything else did, so that for a moment as they went past it it was the only thing in Kaldo that was gold, with the tree inside it a dark shape under the glass, and somewhere under the tree, on a mattress against the trunk with a blanket to her chin, a woman asleep.

Then the bay. Saint Edwy's white spire on the shore with its terraces and Gideon Marl's trees along them, gone over now from blossom to leaf. The sea wall and its light. And away to the north-east, small and white and still lit at the river arm's mouth a dozen miles off, the lighthouse, which had been the first thing they'd built that was only for strangers.

He'd come into this town on a cart, with a chest. He'd sat at the end of Maren's mother's table with nothing in his belly and eaten four bowls of soup and forgotten to say thank you. He hadn't known anybody's name.

He looked at it go past under him, all of it, every lamp, until the K Discovery came out over the bay's mouth and the town went behind the headland, and there was only the sea.

He didn't look back after that. He'd looked enough.



Ferra opened the book past the last island.

She was on the K Amara's bridge deck, at the chart table, because that was where she'd be for most of a year, and the last island had gone by under them at a little after nine, a long low brown rock with a gull colony on it and the K Endeavor coming back up from South Sentinel off its southern end with the Challenger in her cradles under her, black and wet and slung, like a seed in a pod. The convoy was all together. The sea was blue now. Ward was at the forward windows with his hands behind his back. Armand was asleep in a chair in the corner, which he'd said he wouldn't be. Maeve was at the rail with Mira, being shown something about the spear that didn't need showing at a rail.

She opened the book at the page from South Sentinel. The twenty-fifth of June, a year ago less ten weeks, at the long mess table, with Ilenne's two wet fingers walking up the green baize to the jam jar and the sea under the table in a bucket. The column she'd ruled that night and headed Departure. And the line under it, in her own hand, which she'd written while Ilenne watched, and which she'd read perhaps a hundred times since, in the winter, at the bay house table and in the long room and in bed with the lamp low and Imani asleep across her feet.

Not kept. 25 June. Ours.

And under that, the date she'd written at the window on the first of April.

She took the pencil out of the spine.

She wrote beside it, in the same hand, not hurrying: 14 April. Kept.

Then she looked at it a while, with the sea going by under the bridge deck's glass and the hum of the K Amara's veins coming up through the floor into her feet.

And she wrote, under it, the same word again.

Ours.

She shut the book.

Somewhere ahead, a mile off the K Amara's bow, on the forward deck of a small grey unarmed ship at the head of the line, she felt him feel her do it; and felt Imani, on the K Endeavor's hospital deck half a mile to the south, feel it too, and look up from whatever she was doing; and felt both of them, very faintly, along the ring, the way you feel the warmth come back into your hands, smile.



Three nights on, the convoy climbed.

It was the first time. They'd flown low for three days, under the wind, and set down on the sea at dusk each night at the cabin mark and slept on the swell with the sea anchors out; and on the third night, with the weather calm and the moon up and the racks wanting filling, the eight of them went up together into the Band to charge, and while they were up, the K Discovery put the first relay over her side.

In Kaldo, in the line room, it was gone eleven.

Saban had come up at ten, because he couldn't not. He'd told himself he'd come to see Ambrose about a cipher, and then he'd seen Ambrose about the cipher, and then he'd stayed, in the doorway, with his hat in his hand, because the doorway was the place in any room he'd always stood. The line room was quiet at that hour. The switchboard's girls were down to two, with their headsets round their necks and a pot of tea between them. Ambrose was at the long desk with the log open and a pencil. The board's little lamps were lit in their rows, every relay on the home net and BR-01 over the bay and the Well, green, steady, beating; and at the far end of the board, on a new strip that Ambrose had screwed on that afternoon with nothing on it yet, one lamp, dark.

Amara was at the end of the desk in her kit, with the set in her back and her made hands on the desk in front of her, listening.

Harrold was beside her. He'd been beside her since nine. He'd come up from the dome with his coat over his arm and sat down in the chair next to hers and hadn't said anything, and she hadn't said anything, and every so often, without looking, he'd put his hand on the plate of her shoulder and left it there a while and taken it off again.

Saban watched them from the door.

He watched the dark lamp on the new strip, and the clock over the board, and Amara's head; and he thought, because he couldn't help thinking it, because he'd been thinking it since September and had said it out loud then, so it was said, about a ship on the sea four hundred miles west with its lamps out, and a thing put over its side in the dark, hanging on its own stones in the Band, and in a little while, at a time that wandered, a heartbeat going out of it. To its neighbour, down a beam. In Ferra's cipher. And past the beam, faint, the way any sound goes past a wall, to anybody, anywhere, with a set and an ear and a reason to be listening.

A heartbeat was a lighthouse. He'd said that. It was true. It had been true when he said it and it was true tonight, and there was nothing to be done about it, because a net that didn't speak couldn't be heard by its friends either, and the king had built it anyway with his eyes open, and Saban had helped. He didn't say it now. There wasn't anybody in the room who needed telling.

At eleven minutes past eleven the lamp on the new strip came up green.

Ambrose's pencil stopped. The two girls at the board went quiet over their tea.

Amara's head lifted.

It lifted the way it had lifted in the K Discovery's hold five miles up, fifteen times, very slightly, the way a person's head moves when someone speaks to them from behind; and she listened. She listened for perhaps three seconds. Harrold's hand had come up off his knee and stopped halfway to her shoulder and stayed there. Nobody in the line room breathed.

It was the first one past anything Kaldo owned. Past the coast, past the last island, past South Sentinel and its depot and its reef, out over a sea nobody from the land had flown in a thousand years but Zale; a knock on a door in a house she wasn't in, four hundred miles off, in the dark. Her son's ship had put it there. Her son was on the deck beside it, under it, somewhere in the Band with the moon on the cloud.

In the doorway Saban held his hat and knew exactly what else was listening, and said nothing.

Amara's hands lay still on the desk.

"Good," said Amara.
