Chapter 37 - A Jar That Twitches

The needle came out of the dead port.

Abloh got it. Emeric had asked in the soap yard whether anybody in Kaldo still owned a ship's compass, and Abloh had said nobody owned one, and gone across the east bridge that afternoon with a knife, and come back at dusk with a small brass bowl in a wooden box, green at every seam, taken out of the binnacle of a coasting boat that had been rotting at the empty moorings since the port silted. He had his own opinion of the man who had left it there, and gave it while he cleaned the glass with his sleeve.

The needle still swung. It came round, and wavered, and settled, and pointed up the river at the north as faithfully as it had pointed for whoever had steered by it last.

Emeric took the coil off the nail in the back room at the mill, the one Tess had drawn in January, with Abloh's waxed thread wound over every inch of it, and sat in the dyehouse with the compass box between his knees and wound the wire round it, round and round the outside of the bowl, sixty turns, close and even, until the box was a fat brown bobbin with two loose ends of wire sticking out of it and a little window of glass on top where the needle showed.

Abloh watched him do the whole of it, from a stool by the door, with his arms folded.

"That's my thread," he said, when it was done.

"It is."

"Round a compass."

"Yes."

Abloh considered the bobbin and seemed to decide that he would wait and see what it did before he formed a view, which was more than Bram had done.



The jar was a glazed crock from the potter's stall in the market, brown, the kind a household kept its salt pork in. Into it went water from the canal ring, and into the water, slowly, a measure of the oil from the dyehouse, which Tess poured, saying the rule under her breath while she did it, because Emeric had told her she could pour it the day she could say the rule without thinking and she had decided that saying it was safer anyway.

Then two plates. One was copper, a hand wide and a hand and a half long, hammered out of the end of a Grayrock bar by Bram, who had done it in about the time it took Emeric to ask and had not asked what it was for. The other was iron, cut from one of the grates out of the tithe houses, the ones too burnt through to go in the scythes. Emeric stood them in the crock a finger apart, not touching, with a wooden spacer between them at the top, and bent a wire to each.

The principle took him two sentences, and he gave them to Tess and Abloh both, because it was going to be their jar as much as his. Two different metals in an acid each wanted to give up their substance to it, and one wanted to more than the other. Join them by a wire outside the jar, and the difference pushed something round through the wire, the way a head of water pushes through a pipe.

"What something?" Tess said.

"Current."

She waited for the rest of it.

"You can't see it," he said. "You can only see what it does."

He touched the end of the copper's wire to one loose end of the coil, and the end of the iron's wire to the other.

The needle twitched.

It did not swing. It jumped, a little, off north, perhaps the width of a fingernail, and hung there trembling, as though something had leaned on it from the side.

Emeric lifted one wire away. The needle swung back to north. He touched it again. The needle twitched again, to the same place, and trembled.

Tess had stopped breathing. Abloh had uncrossed his arms.

"It's pushing the needle," Emeric said. "The current in the wire makes the coil into a magnet while it runs. Take it away and the coil's only wire again." He lifted the wire and touched it, lifted and touched, and the needle went back and forth on the glass, north and off north, like a dog that has heard its name. "Wire round a compass. Your thread keeps the current in the wire instead of letting it out sideways through the next turn."

Abloh got up off his stool and came and looked down at the needle, very close.

"That's what it was for," he said.

"Some of it."

Abloh looked at the bobbin of his own thread, wound round a dead man's compass, making a needle jump on the far side of a pane of glass, and said nothing at all for long enough that Tess looked at him.



It died within the hour.

It did not die all at once. The needle crept back toward north by small degrees, so slowly that the first time Emeric looked away and looked back he thought he had imagined it, and the second time he knew he had not. Fine bubbles were gathering on the copper plate, hundreds of them, silver, clinging to the face of the metal like dew on a leaf. The iron was going dark and rough. By the time the bell at the church rang noon the needle was barely off north at all, and by the time Tess came back from eating it was at north, trembling a little when the wire touched, and then not trembling.

"It's tired," Tess said.

"It's choking." He lifted the copper out with a pair of Bram's tongs and showed her the face of it, furred with gas. "Every bit of current it makes leaves a little of that on the copper. The gas sits on the plate and keeps the acid off it, and the more it makes, the less it can make." He wiped the plate on a rag and put it back, and the needle jumped off north again at once, as far as it had at the start, and the bubbles began again at once, and the needle began to creep.

"So you wipe it," Tess said.

"Every ten minutes, for ever, by hand." He watched the needle. "And the iron's being eaten whether the wire's on or not. It isn't making current with half of what it's losing. It's just being eaten."

Abloh, who had gone back to his stool, said that it sounded like most of the men he had worked for.



The pouch ran out at the end of May, on the page, as Ferra had said it would in March.

It still had coins in it. It had enough in it to pay the guards into June and to pay for very little else, and the households' money for the last Saturday in May came out of it, and then the line in Ferra's book stopped, and below it she had drawn the rule she drew under anything that was finished.

He had known since March. He had spent most of April on a hill and most of May in a dyehouse, and he had let the thing come at him at its own pace, which was the pace of a month moving down a page. Now it was here, and when he sat down at her table with it he had already decided. He had decided before he came to Kaldo. He had decided in the other life, paying taxes all his working years into schools he never set foot in, and thinking it the one bill he never minded.

"The barony pays it," he said. "From June. Not me."

Ferra did not look up. "With what?"

"With what the barony takes in. They pay the tithe. They pay the rents. It's their money, going back to their children. If a barony can't do that, I don't know what it's for."

"That's not a sum."

"No," he said. "That's the reason. You do the sum."

She had not done it. She did it while he sat there, turning back through the barony's ledgers, the thirty years of them that had come down the hill under sacking and never gone back up, a quarter at a time, and on the third page she stopped and put her finger on a column in the garrison pay.

Sixteen names, struck through in August. The men who drew a wage every quarter and existed nowhere else. Since the day those lines were struck, nobody had drawn their pay, and their pay had gone on sitting in the treasury every quarter, in Silver, doing nothing, because nobody had told the treasury what else to do with it.

The ledger would not lie open at the page. She took his hand off the table, put it flat on the facing leaf, and did the sum in her own book. Her fingers left ink on his knuckles.

"That's the households," Ferra said. "From June, with a little over. It was paying for men who didn't exist. It can pay for children who do."

He looked at the sixteen struck lines, and at her finger beside them, and had nothing to add.

"And the four?" Ferra said. "Your guards."

"The pouch. Till it's empty."

She wrote that too, and the month beside it, which was the end of June.



Hale heard him out in the steward's office with his hands folded on the daybook.

"The barony's revenue is the barony's, Your Highness," he said, when Emeric had finished. "It may be spent on the barony's business. Whether a school is the barony's business is a question for whoever the barony answers to." He did not look at the window this time. He looked at Emeric. "I don't say that to refuse you. I say it so that it is said."

"Then write that I decided it."

"I will enter it as your order, and the date."

"Enter it as the barony's."

Hale opened the daybook and dipped his pen, and paused with it over the page.

"The barony has no hand to sign with but yours," he said, and wrote it, and turned the book round for the signature.

On the first Saturday in June a barony bag came down from the keep to the exchange door, with the barony's mark stamped on the leather, and Rylan paid from it at the trestle while Sela called the names, and nobody in the line noticed the difference, which was how Emeric had wanted it.



The second jar failed on a Tuesday in June.

It was six jars, in a row on the dyehouse bench, copper to iron to copper to iron, each plate joined by a wire to the next jar's opposite plate, so that the six pushes added up into one, the way six men on a rope pull harder than one. For the telegraph he would need current that could go a long way down a wire and still move a needle at the far end. One jar would never do that. Six might.

For the first hour they did. The needle swung a full quarter turn off north, as far as a needle can go, and stayed there, and Tess made a small sound and put her hand over her mouth.

Then the iron plates began to work.

He had made the acid stronger for six than for one, and the water had warmed as the oil went into it, and the crocks were salt-pork crocks and had never been asked to stand warm acid. He was already reaching for the wires when the fourth crock cracked from the rim down with a sound like a knuckle, and the liquor came out of it across the bench in a thin sheet and over the edge onto the floor.

Tess had the water bucket before he had turned round. She threw it the way he had shown her, not at the acid but across it, a great deal of water all at once, and then ran for the second bucket by the door and threw that too, and then stood back, breathing hard, with the empty bucket in both hands, and looked at her own feet and her own arms and said, "Nothing on me," in a high flat voice.

"Nothing on me either," Emeric said. "Good."

The floor steamed a little and ran down into the stone channel under the vats and away toward the lime pit, which he had dug in the yard behind the dyehouse on the day Tess asked her question, and which was earning its keep.

Bram had come to the door at the noise. He looked at the bench, and the cracked crock, and the five others still fizzing quietly to themselves, and the needle back at north, and the water all over his apprentice.

"Twice," he said.

"Twice," Emeric agreed.

He knew what was wrong, and that was some comfort, and not much. The iron was wrong: it gave too little push and ate itself whether the wire was on or not. He needed a metal that pushed harder and could be made to wait. And the copper was wrong, or rather the copper was right and the acid on it was wrong: the gas choked it. He needed the copper standing in something that would not choke it, and the other metal standing in the acid, and the two liquids kept apart in the one jar so that they did not mix, and yet not so far apart that the current could not pass between them.

Two waters in one jar, kept apart, and touching. In the other life the answer had been a pot of unglazed clay standing inside the jar. He did not know whether anybody in Kaldo fired clay soft enough for it, or where he would go to find out, and he stood in the wet dyehouse turning it over.

Tess had put the bucket down and gone to the corner by the door, where the dyehouse water jar stood on its three stones in the shade, the plain brown unglazed one the potter sold for a copper to every household that wanted its water kept cool. She drank from the dipper, and wiped her mouth, and then, being Tess, put the flat of her hand against the side of the jar and held it there.

"It's wet," she said. "On the outside. It's always wet on the outside."

Emeric looked at the jar.

Then he crossed the floor and put his own hand on it, beside hers, and felt the cool damp sweat that came through the clay from the water inside, slowly, all over, through a wall that held the water in and let it out at the same time.
