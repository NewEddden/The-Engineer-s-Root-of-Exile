Chapter 37 - Second Season

The second sowing took eleven days and Emeric was present for four of them, and on the fourth day he understood that he was standing in a field watching a thing happen and that nobody had asked him a question since Tuesday.

The first year had been thirteen miles of trench and a hundred and forty men and a board with the whole thing drawn on it that he had carried from meeting to meeting like a relic. The second year was gates. The water went where the schedule said, the divisions went in in the order the schedule said, and the schedule was on a board nailed to the mill wall where anybody could read it, and it had been there since the first week of February.

He had not written it.

He found that out standing in front of it with his hat off, going down the column looking for the two changes he had intended to argue for, and finding that both of them were already in it, and that a third change was in it that he had not thought of and that was better than either.

"That's Corliss," Halloway said, coming past with a bag on his shoulder. "Corliss and Old Pell and the Hesk boy, over about three evenings in the winter."

"Tam?"

"He can't read. He stood there and told them what happened at every gate last year in the order it happened, and Corliss wrote it down, and Pell argued about the fifth division for two nights and lost." Halloway shifted the bag. "You'd shut the west channel on Tuesdays. It wants shutting on Wednesdays, on account of what comes off the top of the third when the mill's grinding, which you'd not know because you're never at the mill on a Tuesday."

"Nobody told me."

"They put it on the wall in February." Halloway looked at him with a particular kind of patience. "It's a schedule, Your Highness. It's not a petition."

He walked back up to the castle turning that over, and got about half a mile before the ugly part of it arrived, which was that his first feeling in front of the board had not been pleasure. His first feeling had been a small cold drop, the exact one a man gets when he finds out a thing has been decided in a room he was not in. It had lasted perhaps two seconds and then the sense had come back over the top of it, and he had stood there being pleased in a way that was genuine and about a second and a half late.

He had said in a field in June, with everyone watching, that the water was his and that he did not take a vote on it.

He had been right at the time. It had been true for exactly as long as he was the only man on the plain who knew why a corner cost fall, and that had stopped being true somewhere in the middle of last summer without anybody sending word.

The field met on the eighteenth of March, which was the last day it could meet and still act before the seed went in, because Dunnel had written it that way and Dunnel wrote everything that way.

Two proposals stood. It was the first meeting since the franchise had been repaired that had anything on the table anybody disagreed about. Maren's recut had gone through in October by fourteen parts and fifty-one households and nobody had raised a voice, partly because it was fair and mostly because in October everybody in that room had been doing arithmetic about November.

This was March, and the granary in the yard had food in it, and men argue when they have eaten.

The first was Pell's nephew.

He came himself, which cost him something, and stood at the end of the table in a coat he had plainly been made to wear, and said what he had come to say in about forty words without any of them being an apology, which Emeric respected and which did him no good at all in the room.

Forty acres in the middle of the field. A jog in the ring that put two extra corners in a straight run, and two corners cost fall, and the fall belonged to everybody. He wanted in. He would bring the forty acres, and the straightening, which came off his own ground and not the field's, and he would take one part and a half.

"And why now," somebody said from the back, and it was said in the tone that made it a different question.

"Because I've two sons," Pell's nephew said. "And forty acres, and neither of them will hold twenty and live. I could sit on it another ten years and be right the whole time and hand each of them a half of a thing that doesn't feed a house." He looked at the table and not at the room. "And because I watched the water go round for a year and my barley came up thin in the middle of it and everybody else's didn't. I'm not going to stand here and tell you I've had a change of heart about anything. I've had a change of arithmetic."

Emeric had the sheet ready and put it in the middle of the table and did not speak to it.

He had written it in his own hand over two evenings, both columns. What the field gained: forty acres, a straight run, a corner and a half of fall recovered on every turn of the water, and the removal of a set of gates that wanted inspecting twice as often as any other set on the ring. What the field lost: one and a half parts cut into a total that had been twenty and would be twenty-one and a half, which took roughly seven parts in a hundred out of every share in the room, including the barony's four, which he had shown separately at the bottom so that nobody could say the castle had been quiet about its own.

His name was on the honesty of it. It was not on the proposal.

Halloway spoke against it, and spoke well, which Emeric had expected and had dreaded slightly.

"Here's what it costs and it isn't parts," Halloway said. "Last February there was nothing. There was a drawing on a board and a prince nobody trusted and a man from the castle telling us to hand over hedges. Some of us signed that. We signed a thing that did not exist, and the reason we signed it is that somebody had to sign first or it never gets built at all." He put one hand on the table. "Now here's a man who waited. Waited a whole year, kept his forty acres, risked nothing, watched, and comes in on the same terms as the men who signed in the dark. If that carries, then the answer to the next thing anybody wants to build in this parish is wait and see, from every household in this room, and it will be the right answer, and nothing will ever get built again."

Nobody had anything good to say back to it, and Emeric sat with his mouth shut and his hands flat and discovered how much work that was.

Dunnel put the joining charge on the table, which he had drafted for exactly this: two seasons of labour, valued against what the digging had cost per part in the first year, payable in days and not in coin, and forfeit of the part and a half if not paid.

"That's a price on waiting," Halloway said.

"It is a price on waiting," Dunnel agreed. "It is not the same price you paid, because you paid in not knowing whether it would work and there is no way to charge a man for that after it has worked." He put his hat on the table, which in that room meant he was finished. "Nobody can sell you back your February. You may take the two seasons or you may take nothing."

It carried on parts nine and a half to six and a half, and on households by more than two to one.

The nine and a half included Widow Corbell's one and a half, and she said nothing before or after, and if she had gone the other way it would have been eight and eight and no majority and the thing would have been dead.

The nine and a half also included Halloway's, and Emeric got him at the door about it.

"You argued it down and then voted it up."

"I argued it because it wanted saying and nobody else in that room was going to say it, and it's true, and in ten years somebody will remember that it got said." Halloway put his hat on. "And the jog costs the whole field fall every year for as long as there's a field. My objection costs it once. I can count, Your Highness. I'd just rather not be the only man who says the thing out loud and then finds out I've been made a fool of for saying it."

The second proposal was Emeric's, in everything but name, and he lost it.

It came from Ferra through Saban and went onto the table under Bram's name because Bram had two parts and Emeric had four that did not vote, and everybody in the room knew exactly whose idea it was, and Emeric had decided that pretending otherwise would be worse than the thing itself.

Milford threshed by hand. The drum did in an afternoon what eleven men did in three days. In the five slack weeks after sowing, a crew of the field's men could go over the western border with the drum and come back with coin, and the coin would go into the field's book, and there was a debt in this barony with a payment due in October that everybody in the room had heard about.

He had taken it to Vantic first, in January, because he had learned that lesson in a fortnight and not in four months.

"It is permitted," Vantic had said. "You are operating a thing you already own. You are not commencing an undertaking. I will write that down now so that nobody has to rely on my memory of this conversation in August, and I will say that it is close to the line and that I would refuse a second one of these."

So it was lawful, and it was money, and it failed.

It carried on parts, ten and a half to five and a half, and it died on households, twenty-nine to forty-four.

Maren killed it, in about ninety seconds, using Emeric's own arithmetic, which was on the table in Emeric's own hand because he had written it honestly and had written both columns.

"Read the second column," she said. "It's his and it's fair and it's why I'm against. Who goes? Young men. Whose young men? Mine, and the twenty-six, and the four hundred who came in April, because a household with three parts doesn't send its sons away for five weeks and a household with a quarter of a part does, because a household with a quarter of a part needs the coin." She let that sit for exactly as long as it needed. "And where does the coin go? Into the book. And how does the book split it? By parts. So the labour comes out of the small households and the money goes into the big ones, and every one of you knows it, and it is on that page in his handwriting."

"And the five weeks," Halloway said, on her side this time.

"And the five weeks are the hoeing, which is the thing the small households do on their own ground, and which does not get done by a man who is in Milford." Maren sat down. "Vote it if you want it. I'd not have brought it."

Emeric said nothing for the whole of it, because his four parts did not vote and he had put that rule in himself in July standing in a hot field with nothing in his hands, and because there was nothing he could have said that would not have been a lord in a room full of tenants explaining why their sons should go and thresh in Milford.

He walked out with Ferra afterward.

"That was the October payment," Ferra said.

"That was about a fifth of the October payment and we'll find the rest."

"I want to know whether you are being calm at me."

"Bit of both." He looked back at the mill and the men coming out of it. "She was right. That's the whole of it. She was right and she was right using the page I wrote, and if I'd written it any other way it would have carried, and I'd have had the coin and I'd have taken the hoeing off seventy households in a debt year." He put his hands in his coat. "I got beaten by my own honest arithmetic. That's the best thing that's happened to me since Christmas and I would like about a week before I have to be pleased about it."

"You have until October."

"I'll take it."

Two days later he came down the water at first light because he wanted to see the fifth division fill on the new schedule and find out whether Pell had been right about it, and found a boy sitting on the bank with his back against a gate post and a stick across his knees, keeping the schedule.

Finn was eleven and looked about nine and had a piece of chalk and a slate with a copied column on it in a hand that was learning.

"Third's shut at seven," he said, standing up too fast. "Fourth opens when third's shut. That's not till the mill's done."

"Who told you that."

"It's on the wall." Finn thought about it. "Corliss says I'm to sit here and not open anything and shout if the water does something wrong."

"What's wrong look like?"

"Don't know yet," Finn said. "Corliss says I'll know."

He was still there an hour later, and Rylan came down at the end of his watch and stood on the rise for a while with his hands behind his back looking at his son sitting on a bank with a stick, and did not go down, and did not say anything about it to anybody, and went back up to the wall.
