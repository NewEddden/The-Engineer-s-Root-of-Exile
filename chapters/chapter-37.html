Chapter 37 - A Welcome Mat

The squeeze arrived over a month and it arrived as an absence, which is the hardest kind of thing to notice and the hardest kind to argue with.

Saban's people sent it in pieces because pieces were all any of them had. A carter who had been three weeks on the northern road. A factor's clerk who owed Saban a favour and did not know what he was paying it with. A woman who ran a stall at Wayford and heard everything and understood none of it, which made her the most reliable source he had.

Iron was moving out of Grayrock in quantity. That was the first piece and on its own it meant nothing, because iron moving out of a mining hill is the hill doing its job. What made it something was the direction. It was going west and south and none of it was going toward Kaldo, and when Saban's man asked at the scale house about the southern order he was told, politely, that there had been a delay.

Levies were being called in Milford. Not mustered. Called and listed, which is the step before, and which costs a baron nothing and commits him to nothing and is done by a clerk with a book.

Wayford was buying nothing and saying less. Sethe's hall had cancelled two standing orders and given no reason for either, and had gone quiet in a way that a market notices the way a room notices a clock stopping.

And the soap stopped.

That was the one that Saban brought in himself rather than sending, and he came in off the road and sat down without taking his coat off.

Four buyers who had taken Kaldo soap in volume for two years stopped taking it inside eleven days of each other. There was no dispute. Nobody complained about quality. Nobody haggled. There were no unpaid invoices anywhere, not one, and two of them settled outstanding accounts early, which Saban said was the detail that made his skin crawl.

They simply stopped.

"Men do not give up commerce," he said. "It is the last thing they let go of. I have watched men trade with a barony they were at war with, through a third party, and both sides knowing, because the money was good and the money is always good."

He turned his hat over in his hands.

"There is one thing that makes a man stop buying a thing he wants at a price he likes. It is that he has decided he is going to have the whole of it instead."

Ferra priced it in two days and the number was bad.

Kaldo's outside income was soap. That was the entire list. There was no second item on it. The grain that might have covered the gap was in cellars they could not sell out of, because they were going to need it, and because selling grain out of a town that was about to be looked at was the single stupidest thing available.

She gave him the runway in months, the way she gave him everything, with the working shown.

"Twenty-two months. That is the reserve against the standing costs, with the child wage in it and the school out of it and no building of any kind. Twenty- two months is the good number and I want you to hear what is inside it, because it assumes the crop comes in."

"And if it does not."

"Then it is eleven, and then you are borrowing from somebody."

He did not argue. There was nothing in it to argue with.

The town said five hundred and twenty.

He first heard it at the gate, from a man on the Works crew who said it the way people say a number they have heard four times, and by that evening he had heard it twice more, and by the end of the week it was the number. Five hundred and twenty men coming down the river road.

He asked Ferra to find out where it came from.

She took four days over it, because she did not do anything in fewer than four days, and she came back with a chain and the chain went nowhere.

A man at the Works had it from his brother. His brother had it from a carter. The carter had heard it in a Wayford taproom from somebody he could not describe, who had been confident about it, and who had possibly been a soldier and possibly not.

"That is the whole chain," she said. "There is no fifth link. It stops."

"Nobody counted anything."

"Nobody counted anything. It is a number that was said out loud in a room where a man wanted to sound like he knew something, and it has travelled sixty miles in three weeks because it is frightening, and frightening numbers travel."

He did not correct the town. That was a deliberate decision and he wrote down why: because he could not prove two hundred either, because a town that is told the real number will argue about the real number, and because five hundred and twenty was making people work.

He also did not use it. Not once, not in a single calculation, not on any sheet. The number he worked from was the one Saban's people had assembled out of levies called and iron moved and men seen on a road, and it was roughly two hundred, and he stated it once in front of Ward and Ferra and never said it again.

Roughly two hundred fielded, against forty-four in harness and whoever stood.

He did not know the internals of it and never would until afterward. He did not know that Grayrock would bring the most and commit the hardest, because Renner was the man who had done the arithmetic on his own order book and had spent eleven years putting money into men instead of into shafts and had very little left at home. He did not know that Wayford would bring less than its size suggested, because Sethe had spent his money on couriers and on knowing things rather than on soldiers. He did not know that the largest column would be the worst one, because half of Milford's hundred were drovers and herdsmen called up off open pasture who had never stood in a line in their lives.

He knew the number. He knew what the number meant.

And the decision he made about it was the only genuinely important decision in the whole month, and he made it in about a minute because he had made it already in a cold field in the spring and had spent the weeks since looking for a reason to unmake it and had not found one.

No siege.

Not because he could not hold the walls. He could hold the walls. The walls were the best thing the barony owned and would have held four generations of anybody.

Holding walls is how you lose slowly to people who have more of everything. They eat your field. They burn what they cannot eat. They wait, and waiting costs them almost nothing and costs you every single thing you have, and at the end of it you come out anyway, hungrier, with fewer men, onto ground they have had four months to choose.

Field testing started that week. The men came out from behind the rock.

He gave the doctrine to Ward, once, on the west bank, with nobody else within a hundred paces, and he did not give it to the garrison at all.

"They are confident," he said. "I want to be fair to them about it. Their information is old but it is not stupid, their numbers are real, and they have every reason in the world to expect this to be short. If I were sitting in Grayrock with what Renner has in front of him, I would expect it to be short too."

Ward said nothing, which was Ward agreeing.

"And there is one thing they do not know, and it is not the armour and it is not the crossbows."

He was looking at the road, not at Ward.

"I came out of a place that did nothing but this. Four thousand years of it, continuously, with nothing else going on. And the whole time, men wrote it down. They wrote down what worked. They argued about it in public. They taught it to boys in rooms, and then somebody got it wrong and forty thousand people died in an afternoon, and then they revised what they had written and taught it again. Over and over. For four thousand years."

"Every mistake that can be made walking a column down a road has been made, written up, and taught to children by people who watched it happen."

"Magic makes a difference," Ward said.

"Magic makes an enormous difference. It does not make a difference to the part where men have to walk somewhere, in order, while being shot at. That part has not changed in either world and it is not going to."

Then he said the other thing, and his voice did not change at all.

"War is war, and Hell is Hell. They are bringing me the first one."

He was quiet for a second.

"So I am going to give them the second."

Ward had stood next to this man for fifteen years, through a poisoning, a trial, a plague and an exile, and had seen him angry perhaps four times and frightening never.

He did not comment. He looked at the road as well.

The preparation was labour and none of it was clever.

The ground came first and it took nineteen days and it did not look like anything at all when it was finished, which was the entire specification.

Not a wall. A lane. Ditches where a ditch made sense and a spoil bank where the spoil had to go anyway. A wet margin along the low side, where the western arm could be let out through a sluice in an afternoon and turn four hundred feet of good ground into something a man in harness would rather walk around. Brush piles, a cart with a broken axle, a line of stumps nobody had got round to pulling.

Every obstacle placed so that a column coming down the river road would find exactly one comfortable route through it and would take that route without ever once experiencing itself as having chosen.

The high ground on the flank was the second thing and it was chosen for the people who were going to stand on it rather than for the ground.

The men who would shoot were farmers and colliers and a woman who ran the shop rota. They were going to be frightened and they were going to be shooting at moving men for the first time in their lives. A crossbow shooting downhill into a lane forgives a very great deal of that. You do not have to be good. You have to point it into a space that men are in, and the space is below you, and there are a lot of men in it.

The ranges were the cheapest thing he built and the most valuable.

Stakes, a painted stone, a scratch cut into a fence rail, a whitewashed post in the hedge. Every one of them at a measured distance from a marked firing position, so that on the day nobody would have to estimate anything, because estimating distance is the first thing a frightened man loses and he does not notice losing it.

It was done with the same rope and the same iron rule that had laid out the nine divisions, by two of the same men, and on the second morning one of them, a thin quiet surveyor called Merrow who had walked the farm boundary four times, stopped in the middle of the field with the rope in his hands.

"This is the same job," he said.

"It is."

"This is the exact same job." He looked down the line of stakes. "I laid the second division with this rope."

And that was true, and it was the whole of it, and nobody in three baronies had ever once thought of surveying a field and ranging a killing ground as the same skill, because in three baronies those two things were done by two entirely different kinds of men who never met.

The men were not drilled. They were rehearsed.

There is a difference and it is the difference between knowing how to do a thing and knowing where you stand while you do it. They went out onto the actual ground, in the actual armour, in the positions they would actually hold, and ran it, and ran it again, and then ran it in the dark, until the crossbow crews found their own places without being told, and the reload rate stopped being a figure on a slate that each crew's lead kept in his own hand and became a thing Emeric had watched with his own eyes eleven times.

The stones and the rifle he built in the lead-up and tested on empty ground.

Three dead-zone stones, which had taken him most of the winter and which he did not enjoy making. A dead zone does one thing: inside it, for as long as it lasts, nothing works. No enhancement, no working, no charge held in any crystal. It is not a weapon. It is a hole in the world with an edge on it.

The air-crystal rifle was the other thing, and it was the air drill with a tube around it and a great deal of patience, and it was slow to charge and awkward to carry and would put a bolt through a plank at a distance that had made Declan swear out loud.

He tested the stones on empty ground at the far end of the ninth division, three times, with stakes at measured intervals and Rylan walking out from the centre with a charged crystal in his hand calling out where it died.

The falloff came in consistent across all three throws. He wrote the figure in the book, and the radius, and the duration, and underlined the radius because it was the number that men would have to stand outside of.

The ground he tested it on was empty. There was nothing on it and nobody in it and no charged steel anywhere within two hundred feet except the one crystal in Rylan's hand.

Bram's threshold breakers went onto every Enhanced coupling and Bram fought him about it for two days.

"It is another part," he said. "You have spent three years telling those two boys that every part is a thing that can break and now you want four more of them on the four suits that matter."

"Yes."

"And the cost."

"I know the cost."

"Then say the case out loud, because I want to hear you say it and not just write it."

"A crystal drawn to nothing is a dead crystal and I cannot replace one. There are four sets of high-grade in this barony and there is no second source anywhere I can reach. If Osric empties his in a bad minute, he does not get a worse suit. He gets no suit, for the rest of his life, and I get thirty-three men in plate and one in a shirt."

Bram made them. He made them well and he complained the entire time.

And the thing he did not have, Emeric said to Ward without any drama at all, standing on the bank at the end of a long day, was numbers.

"Everything I have built out here works if there are enough men in the lane to make it worth shooting into. I have forty-four."

"Yes," Ward said.

"So I am going to have to ask."

A hundred and thirty-one people took a crossbow.

He asked in the market square and then in the parish hall and then, for four days, one street at a time, and he was careful about how he asked, because there is a way of asking that is not asking.

He said what it was for. He said where they would stand and what would probably happen and that he did not know what would happen after the first ten minutes. He said that anybody who did not want to would be given work inside the walls and that nobody was going to hear about it from him.

A hundred and thirty-one.

Hollis, who had eleven acres and had never once agreed with him about anything. Colm and half the kiln crew, who turned up together as a unit and stood together as a unit for the entire war. Men off the Works. Nine women off the soap shop rota, one of whom turned out to be the best shot on the flank and remained so for the rest of her life without ever becoming interesting about it.

Abloh would not carry one. He said so straight out in the square in front of sixty people, without apologising for it, and asked what else there was, and ended up running the bolt supply for the entire flank out of a cart and two boys, and was very good at it, and nobody in the barony ever said one word about it to him or behind him.

Emeric never called them soldiers. Not once, not in the square, not in the book, not on the day. They were people who had picked up a tool.

But they were people who had been drilled at something every week for two years by a man who did not believe anything improved without repetition. Fire drill. Water drill. The bucket line. The burial rows. The census. The sieve. Two years of being told to stand in a particular place and do a particular thing on a particular signal, over and over, for reasons that were always explained and never optional.

It transferred. He had not expected it to and it did.

Ward gave him the estimate privately and gave it to nobody else.

"They will shoot well for about ten minutes."

"And after that."

"I do not know," Ward said. "Nobody knows. That is what the ten minutes is."

Ferra asked the wall question at the end of it, and it was the same question.

Why not hold a wall that had held four generations. She asked it properly, with the alternative costed, because she never asked anything without having done the work first.

He gave her the attrition answer. She gave him the money answer, which was that a siege of any length ended with Kaldo borrowing from somebody and that the somebody would own them.

They arrived in the same place from opposite directions, which was that Kaldo could not afford a long anything, and there was a small silence when they got there.

"You have a great many knives now," she said.

"That is how you end up with a great many hands."

It did not work.

It had worked in the autumn, in a warm room, with a harvest in the cellars. It came out of both of them at the right time in the right order and it lay on the table and did nothing at all, and both of them heard it not working, and neither of them tried to rescue it.

She gathered her sheets.

She did not tell him to be careful. She did not say she hoped he knew what he was doing, or that the barony needed him, or any of the four or five things that were available and true.

He did not tell her anything either. Not where the fourth cellar was, though she knew. Not what he wanted done if it went badly, though he had written it and it was in the drawer she would open. Not one word about any of it.

She said goodnight. He said goodnight. She went out.

Paxton's grace he gave to the four and to nobody else.

If Milford's colours hang back, Milford's colours are not engaged. Not shot at, not pursued, not counted. If they sit on that road all day doing nothing, they go home at the end of it.

"And if he commits," Ward said.

Emeric had known the question was coming and had known the answer for six weeks and it still took him a second.

"Then he dies with everybody else."

He did not enjoy saying it. Osric looked at the fire.

He walked the lane once more at dusk on the last evening.

There was nothing to check. He had checked all of it, twice, and the crews had checked it after him, and the ranging stakes were where they had been that morning and the sluice gate was greased and the brush piles had not moved.

He walked it anyway, from the top of the flank down to the wet margin and back.

The crop stood green on both sides of the road, high enough now to move when the wind came across it. The cellars were full and scattered under a town where four people could have named them all. The old granary stood at the end of its tidy yard with the doors shut and nothing whatsoever in it.

And the lane ran down the west bank in the last of the light, and from where he was standing, and from four miles up the river road, and from anywhere at all except directly above it, it looked like a field that somebody had been working in.
