Chapter 364 - The Board

Ilenne went into Kaldo's harbour at a quarter past five on the fourteenth of August, off the steps at the end of the fish quay, with her cloak folded on the bottom step and Finn sitting beside it in a dinghy with his oars across his knees.

She didn't dive. She walked down the steps into the water the way a person walks downstairs in her own house in the dark, one hand on the wall, and at the third step under she lay forward and was gone. Finn watched the place where she'd been. The ripples went out across the slack water between the moored lighters and came back off the quay wall, and then the water was flat and grey and full of the smell of fish and coal smoke.

"She does that," said Emeric, from the quay.

"I know she does that." Finn didn't look up. "I've seen her do it at the island a hundred times. It's different when it's our harbour. It feels like she's gone down a drain."

The town was only just starting. A tram went along the top of the quay with its lamps still on and nobody in it but the driver. Up the hill the clock on the school said twenty past. A gull stood on the dinghy's bow and looked at Finn as if it had a claim.

Emeric had his sense open, out across the water, and he could feel her going. It wasn't hard; she was the densest thing in the harbour by a long way, and she rang in the thin water like a struck bell in an empty room. She went out low along the bottom of the inner basin, past the keels of the lighters and the coal barges and the fireboat on her mooring. She went out under the sea wall's shadow toward the mouth, where the tide had just turned and was starting to run. Then she stopped. He felt her stop. She stayed where she was a long time, in the deep part of the mouth where the chart said eleven fathoms, holding still against the start of the ebb, and around her the harbour's own small notes, the fish and the crabs and whatever lived in the weed on the wall, went on doing what they did.

And something else came to her. Several somethings, close together, quick and warm, in from the open sea past the wall's end on the turning tide. He knew them, faintly. He'd felt them go past South Sentinel at the change of the tide for years and never paid them any attention.

"Porpoise," he said.

Finn looked up at him.

"A pod. Six or seven. They've come in to her." He held still and let them go round her. They went round her twice, and then they stayed. "She's talking to them."

"With them," said Finn. "Ferra wrote it down. You're not supposed to say to."

She was gone fifty minutes. When she came up she came up beside the dinghy, not at the steps, with no warning at all, and put both forearms on the gunwale so that the whole boat tipped toward her and Finn grabbed for the far side. She was grey-white and very bright, brighter than she'd been on the island in weeks. Her hair was flat to her skull and running.

"Ferra's map," she said.



The Harbour Board didn't exist yet, except in the old customs tower at the root of the sea wall, which Bram had gutted to the stone in June and which now had a floor of new boards, a row of eight empty brass housings along one wall waiting for their switches, a painted chart of the harbour leaning against another, and a smell of wet plaster. There was a table. There were chairs, some of them. Ferra had brought a cloth for the table and nobody had asked why.

They met there at ten, the people the water line belonged to. Bram for the machines. Ferra, whose chart it was, drawn from three summers of soundings off the harbour launch. Osric, because his fire company would crew the fireboat and the monitors on the wall. Saban. Tess on her stick, who had come for the stones and stayed standing by the window for the first half hour because she said the chairs were an insult. Finn, still damp. And Ilenne, in a dry cloak, with her hood down and a towel round her shoulders and a cup of the customs tower's dreadful tea in her hands, which she held for the warmth and didn't drink.

Ferra unrolled the chart across the table and weighted the corners with Bram's steel rule, Tess's stick, Saban's hat and the teapot.

It was a good chart. Emeric had watched it being made. It had the harbour in its long curve, the inner basin and the quays, the sea wall going out from the root where they sat in its long arm to the light at its end, and the shoal on the north side, and the mouth between, narrow, a quarter-mile of deep water with the tide running through it twice a day like a mill race. The soundings were in Ferra's hand, hundreds of them, small and neat. Across the mouth, in pencil, Bram had drawn the current gate: a line of eight squares on the bottom, numbered, from the wall's foot to the edge of the shoal. And on the seaward side of it, a little inside the light, three circles. A, B and C. The whirlpool mines.

"Three anchors," said Bram. "Down already. Cade's men laid them Tuesday, on the chart's marks, and the chains are shackled on and lying on the bottom, waiting. The mines go on when Tess has the stones." He looked at Tess. "Five weeks."

"Four and a half."

"Four and a half. They sit on the bottom on their chains, quiet, ballast full. Somebody here turns a key, the ballast blows, they come up to their depth on the chain and hold there, and somebody turns another key and they spin." He put his finger on B, the middle one. "B's the big one. B's the one that takes a hull off its line if it comes in on the flood. A and C are to herd it to B."

"Not B," said Ilenne.

She had put the cup down on the cloth, and it had made a ring.

Bram looked at her. He didn't argue at once, which Emeric noticed; three years ago he would have.

"It's the deep water," he said. "It's where a hull has to come. That's why it's there."

"Yes. Deep. Hull come." She put her finger on the circle, pressing, so that the pencil blurred a little. "Also..." She did a thing with her other hand, quick, a curved line going over and under, over and under, through the circle and out the other side and away into the harbour, and up along the inside of the sea wall toward the fish quay. Then she did it again, the same way, and then the other way, out. "In, out. Tide. Every tide." She looked at Emeric. "Say."

"Porpoise," he said. "A pod of them comes in through the mouth on the flood and goes out on the ebb. They feed along the inside of the wall where the fish quay drains. Every tide."

"Also seals," said Ilenne. "On the shoal." She tapped the north side, where the soundings went to nothing. "Small ones. Spring." She made a small shape with two hands, a pup, and then looked at Bram's B again and opened her hands, flat, and pushed them apart, a vortex throwing things out of it. "Turn it. Here. They die."

"It's switched off," said Bram. "It only turns when there's a ship. It's not turning all day."

"Practice," said Ilenne.

Bram stopped.

"Drill," said Osric, from the end of the table, helpfully. "You'll want to drill it. Once a week, Ward'll say, or it's no good to anybody. That's fifty times a year you spin it with nothing coming in."

"I know what practice means," said Bram.

"She meant drill."

"I know what she meant." Bram looked down at his circle for some time with his big arms folded, and Emeric watched him do the sum and not like it. A mine you never fired was a mine nobody could fire when it mattered. And a harbour mouth with a pod of porpoise coming through it on every flood tide was a place where, sooner or later, some Tuesday morning, a drill was going to throw them against the wall.

"Where, then," said Bram.

She took the pencil out of Ferra's hand. Ferra let her. She leaned over the chart, with her hair dripping a little on the soundings, which Ferra watched and didn't stop, and she drew. She drew the pod's road first, in from the open sea and through the mouth close under the light, on the wall side, and along the inside of the wall. It was a narrow road. It was a lot narrower than Emeric had thought, and it kept to the wall the whole way. Then she drew a second thin line on the north side, along the shoal's edge, and made a small mark on the shoal itself: the seals. Then, slowly, she drew a long oval between the two, through the middle of the mouth, where the soundings said nine and ten fathoms and the bottom was sand.

"Here," she said. "Nobody."

"Nobody lives there?"

"Nobody stay. Sand. Fish go through. Fast." She drew the circle for B inside the oval, small and careful, a little seaward of Bram's. "Here, B. Turn, nobody die." She put A further out than Bram had, past the light, in open water. She looked at C on the north side for a long time, and then rubbed it out with the side of her thumb, which smudged a good deal of Ferra's work, and drew it again a hundred yards in from the shoal's edge. "Spring, no C," she said. "Small ones."

"No drills at C in the pupping season," said Ferra, writing it on her pad.

"Yes."

"And the anchors?" said Bram. "I've three anchors down already, on my marks, with chains on."

"Move," said Ilenne, as if it were obvious, and gave Ferra back her pencil.

There was a short silence, during which Bram looked at the ceiling.

"A's fine where you had it," said Finn. "I'll say that for it. She swam A. She didn't say anything about A."

"Two anchors, then," said Bram. "Cade'll love that."

"Cade's men laid them Tuesday," said Saban. "It's Thursday. They've not had time to rust." He hadn't sat down either. He'd been standing behind Ferra's chair the whole time with his hands in his pockets, looking at the chart the way he looked at a man's face across a table, and he hadn't said a word. "And while we're moving things."



Saban's point was the keys.

Bram had drawn them in already, on a corner of the chart: two keyholes at the Board, side by side, a yard apart, so that one man couldn't turn both. It was the old rule. Ward had made it at the table twelve years ago, after the stair and Rylan: nothing in this barony wakes one man. Every gate key in Kaldo had two holders since. Every strongbox. The wall Board itself, the old one, had two seats.

"Two keys, two men," said Saban. "That's good. That's very good, as far as it goes. It stops a man going mad and sinking the herring fleet." He took one hand out of his pocket and put a finger on the two little keyholes. "It doesn't stop somebody buying both of them."

"Who'd buy the harbour keys?" said Osric.

"Anybody who wanted to bring a hull in through this mouth some night next summer, while the king and a good part of his knights are on the other side of the world." Saban didn't raise his voice. "Or anybody who wanted to sink one of ours coming home, and make it look like an accident with a drill. Or the Church, which has a great deal of money and nothing much to spend it on just now but hulls it can hide in the Band and men it can buy on the ground." He let that sit. "Two men who stand the same watch, go home up the same lane and drink in the same house can be bought in the same evening, with the same purse. That's not two keys. That's one key and a friend."

"So?" said Tess.

"So the two keys don't stand the same watch. Ever. One's the Board's own, the officer of the watch, here, from the fire company's men, Osric's choice." Osric nodded slowly. "The other key is a stranger to him. It comes from the wall watch, Rylan's men, and it changes every night on a roll that nobody sees until the evening it's posted, and that roll is drawn up by somebody who isn't Rylan and isn't Osric and doesn't drink." He looked round the table. "And it's never mine either. I'll draw up the roll, for now. I'd like somebody to watch me do it."

"I'll watch you," said Ferra.

"I thought you might."

"And when the king's away?" said Bram.

"Then nothing changes," said Saban, "which is the point of it. The king doesn't hold a key now and he won't hold one then." He turned to Emeric, and for once he didn't turn his hat, because Ferra had it holding down the corner of her chart. "With respect, Majesty. If this thing only works when you're here, it's not worth the brass."

"Agreed," said Emeric.

"Then I'll want it in writing that you agreed."

Ferra wrote it. Board: two keys, never one watch; the second from the wall roll, drawn nightly by S., F. watching. No crown key. Then she drew a box round it, which she did for rules.

Ilenne had followed the keys better than she'd followed the porpoise, Emeric thought. She'd been watching Saban's face the whole time, not the chart. When Ferra drew the box she leaned over to look at it, and then up at Saban.

"Bought," she said.

"Yes."

"At home also." She said it the way she'd said fork, at the table in June: as a thing she'd recognised from the inside. Then she said a word in her own tongue, and made a sign with her hand, a sharp downward turn of the wrist, and didn't translate it. Ferra wrote the word down anyway.



It was Bram who started the other thing, and he started it by complaining.

They'd gone on to the current gate, the eight housings on the floor of the mouth, and the footings for them. The footings were the trouble. You poured concrete underwater in a box and it went off well enough, but the tide scoured round it and the sea got into it, and in ten years you were back with a diver and a crowbar. He'd done the harbour's piers that way and he'd been back with a diver and a crowbar on the old north pier twice. Then there was the Challenger's shaft gland, which had sprayed at depth on her first deep dive and nearly sunk her, and which he'd packed and re-packed four times since, and it still wept.

"There's no packing in the world that stays tight in salt water," he said. "Not for long. Everything I put in the sea, the sea takes back. It just takes its time about it."

Ilenne had been looking at the footings on the chart. "Why put?" she said.

Bram looked at her.

"Why put, stone?" She pointed at the little squares. "Grow."

"Grow what?"

It took her a while, and Finn, who had seen a good deal of her hands by now, helped, and in the end Tess helped most of all, because Tess got it first. Ilenne made a frame with her fingers, a lattice, a lot of little squares like a grid of iron rods. Then she put it in water. She made the water run through it, slowly, with her hand. And the stone came. Not put; came. Out of the sea, onto the iron, a little every day, white and hard, and thicker, and thicker. She showed them a crack and the crack filling in. She said a word for it, short and grey, which Ferra spelled as best she could and Finn put into Kaldo as saltstone, because that was what it was.

"Out of the water," said Tess. She had come away from the window at last and sat down, hard, in the nearest chair, without looking at it. "There's lime in seawater. And magnesium. Dissolved. If you could get it to come out of the water onto something..."

"You'd need to give it a reason," said Emeric.

He was sitting very still. He'd known the thing as soon as her fingers made the grid; he'd read about it once on the other side of everything, a long time ago, in a magazine in a waiting room, and the page came up for him now whole, with the photograph on it of a coral reef grown back over a wire frame on a sea floor somewhere warm. A steel frame in the sea. A weak electric current through it. The current made the water at the steel give up its minerals, and they built up on the frame as stone, slowly, a little thicker every day. Mineral accretion, the magazine had called it. They'd grown whole reefs that way. They'd grown breakwaters.

"A current," he said. "A weak one. Low voltage, through the frame. The steel draws the lime out of the water onto itself." He looked at Ilenne. "Your people do it with mana."

"Mana," she agreed. "Grove water. Better. Hard. Fast."

"And we could do it with a battery," said Tess. "Slower."

"Slower and weaker, and it'll work in a dead zone," said Emeric, "and hers won't, so we want both." It was the layer rule falling out of the sea in front of them, the mundane underneath and the magic on top, and he could see Tess see it, and see Bram see it a second later. "We'll grow the footings. A frame in the mouth with a cable on it. It'll heal itself every time the tide scours it."

"I'll want her way as well as ours," said Bram. He had unfolded his arms. "The seeding, whatever she does. If it's harder."

Ilenne nodded. Then she looked at the Challenger's gland, which Bram had drawn on the corner of a scrap with a lot of very angry crosshatching, and said, with the air of someone getting to the real business, "Also. Glue."

This one took longer. It was an animal first, and then two animals, small, in shells, that held onto rock. She showed them holding on, with a fist, against a wave, with the other hand being the wave. She showed somebody taking what the animals held on with, and keeping the animals for it, a lot of them, in rows, farmed, the way the Milford men farmed hens. She showed it going onto a joint, wet, in the sea, and the joint never letting go. She showed it under a pane of glass, all round the edge of the pane, and the water outside the pane, deep and heavy, and the pane not moving.

"Mussels," said Finn. "That's mussels. The stuff they hang on by. The beard."

"Yes."

"It sets in the water?" said Bram. His voice had changed.

"In salt. Better in salt." She tapped the gland. "This. Glue. No water. Never."

Bram looked at his drawing for a long time. Then he said, quite quietly, "I'd give a year off my life for that," and nobody at the table laughed, because he meant it.

"Can we grow those?" said Emeric. "The animals. Here."

Ilenne shook her head, too far each way. "Not yours. Yours..." She made a weak fist that let go. "Ours. From home. Beds." She cupped her hands round a small space, a starter bed. "My mother's."

So that was the shape of it. Kaldo could grow saltstone on its own with a cable and a frame, badly, and better with her people's way of seeding it. Kaldo couldn't make the glue at all without the right animals, and the right animals were a thousand miles past the wall in beds that belonged to a queen. And the harbour mouth, the current gate's footings and the Challenger's gland and the sea wall itself all wanted both, now.

Saban had sat down at last. He had his hat back from under the chart's corner, and he was turning it.

"What would your mother want for them?" he said. "For the beds. For the way of growing stone."

Ilenne didn't need to think about it.

"Iron," she said.

"Iron."

"Iron. Coal, for..." She made a fire with her fingers, and something being hammered in it. "And glass. Good glass. Yours." She pointed out of the window, up the hill to where the glassworks' stacks stood over the roofs of the lower ward, and then, with great precision, at the brass housings on the wall, at Bram's steel rule, at the hinges of the door. "We have no iron. Never. We buy." Her face did the sour thing it had done at the reading in July. "From short ones."

"Who cheat," said Saban.

"Always."

"Then we'd be cheaper," said Saban, "and not cheat." He turned the hat a quarter turn. "Which would make us some friends at your mother's court, and one or two enemies under a mountain somewhere, and possibly some at your mother's court as well, depending who's been buying the iron from the short ones and selling it on." He watched her face as he said it, and Emeric watched her face too, and it did something very slight at the jaw. "Ah," said Saban. "Yes."

He didn't press it. He turned to Ferra instead.

"Put it down as a trade," he said. "Not a gift. Not a favour. Not a thank-you for keeping her alive. A trade, between two crowns, priced. Iron, coal and glass from us; starter beds of the glue animals and the method for the stone from them. First delivery whenever the convoy reaches her door. Terms to be settled there, by her, for her side." He looked at Ilenne. "If you'd carry that to your mother."

"I carry," said Ilenne.

Ferra wrote it. Then she sat back and looked at what she'd written, and Emeric saw her see what it was, and she said so, because somebody ought to.

"That's the first one," she said. "Isn't it. Between us and anybody. The first trade either side's made with the other in a thousand years."

"It's the first one we know of," said Saban.

"It's the first one I'm going to have to put in a ledger."

"And what's the first thing we spend it on?" said Tess. She had pulled the chart toward her and was looking at the mouth, at Ilenne's oval and the eight little squares of the gate along the bottom. "Footings. Gland. Wall." She laughed, short. "The first thing Kaldo ever buys from past the wall goes straight into the harbour defences."

"Of course it does," said Saban. "What else do you buy first from a new neighbour? A lock."



Imani had cleared him on the Tuesday, and she had made a great deal of it, because she didn't want him thinking it was nothing.

"Your vessels went," she'd said, at the island, with her fingers on the inside of his elbow and her sight open, slowly going up his arm the way a woman feels along a shelf in the dark. "Out past the wall, in the thick water. That's what did it. Not the depth and not the cold, the thickness, the mana in it, coming into a body built for thin. You took in more than you could hold, and it came out wherever you were weakest." She'd let go of his arm. "Kaldo's harbour is thin. It's thinner than this island's. It's about the thinnest water there is. Twenty feet and twenty minutes, Finn on the line, the cave suit and the flasks. And if your nose bleeds you come up, and if your knee aches you come up, and if you feel anything at all that you'd describe to me afterwards with the word funny, you come up."

"Twenty feet," Zale had said. "I've been deeper in a bath."

"You've not been anywhere in a bath since June. I've been watching you." She had put her hands in her lap. "It's not for you, this. It's for me. I want to see what your legs do in water before I let you near anything that matters."

So on the Thursday afternoon he went into the harbour mouth off the side of the launch, in the borrowed rubber suit from the cave dive, which had been a borrowed rubber suit for five years now and was his. The flasks were on his sides the way the king had made them carry them in the year eleven, two of them, because one fails. The mask with the round glass sat over his whole face with the demand valve inside it, and Finn's line was clipped to his belt. He went in backwards, because the stick was in the boat.

The water closed over him and was cold and green and thin, and the sound of the harbour stopped.

He hung there a moment below the launch with his breath going in and out of the valve, loud, and looked at nothing. Then he looked down.

The anchor for A was right below him, where Cade's men had laid it and where Ilenne had let it stay: a great iron mushroom half sunk in the sand, with a chain going out from it along the bottom in loose loops, and the mine's shackle lying open at the end of the chain, waiting. He went down to it. He went down slowly, letting the weight take him, and the pressure came in at his ears and he cleared them the way he'd cleared them over a thousand ships' sides, and nothing hurt.

His legs didn't hurt.

He noticed that at about fifteen feet, with some surprise. They'd hurt every day since June, in the bones, a deep slow ache like an old tooth, the vessels in them gone to wet paper, and on the stick and off it they'd hurt. Down here they didn't. Down here he wasn't standing on them. Nothing was asking them to hold him up.

He knelt on the sand by the anchor and looked at the shackle, because that was what he was there for. It was a good shackle. Cade had greased the pin. The chain was clean and new and the mooring was sound, and he checked the first three links of it with his hands the way Fletcher checked a weld, and they were sound too. He looked up the line of the chain toward where B's anchor ought to be, and wasn't, because it was going to be moved. The water there was green and empty, with a little silt moving in the beginning of the flood.

Something went round him.

He didn't see it at first. He felt the water move on his back, a long push, and turned on his knees, and it was gone round the other side of him. He turned again. She went past his mask a yard away, grey-white and long, her hair streaming back from her head. She swam on her legs, held close together and driven from the hips as one, the way the thin water had left her to swim, and she made it look like nothing. One stroke and she was round him again. She went round and round him, slowly, at the depth of his chest as he knelt, a wide easy ring with him at the middle, the way a dog goes round a man it's known a long time who's come home.

He laughed into the valve, and it came out in a great noisy rush of bubbles past the round glass and up out of sight.

She came round in front of him and stopped, holding still against the flood with no effort that he could see. She looked at him through his glass. Her eyes in the water were the same as on land, he found. He didn't know why he'd expected them to be different.

Then she reached out and tapped the mask, once, with one knuckle, very lightly, the way you'd knock on a door you knew there was somebody behind.

He held his thumb up. She looked at the thumb with great interest, as if it were an item of Kaldo grammar she hadn't come across, and then she held up her own.

Finn's line tugged twice, which was ten minutes.

He stayed down for the rest of it. He didn't do anything with it. He looked at the chain and the shackle and the empty water where B was going to go, and he knelt on the sand with the light coming down through the thin green water in long slow bars, moving, the way it came down through any water anywhere, and she went round him three more times and then lay on the bottom a little way off with her arms folded under her chin and watched him. At twenty minutes Finn pulled four, and he went up the line hand over hand, with his legs trailing, and broke the surface into noise and wind and gulls and the smell of the coal barges.

Imani was in the launch with Finn. He hadn't known she'd come. She had her bag between her feet and her face set, and she took his wrist before he'd got his mask off and held it, and looked at the inside of his nose, and felt behind his knee through the rubber, and said nothing for a long time.

"Well," she said at last.

"Well?"

"Twenty feet and twenty minutes. Again next week." She let go of the wrist. "Your pulse is the best I've had off you since May. I don't know what to make of that, so I'm not going to make anything of it."

He got his arms over the launch's side and hung there with his chin on them and didn't try to climb in. He wasn't ready to be heavy yet.

Ilenne came up beside him. She put her forearms on the gunwale next to his. She was breathing air again, the slits at her throat lying flat, and she looked at him sideways for some time with the water running off her face, and then she gave her judgement, the way she gave it on fish.

"Zale swim good," said Ilenne. "For land man."

"That's high praise."

"Yes." She considered him. "Down there. You..." She hunted for it, and didn't have it, and put her hand flat on the water between them and smoothed it, slowly, as if it were a sheet on a bed. "Not like up."

He thought about it. He looked down past his own arms into the green, where the anchor was, and the chain, and the light, and he could still feel it, the place where his legs had stopped hurting, like a warm room you've just come out of into the cold.

"It calls me," he said. He hadn't meant to say anything. "The water. It always has. Since the first time I went over a ship's side." He moved one hand, a little, on the gunwale. "I'm at peace down there. I don't know why. I never have been up here. Not really. Not for long."

She didn't say anything to that. She looked at him a while longer with her chin on her forearms. There was a little colour at her throat that she didn't do anything about, and that he took to be the cold.

Then Finn said, "Up you come, the both of you, I've only the one towel," and Imani said, "Give it to him, she doesn't feel the cold," and Ilenne said, in Kaldo, quite clearly, "I feel," and took the towel, and they were all of them a long while getting back to the steps.
