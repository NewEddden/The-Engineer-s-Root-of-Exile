Chapter 165 - Assembly

He knew the numbers.

He had known them before he read them, from the moment Imani's sheet went down on top of his mains and he saw the camp's pit and the straight line going away from it, south, down the fall of the flat. He had drawn that line. He had drawn it in pencil in the summer, fast, on the back of a mains sheet, because there were eight hundred men and their families coming onto the thorn ground within the week and they needed a drain before they needed anything else, and he had cut the drain, and taken the overflow off the pit and run it down the fall to the marsh, and written temporary, until the beds beside it, because the beds were not drawn yet, because he had not chosen the ground.

He had not walked the ditch to its end. He had never been below the edge of the flat on that side. The survey stopped there. His room stopped there. And the lower water's four wells sat on a gravel shelf just past the place where everything he knew about the ground ran out.

He read the margin. The weeks, the sick. Eleven dead. Seven children.

It was the first thing he had ever done in Kaldo. Before the soap, before the foundry, before the wall or the board or anybody at the table but Ferra and Bram. He had come to a town that buried its children every summer and been told it was the heat, and he had walked the north well lanes and seen that it was not the heat, and he had made the town boil its water and dig its privies away from its wells, and the children had stopped dying. He had built everything else on that. It was the one thing he had never once doubted he had done right.

He had caused it. The same death, the same way, out of a pencil line.

He did not argue. There was nothing to argue. Imani stood at his shoulder in her wet coat, not sitting, and the whole table sat and looked at the sheet, and he found that his mouth was dry and that he had to wet it before anything would come out.

"The ditch comes up tomorrow," he said. "The overflow goes into a pit on the flat, lined, and gets carted, till the beds are cut. The wells are shut. The hamlets drink from a cart off the market tower till we can dig new ones on the other side of the stream." He looked at the numbers again. "The beds get drawn this week. Not the spring. This week. Below the lower water, below the hamlets, below everything, where the ground's ours and nothing drinks downstream of it but the sea."

Nobody said anything.

"And they'll cost what they cost," he said, "and we'll pay it now."

"Out of what?" said Ferra. She said it quietly. It was the only question she ever asked.

"I'll tell you when they're drawn."

The ditch came up the next morning. He was there when it did, with a spade, in the rain, with forty men from the camp who had been told why and had come anyway. He did not say anything to any of them about why he had a spade. By noon the ditch was filled at the top and a pit dug on the flat and lined with the ridge's clay, and by dusk the first cart had gone down to it and come back.

The wells were shut that week. He did it himself, with Imani. They went down to the gravel shelf together with Bram's men and a cart of timber, and capped all four, and nailed a board on each with a notice on it, printed, that said in large letters THIS WELL IS SHUT, and under it, smaller, Drink from the cart at the green, boiled. By order of the barony, for the fever. And a picture, because half the hamlets could not read: a well with a bar across it, and a cart, and a cup.

Imani did not speak to him the whole morning, except about the wells.

He drew the beds in four nights.

He drew them properly, the way he had drawn the city, all of it before a spade. Settling first: three long shallow tanks in a row, lined with the new lime that set under water, each lower than the one before, so that what came down from the city went into the first and sat, and the heavy part of it went to the bottom, and what ran off the top went into the second and sat, and so on. And below the tanks, a farm. Forty acres of the flat ground on the far side of the salt marsh, below the lower water, below the hamlets, ditched and drained, where what came off the last tank was let out onto the land in turns and the land drank it and grew grass and the grass was cut for hay.

It was the least glamorous thing he had ever drawn. It was a place for filth to go and stop being filth. There was nothing in it anybody would ever come to look at.

It came to eleven Platinum.

He took the figure to Ferra and she worked it again from the bottom and got eleven Platinum and four Gold, and he did not argue with the four Gold. It was the lime, and the stone for the tank walls, and the ditching of forty acres of marsh, and the men, and the men were the most of it. The House's draw would not cover it; the House lent against the city and the works, and this was neither. The barony's purse could pay eight. It could not pay eleven and four and buy grain in the spring for the thin harvest and pay the harvest bounty and the mages and the kilns.

"Then we ask," he said.

"Ask who?"

"Everybody who drinks."

The assembly was held in the market square on the first dry day of the winter, because there was no room in Kaldo big enough to hold it.

He had asked for it. That was the thing he had not done since the child-wage winter years ago, when Hollis had stood up at the back of a hall and asked the one question that mattered. He had not needed to. Kaldo had not been asked for anything since that it did not understand. Now he was going to ask it for money for a thing most of it would never see and would not understand if it did, and he was going to do it in the open, standing, in front of everybody.

They came. The island came, filling the square and the lanes round it, the old families and the new, three to a doorstep. The camp came up the river road from the flat in carts and on foot, hundreds of them, men from four baronies with their families behind them, and stood at the back of the square and along the wall. The lower water came, all of it that could walk. They came up the road from the marsh in a body and stood together at the east side of the square by the market tower, where the water came out of the brass taps, and they did not mix with anybody.

Ferra had a table by the church steps with her book on it. Ward stood behind Emeric. The rest of the table stood on the church steps where everybody could see them. Imani stood at the end of the row, apart, in her grey coat, with her hands folded.

He did not stand on anything. He stood on the cobbles in front of the church steps where everybody could see him, and he told them.

He told them what had happened. He told them about the ditch and the pit and the fan at the top of the marsh and the gravel shelf and the wells. He told them that he had drawn the ditch, himself, in the summer, in a hurry, and had not walked it to its end, and that the fever at the lower water had come down it. He did not say it was a mistake. He said I drew it. He told them how many were dead. He said the number out loud. Eleven, seven of them children. He said the names, because Doran had given him the names, and he had learned them the night before so that he would not have to read them.

It was very quiet in the square when he finished the names.

Then he told them about the beds. What they were, where they would go, what they would do. That they were the part of the city nobody would ever see and the only part that would keep the camp and the city from doing to the lower water what the ditch had done. That the lower water would have new wells, across the stream, sited again, uphill of everything. And that the whole of it cost eleven Platinum and four Gold.

He said the figure out loud. Most of the square had never heard a sum like it said in one breath. Some of them had never heard of a Platinum.

"The barony can pay eight," he said. "It'll pay eight. It can't pay the rest this year without taking bread off the table in the spring, and the spring's already going to be thin." He did not look at Hollis, at the front of the crowd. "So I'm asking. Everybody who drinks in this barony will drink from what these beds make safe. The island and the camp and the lower water. I'm asking every household that draws water from a barony tap or a barony well or a barony cart to pay a Copper a month for three years. A Copper a month. That's the price. And at the end of three years the beds are paid for, and they're yours, and nobody's children dies of a pencil line again."

Nobody spoke for a long time.

Then a man at the front of the lower-water crowd by the market tower said, "A Copper."

He was a big man in a fisherman's smock, with a face burned dark by the wind off the salt. Emeric did not know him. He stood with his arms at his sides and his feet planted on the cobbles, and he said it again, louder.

"A Copper. You're asking me for a Copper. For the privilege."

"I'm asking everybody," said Emeric.

"I buried my girl on the Tuesday," said the man. "Four years old. She drank from Imani's well. It was a good well. We were proud of it. We were the first in the south with a well to the standard. Imani dug it. You paid for it." He did not look at Imani. He looked at Emeric. "And your camp shat in it. And now you want a Copper a month from me so it won't shit in the next one."

"Yes," said Emeric.

The man stared at him.

"Yes," said Emeric again. "That's what I'm asking. I'm not going to tell you it's fair. It isn't fair. You didn't do anything and your girl's dead and I drew the ditch. If there were a way to pay it without asking you, I'd pay it. There isn't. So I'm asking. And I'm asking you to your face, here, in front of everybody, so that nobody can say later I did it behind a notice."

The man in the smock stood there with his fists at his sides and his face working.

Behind him a woman spoke. It was the eel-trap woman, Tollin's mother. Emeric knew her face. She had two sons in the ground this year, one in the sailors' ground above Rowena's harbour and one outside the wall by the lake.

"I'll pay it," she said.

The man in the smock turned round and looked at her.

"I'll pay it," she said, not loudly, to him, and not to Emeric at all. "I'll pay it because I've got two more, and they drink." She looked past him at Emeric. "And I'll pay it because you came down here and said it. Sethe never came down here. Wexler never came down here. Nobody ever came down to the lower water to say what they'd done." She folded her arms. "I'll pay my Copper. And I'll watch where it goes."

"The Chancellor's book is open," said Emeric. "Anybody can come and read where it goes. Any day."

The man in the smock turned back to Emeric. He stood there a while longer. Then he spat on the cobbles, deliberately, at his own feet, and did not say yes, and did not say no, and turned and walked back into the lower-water crowd.

Others spoke after that. Some of them from the camp, men from Milford and Wayford who had come for the work and did not see why they should pay for Kaldo's drains, and some of them from the island, who had never drunk from the lower water's wells and never would. And some from the lower water, who had buried children. They said what they thought and he stood on the cobbles and let them say it, all of it, to his face, for most of the afternoon, and did not answer anything that was not a question, and answered every question.

At the end the square did not vote. Kaldo had never voted on anything. But Ferra's table by the church steps had a line of people in front of it by the time the light went, putting their names in her book beside a Copper, and Hobb and Wenna and Dace and Ilsa were all four taking names as fast as they could write, and the line went back across the square to the market tower.

Not everybody. Not the man in the smock.

Emeric stood on the cobbles until the line was gone. Ward stood behind him the whole time. When it was dark and the square was empty Ward said, "You can sit down now," and Emeric found that he could not remember when he had last sat down, and that his legs did not want to.

Ward said the other thing at the table that night.

It was a short table. Everybody was tired. Ferra read off the day's count, which was a little over half the households in the barony, and said that the rest would come or would not and she would chase them. Then there was a silence while everybody looked at nothing.

"The roof," said Ward.

Emeric looked at him.

"The ward roof." Ward did not look at Imani, who was at her place at the end of the table, sitting this time, with her hands folded. "Plate comes off the rolls next week. My men's breastplates are next. They can wait. They've waited years. Put the roof before them."

Nobody said anything. Imani did not move.

"It's been raining on the end room," said Ward, flatly, as if he were reporting a gate. "I walked past it on the round. There's a pail."

"The roof goes before the breastplates," said Emeric.

Ferra wrote it. Imani looked down the table at Ward for a moment, and Ward looked back at her, without any expression at all, and then looked at the door.

He built the colour that week, in the evenings, in the finishing room, and he did not tell anybody what he was building until it was done.

It was not a new machine. That was the thing he had understood in the spring, when Imani first said the plates were grey and not enough, and he had put it on the list for later, behind everything else, because it was not urgent. The printer was the printer. It moved a head along rails to a place and put ink down, and moved again. It did not know or care what the ink was.

What it needed was three heads, or one head three times, and three inks, and a way to put a sheet of paper back exactly where it had been, to the width of a hair, three times running.

The inks were Colm's and the finishing room's between them. He had had them trying colours since the summer, on the list for later: a red from madder root, which Saban could get from the coast; a blue from woad, which grew on the lake farms and had always grown there; a yellow from weld, which grew wild in every hedge in the south and which nobody had ever bothered with because it faded. He did not need them to last a hundred years. He needed them to last as long as a student took to learn a spleen.

The registration was the stones.

He had written the printer's page stones in the first year of it to lay a page down in black, one pass. He wrote three new ones now, in the delve, in the finishing room, over four nights with Wenna at the door counting. A stone for each colour. And a fourth, smaller, that did nothing but hold the sheet: that knew where every corner of the sheet had been at the start of the first pass and would not let the frame close until every corner was there again, to the hair, for the second, and the third. He had never written a stone to do that before. It took him two nights to make it hold, and he was more tired at the end of it than he had been since the barrel.

Then he drew the plate.

He drew it himself, off Imani's notes from the spring, and off what he knew, which was a great deal more than he had ever told her he knew, from another world, from books with pictures in them that he had looked at once at a desk and never thought of since. He drew it three times: once for each colour, in the delve, laid over one another, the red and the blue and the yellow, each where it should go, so that where the red and the blue lay on each other they made a dark reddish purple, and where the yellow and the red lay they made the colour of a healthy liver, and where only the blue lay thin it made the grey-blue of a vein.

He printed it on the ninth evening, three passes, with the finishing room empty and the lamp lit.

It was a spleen.

He had chosen it because it was the one organ Imani had said, in the spring, that nobody could learn off a grey plate. It had no shape to speak of. It was a fist-sized lump tucked up under the ribs on the left, and in the grey it was a lump outlined in black like every other lump. In life it was a colour nothing else in the body was: a deep dark reddish purple, like a bruise, like a plum, like old wine in a cup. A student who knew that colour would find it in a body in a breath. A student who did not would cut through it looking for something else.

It came off the third pass dark reddish purple. The edges were true. Where it lay against the grey-blue of the vessels at its root, the two colours met and did not bleed. He held it up to the lamp and looked at it for a long time.

He hung it on the drying rack with the others, and went to bed, and did not sleep much.

He did not take it to her. He sent Wenna in the morning to the long ward to say there was something in the finishing room for the Secretary of Health, when she had a moment.

She came at noon, in her apron, with her sleeves rolled, from the ward. He was at the far end of the finishing room by the black printer, with Nevin's second punch-cutter, doing nothing that needed doing. He did not go over to her.

She went to the rack.

She stood in front of it and looked at the plate for a long time. She did not touch it. She stood with her hands at her sides and looked at the spleen, dark reddish purple under the lamp, with its vessels at its root in grey-blue and its name beside it in small black letters in Orrin's hand. Then she looked along the rack, at the other sheets, drying, at the same plate a dozen times, the same colour on every one of them.

He did not say anything. He had thought about what to say, all night. He had thought about I'm sorry, and about it should have been in the spring, and about the roof, and about the ditch, and the pencil line, and the eel-trap woman and the man in the smock who had spat on the cobbles. There was nothing in any of it that would not have been an excuse. So he said nothing, and stood at the far end of the room, and she stood at the rack.

She put her hand out, at last. Not on the plate. On the rack's frame beside it, flat, on the wood, the way she put her hand on a patient's arm when she wanted him to be still.

She stood there like that for a while longer.

Then she said, without turning round, "The liver next. Then the bowel. Tamsin can't tell a healthy bowel from a sick one off the grey." She took her hand off the rack. "And the lung. The lung's the worst."

"The liver next," he said.

She nodded, once, still not looking at him. Then she went back to the ward.

Saban brought his first finding on Vantic three days later, and he brought it to a table with only Emeric and Ward at it, in the long room, after the others had gone.

"It's not much," he said.

He put a sheet of paper on the table. It had very little on it, in his own quick merchant's hand.

"Vantic," said Saban. "The name. Ducatis keeps registers of its people. Every House does. Who they took on, and when, and from where, and who stood for them. The clerks' register, the factors' register, the principals'. I've got a man who's seen them. He's seen the House's own copies, not the ones they show." He tapped the sheet. "Vantic is in the factors' register from the year she was nineteen. Taken on as a factor's clerk at the Ducatis house on the coast, not the capital. Stood for by a principal who died two years later. Before that she's not in anything. Not the clerks' register. Not the apprentices'. Not the school the House keeps for its people's children. No father's name in the House books, no mother's. She came to the House grown."

"That happens," said Ward.

"It happens with a clerk off the street," said Saban. "It doesn't happen with a woman who's a principal as young as she is and draws the House's largest instrument in the south. The House doesn't do that for a clerk off the street. Somebody stood for her who mattered, and the man they wrote down as standing for her is dead." He turned the sheet round. "And the second thing. Her letters. The House's letters go by the House's riders, and I know every one of them, and I know the road. Her own letters, the private ones, don't go by the House. They go by a courier of her own. One man. He comes to Kaldo on the quarter days, same as her, and takes her letters, and goes. I've had him followed. He doesn't ride north to the capital. He rides west. He's lost at the coast road past Milford, every time, and my men can't find where."

He sat back.

"That's all," he said. "That's the whole of it. A year and more."

Emeric looked at the sheet.

It was very little. It was a woman who had not existed before she was nineteen, and a man who rode west. He sat with it in front of him and thought about Vantic at the long-room table in the spring, looking at the loan figure for longer than it took her to write it. And Vantic at the same table, when he asked her which the House would do, saying I've never known. And Vantic at supper in the autumn of last year, saying my brother would lose his mind, and then saying nothing else about him ever again. And Vantic at the loan table this autumn, with her finger on the gravity line in Ferra's book, not writing the answer down.

He could have sent for her. She would be at Kaldo for the winter quarter day in a fortnight. He could have put Saban's sheet on the table in front of her with Ward at the door and asked her who she was.

"Keep looking," he said.

Saban looked at him.

"Keep looking," said Emeric. "Don't let her know. The courier, the coast road, the principal who stood for her and died. Find out where he rides to."

"And when she comes for the quarter day?"

"She'll come for the quarter day. She'll copy what the House needs. She'll go." He folded the sheet and gave it back. "I'm not going to ask her. Not yet."

Saban took the sheet. He did not say anything, but he looked at Emeric in a way Emeric had seen him look before, at the long-room table, when Ferra had said something about a case getting heavier.

Ward, at the end of the table, said nothing either.

Emeric knew why he had decided. He knew most of it was sound. A woman who had hidden who she was for as long as anybody had known her and had never once, in all that time, done anything against Kaldo that he knew of, was not a woman you frightened into running by putting a sheet on a table. If she ran, she ran with everything she had copied, and whatever she was drawing round in every clause she wrote would stop being drawn round. It was better to know more first.

And he knew that some of it was not sound, and that the part that was not sound was that he liked her. He liked her dry voice and her small neat hand and the way she very nearly smiled when Ferra laughed at a table. He did not want to put a sheet in front of her and watch her face close.

He knew that. He decided anyway.

The colour plate was still on the rack that night when he went down to the finishing room to see about the liver.

It had dried. The dark reddish purple had gone a shade darker as it dried, and a little richer. There was a lamp on the table by the rack, turned low. And beside the plate, on the rack's frame, somebody had left a mark in the fine dust that settled on everything in the finishing room: the print of a hand, flat, with the fingers spread, as if it had been laid there and held there for a while.

It was a small hand. He knew whose.

He looked at it for a long time. Then he left it where it was, and did not dust the rack, and went to his bench to begin the liver.
