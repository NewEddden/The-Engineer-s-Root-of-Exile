Chapter 165 - The Harvest Dance

The first grain train from Milford came into the yard below the works on the twenty-third of September a little before noon, and Paxton was on it, in the first wagon, on a plank, with a sack of his own wheat on his knees.

Nobody had asked him to come. The steward of Milford did not leave Milford in harvest; he had said so at his own table, to Ferra, in the summer, when she asked him to send the first train's tally up the wire. He had sent the tally up the wire. Then he had got on the train.

It was twelve wagons, which was all the Milford line had, and Odo's engine from Grayrock to pull them, borrowed for the week and run out along the new rails west at dawn with Bram on the footplate; and every wagon was full of Milford's grain in sacks, wheat off the bench and barley off the half of the bottoms the drains had dried in time, more than Milford had ever had to send anywhere in a harvest in Paxton's lifetime. It came over the old stone bridge with its whistle going and the whole of the lower ward on the bank to see it, and into the yard, and stopped, and stood there hissing, with Paxton in the first wagon looking straight ahead.

He got down. He stood in the yard with his sack, a square grey man in his good coat, and looked at the yard, and at the crowd, and at Emeric, who had come down to meet the train, and at Ferra beside him with her book.

"Twelve wagons," said Paxton. "Four hundred and twenty sacks. Less the eight I've kept back for the seed I don't trust your man Farris with." He put the sack down at Ferra's feet. "That one's the steward's. For the castle. It came off the bottoms." He looked at it. "The bottoms have never given a sack of anything but sedge since my grandfather drained them wrong." He was quiet. "The bay's got a granary in it. Did you know? With a rail up to the door."

"I did," said Emeric.

"I'm told I'm to report Monday," said Paxton. "I'll report Monday." He did not move. He stood in the yard by the sack and looked at the twelve wagons, and his square grey face did not do anything at all, and Emeric understood that the steward of Milford, who had been a baron and had been on the wrong side of a war and had lost the barony his family had held for six generations, had got on a train he had not been asked to get on so that he could stand in Kaldo's yard and watch Kaldo's men unload his harvest, and see it with his own eyes, and that it was the most he was ever going to say about it to anybody.

Ferra wrote something in her book. She looked up.

"Will you stay for the festival, steward?" she said. "It's Saturday."

Paxton looked at her for some while.

"I don't dance," he said.

"Nobody in this town does," said Ferra. "They do it anyway."



The third Commencement was on the Friday, in the square, and the town came, the way it had come the first year and the second.

Fourteen this year. Sela read their names from the roll in front of the teachers' bench in her dark dress, and each one stood and came forward and chose: medicine under Imani, four of them, which was more than had ever chosen it; the books under Ferra, three; the works under Bram, five, two of them girls, which nobody in the square remarked on now; Ward's department, one, a narrow serious boy from the dock quarter who had watched Ward's yard through the gate every morning for two years. And one, the last, a farm girl from the west side with clay ground into the creases of her knuckles that would not come out, who stood in front of the teachers' bench and said that she had chosen already, the summer before, and had been working at it, and would like it written down: earth. Under Master Paskin.

Paskin was not on the teachers' bench. He was at the back of the square with his pipe, because he would not sit on a bench in front of a crowd, he had said, if the king himself asked him to. When the girl said his name the whole square turned round to look at him, and he took his pipe out of his mouth and looked back at them, and said, loudly enough for the square to hear: "She's still falling on the door."

The square laughed. The girl went red to the roots of her hair, and grinned, and sat down.

Pell was not among them. She was fifteen, and had a year yet. She was on the second bench with the rest of the Seers' Table, with her slate on her knee, and Emeric saw from the teachers' bench that she was not listening to Sela at all. She was writing something down. Afterward, going past, he saw what: a column of figures, dishes and days, and at the bottom of the column, in a box, a number.

"Forty-one," said Pell, without looking up. "Of a hundred. The dishes with the mould on them didn't go bad. Forty-one out of fifty." She looked at the slate. "Out of the fifty without it, six." She looked up at him then. "Mistress Imani says it's not a hundred yet. She says forty-one out of fifty's a reason to do the next hundred." She went back to her slate. "So I'm doing the next hundred."



The stewards ate at the castle that night, because for once all three of them were in Kaldo at the same time: Paxton because he had got on a train, Aster in on the coast boat with Wayford's figures a week early, and Bryda down on the evening train for the festival with a basket of Grayrock pears for Maren that Maren had not asked for and would use. Ferra said that if they were going to be in the town they might as well be at a table, and set one in the long room, with the six at it.

It was Bryda who asked. She had watched the Commencement from the back of the square, standing, and over the soup she had the look of a woman who had been adding something up since noon and had got to the bottom of the column.

"Fourteen," she said. "Where do they go?"

"To us," said Imani.

"And who's us?" Bryda put her spoon down. "I've sat at this end of your table three times now, and I know the figures, and I don't know who I'm talking to. I'd like to."

There was a small pause, the kind a table has when nobody wants to go first. Bram went first, because he was hungry and wanted to get back to his soup.

"The works," he said. "Forty-odd on the floor. Nine of them off Sela's bench, and those nine are the ones I'd keep if I could only keep ten. Tess has three more on her line." He picked up his spoon. "Five more from today. Two of them girls. That's mine."

"The ward," said Imani. "Both wards, and the lying-in room. Eleven nurses and a midwife. Seven of the nurses stood up in that square." She did not look up from her bread. "Four more tonight. I'll have them washing floors on Monday and hating me by Wednesday, and the ones who stop hating me by Christmas I'll keep."

"Six clerks in the counting room," said Ferra. "Four of them from the square. And the gate tower's slips, which are every child in the school by turns." She wrote nothing. "Three more today."

"Four in the yard," said Ward. "Five now."

Bryda waited for more. There was no more.

"One," said Armand, from the end, with his spectacles on his forehead. "In three years. There is not another child in Sela's room with the faculty, and I have tested every one of them twice, and some of them three times because they asked." He looked at his soup with something like regret. "Stone work doesn't choose by wanting."

Everyone looked at Saban.

"I don't count mine out loud," said Saban pleasantly. "It spoils them."

Bryda looked along the table at the six of them, slowly, the way she had looked at a pay sheet that did not add up, before she said which line.

"Then send me one," she said. "Out of all that. A mason who knows a drain, and somebody who's stood in that ward. Grayrock has a hill full of men and one woman who sets bones, and she's seventy, and her hands shake."

"And a teacher, if there's one spare," said Aster. He said it to the tablecloth, carefully, the way he said everything. "Wayford has a church that teaches the catechism and calls it reading."

Paxton ate his soup and said nothing at all.

Emeric looked at the six faces down his table before he answered, because the answer was theirs as much as his. Bram's, with his spoon stopped. Imani's, already counting her eleven. Ward, who did not need to look at anybody.

"Not yet," he said.

Bryda's eyebrows went up a fraction.

"Everyone at this table is teaching two while doing the work of three," he said. "The ones off that bench are a year into their trades, two at the most. If I send you one now, I send you a boy who knows half of a drain and has nobody up the line to ask about the other half, and he lays it wrong, and your rows hate drains for a generation." He turned his cup. "A year or two. When they can teach it without us."

"And Kaldo's ways stay in Kaldo a little longer," said Saban, to nobody, "which I'll not weep over."

Bryda looked at Emeric for a long moment.

"A year or two," she said.

"Yes."

"I'll remember that," said Bryda, and picked up her spoon.

Ferra had her book open by her plate. She did not write while people were eating, as a rule; she wrote now, a line, small and square, and Emeric, sitting at her elbow, read it upside down: the date, and GRAYROCK, A MASON, A NURSE. WAYFORD, A TEACHER. And against it, in the column where she put what had been done about a thing, one word.

DEFERRED.

It was a fair word. It was the right answer that autumn, and he believed it, and the soup was good, and Paxton asked Bram across the table whether Odo's engine would be back at Milford by Monday, and Bram said Tuesday, and Paxton said Monday, and the evening went on.



The festival was on the Saturday, the last of the month, in the square, and the whole of four baronies seemed to have come to it.

That was the line. Emeric had not thought of it until he stood on the keep stair at four in the afternoon and looked down at the square filling up, and saw faces in it he did not know: Grayrock men in their dark mine coats, who had come down the line on the morning train with their wives; Milford farmers in the smocks they wore at home, standing in a knot by the church with Paxton among them and Hollis in the middle of them, talking; a man from Wayford in a merchant's hat, who had come in on the coast boat and been told at the river inn that there was a festival and had stayed. The line had made it a day's outing from Grayrock and an afternoon's from Milford. And they had come.

There was beer, the castle's, in barrels on a cart, at a copper a cup. There was Maren's barrow, both of them, the second one new that week, with a queue that went twice round the well. There were the four baronies' stewards on a bench by the church with cups in their hands, Bryda very straight in her dark dress, Paxton square and silent, Aster in his clerk's coat, and the reeve from the dock quarter, who was not a steward but had sat down on the bench anyway; Ferra had looked at the four of them sitting side by side and written something in her book and not shown it to anybody.

And there was music. There was always music at a harvest; Kaldo had had a harvest festival every year since there had been a Kaldo, the old men said, except the plague years, and every year the music had been the same three men from the dock quarter with a fiddle and a pipe and a drum, playing the same seven tunes. They were there. But this year there were five of them, because the Grayrock men had brought a fiddle of their own and a man who played it, and he and the Kaldo fiddler had looked at each other across the square for a quarter of an hour with great suspicion and then begun to play the same tune from opposite sides of the well, and then the same tune from the same side of it, and by dusk the five of them were playing tunes nobody in Kaldo had heard before, and the square was full of people dancing to them.

Emeric did not dance.

He had never danced. Not in this life. The prince who had stopped casting at eight had been taught to dance at ten, as royal children were, by a dancing master with a cane, and had stopped dancing at fourteen with everything else; and the drunk who came after him had danced at court functions in a way that the court had found very funny, which had been the point. And in the other life Marcus had danced twice, at weddings, badly, and been told so.

So he stood at the side of the square by the steps of the old exchange with a cup of the castle's beer, and watched, and that was where Imani found him.

"You're not dancing," she said.

"I don't."

"Neither do I." She took the cup out of his hand and gave it to the boy from the dock quarter with ink on his chin, who was standing nearby and took it with an expression of pure astonishment. "I've been on my feet in the ward since five this morning, and I've three in the lying-in room who'll want me before midnight, and I can't dance. My mother tried to teach me at the north well when I was twelve and gave up." She took his hand. "Come on."

He went. There was nothing else to do. She walked him out into the middle of the square among the dancers, with half of Kaldo seeing it and the other half being told, and put his hand on her waist and took his other hand in hers and held it up at the height of her shoulder, in the way the dock quarter danced, and looked at him.

She was tall. He had always known it. He had not known, until she stood in front of him in the square with her hand on his shoulder, that she had only to lift her chin a little to look him in the eye. Hers were darker than he had thought. Nearly black, with the lamps in them.

"You lead," said Imani.

"I don't know how."

"Neither do I. That's why you lead." She was smiling. It was not a smile he had often seen on her; it came up slowly, from somewhere he did not usually get to look. "If we both go wrong, it'll be your fault."

They went wrong. They went wrong at once, and often. The tune was a fast one from Grayrock, and he did not know it, and she did not know it, and the dock quarter's steps did not fit it, and twice he trod on her foot and once she trod on his, hard, on purpose, he was almost sure. And it did not matter. Nobody in the square was dancing well. The Grayrock men danced the way they swung picks, the Milford farmers the way they walked behind a plough, and the Kaldo works danced as if they were all being paid by the step; and in the middle of it the prince and the healer went round and round, badly, with her laughing at him and him laughing at himself, and the square, which had stopped to look, began after a while to look at something else.

When the tune ended she let go of his hand.

"There," she said. She was a little out of breath. "Now you've danced." She looked across the square, past him, at somebody; and then back at him, and her face did the thing it did when she had decided something and was not going to say what. "Go on," she said. "I'll be at the ward." And she went off through the crowd toward the hospital lane with her hair coming down out of its tie at the back, and did not look round.



Ferra was at the stewards' bench.

She had been there all evening, with her book, writing. She was writing when he came across the square to her. He stood in front of the bench, in front of Bryda and Paxton and Aster and the reeve, and Ferra did not look up.

"You'll want to dance," said Bryda, to nobody in particular.

Ferra wrote.

"I've just been told," said Emeric, "that I lead. That if we both go wrong it's my fault."

Ferra stopped writing. She looked up at him then, and he saw her see it: the square, and the dancers, and Imani's back going away down the hospital lane, and him in front of the stewards' bench with his hand half out. He saw her work out exactly what had been done, and by whom, and why, as if she were adding a column, and arrive at the total.

She shut the book. She gave it to Paxton.

"Hold that," she said. Paxton held it. She stood up and took Emeric's hand.

She danced the way she did sums.

He had known she would. Correctly, and slightly ahead. She did not know the Grayrock tune either, but she listened to the first two bars of it with her head on one side, the way she listened to a clerk reading back a column, and by the third bar she had it, and she took him round the square with it as if it were an entry she had made a hundred times. She did not tread on his foot. She did not let him tread on hers; when he was about to, she was not there. She counted under her breath, he realised. One, two, three. One, two, three. He could see her lips move.

"You're counting," he said.

"Of course I'm counting." She did not stop. "How else would anybody know where they are?"

He laughed, and lost the step, and she put him back on it with her hand on his shoulder without missing a count.

The square was looking again. He could feel it, in his sense, which was open thin as it always was now, a field of small warm notes all round them in the dusk: the town, and Grayrock, and Milford, and the man from Wayford in his merchant's hat, all turned toward the middle of the square where the prince who held four baronies was dancing with the smith's daughter who kept his books. He felt the field lean. A ripple went across it, the way a ripple had gone along the river wall on the first Lantern Night when she took his hand.

Ferra felt it too. Not with any sense; with her eyes, which missed nothing. She looked past his shoulder at the square, at the faces, at the stewards' bench, where Paxton sat with her book on his knees and Bryda sat very straight beside him and the man from Wayford stood behind them with his merchant's hat pushed back, watching.

The tune came to its end.

And Ferra, who did sums, and did them correctly and slightly ahead, and had worked out in front of the stewards' bench exactly what was being given to her and by whom, stopped counting, and put her hand up behind his head, and kissed him.

In the middle of the square. In front of the town, and Grayrock, and Milford, and the four baronies' stewards, and the man from Wayford in the merchant's hat. Not long. Long enough that there was no mistaking it for anything else, by anybody, from any side of the well.

The square cheered.

It was not polite. It was the dock quarter's cheer, the one it gave the steamboat and the first train and Fennick's green star, loud and long and from the belly, with whistles in it, and the Grayrock men joined it without knowing quite what they were cheering, and the Milford farmers looked at each other, and the five fiddlers and pipers struck up again on their own, something fast and silly, and somebody on the church steps began to beat a pan.

Ferra went red. He watched it happen, close, from a hand away: red from the collar of her dress, up, past the freckles across her nose and both cheeks, all the way to her hair, until there was no telling where the one stopped and the other started, a deeper red than the line room or the long table, the reddest he had ever seen her.

She did not let go.

She stood in the middle of the square with her hand still on the back of his neck and her face scarlet and the whole of four baronies cheering at her, and looked at him, and did not let go, and did not look away, and said, quite steadily, under the noise:

"Two's even."



It was talked of down every road by the end of the month.

Saban heard it from his man at the Wayford posting house, who had heard it from the man in the merchant's hat, who had gone north on the capital road the next week with a cartload of soap and a story, and told the story at every inn he stopped at. The prince of Kaldo had danced with two women at the harvest. One in the middle of the square, with half the town watching, the healer, who had trodden on his foot. And then the other, the smith's daughter, who kept his books, and kept, it was said, a great deal more than his books; and she had kissed him in front of the whole town and his four stewards and gone red as a beet and not let go.

"They're saying which," said Saban, at the table, with his hat on his knee. "On the road. Which one. They've got bets on it at the river inn." He turned the hat. "The odds have the bookkeeper, two to one."

Ferra did not look up from her book.

"Then they'll lose their money," said Imani, from the other side of the table, very pleasantly. "Both ways."

Saban looked at her. Then at Ferra. Then, very slowly, at Emeric, who was looking at the ceiling.

"Both ways," said Saban, as if trying the words for weight. He put his hat back on his knee. "I'll not tell them that," he said. "I like the inn."
