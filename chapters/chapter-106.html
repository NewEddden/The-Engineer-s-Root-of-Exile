Chapter 106 - Coke

The book was not finished, and he gave it to Ward anyway.

It was forty pages of the good paper from the stationer's in the capital, stitched along one edge with waxed thread because Ferra would not give him another ledger for it, and the last nine pages were blank. He had written it in the week after the seven days, at night, at the table under his window, with Rylan sitting on the end of his bed in his shirt showing him with his hands what the words were for. A man comes at you with a knife held low. A man has you round the neck from behind. A man is on top of you on a floor and you are on your back. Rylan had done each of them slowly in the air in front of him, and then, when Emeric still did not have it, had done it to him, gently, on the boards between the bed and the hearth, and then got up and dusted him off and said, "Write that," and Emeric had written it.

He put it on the guardroom table in front of Ward on the first Monday of the year, at the end of the dawn drill, with the frost still white on the yard outside and Ward's breath still showing in the cold room.

Ward did not pick it up. He read the outside.

Close Quarters, it said, in Emeric's hand. And under it, smaller, in a hand that pressed too hard and ran downhill: Rylan.

"He wanted his name on it," Emeric said. "I told him it was his book."

"Is it?"

"Most of it. The holds are his. The knife is his. The part about stairs is yours, from the yard, from what you told the levy before the war. The part about the boat is mine." He looked at the frost on the window. "I had a bad night on a boat."

Ward opened it. He turned the pages one at a time, without hurrying, the way he had turned the pages of every order Emeric had ever handed him, and Emeric watched him stop on the drawings. There were not many of them, and they were not good. Two men on a floor, one on the other, with arrows. A man's arm round another man's neck, and a smaller drawing beside it of the same arm with the wrist turned, and the word Here.

"Knights don't crawl," Ward said.

"There's no crawling in that one."

"There is crawling on the second page from the end." Ward put his finger on it without looking for it. "A man on his belly, under a cart."

"That's the next book. It got into this one by mistake."

Ward looked at him over the page.

"There are three," Emeric said. "This one. One on moving at night, quietly, and getting close to a man who's awake without him knowing, and what to do when you get there. And one on water." He waited. "Forty-one men in the garrison, Ward. How many can swim?"

Ward did not need to count. He had counted on the night, on the river stair, with the gate crew's skiff half off its rollers and the prince coming across the water on another man's back.

"Nineteen," he said. "Twenty with Rylan. He was not on my roll that night."

"Nineteen. And the night before last year's war, you had every man in the garrison on the wall, and the wall is a hundred feet from a river, and not one of us thought about that." Emeric put his hand flat on the cover of the book. "Every fight we've had, we won with something nobody wrote down. The leg hold was Rylan's. He taught it to six men in the yard that morning because Osric laughed at him. If he hadn't, I'd be at the bottom of the river with a guard on top of me."

Ward closed the book.

He did not say anything else about it, then. He put it inside his coat, against his chest, which was where he kept the things he meant to read, and went out to the yard to stand the levy down. But the next morning Rylan was in the yard at the dawn drill, which he had not been since the boat, and Ward put him in front of the garrison with the book open on the bench behind him, and the garrison stood in the frost and watched Rylan take Osric's knife off him four times running, slowly, and then fast, and nobody laughed.



The coke ovens had been built in December from the tithe houses, which was to say from nothing.

Vantic had walked those houses herself, in October, the empty rows behind the lower ward that the barony had taken against tithe nobody would ever pay, and stood in their doorways with her book while Ferra told her what they were worth, which was nothing, because in a town with a third of its doors still shut nobody would buy a brick. "If it is worth nothing," Vantic had said, "then nothing is under the cap." And had written it down, and it had become the thing in Kaldo that people said when they wanted to build something and had no money, so that by Christmas Bram's yard boys were saying it to each other about a broken barrow.

So the brick came out of three roofless houses on the lane behind the old tannery, and went across the east bridge in Hollis's carts, and became six ovens in a row on the far bank beside the works, behind the engine house, where the smoke would go downriver and out over the marsh and nobody's washing.

They were beehives. That was what they looked like and that was what the book in his head had called them, a mining engineer's handbook with a coal-stained cover that he had read on a train a lifetime ago: low round brick domes, a man's height at the crown, with a hole in the top and a door in the side. You filled one with coal through the top and bricked the door up with a gap left at the head of it, and lit it, and let it burn with almost no air, and the coal did not burn so much as cook. The water came out of it. Then the tar, and a great deal of smoke, and a gas that burned yellow at the top hole like a torch. What was left after two days and two nights, raked out red through the door and quenched with water, was grey and light and full of holes like a sponge, and rang when you dropped it.

Coke. It burned hotter than coal and cleaner than charcoal, and it would hold up the weight of a furnace full of ore without crushing to dust in the bottom, which charcoal would not, and that was the whole of why he wanted it.

Haddon ran them. Nobody had asked him to. He had come up the river on a coal barge in September with his boots black to the ankle, wanting to know what Kaldo did with Grayrock's coal, and had stayed to burn it under the boiler, and when the first oven was bricked he had simply moved his sack out of the boiler pit and into the end of the oven shed, and slept there.

"Grayrock's coal's good for this," he told Emeric, on the cold morning in the second week of January when the third oven was drawn. He said it the way he said everything about coal, as a fact that it would be rude to argue with. "Sticky. It swells in the heat and runs together, so it comes out in a lump and not in crumbs. Not all coal does." He kicked a piece of coke on the brick floor and listened to it ring. "Your Bryda doesn't know she's got it. She sells it for burning in grates."

"She's not my Bryda."

"She sends you a barge a fortnight," said Haddon, "at a price my father wouldn't have sold a bucket for. She's somebody's."

The contract had been Bryda's harder terms of the summer: grain to her landing first, ore weighed at her pithead with Kaldo's man watching, three years renewable. The coal had come into it at the end almost as an afterthought, a line Ferra had added in her own hand under the ore, because Ferra added lines under things. Emeric had been grateful for the line every fortnight since.

Tess was at the end of the row in her chair, wrapped to the chin in a blanket and Bram's old coat, watching the top hole of the fourth oven, where the gas was burning off in a long yellow tongue that leaned with the wind.

"You're burning it," she said, when he came up beside her.

"It's what you do with it."

"It's light." She did not take her eyes off it. "That's a flame as long as my arm, coming out of a hole, by itself, all day and all night for two days, and nobody's feeding it. And you've got six of them." She looked at him at last. "You could put that in a pipe."

He had thought it. He had thought it in December, standing where she was standing, watching the first one burn. Coal gas. On his other world, cities had been lit by it for a hundred years before anybody strung a wire, pipes under every street and a lamp on every corner, a man going round at dusk with a pole. You did not cook coal in a beehive to get it. You cooked it in a closed iron box, a retort, and led the gas off through pipes and washed it, and kept it in a great tank that rose and fell on water.

"Not this year," he said.

"Why not?"

"Because we've got lamps already." He nodded up the river, at the wall, where in the day the eight lamps stood dark on their posts. "And because a gas pipe that leaks under a street full of thatch is a thing I'd want to have thought about for a long while first. And because it's a retort, and pipe, and a holder, and Ferra will tell you what that costs before I've finished the sentence."

Tess considered the flame.

"I'm going to write it down anyway," she said, and did, on the slate she kept under the blanket, and put the slate back.

Ferra did not want the gas. Ferra wanted the tar.

She came across the bridge at noon with a column for him, because the ovens had been running a month and that was how long she gave anything before she made it account for itself, and stood in the oven shed in her old coat with her hands in her sleeves and looked at the black stuff running out of the cooling pipe into the tub at the end, where Haddon had set a pipe and a tub to catch it because it had been running down the bank into the river and Imani had come across the bridge and stood on the bank and looked at him.

"What do you do with it?" she said.

"Nothing, yet."

"Fletcher would." She did not look up from the tub. "Every boat on the coast is paid for with pitch. Pine pitch, from the north, and it's dear, and it comes down on the salt boat in barrels." She turned her head and looked at him then, and he saw her see that he had not thought of it. "That's a barrel a week. You're pouring it in a tub."

"I wasn't going to keep it in the tub."

"You were going to keep it in the tub until somebody told you what it was for," said Ferra, "and then say you'd always meant it for that."

Haddon laughed, once, at the far end of the shed, and turned it into a cough.

So the tar went into Ferra's book on its own line, and the gas went onto Tess's slate, and the coke went into a heap behind the shed under a roof of the tithe-house slates, where it rose all through January into a long grey bank that rang when the boys walked on it, which they were told not to do and did.



Saban came in on the twentieth, from the north road, at dusk, on a horse that had been somebody else's that morning, and went to the exchange before he went anywhere else, which meant it was business.

Emeric was already there. Ferra had sent Hale up the hill for him when Saban's hat came through the South Gate, because she knew the hat at two hundred yards.

Saban did not take his coat off. He sat on the stool by the trestle with his hat on his knee and his boots steaming in front of Ferra's brazier, and drank the cup she had put into his hand, and told it plainly, the way he told a price.

"He's hired," he said. "Sethe. Three companies. Not the kind you pay by the season, the kind you pay to sit still and be ready, in their own halls, a day's ride from his. Retainer. It's dearer than wages and it's quieter." He turned the cup in his hands. "Two mages besides. Not hedge men, not like the ones at your gate in the war. Proper. They came down from the north in November and they're living in his hall like guests, and his steward buys their wine."

"You know who they are?"

"I know who sent them. I've a name for the master they answer to." Saban looked at Ferra. "I'll not say it out loud in a room with a window. It's a name that means nothing to me yet, and that's a thing I don't like."

Ferra held out a slip of paper and her pen, and Saban wrote on it, and she read it without any change in her face at all and put it in the box on the left of the trestle, the box that had Paxton's four lines in it, and closed the lid.

"Paxton," Emeric said.

"Read your letter in front of me." Saban drank. "In his own yard, standing up, with his dogs round his feet. Read it twice. Then he folded it and put it in his coat and said there was no answer." He looked at Emeric over the cup. "Then he said, not yet. Then he gave me a horse, because mine was lame. That's his horse you heard come in."

"That's all?"

"That's a man who's lost ninety-one head and a barn and his factor," said Saban, "and gave the man who cost him them a horse." He set the cup down on the trestle. "I'd call that a great deal. I'd not call it an answer."

Ward had come in during it and stood in the door. He did not say anything. He did not need to; Emeric could see him doing the sum. Three companies, a day's ride from Sethe's hall, and two mages, and the roads.

When Emeric went out through the gate passage the next morning to the drill, the rota on the guardroom wall had been taken down and another put up in its place, in Ward's square hand, and every man on it was on half again the nights he had been, and there were two on the river stair where there had been one, and the levy's names were on the bottom of it, with a day against each name to come in and draw a breastplate and a crossbow from bay three and show Ward they knew which end was which.

"I didn't order that," Emeric said.

"No," said Ward.

That was all he said, and it was enough.

Saban went out again two days later, north-west, on the Milford road, on his own lame horse which had come right in the barony's stable, leaving Paxton's in it. Emeric walked him to the gate. He watched him go under the arch and out across the moat bridge with his hat pulled down and his collar up, a small dry man on a brown horse, getting smaller, the man in Kaldo who was outside its walls more often than anyone.

He had not told Saban about the room. He was not going to, yet. Nobody could make a man go into it. But anybody with a fire and enough time could make a man talk about one.



The canal ring froze on the night of the twenty-second.

It had iced at the edges twice already that winter and gone back to water by noon, but on the twenty-second the wind went round into the north-east and stayed there, and in the morning the ring was white and solid all the way round the inner ward, and the children from the north well were out on it before Sela had the school door open, sliding on their boots under the little arched bridges and shrieking, and Sela stood on the bank with her arms folded and told them all that they would go through and drown and she would not come in after them, and did not call them off.

Emeric went down to the ring at noon with a bucket and a hatchet and came back with the bucket full of ice.

Maren watched him come into her kitchen with it. She had her sleeves rolled up and her arms in a trough of dough, and she did not take them out.

"That's my canal," she said.

"It's the barony's canal."

"It's the canal I get my water out of when the pump's frozen, which it is." She watched him set the bucket down on the end of her table. "What's it for?"

He told her. Cream, from the baron's farm, out of the cold room off the dairy, a jug of it. Honey. Two eggs. A pinch of salt in the cream and a fistful of it in the ice. A tin from Bram's shop, a tall one with a lid that fitted, that Bram had soldered for him that morning and asked no questions about, because Bram had stopped asking questions about tins.

Maren took her arms out of the dough.

"You're going to freeze cream," she said. "On purpose."

"Yes."

"In January." She wiped her hands on her apron. "When the whole town's trying to keep things from freezing."

"Yes."

She folded her arms and leaned on the end of the table and watched him, which he understood was the most she was going to do to help, and was more than he had expected.

He broke the ice up small with the back of the hatchet in a sack and packed it round the tin in the bucket, a layer of ice and then a fistful of salt and then ice, and the salt bit into it at once and the ice began to go grey and wet and slushy in the bucket, and the side of the bucket went so cold it hurt to hold.

"You're melting it," Maren said.

"That's what the salt does. It melts the ice, and to melt it has to take the heat from somewhere, and it takes it from the tin." He put the cream in the tin, and the honey and the eggs beaten into it, and put the lid on, and began to turn the tin by its lid, round and round in the slush, and every so often stopped and took the lid off and scraped down the sides with a spoon. "The ice by itself only gets as cold as ice. With the salt in, it goes colder than ice ever does."

"Why?"

That was not Maren. That was Tess, in the doorway to the yard, in her chair, with Bram behind it. They had come up from the works for the noon bowl, as they did on days when the forge was down, and had stopped in the door because the prince was squatting on the kitchen flags turning a tin in a bucket like a man churning butter.

"Because salt water has to be colder than fresh before it'll freeze," Emeric said, turning. "The sea doesn't freeze when the river does. So the salt gets into the ice and the ice turns to salt water, and the salt water's happy to sit there colder than the ice was, and it pulls the heat out of anything that's touching it to make itself water." He scraped the sides of the tin again. They were thickening. "It's the same reason the dock quarter salts its steps."

Tess had the slate out from under her blanket before he finished.

It took most of an hour. His arms ached and his fingers went white and stupid with the cold of the tin, and Bram took a turn without being asked and turned it too fast and was told so and turned it slower, grumbling, and Maren went back to her dough and did not look at them more than once a minute. And then the spoon came up out of the tin with something on it that held its shape.

He gave the first of it to Maren, because it was her kitchen.

She put it in her mouth with the face of a woman who was going to say it needed salt. Then she stood quite still with the spoon in her mouth and did not say anything at all.

Bram had three bowls. He ate the first standing up and the second sitting on the end of the bench and the third slowly, with his eyes shut, and when it was gone he looked into the bowl as though there might be more of it at the bottom, and said that it was cold milk, that was all it was, it was cold milk with honey in, there was nothing to it, and held the bowl out for a fourth.

Tess had one and wrote down the time it had taken.

Ferra came in while Bram was eating his fourth, through the door from the passage, with a sheaf of the wage lines under her arm and a question about the oven men's nights already half out of her mouth, and stopped.

The prince was on the floor of Maren's kitchen with his sleeves wet to the elbow, and her father was sitting on the bench with a bowl, and Tess was writing, and Maren was standing at the table looking at a spoon.

"What is that," Ferra said.

Emeric held out a spoon to her.

She looked at it. She looked at him. Then she put the wage lines down on the end of the table, squared, so that they would not slide, and took the spoon.

He watched her eat it. He watched her not do a sum about it. It went into her mouth and he saw her start to, out of habit, the cream and the honey and the eggs and the hour of his time, and then he saw her stop, and stand there in Maren's kitchen in her old coat with the spoon in her hand, and just eat it, and he thought that he had not seen her do one thing in two years without counting what it cost.

"It's cold," she said, after a while.

"That's the idea."

"In January."

"Maren said that."

"Maren was right." Ferra took another spoonful. Then she looked at the tin, and at the bucket, and at the slush in it going grey, and he saw the sum come back, and was almost glad of it. "How much salt is that?"



He walked her back down to the exchange because the lane from the kitchen gate was ice from end to end, and because he had been trying for a month to find a way to say a thing and had not, and the lane was the first time in a month there had been nobody else in it.

The light was going already, at three in the afternoon. The sky over the river was the colour of a knife. Below them, at the foot of the lane, the town went down to the east wall in steps of white roofs with the smoke standing straight up from every chimney in the still cold, and past the wall the river ran black between white banks, and the water gate was a dark mouth at the bottom of it with the river stair going down into the water.

He had not been down that stair since the morning he went to look at the stone under the top step. He found he was looking at it.

"You were there," he said.

Ferra did not ask where. She had been walking with one hand on the wall of the lane for the ice, and she did not take it off.

"Yes."

"On the stair. You came down with the crossbows. With your hair down." He did not know why he said that. "You came all the way down. And then you stopped three steps from the bottom. And you didn't say anything. You put your shawl round Finn and you went up again after Rylan."

"Yes," she said.

They walked. The ice creaked under their boots. Somewhere below them a door banged and a woman's voice called a child in.

"I don't know why it's bothering me," he said.

That was true, and it was not the whole of it, and he could hear that it was not, and so could she. It had bothered him on the stair with the river running out of his boots, and it had bothered him in front of the guardroom fire wrapped in Ward's blankets, and it had bothered him at the pay window on the last Friday of the year, sitting at the trestle behind her, watching the back of her head. Imani had held his wrist until his hand stopped shaking. Vantic had paced the bank from the float to the marks in the mud and told him her House would be very much obliged. Armand had been frightened, and said so. Ferra had counted to three under her breath and gone home.

And he had wanted, and it was stupid, and he was a grown man and a prince of the realm and he knew it was stupid, for her to have come down the last three steps.

"You'd rather I'd made a noise," Ferra said.

"No."

"Cried. On the stair. In front of the garrison." She said it quite flatly, the way she read a column back to him to make sure she had it right. "With the boy standing there wet."

"No." He stopped. She stopped a step below him, because the lane was steep there and her hand was on the wall, and that put her face nearly level with his for once, and he looked at it and could not tell anything from it at all. "I don't know what I'd rather. That's what I'm saying."

Ferra looked down the lane at the water gate.

"I counted three," she said. "Coming up out of the water. That's what I could do, so I did it." She was quiet. "If I'd come down the last steps I'd have had to do something when I got there. I didn't know what. I'm not Imani. I can't bind a wrist. And there were a dozen men on that stair with crossbows looking at a knight who'd sold you, and every one of them would have seen what I did, whatever it was, and talked about it in the guardroom for a year." She took her hand off the wall, and put it back. "So I gave Finn the shawl. He was the one who was cold."

"I was cold."

"You had Ward's cloak."

He could not argue with that, and did not try.

They went on down. At the corner by the cooper's, where the lane turned along the wall toward the square, she stopped again, and he thought she was going to say goodnight, and she did not.

"I went up to the exchange," she said. "After. I didn't go home. I let myself in with Hale's key and lit the lamp and sat at the trestle." She was looking at the cooper's shutters, not at him. "And I did the sum."

"Which sum?"

"Kaldo without you." She said it the way she would have said the name of a column. "If you'd gone into the river. I wanted to know what would happen. So I wrote it down. The covenant first: the House lent to the barony under your seal, and the barony falls back to the crown on your death, and the crown is your brother, and the House's paper is good against the crown and the crown has never paid a debt it could argue. Then the school, which is your purse. Then the wage lines, the half of them you sign. The ward. The board, and who would mend it. The lamps. Bram's works, which are on your word and nothing else, because I've never had them put on paper and you never asked me to." She stopped. "Nine pages. I did it until it was light."

He did not say anything.

"It didn't come out," Ferra said. "I ran it three ways and it didn't come out any of them. And at the bottom of the last page there was a line I couldn't do anything with at all." She looked at him at last. "The stones. Who cuts them. Who fills the board's stones when they fade. What's in the room and how anybody gets into it if you're not there to take them. I wrote, the stones, and then I wrote, nobody. And I sat and looked at that until the window went grey." She put her hand back on the wall. "Then I burned it. In the brazier. All nine. Because Hale comes in at seven, and I wasn't having Hale read it."

The cold had got into his hands. He put them in his coat.

"That was the worst sum I've ever done," Ferra said. "Worse than the plague. In the plague I only counted what had happened." She looked down the lane at the black water beyond the wall. "So, no. I didn't come down the last steps. I was busy."

He looked at her, and she let him, which she did not often do, and then she looked away first.

"I don't want to do it again," she said. "That's all. If you go in the river again, I'll count you out of it again. That's all I'm promising."

And she went along the wall toward the square, carefully, on the ice, with her hand on the stones, and in at the exchange door, and after a while there was a light in the back room window.

He stood at the corner by the cooper's until his feet were numb.

The stones, and then, nobody. She had been right. She was nearly always right about what a page said, even when it was a page he had not read. If he had stayed in the hold, everything he knew about the stones would have gone down the river with him. The board would have run until its stones faded, and then stopped. The wall would have gone blind, a stretch at a time. And nobody in Kaldo would have known how to cut another, or that there was a room to cut them in, or that the grey stone in a drawer in his room was anything but a court's joke in a velvet case.

Ward had written it on the card in the line room, in his square hand. The prince does not go down alone.

He went back up the hill to the keep, and did not go to his own room. He went to the door off the hall, and knocked.



Armand was in his chair with the blanket and the board, and the fire was low, and he did not look surprised.

"You've come about the twelfth test," he said.

Emeric stopped in the door.

"You have the notebook in your coat," Armand said. "You've had it in your coat since the day you left the capital. You've been carrying it round like a man carries a letter he hasn't the nerve to answer." He put down the book he had not been reading. "Shut the door. It's cold. And sit down, and ask me, and let's have it done."

Emeric shut the door. He sat down on the stool across the fire, and took the notebook out of his coat, and opened it to the last of Armand's own pages, the small cramped hand, the eleven tests over nine years, and under the last of them the strip where the paper had been scraped back to bare and gone rough.

He held it out. Armand did not take it.

"In the ninth year," Armand said, "I fell asleep."

He said it to the fire.

"The tower keeps one of them in the vault, under the stair. A grey stone in a lead box. Every crystal theorist in the tower is given the box at some time or other, the way a boy is given a knot to untie, and every one of them gives it back. I had it nine years. Nobody else had it nine weeks." He folded his hands on the board. "I'd run the cold test that winter. A stone packed in snow on the roof for a month, and then into the study, and held while it warmed. Hour by hour. I had to hold it, you see, to feel the warmth come back into it, and I was old even then, and the study was warm, and I fell asleep in my chair with the stone in my hands."

The fire settled. Armand watched it.

"When I woke the candle had hardly gone down. A finger's breadth. And I knew, the way you know a thing in your body before you know it in your head, that I had been a long while somewhere. Somewhere I couldn't describe. Not a dream. A dream has a shape when you wake, even if it's a stupid shape. This had no shape at all. Only a long while, and quiet, and a light." He was silent for a little. "I wrote it up. That's what the tower requires. Every test, every result, in the tower's book, in the theorist's hand, and the same in his own. So I wrote one line in the tower's book, and the same line under my last entry here." He did not look at the notebook. "I wrote that the subject had fallen asleep holding the stone, and on waking had a strong impression of time passed out of proportion to the candle, and that a twelfth test was indicated."

"And then."

"And then within the month the order came down." Armand's hands did not move on the board. "Through the master of the tower, from above the tower. I never learned from where above it. The master didn't tell me and I didn't ask him. The question of the grey stones was closed. The box went back to the vault. The theorist was thanked." He smiled at the fire, a little. "I was thanked. It was very warm. It was the warmest thing that man ever said to me in thirty years."

"And you obeyed."

"I obeyed." He said it simply. "A year later I went to the tower's book to look at the line, because I couldn't stop thinking about the candle. The page was gone. Cut out, close to the stitching, with a good knife. You'd not have known unless you counted." He looked at the notebook in Emeric's hand then, for the first time. "So I came back to this room, in the tower, and I scraped my own copy. Myself. With my own penknife. So that it would match."

Emeric looked at the strip. The rough place under his thumb, that he had rubbed with his thumb a hundred times on the road and at his window and never once asked about.

"You never ran the twelfth test."

"I never ran the twelfth test." Armand let his breath out. "I've thought about it since. A great deal, since you stood in that courtyard and told me I'd stopped too soon, loud enough for the gate. I said I'd found nothing a shade louder than I needed to, you'll have noticed. That was for the courtyard. It was also true. I'd found nothing, because I'd stopped looking, because I was told to." He moved one hand on the board, and put it back. "I count it with the other things. With your rite, which I stood and watched, and with the tutoring post, which I took because it was a warm room and a quiet one and nobody asked a tutor to the vault. Three times I was frightened and called it obedience. You'll forgive an old man for not enjoying the count."

"Who sent the order?"

"I don't know."

"You must have thought."

"I have thought," said Armand, "for twenty years, and I have thought of a great many people, and I don't know. And I'm not going to say a name in a room, in a barony on the edge of the kingdom, about a thing I don't know, because a name said in a room has a way of getting out of it." He looked at Emeric. "You've thought of one. I can see it. Keep it in your mouth."

Emeric kept it in his mouth. It was the Church. It had been the Church since the first night in Kaldo when he had sat with the stone in his lap and thought that a thing this useful does not stay buried four hundred years by accident. He had no more evidence for it now than he had then. He had a cut page, and an old man's penknife, and an order that came down from above the tower, and above the tower there were not many things.

"I came to ask you about the strip," he said. "And to keep the other thing. From the courtyard."

Armand was very still.

"Put your hands on it," Emeric said. "You said not tonight, the first night. You'd been a fortnight on the road. That was more than two years ago."

"It has." Armand's voice was quite level. "Why tonight?"

"Because if I'd stayed in the hold," Emeric said, "it would have gone in the river with me. All of it. Everything I know about the stones and where I learned it. The board would run till its stones faded and then the wall would go blind and nobody would ever know why. Somebody pointed that out to me this afternoon." He took the grey stone out of his other pocket, his mother's, the one he had carried since the castle, and held it in his open hand between them. "Ward changed the card. The prince doesn't go down alone. I'm turning it round. The stones don't go down with the prince."

Armand looked at the stone in his hand and did not answer. Then he put the board aside, carefully, on the floor by the chair, and the book on it, and folded the blanket back off his knees.

"How?" he said.

"The way Imani did. You hold my wrist. I hold the stone. You put your other hand over it, so it's between our palms." He moved the stool closer, until his knee was against the arm of the chair. "When I go, I'll feel you on my wrist, at the edge. You'll want to hold on. Don't. Let go of it. Let it take you."

"Let it take me." Armand almost laughed. "I've been trying to make it take me for thirty years."

"I know. That's why you'll fight it. Everybody fights it at the start."

"And at the end?"

"At the end you stop."

Armand put two fingers on the inside of Emeric's wrist. Emeric felt them settle there, dry and light and very cold, the way they had settled on the inside of Armand's own wrist in the courtyard while he decided whether to come. Then the old man's other hand came over the stone, and the stone was between their palms, and Emeric felt the pulse in Armand's fingers against his own, and it was not steady.

He had felt it from the outside in December, by the fire, thin as breath, with his eyes shut: a great slow brightness wide as the room, and in the middle of it a place that stuttered and caught. He felt it now through the skin. Beat. Beat. A space where a beat should be. Beat. He nearly took his hand away.

"No," Armand said, without opening his eyes. "I know what you're feeling. I've felt it every night for two years. Go."

He went.

He felt for the place behind the stone where the room was, and the room was there, as it always was, the light that came from nowhere, the quiet, the long bench under the far wall. And at the edge of him, on his wrist, an old man's fingers holding on. Holding very hard. Holding the way a man holds a rope over a drop, with everything he had and all his experience telling him that to let go was to fall. He felt Armand pour himself into the holding, a great deal of him, more than Imani had had to give and much more than Ferra, and felt it go nowhere, because you could not get into the room by pushing. Nobody could. That was the whole of the joke.

Let go, he thought, as hard as he could, and did not know if it could be heard.

And then, at the very edge of him, he felt the old man's fingers open.

Armand was standing beside the bench.

He was not in his chair. There was no chair. He was standing, without a stick, in the grey wool coat he had come south in, and his back was straight, and he was looking at the room.

He did not say anything at first. He turned, slowly, all the way round. He looked at the walls, which were not walls so much as the place where the light stopped. He looked at the floor, which was there, and was not wood or stone. He looked at the bench, and at what was on it: the twelve watch stones of the wall, laid out in a row, each one as Emeric had last left it, opened up like a clock with its back off, the fine lines of what had been cut into it shining faintly inside the grey.

"It's quiet," Armand said.

"Yes."

"And there's a light." He looked up, at where a ceiling would have been. "There isn't a lamp. There isn't a window." His voice was very calm, the voice he used in a lecture hall when a student had said something so wrong it was interesting. "I was here. In the tower. In my chair. With the candle. This is where I was."

"I think so."

Armand looked at him across the bench. He looked a good while. It was not the look of a teacher at a boy of seven.

"How long have you been coming here?"

"Since my first summer in Kaldo. The night the stone woke."

"Nearly four years." Armand put one hand on the edge of the bench, and Emeric saw him notice that he had done it, and that the bench was there, and solid under his palm. "Nearly four years, and I gave you the notebook with the pages about it in my own hand, and you read them in a carriage."

He went to the watch stones. He bent over the first of them, the earth stone from the stretch of wall beside the South Gate, opened like a clock, and he did not touch it. He looked into it. Emeric watched him see the lines, the fine bright threads of instruction running down into the grey and branching and joining again deep inside the stone, a hundred times finer than any sign a mage had ever cut on the face of anything.

"You did this from inside," Armand said.

"Yes."

"There's nothing on the face of it because there's nothing on the face of it. You went in. You went into the stone, the way we've come into this, and you cut it there." He straightened. "That's the how. You said you'd show me how. In August, over the drawings." He did not look round. "You might have warned me it would be this."

"You wouldn't have believed me."

"No," said Armand. "No, I wouldn't."

He walked the length of the bench. He walked the way a man walks who has not walked without a stick for two years and has not yet remembered that he should not be able to, and Emeric watched him remember it at the end of the bench and stop, and stand there with his hand on the wood, breathing.

He was counting. Emeric could feel him do it. The old man had his whole attention turned inward, on the thread that ran from here back out to the chair and the fire and the cold body sitting in it with its fingers on a wrist, and he was measuring how much went down it, how fast, like a man watching sand in a glass.

"It costs," Armand said. "Coming here. It's going out of me in a steady draw, like a lamp stone on a long night. Not much. More than nothing." He turned his head. "More for me than for you. You're used to it. Or you're bigger than you look."

"It costs everybody the first time."

"Does it hold?" Armand said. "If you're not looking at it. If you go out, and I'm here, does it hold its shape, or does it go?"

Emeric did not answer. He went to the far end of the room, to the place where the light stopped, and turned his back on all of it, and stood there with his attention on nothing but the end of the light and his own breathing, and counted twenty.

When he turned, the bench was there. The stones were on it, each in its place. Armand was standing beside it with his hand flat on the wood, watching him.

"It holds," Armand said. "It doesn't need you looking at it." He was quiet. "That's not a dream, then. A dream is gone the moment you look away."

"No."

"Then what is it?"

"I don't know," Emeric said. "I've been coming here nearly four years and I don't know. I know what it does. I know it holds what I put in it at the fidelity I put it in, and it holds it unwatched, and that time goes differently here, so an hour in here is a few minutes in your chair. I know it's in the stone, or behind it, or the stone's the door to it. I don't know what it is. I was hoping you might."

Armand looked round the room again, slowly, at all of it.

"I don't," he said. "But I'd like a few years to find out."

He did not stay long. Emeric could feel the draw on him growing, and could feel, under it, at the far end of the thread, the chair and the fire and the old heart, beating, and stopping, and beating. He did not show him anything else. He did not show him the place in the corner where he had once lifted a coin with nothing but attention, or the drawings of the beam and its stones, or anything at all that was not stones and the cutting of them. When he felt the draw on the old man pass what he would have let himself spend, he said so.

"Not yet," said Armand.

"Yes. Now."

"I'm counting it," Armand said, with some irritation. "I know what I can spend."

"So do I," said Emeric. "I can hear your heart."

Armand looked at him across the bench.

Then he took his hand off the wood, and closed his eyes, and Emeric felt him reach back down the thread for the fingers on the wrist and the cold skin under them and the hardness of the stone between their palms, and go.



The fire had gone to embers. It had been about a quarter of an hour.

Armand sat in his chair with his eyes shut and his fingers still on Emeric's wrist, and did not move for some time. His face was grey. His breath was short and shallow and came in pieces. Emeric sat very still on the stool with the stone in his palm under the old man's hand and listened to the pulse under his own fingers, beat, and space, and beat, and beat, and did not count it, and could not stop himself counting it.

After a while it steadied. Not to steady. To what it had been.

Armand opened his eyes.

"Water," he said.

Emeric got him water from the jug, and held the cup while he drank, because his hands would not do it. He drank half and pushed it away.

"Nobody," Armand said. His voice was a thread. "You understand me. Nobody in the tower. Nobody in the Circle. Nobody I ever wrote a letter to." He put his hand back on Emeric's wrist, and held it, quite hard. "I've had students who'd sell their mothers for a tenth of that. Good students. Kind ones. Linnet would never sell anything and she'd tell it to the next mage she liked the look of, the first week, because she'd think he ought to know." He swallowed. "Nobody. On my life. On what's left of it."

"On your life," Emeric said.

"And the cutting. You'll teach me the cutting. Not the doing of it. I'll not live to do it. The how. What you cut, and why, and what the lines mean. So that if you go in the river, somebody knows." He let go of the wrist. "That's the second key. That's what you came for. You don't need me to cut a stone. You need me to know a stone can be cut."

"Yes."

"Then I'll know it." Armand put his head back against the chair and shut his eyes again. "Now go away. I'm going to sleep in this chair, and I'm going to dream, and it's going to be a stupid dream with a shape to it, and I'm going to be glad of it."

Emeric built up the fire before he went. He put the blanket back over the old man's knees, and the board beside the chair where he could reach it, and the book on the board, and the cup of water on the book. Armand did not open his eyes. At the door he turned round.

Armand was asleep already, with his mouth a little open and one hand lying loose on the blanket, palm up, as if there were still something in it.

It had been the man, then, who came south. Not the furniture.

He went up the stair to his own room, and sat at the table under the window in the dark, and took out the notebook, and opened it to the strip under the last of Armand's entries. He did not write anything in it. He looked at it until the candle had gone down a finger's breadth, and then he went to bed.
