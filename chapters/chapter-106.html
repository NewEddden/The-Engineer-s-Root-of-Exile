Chapter 106 - The Flask

Bram had said it with his hand on the pack in the foundry, in the dark, with the sand still falling off the far wall. The stones are cold. It's the mortar that's hot.

Emeric had heard it then as a place the charge had gone. It took him most of the late summer to hear it as the other thing it was.

The pack had been twenty lamp stones bedded in grit, and the grit had been stone too, the same charged stone ground sand-fine, the way the wall carried its report. The charge had gone through twenty solid stones and through the grit between them, and the stones had stayed cold and the grit had gone hot as a pan. It was the same stone. The only difference was that one of them was in one piece and the other was in ten thousand, and every grain of the ten thousand touched the next grain at a point, and every point was a join. Charge crossing a solid stone crossed nothing. Charge crossing a hand's length of grit crossed ten thousand joins, and paid at every one.

So a stone would carry charge better than any groove ever cut, if it were long enough to reach and in one piece the whole way. Long and thin. A rod.

And the bead Neta had rolled round in the summer held more than rough stone of the same weight. Armand had read it twice and written it down and did not know why. Emeric did not know why either, and had stopped minding. Round held. Long carried. He had the two findings on two different pages in two different hands, and for a fortnight he looked at them separately, and then one evening in the long room he looked at them together and drew a shape in the margin without thinking about it, and looked at what he had drawn.

A body like an egg, that held. And out of one end of it, a neck, long and thin and in the same piece of stone, with no join anywhere between the two, that carried.

It looked like a flask. It looked like nothing else he had ever seen made of stone, because nobody had ever made a stone into anything but what a chisel could leave behind.

He could not make it with a chisel. Nor could anybody. A neck that long and that thin, cut out of a lump by a man with an iron, would snap on the second stroke, and a body that round would take Neta a week. It had to be shaped the way he shaped steel, with the form, in the forge, on the fed plate. And a charged stone could not be shaped by anything. Armand had told him so in the spring without meaning to: a raw stone given work does something with it, and nobody can say what. A charged stone was a raw stone that had been given a great deal of work, all of it waiting. Put the form into that and the form would meet the charge, and something would have to give.

It would have to be empty first.

That was simple to say. Nobody in Kaldo had ever emptied a stone on purpose. Stones were charged, and used, and charged again, and a lamp stone that went dark in a mine went back up the shaft to be filled. The only thing he had ever built that emptied stones faster than they could be filled was the forge, and it had done it in an evening, to four of the wall's spares, while he was busy with something else.

So he fed the forge with the stone he meant to shape.

Bram put the lamp stone in the feed cup beside the port, and Emeric woke the deciding stone, and the plate came up off its legs and hung, correcting itself against nothing, the loose rule letting it sway a hand and answering once. The lamp stone paid for it. By the end of the evening it was grey, and in the medium it was a small dim place where a small bright one had been.

He took it out and put it on the plate, and took up the form, and gave the stone the shape he had drawn.

He felt the form meet it, and meet something in it, a sliver, and the sliver met the form the way cold water meets hot fat.

The stone went. It did not burst. It let go of what it had left all at once, and what it had left came out as heat and as a push, both together, and the heat went into the plate and the push went sideways into the firebrick at the back of the chamber with a sound like a board breaking. The plate rang. The oil in the dish went up the side and over. Then it was quiet, and there was a crack running up the firebrick at the back of the chamber from the sand to the height of a hand, and a smell of hot iron coming out of the port, and the air stone in the flue drawing it away up the chimney as if nothing had happened.

The sand had taken what fell. The lining had taken the push. The flue had taken the smoke. Bram's hands were at his sides, on the far side of a closed shutter, exactly where the plan said they would be, and he looked at the crack through the smoked glass for some time, and then at Emeric.

"Empty," Bram said, "wasn't empty."

"It was grey."

"It was grey," Bram agreed. "The pack was cold, too. Wasn't cold where it mattered." He opened the shutter and looked at the crack with the lamp. "I'll reline the back. What do you want to know it's empty by, next time, that isn't what it looks like?"

That was the right question, and it had taken Bram one breath to ask it, and Emeric had not thought to ask it himself in a fortnight of looking at two findings in a margin.

"Nothing," he said. "I want it to have nothing in it at all."

They left the next stone in the feed cup all night. In the morning the plate was down on its legs, because there had been nothing left to hold it with, and the stone in the cup was grey, and in the medium it was not a dim place any more. It was a hole in the shape of a stone. He had never felt one before. Every stone he had ever held in the medium had had something in it, and this had nothing, and it was very strange to feel, like putting a hand in a pocket he knew was full and finding the lining.

He put it on the plate, and took up the form, and gave it the flask.

It went like cold wax. Not the way steel went, grudgingly, a hair at a time under the heat. It went the way wax goes under a warm thumb, easily, all of it at once, so that he had to hold the form back to keep from pushing it past where he meant. He gathered the body round and drew the neck out of it, long and thin, the way he had once watched glassmen in another life draw a rod out of a gather on the end of a pipe, and the neck came out of the stone as if it had always been in there waiting. It took him perhaps a quarter of an hour. When he let the form go the stone lay on the plate in the shape he had drawn in the margin, and it was one piece, and there was no join in it anywhere, because nothing had ever been joined.

Bram took it off the plate with tongs, although it was not hot, because it was the kind of thing a man took off a plate with tongs, and turned it in the lamplight.

"That's a stone," he said.

"That's a stone."

"Looks like a bottle."

"It'll hold like one."

Bram made the casing. It was a shell of iron in two halves, shaped round the body and bolted closed over it on a seam of lead, so that the body lay in the dark with nothing touching it but iron, and the neck came out through a collar at one end. On the collar went a cap that screwed down over the end of the neck and sealed it. With the cap on, nothing could get in or out. With the cap off, and the neck seated in a socket, the charge went where the socket went. In the cap, because Emeric asked for it, Bram set a splinter of crystal like the one that had read the glass furnace for three years: a sliver that glowed when there was charge behind it and went dull when there was not, so that anybody could look at a cell and know what was in it without asking the one man in the barony who could feel it.

Orrin charged it that evening.

He put his hand on it the way he put his hand on the printer's stones, and poured, and after a moment he stopped and took his hand away and looked at it.

"It drinks," he said.

He put his hand back. He poured into it for longer than a quarter of an hour, which was a lamp stone, and longer than half an hour, which was two, and at no point did it begin to warm under his hand the way a lamp stone warmed when it was nearly full. It took what he gave it as fast as he gave it and did not complain about any of it. When at last it began to warm, very slightly, at the base of the neck, Orrin took his hand away and sat down, and said he had not poured that much into one thing since the glass furnace, and asked what it was.

Armand read it on the bench on three days, as he read everything, and weighed it, and wrote it against the lamp stones in the column he had made in the summer. It held about three times what a rough lamp stone of the same weight held. He wrote the figure, and the note beside it, near enough and not a measure, and then after a moment he crossed out the note, which Emeric had never seen him do.

"Near enough," the old man said, "three times over, on three days, is a measure."

They capped it and put it on the shelf in the pattern store, and Armand read it again at the end of the week, and at the end of the next, and at the end of the third. It read what it had read the day Orrin filled it.

Then Emeric put it to work, to find out the last thing, which was how it gave its charge back.

He seated the neck in a socket on the forge's feed and woke the plate, and watched in the medium. The charge came out of the neck and went up the groove to the deciding stone, and the neck stayed cold. It stayed cold the whole evening, with the plate hanging and correcting and the master pound going on and off, which had cooked the wall's spares in their cups. It gave what the plate asked for, steadily, without any fuss at all.

Then he did what he had to do to know, and asked it for everything at once. He took the deciding stone off the rule and told the plate to lift as hard as it could, and felt the draw go up the neck like water up a straw. The neck warmed. Not much. Enough that he took the rule back before it got worse. The cell would give a great deal, for a long time. It would not give it all at once. The neck was the only way out, and the neck was thin because thin was what carried, and a thin neck let out what it let out and no faster.

"Deep well," Bram said, when Emeric told him. "Narrow bucket."

"That's it exactly."

"Doesn't matter for this." Bram nodded at the plate. "Wouldn't want it for a furnace."

It was an odd thing to say and Emeric remembered it later.

He made the second cell himself, the next night, and the third. On the fourth night he did not make it.

Bram had cut a cup into the plate itself, near one corner, and over it, on a bracket off the frame, hung a housing with a raw force stone in it from the etching floor, fed off the same groove as the blanks. A lamp stone set in that cup fed the plate it sat on. Emeric had written the shape of the flask into a deciding stone the way he wrote a page for the printer, once, in the delve, and he had written the drain into it too: feed the plate from the stone in the cup until the stone in the cup is nothing, and not before. When the cup read nothing, the deciding stone told the force stone the shape, and the force stone pressed the empty stone into it where it lay. Then it stopped, because it had been told to stop, and waited to be given the next.

It was every rope off him at once. The hold on a feed. The form in a force stone, on a feed, told by a deciding stone when to press and when to be still. And no heat at all, because a stone that was truly empty needed none.

It made the fourth cell alone, in the pattern store, in the night, with Bram and Emeric watching it through the smoked glass and nobody touching anything. And the fifth. And in the morning there were two more on the plate, and the lamp stones that had been sitting in the cup the night before had fed the forge that shaped them, all the way down to nothing, and then been shaped by it into something that held three times what they had.

Bram stood looking at that for a long time.

"It ate the stone," he said, "and then made the stone into a better one."

"Yes."

"With itself."

"Yes."

He did not say anything else. He had said the forge would stand idle, and it had not stood idle for a single night since, and he had watched it make a thing with nobody's hands on it, and the look on his face was the one he had worn in the finishing shop the spring the lathes were made, holding a bearing that would not stop turning.

Ferra came down at the end of the week to see it, because it was going to be a line in her book and she would rather see a line than be told about one.

She watched it take a lamp stone out of the cup, empty, and set it under the force stone, and draw it into a flask, and set it down. She watched it do that twice. Then she opened her book.

"How many a day?"

Emeric had done the arithmetic. The drain was the slow part. With the master pound set on the plate to make it eat faster, a lamp stone took the better part of an hour to feed it down to nothing, and there was only one cup.

"Twelve," he said. "If Bram cuts a second cup."

Ferra wrote it down. He read it upside down, because he could read her book upside down now, and had since the spring. She had written six.

"Twelve," he said.

"You said twelve about the spares, too." She did not look up. "Six. When it's made twelve on six days running, I'll write twelve."

He did not argue. She was right about the spares, and she would very likely be right about this, and he had learned, over ten years, which of her halvings to fight and which to leave alone, and he had never yet won one of the first kind.

Bram waited until she had gone.

He had been standing at the port through all of it, with his arms folded, the way he stood at the far end of every bench Emeric had ever used, close enough to see and never close enough to touch. When Ferra's steps had gone down the passage he unfolded his arms.

"The stone that tells it," he said. "The shape one. You made that in the room."

"In the delve. Yes."

"The printer's stones. The forge's. The one on Ward's breast." Bram looked through the smoked glass at the plate, where the next lamp stone sat in the cup going slowly grey. "Every one of them, you made in the room. Nobody else has."

"Nobody else has."

"The old man asked you once what happens if you die on a road," Bram said. "You didn't answer him. He wrote half an answer on his paper and said he'd take half." He had never said a word about that sheet, in all the seasons since, and Emeric had not known he knew of it. "I'd like to learn it. To make one. To tell a stone a thing, the way you do. So there's a second man."

It was the first time in ten years that Bram had asked him for anything that was not iron.

Bram could go into the delve. He had been the first of the six to do it, and the most patient, and he had sat in the room above Kaldo with a bearing turning in the air in front of him and understood it in one look. He was slow with words and he was not slow with anything else, and he had never once in his life made a part by eye that could be made against a gauge.

"Yes," Emeric said.

Bram nodded, once, as though it had been a price agreed.

"Start me on the shape one," he said. "It's the simplest, and I've watched it the most." He turned back to the port. "And I'd like it written down that I asked."

"Ferra will want it written down that you asked."

"Then she'll have it twice," said Bram, and went back to watching the stone go grey.
