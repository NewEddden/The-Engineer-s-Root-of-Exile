Chapter 140 - Paper Planes

Kaldo had more paper than anybody had ever had.

That was the thing nobody had thought about. The mill's stamper pounded rags all day on the works' wheel, and Tess's finest moulds lifted a sheet a minute out of the vat, and the loft above the mill was hung from end to end with drying sheets like washing, and by the end of February there was more paper in the barony than the counting room and the school and the press and the Seers' Table could use in a year, and the stamper went on pounding. Ferra had begun to sell it on the coast. But it went on coming, and the dyehouse, which had a great many bad batches of a great many colours, had begun dyeing the sheets that came off the moulds too thin or too thick or with a fault in them, for no reason anyone could later explain, and there were stacks of red paper and blue paper and a terrible yellow in every corner of the mill.

Emeric took a sheet of the terrible yellow up to the long room on the twenty-eighth, at the end of his Thursday figures, and folded it.

He did it at the front of the room, at Sela's table, without explaining. A sheet of paper, folded in half the long way, and opened. The two top corners folded down to the middle crease, so the top was a point. The two new edges folded down to the middle again, sharper. And the whole thing folded in half along the crease, and each side turned down, flat, to make wings.

The room watched him. Fifty-four children who had been about to go home, with their slates under their arms, stood at their benches and watched the prince fold a piece of yellow paper at the front of the room, and did not know why.

He held it up. A dart of yellow paper with two flat wings and a point.

He threw it.

It went across the long room. Not fast, not far, but straight, level, a hand's height above the children's heads, gliding, the whole length of the room from Sela's table to the back wall, where it nosed into the plaster beside the window and dropped, quite gently, onto the back bench.

Nobody said anything.

Then the whole room was out of its seats.



By the end of the week every child in the school could fold one. By the end of the next, every child in Kaldo could. Sela said it was the worst thing he had ever done to her school, and that she would rather have had the plague back, and then she confiscated a dozen of them in one morning and was seen on the second Sunday on the wall walk with Arthur, throwing them, one after another, and watching where they went.

They went everywhere. The children worked out in a week what he had not told them: that you could bend the back of the wings up, a little, and the plane would climb; down, and it would dive; one up and one down, and it would turn. That a heavy paper went further and a thin one went higher. That a plane thrown off the wall over the river, on a still day, would go out and out over the water, further than anybody could throw a stone, and turn in the air on its own, and come down, sometimes, on the far bank, in the dock quarter, where the dock quarter children picked them up and threw them back.

It was the dyehouse's colours that did it. Red and blue and yellow and a pale green that had been meant for something else, going out over the black river in the winter sun. The town came out to look.

So Sela said, at the table in the long room, on the Wednesday, with her arms folded, that if the school was going to throw paper off the wall it was going to do it once, properly, all together, on a still day, and then it was going to stop and learn its letters. And everybody understood that she meant it as a punishment and that it was nothing of the kind.



The still day came on the fourth of March.

Clear and cold and windless, the kind of winter afternoon the south gets three times a year, when the sky goes the colour of a blue paper and the smoke from every chimney in Kaldo goes straight up into it and does not bend. The river below the east wall lay flat and dark and still as glass.

They lined the wall. The whole school, every grade, fifty-four of them, along the river stretch from the water gate to the north tower, at the parapet, each with a plane. And then more. Children from the lower ward who were not in the school yet. Children from the dock quarter who had come across the bridge. The garrison's children, Finn and Wren among them, Finn with a plane he had folded from a sheet of red that was nearly as long as he was. By the time Sela came along the wall walk with the school roll to count them there were three hundred and some, she said afterward, and she had stopped counting.

Half the town was below, on the bank, and in the boats on the river, and on the far bank in the dock quarter, looking up.

Armand was at the end of the line, by the water gate, on a stool. He had asked to be. Arthur had carried the stool down and Linnet had carried Armand, very nearly, down the steps of the wall walk, with his stick, and set him on it at the end of the line with a blanket over his knees. He had a plane in his lap. A small white one, folded by himself, very neatly, from a sheet of the plain paper. He looked at it as if it were a stone he had been asked to read.

Emeric was at the other end, at the north tower, with his hands in his pockets.

Sela counted.

She counted from ten, at the top of her voice, which was a very large voice when she wanted it to be, and three hundred children counted with her along the wall, and the town on the bank below counted too, and the dock quarter on the far bank, and the boats. And on one, three hundred paper planes went off the wall into the still air over the river all at once.

Red and blue and yellow and white and the pale green that had been meant for something else.

They went out. Most of them went out a little way, and wobbled, and turned, and began to come down toward the water in long slow spirals, the way a paper plane does on a still day, because a still day has nothing under it to hold a paper plane up.

And then Armand lifted his hand.

From his stool, at the end of the line, with his blanket over his knees. He lifted his hand, palm up, toward the river, the way a mage of the tower lifts it, and Emeric saw the glyph come up off his palm in the winter sun, faint, the lines of it pale, written on the air: air. And under three hundred paper planes going down toward the black water, a wind came up off the river.

Not a gust. A breath. A long slow lifting breath, warm from the sun on the water, coming up the face of the wall and out over the river under the planes, and the planes rose on it. All of them. All together. Three hundred paper planes going up again, out over the river, higher than they had been thrown, in the still clear air, with the sun coming through the coloured paper.

The town on the bank made a sound. The same sound it had made on the bank of the works when the iron came out of the furnace white.

Emeric did not lift his hand.

He stood at the north tower with his hands in his pockets, and looked at the planes, rising on Armand's breath, out over the water. And he made a way for the crowd. Linnet's way. Not a push. A way. Out over the middle of the river, under the highest of them, he made the air turn: slowly, in a wide circle, the way water turns in a slow eddy below a weir. Nobody saw anything. There was no glyph. His hands were in his pockets and his eyes were on the planes, the way every other pair of eyes on the wall were on the planes.

And the flight turned.

Three hundred planes, rising on Armand's breath, caught the turn at the top of it, and wheeled. All of them. Out over the river in a great slow circle, red and blue and yellow and white, like a flock of birds turning all at once over a field, round, and round again, over the black water, in the sun, with the town on both banks shouting now, and the boats, and the children on the wall jumping and pointing and screaming each other's names.

They went round three times. Then he let the turn go, and Armand let his breath go a moment after, and the planes came down. Slowly. In long spirals. Over the river and the bank and the dock quarter and the lower ward and the roofs of the town, everywhere, red and blue and yellow, like leaves.

At the other end of the wall, on his stool, with his hand back in his lap on his small white plane, which he had never thrown, Armand turned his head and looked along the line of children to the north tower. At Emeric. With his hands in his pockets.

He did not say anything. He looked at Emeric the length of the wall. And then he turned back to the river, and folded his hands on his blanket over the white plane, and his face did nothing whatever, which was the most eloquent thing it could have done.



The planes came down all over Kaldo for a week. In gutters. On roofs. In the canal ring, where they floated and sank. In the market. One came down in Maren's stock pot in the castle kitchen, through the open window over the range, a blue one, and Maren fished it out with a ladle and carried it up the keep stair dripping and laid it on Emeric's table without a word and went down again.

One went through the window of the east tower.

Emeric found out because it came back.

It came back on the sixth, at noon, while he was in the keep yard with Bram looking at the chair's new cooling tube. A plane came down out of the sky into the yard, white, folded very neatly, and landed on the cobbles at his feet, nose first, and lay there.

He picked it up. It was not a child's plane. A child's plane was a dart, folded the way he had shown them. This one had been folded by somebody who had taken one of the children's planes apart to see how it was made and then made it again, better: every crease sharp, every edge true, the wings exactly matched. And on one wing, in a small upright hand he knew from two years of the House's book, in grey ink, there were two lines.

The first was not hers. It was a child's, in pencil, large, and it said: where did this land.

And under it, in the grey ink, small: In the House's factor's lamp, which it put out. The House would be obliged if the next one aimed lower.

He looked up. The east tower stood over the keep yard, and its top window was open, and nobody was at it.

He folded the wing back the way it had been and put the plane inside his coat, against his chest, and did not show it to anybody.



Sorrel Amesbury came in at the South Gate on the eighth, on foot, with a bag over her shoulder and a limp, and asked the gate crew who had folded the planes.

Saban had found her. Saban's man in the capital had written in January, in his dry way, that there was a woman in the capital who jumped off the Circle's tower in a frame of cane and bedsheets and had glided once nearly to the river, and that the Circle called her an embarrassment and the city called her mad. Saban had read it out at the table. Emeric had asked whether she was. And Saban had said: she's still alive.

She was thirty or so, small and wiry and brown from the sun, with her hair cut short like a boy's, and the limp was in her left leg, from the time, she said cheerfully at the gate, that it had gone wrong. She was an air mage. She had a ring on her finger that said so and she wore it on the outside of her glove, the way the Circle's mages did, as if she wanted the Circle to see it on her and be annoyed.

She had seen the planes. Not in Kaldo. On the road, four days north, at an inn, where a carter come up from the coast road had one in his hat band, a red one, that his boy had picked up in the dock quarter, and was showing it to the inn as a curiosity. She had looked at it, she said. Turned it over. Thrown it, once, across the inn's long room, and watched it go. And then bought it off the carter for a Copper and walked the rest of the way.

"Who folded it?" she said, to Emeric, in the gate yard, holding it up. The red one, from the carter's hat band, with a dent in its nose.

"A boy called Finn. Nine years old."

"No." She looked at it. "Who showed him how?"

"I did."

Sorrel Amesbury looked at him. At his face, and his eyes, and back at the plane.

"Then you know," she said. She held it up between them. "You know it's the wing. Not the push. The shape of the wing. It doesn't fly because something's pushing it. It flies because of what the air does when it goes over a shape like that." She was not asking. "Every mage in the Circle flies by holding his weight off and pushing with air. Like a fat man climbing stairs. A bird doesn't do it like that. A bird's wing does it." She shook the plane. "And so does this. And a nine-year-old can fold it out of paper." She lowered it. "And they called me mad."

"People here say you're mad," said Emeric. "The gate crew did. Just now."

"They do."

"I don't think you are." He looked at the plane in her hand. "Do you want to come and work for me?"

Sorrel Amesbury looked at him a moment longer. Then she laughed, short and loud, the way a woman laughs who has jumped off towers.

"Doing what?"

"That," said Emeric, and nodded at the plane.



The forge was finished on the tenth.

It was in the room behind the cellar, the room they had dug since November, three nights a week, Emeric and Ward and Bram, at low draw, with picks. It was a room now. Square, cut out of the rock under the keep, its walls dressed smooth and lined with the new cement over Bram's wire, its floor flagged, its ceiling vaulted in brick, with the spoil of it all gone up the cellar stair in the renovation's barrows and into the new footings on the keep's river side, where nobody had counted it.

The way in was not through the cellar. That had been walled up again in January, behind the old wine racks, with the coal hole door taken off and the wall plastered and limewashed and racked with the old baron's dusty bottles exactly as before. The way in now was from Emeric's office on the ground floor of the keep, the room where Bram and Ferra and Ward came to meetings on the day's list: down a narrow stair behind a panel, cut in the thickness of the keep's old wall, that the old castle's builders had made for some reason of their own two hundred years ago and every baron since had forgotten. At the foot of the stair, a door.

A slab of stone, set in a rebate in the rock, so that it could not be pushed in or pulled out or levered. Only lifted, straight up. And in the slab, set flush and cemented over so that nobody looking at it would see them, four gravity stones from the new mine, the first to come down the river, cut from inside to add their push to the slab's weight when it was locked, and take it off when he told them. Nobody but Emeric could tell them. And on the inside of the slab, for anyone sealed in, a counterweight on a chain and a pin, that one person of ordinary strength could work.

Inside, the forge.

It did not look like a forge. It looked like the inside of a bell. A chamber the size of a large cask, lined with firebrick and the furnace's best clay and, inside that, with a skin of the smoothest glass Ivo had ever made. A feed cup at the top, where material went in. A cradle of iron round it all, and in the cradle, set at every angle, like the ribs of a basket, gravity stones from the mine. Twenty of them. The lattice. They would hold whatever was in the chamber in the very middle of it, floating, touching nothing, turning it however it needed to be turned, so that it could be heated and pressed and shaped from every side at once and never once touch a wall.

And under it all, in a bank like a row of loaves on a shelf, stones to feed it. Charged stones. Dozens. Because a forge that shaped metal from the inside, the way he cut a stone from the inside in the room, but out here, in the world, with real heat and real metal, was going to eat more mana in a night than the whole of Kaldo's lamps ate in a month.

A Mana Forge. He called it that, standing in front of it, on the night of the tenth, with Bram and Ward behind him in the doorway. Bram said it was the second stupidest name he had ever heard, and did not say what the first was, because he did not need to.

They ran it that night.

A stone. That was all. The first run was meant to be nothing: a small grey stone from the charging room's bucket, drained, spent, the kind that went for dust for the ink, to see whether the lattice held it and the chamber took the heat and the feed bank fed. Emeric put it in the feed cup himself, and closed the cup, and put his hand on the control stone in the lattice, and told the forge what it was.

A drained stone. Empty. Shape it into a disc.

The lattice took it. He felt it: the stone lifted in the middle of the chamber, floating, held, turning slowly. The chamber heated. The feed bank under it began to drain, the stones going dull one by one along the row, and the heat rose, and the stone in the middle of the chamber began to glow.

It was not empty.

He had told the forge it was. The forge believed what it was told. It had been told that the stone was drained, that there was nothing in it but stone, and it heated it as stone, and pressed it as stone. And at the heart of the stone, under the grey, where no reader would have found it, there had been a little left. A dreg. The last of a charge that the charging room's readers had felt for and missed, because grey does not mean empty, it only means grey.

And the heat found it.

It came out of the stone all at once, the little that was left, the way a dead zone comes out of a stone that is forced, only small. Very small. A soft hard thump inside the chamber, like a fist on a door, and a wash of heat that came out through the forge's walls into the room and made all three of them step back, and a push, sideways, that knocked the stone out of the lattice's hold and threw it against the glass lining of the chamber with a crack.

Then nothing. The lattice holding nothing. The chamber cooling, ticking. The feed bank, half dull.

Emeric opened the feed cup.

The stone was in the bottom of the chamber, in two pieces, black. And the glass lining, where it had hit, had cracked, in a star, from the size of a coin out to the size of a hand.

Bram looked at it over his shoulder.

"It was drained," said Bram.

"It was grey," said Emeric. He looked at the star in the glass. "Grey's not empty."

He went up the narrow stair to his office. He sat at the table. And in the margin of the forge's book, which he had begun that week, the first page, under the date, he wrote it, so that he would never once forget it, so that nobody who ever ran the forge after him would forget it either. Grey is not empty.



Vantic felt it.

He learned that afterward. Much afterward. She was in the east tower that night, at the top of the stair, at her desk, with her lamp lit, reading; and the east tower stood over the keep's south side, and the dug room was under the keep's north. But she felt it. Something in the stone of the keep under her, deep down, a long way off. Like a draught. Like a door opening somewhere in a house and the air moving through all the rooms. And then a soft hard thump, very faint, felt more than heard, in the floor, in her chair, in the pen in her hand.

She did not say anything about it. To anybody. Ever, until much later.

Because to say she had felt it, she would have had to say what she had felt it with.



The chair failed for the fifth time on the twelfth, halfway across the keep yard, at noon, with the whole of the castle kitchen at the window.

It was the cooling. Imani's schedule had it: for the summer, a stone under the seat set to pull, drawing the heat out of the cushion and out of Tess and pushing it away, out through a tube to a plate at the back of the chair, where it would go into the air. A cold stone, the way the cold store under the laundry had one. And a tube, copper, from the stone to the plate, to carry the heat away. Bram had made the tube. And the joint where the tube met the stone's housing, a joint Emeric had made himself, quickly, the night before, because he had wanted to test the cooling before the forge and had not had time to do it properly.

Halfway across the yard, with Emeric in the chair a hand's width off the cobbles and the stick in his hand and the cold stone pulling, the joint let go.

The tube came off the housing. And the cold stone, which was set to pull the heat out of whatever was over it and push it down the tube, went on pulling, and pushed it nowhere; and the heat it had already pulled, the heat of a February noon and the heat of his own body for a quarter of an hour, all of it, came out of the open end of the housing in one long hot breath, straight up, into his lap.

It did not burn him. Imani's schedule had seen to that; the stone could only pull so much. But it was hot, very suddenly, very hot, exactly where a man least wants a thing to be very suddenly hot, and he made a sound that the castle kitchen heard through the window, and let go of the stick, and the counter-burst fired, as it was meant to, and the chair stopped where it was, in the middle of the yard, floating, and he was out of it and on the cobbles and on his feet, holding his breeches away from himself with both hands.

The kitchen window was full of faces. All of them laughing. Maren's at the front, not laughing at all, with her arms folded, looking at him with the deep satisfaction of a woman who has had a blue paper plane delivered into her stock pot and been waiting a week to see the account settled.

Ward was at the gate. He had seen all of it.

He came across the yard, without hurrying, and looked at the chair floating a hand's width off the cobbles, and the tube hanging off it, and the prince standing beside it holding his breeches away from himself, and the kitchen window.

"I'll explain it to the watch," said Ward.
