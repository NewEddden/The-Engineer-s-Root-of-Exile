Chapter 140 - Marrow

The bag came in on the last day of the summer's heat, with the rider, sealed, and Ferra opened it at her table in the afternoon with the window open and the smell of the lake coming in through it, and there was a page in it from Placit.

Wenna had flagged it. She had flagged it in red at its head, the way the rules said, hand known: Placit, and under that, in the smaller hand she kept for notes, up, and under that, which the rules did not say and which Wenna had never once done in the whole summer, a second line: please read first.

Ferra read it first.

It was under PLACIT. 2. and the root's count, which Wenna had turned into a day and an hour in the margin: five days ago, in the evening, in the little room over the kitchen. It was a letter. It began with a name and nothing else on the line, the way Placit began all his letters, and the name was one Ferra had never seen written anywhere.

Marrow.

I have yours of the ninth. You are right that he is not what he was, and right that it is no reason to trust him, and I do not. But I did not know until yours that he was anything to you. So you also have your quarrel with the prince, and nearly had him once through his own guard. Sethe never told us whose hand that was. I had thought he hired a man out of the north for it and was sorry after that he had not been told the price. It seems he was told the price, and it was forty and not a Copper more, and I am glad to know it at last.

There is no call for anything of that kind now. The boy sits at the Staple's table and eats what he is given and asks to be taught. Keep to the stream ground and to the work. Come on the usual night, and bring the book up to the quarter.

P.

She read it twice. Then she put it down on the table in front of her and laid her hand flat on it, the way Wenna had laid her hand on the first of Placit's letters to the woman in Milford, and sat there.

She did not know the name. She had never heard it said. She went through what she knew, which was her work, and it did not take her long, because she knew a great deal and very little of it was this.

His own guard. There was one guard. There had only ever been one, in all the years, and he stood his watch on the western stretch of the wall every night in black plate with a lion on each shoulder and had not asked for anything since the stair.

Forty and not a Copper more. She did not need to open the box for that one. She could see the line in Sethe's small account book as though it were on the table in front of her beside this page: To M., for the second matter, in full. Forty Gold, in Sethe's hand, and the date beside it, three days before the night on the channel. She had read it in the spring with Wenna and Dace across the table and had written it into her own book, and she had looked through all four boxes for anything from M. to go with it, and found nothing, because M. had not written anything down, which she had thought at the time was the one intelligent thing anybody in that whole correspondence had done.

M. was Marrow. Marrow had had the prince through his own guard. And Marrow was alive, and in Wayford or near it, and was going to the Staple on the usual night with a book.

She did not know what the book was. She did not know what the usual night was. She did not know who Marrow was, except that the prince might, because the prince had been in the hold of that boat with a hood over his head for most of a night, and men talk in front of a hood.

She took the page with her to the relay.

The relay room at Kaldo was at the top of the keep, in the old bell loft, with the two stones in their housings under the louvres and a man on a stool between them. Ferra sent him down the stair for his dinner. Then she sat on his stool with the key open on her knee, in her own book, in her own hand, which nobody else in Kaldo could read at speed and nobody else in the south could read at all, and put the message onto the Wayford line herself, one mark at a time, with the lamps of the speaking stone coming up and going down in front of her under her hand.

She kept it short. She put the name in whole, spelled out, one letter to a mark, because she did not want the prince to have to guess at it.

MARROW. PLACIT'S HAND, FIVE DAYS OLD. HIS OWN GUARD. FORTY, NOT A COPPER MORE. THE USUAL NIGHT, THE STAPLE, A BOOK. URGENT. F.

It went out along the line into the afternoon, post to post, a string of marks that meant nothing to any man who sat between the two ends of it, and she sat on the stool after it had gone and watched the lamps go dark and did not go down.

Emeric had a letter from Ferra that morning already, by the rider, five days old, in the ordinary way.

It was a long one. Most of it was the barony. The harvest in the lower fields was coming in heavy; the beds behind the barn had been turned again and Bram said they smelled worse than the tannery; Rowena had written, briefly, to ask whether he meant to come by boat this year or the next, and Ferra had put the letter aside for him to answer himself. Imani wanted more lime. Vantic had asked about the price of lamp oil. And at the end of it, a line by itself, with a space above it:

The converter's first three bars split hot on the rolls. The fourth rolled whole with black grit from the Grayrock spoil in the pot. Found by C.

He was at the inn table with his breakfast in front of him and Ward across from him, and he read that line and put the letter down.

Manganese. It was manganese; the black grit was manganese, or had enough of it in it to do the work, and a steel that split hot on the rolls had sulphur in it, and manganese took the sulphur up and put it where it could do no harm. He had known that since before he was Emeric. He had known it for longer than he had known how to hold a sword. It was one of the handful of things about steel that he could have written on the back of his hand at any hour of the day or night, the thing that had stood between one man with a converter and every other man with one for years in the old world, and he had known it, and he had stood in Colm's glasshouse in the spring with a clear pane in his hand that Colm had made clear with a pinch of that same grit, and turned it in the light, and been pleased for Colm, and put it down. And not long after that he had stood in the far corner of the foundry yard with a wet cloth over his face and Ward upwind at the gate and made a thread of green gas out of the same grit with his own hands, and coughed for an hour, and never once, in either place, thought of steel.

It had been in his hand twice. Colm had been right the first time he looked.

He sat with that. Ward, across the table, went on eating, and did not ask what was in the letter, and Emeric was grateful to him for it.

What he felt was not what he would have expected to feel. He had expected, if he had ever thought about it, that the first time somebody in Kaldo did a thing he should have done, he would mind. He did not mind. There was a kind of vertigo in it, the feeling of a man who has been carrying a load up a stair alone for a long time and has just felt somebody else take the other end of it without being asked. It was very like relief. It was also, underneath the relief, a small cold thing he did not like looking at: that he had held the answer in his hand twice and not seen it, and that he did not know how many other answers he was holding that way, in the notebook, in his head, in the things he had done this year without once thinking what else they were for.

Found by C. He could see the line in Ferra's hand in the forge's book. He could see Bram's face.

He folded the letter and put it in his coat.

"Good news?" said Ward.

"Colm fixed the steel."

Ward considered that. "Colm?"

"Colm," said Emeric, and found that he was smiling, and went out to the counting house to see Pennock about the tolls.

The marks came in at the counting house a little after the noon bell.

He was in Pennock's inner room with the toll books when the keeper came through from the relay room at the back with the day's sheet in his hand, a string of marks in the keeper's careful log that meant nothing to him, and gave it to Pennock, who looked at the head of it, where the post that had first spoken it was shown, and then looked at Emeric, and put it on the table in front of him and went out and shut the door.

Emeric opened his own book and read it through against the key.

He read it twice.

MARROW.

He was in the hold. He was sitting against the curve of the hull with his shoulder on a rib and his hands tied behind him and a hood over his head, and the cold coming up through the planks into the backs of his legs, and somewhere to his left a boy breathing the way a child breathes who has been told to stop crying, and Rylan talking to his son in a low flat voice about nothing at all, and over their heads two men talking to each other across the hatch the way men talk in front of a thing they have been told does not matter. The cold. The wait. That the whole night could have been done at the stair. Marrow wants us down here till he whistles. So we are down here till he whistles.

He had put the name next to the voice from the ledge. He had put it there and done nothing else with it for as long as he had had it, and he had had it a long time.

Two people on Kaldo's side had ever heard that name spoken, in the dark of that hold, and neither of them had ever said it aloud since. Not in the long room. Not in the gatehouse at the third bell with the card between them. It was in a book with a date on it, and the two of them had agreed without agreeing that it did not need saying twice in a warm room.

Ferra did not know it. Ferra had found it anyway, in a page five days old, from his own guard and forty, and put it on the line spelled out one letter to a mark so that he would not have to guess.

He sat very still in Pennock's inner room with the toll books open at his elbow and the sheet in front of him.

Five days old.

That was the one thing on the sheet that was worth anything, and it was the thing he could do nothing about. Placit had written it five days ago in the evening in the little room over his kitchen. Marrow had written to Placit on the ninth, before that. Where Marrow had been on the ninth, and where he was now, and whether the usual night had come and gone in the five days it had taken the page to go south in a sealed bag and the name to come north on the line in a morning, the sheet did not say, and could not, and there was nothing in the world that could tell him now, except the next page, if there was one.

The bag was five days behind the pen. Ferra had written that down in the spring, in her column for risks, and he had read it and agreed with it. He had not, he found, believed it until now.

He took a clean sheet from Pennock's desk and wrote two lines on it in the key, and got up and took them through to the keeper himself.

"Draft a letter to Rylan," Emeric said. "We found him something."

He said it aloud in the relay room at the back of the counting house while the keeper put the first of the two lines onto the line, and the keeper, who was a former courier Saban had hired and was paid not to listen, did not listen, and Ward, in the doorway, did.

The second line was longer. The second line was the column.

It went to Ferra in her key and it said that Ward's list was to be called: the knights on his roll for the road, mounted, with the carts, under the orders Ward had left with her in the spring, on the Wayford road, starting in the morning. It said that Kaldo was to be held by Armand and the rest of the garrison as Ward's orders said. And at the end of it, under the column, because he knew what Rylan would ask and he knew that Ferra would have to stand in front of him with no answer if he did not give her one, one line more:

If he asks: alive. That is why it is him.

The lamps went up and down. The keeper wrote the hour in his log. Emeric stood in the doorway beside Ward and watched the last of it go out, and did not wait to see the lamps go dark.

Saban had most of it by the evening.

He had been working toward it all summer without a name to put at the centre of it, a kitchen at a time and a groom at a time, and a name was what he had needed. He said so at the inn table, with a map of Wayford under the dish, drawn by himself and therefore bad. There was a man called Marrow. He had been in the town on and off for three years. He was not a Wayford man and nobody knew where he came from. He was not in any house's livery and not on any house's roll and he did not live in the town; he came in and went out, and when he was in, he was at the Staple, and he ate at a cook shop on the stream ground where Saban's man had drunk with his potboy for a fortnight in the spring without ever once hearing the name, because nobody there said it. They called him the Staple's man. They called him the book-man. They said he carried the Staple's books up and down between the five houses on the usual night, whatever that was, and that it was not a night anybody in the cook shop would go out on.

"And the rest," Saban said, "you had in the spring, and now it has a name on it." He moved the dish. "Sethe's second matter was yours. The boat, the channel, the stair. Paid out of the five's money, through Sethe, to Marrow, in full. The five have had Marrow on their books since Sethe went, doing whatever it is he does for the Staple. And two of them, Placit and Vennor, took their wives out of houses that sit at Aldane's table." He put his finger on the map, on the north side of the square, and then on a street off the west side where Vennor's townhouse stood, empty most of the year. "That's not new either. It's only that now it's all one thing."

Nobody at the table said anything for a moment.

"Aldane," said Ward.

"I don't know that," Saban said. "I know two marriages, and that Placit's wife goes north to her people every winter. That's all I know."

In Kaldo, at the long-room table that evening, Ferra read the Staple's two tallies aloud.

She did not show anybody the pages. The rules said who read, and it was not the table; but the rules also said that what went up to her went up so that it could be used, and what she flagged was the table's, and she flagged this. She read out the heading and the five short lines, twice, the first fortnight and the second, with the figures, as figures, the way she read out every other figure she had ever read at that table. Armand listened with his eyes shut. Bram listened with his hands round his cup. Vantic was not there; it was not a supper night.

"Intake," said Armand. "Of what?"

"It doesn't say."

"Rags," said Bram. "Wool. The Staple takes in rags and wool. That's what it's for."

"By the bale. Not like this. These figures are too small for bales." Ferra looked at the page she did not need to look at. "Four in a quarter. Twenty-three held."

"Held against rent," said Imani.

She had been sitting at the end of the table with the day's register in front of her, not reading it, and she said it the way she said the name of a thing in a patient that she had seen before and did not like seeing.

"You know it," said Ferra.

"It's the tenancy law." Imani closed the register. "A man's free to leave his lord's land. Unless he owes the lord. If he owes the lord, the lord can hold him till it's paid. Held against rent. That's the words in the law. I've had three men in the ward in six years who were held against rent somewhere, and got clear of it, and came to Kaldo because they'd heard the prince doesn't hold anybody." She looked at the page in Ferra's hand. "Twenty-three."

Nobody said anything. The lamp hissed a little. Down in the yard somebody was shutting the forge up for the night, and they could hear the bar go across.

"Held where?" said Bram.

"It doesn't say," said Ferra.

"Yielding what?"

"It doesn't say."

Imani looked at the page for a while longer. Then she opened the register again and turned to the day, and wrote nothing in it, and closed it.

Nobody at the table knew what intake meant. Ferra wrote in her book that nobody did.

Ward laid out his plan at the inn that night, over Saban's bad map, and they all knew while he laid it out that it would very likely not be used.

It was a good plan. He had built it on Kaldo's own houses in the spring, a week of nights on the island, with the four elites and a dozen of the garrison playing the Staple's house men in their shirts: five houses taken at once, in the dark, one elite to a house with four knights under him, in at the back and out at the front with the man in a blanket before his own household had lit a candle, and the fifth house, which was always the problem, taken by Ward himself with the rest. It worked on Kaldo's houses. It would work on Wayford's. It depended on five men being in five houses on one night.

"And they won't be," said Emeric.

"Not if they've any sense." Ward looked at the map. "The day we send the summons, they'll go to ground together. Somewhere with a wall and a strong door and their own men round them. I would."

"The Staple."

"The Staple," Ward agreed. "So I'll have wasted a week of Kaldo's nights." He did not sound as though he minded. He folded the map in half and in half again. "Better a plan you don't use than a night you didn't plan."

The order of it was set before the candle was halfway down. The column would be called in the morning; it had been, by now; it would be on the road by first light. On the column's fourth day on the road, a day short of Wayford, the summons would go to the five houses, on Kaldo paper, off the office's own printers. And Emeric would leave Wayford the same day, openly, by the south road, in daylight, the prince going home.

Ferra gave Rylan the letter in the pattern store the next morning, at the second bell, among the frames.

She had drafted it in the night in her own hand and not in Hobb's, and read it over three times, and cut it by half, because the prince had said draft and a draft was a thing you made shorter. It said that a man called Marrow had been found in Wayford, alive, in the service of the five houses of the Staple. It said that he was the man who had been paid for the second matter. It said that the column was called and would march in the morning on the Wayford road, and that Rylan was on Ward's list, and that the prince asked for him by name.

Rylan was standing by his own frame when she found him, with his suit on it and his helm on the shelf above, doing something to the catch of the cape with a small knife. He had come in off the wall at the first bell. He took the letter from her without saying anything and read it standing, once, slowly, and she watched him read it and did not look away.

He did not do anything when he had read it. He did not move at all for some time. Then he folded it along its crease, and folded it again, and held it.

"Alive?" he said.

He did not say it to her. He said it the way a man says a thing to himself to find out what it sounds like out loud.

Ferra had the answer in her book. She did not need the book. She said it the way it had come up the line, word for word, in the prince's hand turned into marks and turned back into words at her end and nowhere in between.

"Alive. That's why it's you."

Rylan looked at her. He looked for a long moment, and she did not know what he was looking for, and she let him look.

Then he put the letter inside his shirt, against his chest, and turned back to the frame, and began to take his suit down off it, piece by piece, to pack.

Emeric walked out to the Wayford relay post on the ridge that evening, alone with Ward, because he wanted to see it.

There was no reason to. The messages went from the counting house in the square, and the post on the ridge was only the first of the line, a squat stone tower with a keeper's cottage at its foot and its two stones under the louvres at the top, one listening back toward the town and one speaking forward toward the south. He had been past it a dozen times. He stood at the foot of it now in the last of the light with the town behind him and the road running away south under the ridge toward Kaldo, empty, pale in the dusk, five days long.

Up under the louvres the speaking stone lit, and went dark, and lit, handing something on down the line toward the next post, and the next, and home. It was not his message. His had gone in the morning. It was some other thing, the day's ordinary business, a figure for a figure, going south.

He watched it for a moment. Then he turned his back on it and walked down the ridge toward the town, and did not wait to see it finish.
