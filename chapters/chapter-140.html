Chapter 140 - In Public

He answered the letters on the twenty-fourth, all of them, on the new paper, in his own hand, not Hale's.

Six of them, and a seventh that had come since. He did not answer them the same way. Ferra had told him that a man who answered seven houses with one letter copied seven times would have seven enemies by the spring, and a man who answered each of them in its own words would have only five, and she had been right about smaller things. So he wrote each one. To the old poor house in the north-west that wanted Grayrock's stone, he wrote that Grayrock's stone would be sold, in time, through the House of Ducatis, at a fair price, to anyone, and that the house would be welcome to buy it. To the rich new house in the capital that wanted an old name for its grandchildren, he wrote that his own name was older than was good for anybody and that he would not wish it on their daughter. To the house that owed money to a house that owed money to the House of Ducatis, he wrote nothing about money at all, and Saban, reading it over his shoulder, said that was the most cutting thing he had ever seen written on paper.

And in every one of them, somewhere, in its own words, the same thing. That the holder of Kaldo was grateful, and honoured, and would not marry their daughter. Because he had already given his word, at home.

He did not say to whom in the letters. He did not have to. By the time the riders had carried them out of the South Gate, the whole of the high street knew.



It cost him. He knew exactly what.

He sat at the table on the Monday and Saban priced it for him, the way Saban priced everything, without being asked, because Saban said a man should know what he had paid for a thing before he decided whether he had been robbed.

The north-west house had a cousin on the king's council who would have voted with Kaldo on the stone. He would not now. The rich new house in the capital lent money to half the merchants on the coast road, and every one of those merchants would hear by the spring that the prince of Kaldo had turned down the house's daughter, and the coast road's price for Kaldo's soap and spirit would go up a little, because it could. The earl's second son had a brother who commanded the crown's garrison at the Isle of Shards. And Sallow.

"Sallow," said Saban, and stopped.

Nobody at the table said anything.

"The first queen's brother," said Saban. "The first queen. Your brother's mother." He turned his hat once on his knee. "She sent you her niece. Her own niece, in her own colours, with her own steward riding at the carriage window. It was the best match in the kingdom for a disgraced third son in a plague barony, and every house at court knew it, because she made sure they did." He put the hat down. "And you'll send the girl home."

"Yes."

"For the Chancellor." Saban did not look at Ferra. "And the Secretary of Health." He did not look at Imani either. "A smith's daughter and a net-mender's daughter. No title between them. No house. No name in any book but Keller's and Doran's."

"Yes."

Saban looked at him for a while across the table. Then he picked his hat up again.

"I'd have done the same," he said. "I'd have done it worse. I'd have told the steward why." He put the hat on his knee. "She'll take it as an insult to her house. That's what she'll take it as. Not a man choosing. An insult to Sallow, from Amara's son, in front of the court. And two of them makes it worse, not better." He turned the hat. "One, she could have told the court was love, and laughed at. Two means you could have had her niece as well, the way any lord in the capital would, and you didn't want her." He looked at the table. "She'll remember it."

"I know."

"I know you know," said Saban. "I'm saying it so it's in the book." He looked at Ferra. "It's in the book?"

"It's in the book," said Ferra, and did not look up.



He told Isolde himself, in person, on the twenty-sixth, before her steward could hear it from anybody else.

He found her in the counting room, at the end of Ferra's table, with Ilsa Renner's Grayrock ledger and a pen. When he came in she looked up, and saw his face, and put her pen down.

"Ah," she said. "Something real."

They walked on the wall again, on the north stretch, in the thin cold sun. He told her plainly. That he would not marry her, and that it was not because of anything she was or had done. That he had given his word at home, to two women, and that neither of them would marry him yet, and might not for years, and that he would not marry anybody else whatever they decided.

Isolde walked beside him with her hands in her cloak and listened to all of it.

"Both," she said, when he had finished.

"Both."

"And they agreed." She stopped walking. "To each other. Not to you. I can see it in your face. They went away and agreed it between themselves and came back and told you." She looked at the river. Then she laughed, short and real, and put her hand over her mouth as if she had not meant to. "In my aunt's house it would have been the man arranging it, and the two women finding out at dinner. I think I'd like to see my aunt's face when she hears it was the other way round."

"She won't hear that part."

"No," Isolde agreed. "She'll hear a smith's girl and a net-mender's girl. I'll let her." She was quiet. "I don't want to marry you. You know that. There's a knight in my father's household. Jabari. In the north. He has no money and no lands and he's been in love with me since I was fifteen and he's never once said so, because he knows exactly what I'm for." She looked at the shed under the north wall. "If I go home unmarried, from the prince of Kaldo, in disgrace, the way my aunt will say it, my father may decide I'm not worth sending anywhere else. And then there's the knight." She turned and looked at him. "So you see. You've done me a kindness. You won't know it. My aunt won't. But you have."

"I'm glad."

"So am I," said Isolde Sallow. She put her hand out, as a man would have, and he took it, and she shook his hand once, firmly, the way Ferra had taught her to at the counting room table. "Tell the Chancellor that I'll miss her table," she said. "And tell the healer my aunt's steward's cough is gone. And tell them both that if they ever need eyes in my aunt's house, they'll have them." She let go. "They'll know what I mean."

She went back along the wall toward the keep stair. At the head of the stair she stopped and looked back.

"Your speech," she said. "The one you didn't make. Did one of them stop you?"

"Both of them," said Emeric. "One at a time."

Isolde looked at him a moment longer.

"And the other?" she said. "The second one. What did she say?"

"That nothing in a body comes in threes. And not to go looking."

Isolde Sallow laughed, out loud, on the head of the keep stair, so that Osric on the gate below looked up; and went down the stair still laughing.

She left on the first of March, in the carriage with the green and silver arms, behind the four matched greys, with her eight riders and her aunt's steward on his tall bay at the carriage window with a face like a closed door and no cough at all. The high street came out to watch her go, as it had come out to watch her come. At the South Gate the carriage stopped, and the window came down, and she put her head out and looked back up the high street at the keep, at its windows, once, counting; and then at the counting room's window on the ground floor, where a red head was bent over a book and did not look up.

Isolde raised her hand to it anyway. Then the window went up, and the carriage went out under the arch, and was gone down the south road.



It was the middle of March before Saban's man in the capital wrote, by Saban's carrier, in nine days.

The first queen had received her niece home. She had received her in her own rooms, in private, and nobody knew what had been said. But the next day, at the king's dinner, in front of the whole of the high table, the first queen had said, to nobody in particular, that she had heard Amara's son had refused a daughter of Sallow for a smith's girl with ink on her hands and a net-mender's daughter from a plague ward, both at once, and that she supposed it was what came of sending boys to plague towns, and that she wished him joy of them both. And the high table had laughed, because it was expected to. And the king had not.

That was all Saban's man knew. The king had not laughed. He had gone on eating. He had said nothing at all. And after a while the high table had stopped laughing, one after another, and finished its dinner in silence.



At the dawn drill on the twenty-seventh, in the yard, in the frost, with the garrison doing Rylan's sets along the wall and Ward watching, Emeric stood at one end of the yard with his hands at his sides and looked at the sand sack at the other end, hanging from its post, forty feet away.

He did not lift his hand.

He saw the fire. He saw it where he wanted it: on the sack, at the height of a man's chest, a thumb of flame, no bigger. And he let it go, out of himself, without his hand, without pointing, the way he had let it go in the hold of the boat in December with his wrists tied behind his back.

A thumb of flame stood on the sand sack, forty feet away, at the height of a man's chest. It burned for a breath. Then it went out, and left a black mark the size of a coin on the canvas.

The garrison at the wall had not seen. They were counting sets. Ward had.

He did it again. And again. Ten times, before the drill ended. And every time, he had to see the sack. He had to be looking at it. He tried it once with his eyes shut, seeing the sack in his head instead, the way Rylan said a knight saw the jump; and the flame went somewhere, he felt it go, and nothing happened on the sack at all, and a little later Maren came out of the kitchen door at the other side of the yard and wanted to know who had singed her drying cloth.

Line of sight. Still. He could put fire anywhere he could see. Not anywhere he could not.

Ward, at the guardroom door, wrote something on Rylan's board, in a square after Emeric's name, that Emeric could not read from where he stood. When he went to look at it afterward, it said: ten. And under it, smaller: one to Maren.



They answered in public the same afternoon, the twenty-seventh, at a sitting of the table that Ferra and Imani had required between them under the eighth clause, which was the first time in the Standing Order's life that the clause had been used by anybody but Ward, and in front of all six, and Hale, and the minutes.

Imani did not sit at the end by the door. She sat beside Ferra, across the table from him, and Hale, coming in with the minutes, looked at the empty chair by the door and then at Imani and wrote something very small in the margin before he had even sat down.

At the end of the business, when Hale had read the last item and put down the sheet, Ferra did not close her book.

"There's another matter," she said. "For the minutes."

Everybody at the table looked at her. Hale picked his pen back up.

"The holder has given his word to two women at this table," said Ferra. "Me. And the Secretary of Health."

She said it to the table. Not to him. In the voice she used for a total. Bram, on her other side, went very still. Ward did not move at all. Armand, at the fire end, folded his hands on his blanket. Saban, who had priced it on Monday and therefore already knew, put his hat on his knee with the air of a man settling in to watch a sale go through.

"He's refused seven houses in writing," said Ferra, "and the first queen's niece in person, and the minutes have the cost. Saban priced it." She put her hand flat on the book. "We're answering here, because it has to be said here, at this table, in front of all of you, and in the book. Not in a room. Not on a stair."

"Yes," said Ferra. "To him." She did not look at Emeric. "And not yet to a wedding."

"Yes," said Imani, beside her. "To him. And not yet."

The table was silent.

"Clause nine," said Ferra. "The Chancellor's stay. It's the only thing at this table that isn't his. The day I marry him, every man in the four baronies who wants to say he's wrong and can't will look at the stay and see his wife. I won't have that. Not until I know how a Chancellor marries the man her stay binds, and can show the four baronies that she has. I'll work it out." She lifted her hand off the book.

"And I keep the ward," said Imani. "And the hospital, and every bed in it. Neither of us is anybody's second and neither of us sits at a high table in green wool while somebody else does her work. That's the arrangement. We made it. He agreed to it." She looked down the table, at each of them, the way she looked down a ward. "If anybody at this table has a view, now's the time, and then it isn't."

Nobody had a view.

"Write it down," said Ferra.

Hale wrote it down. His pen was the only sound in the hall.

Then Bram put both his hands flat on the table and pushed himself up out of his chair and stood there, a big grey man in a clean shirt at a table he had never once in two years stood up at, and looked at his daughter, and opened his mouth, and shut it, and opened it again.

"Your mother," he said. And did not say anything else. And sat down again, very heavily, and put his face in his hands.

Ferra did not look at him. She looked at the book. Her face had gone white, the way it went, with the freckles standing out like something spilled. Imani, on her other side, without looking at either of them, reached across behind Ferra's chair and put her hand flat on Bram's broad back, between the shoulders, the way she put it on a man in her ward who needed to be told he was still there, and left it there.

And under the table, where nobody at the table could see it but the man sitting across from them, Ferra's foot found his. And a moment later, from the next chair, another one did. And Ferra did not look at Imani, and Imani did not look at Ferra, and he sat at the head of his own table with a foot against each of his and his face doing he did not know what.

Ward said: "Good."

Armand said: "About time." And then, after a moment's consideration: "Twice."

Saban said that it was the worst bargain he had ever seen struck at any table, priced at both ends, and that he'd have made it himself in a heartbeat if anybody would have had him.



That night he went up to the line room late, as he always did, past the foot of the east tower's stair.

The light was on under Vantic's door, at the top of the stair, as it always was at that hour. He had stopped at the foot of the stair every night since the spring, for about as long as it took to read a line, and gone on. She had told him on the river that she heard him. That she had never once asked what line he read.

He stopped.

He stood at the foot of the stair and looked up at the light under the door. And found that he did not know what line he was reading, tonight, and that he had never known, and that it had always been something like the shape of a person on the other side of a door, quiet, reading too, at the same hour, in a room that smelled of damp and not of mice.

He did not go up. He had never gone up.

From behind the door, very faintly, he heard a pen. Writing. A short line. And then stopping. And then nothing at all, for as long as it would take a woman to sit very still at a desk with her pen lifted, listening to a man at the foot of her stair.

He went on up to the line room.

He did not stop at the foot of the east tower's stair again after that. He thought she would know why. He thought, going up, that she would be the one person in Kaldo who would understand it exactly, and would write nothing about it to anyone, ever, and he was right about both.



He could not sleep after that. He came down from the line room and sat at his table with the shutters closed and the candle lit and his heart still going, and in the end, because he could not sit still with it, he did a thing he had been thinking about for a year.

It had started with the board. With a lamp stone filling under his hand in the ward, and Imani watching him do it without looking at it. Mana went out of a man told what to be: fire, air, a weight taken off a coin. Or into a stone, told what to do. But it could go out of a man told nothing at all. He had done it every night since the spring, to keep his sense open: let it out thin, with no glyph and no shape, and leave it there. Mana with no instruction. It went out of him, and it went into the air, and it stayed there round him, thin, for as long as he kept letting it, and then it was gone.

On his other world there had been air that men made into a liquid. You took it and squeezed it and cooled it until it could not stay a gas any more, and it ran, pale blue, in a flask, and boiled away the moment it was let go. He had read about it in the trial, from the inside, as if he had made it with his own hands. Air was made of things that would do that, if you made them cold enough and pressed them hard enough. And what went out of him, told nothing, felt to his sense like air.

So he took a glass jar from Ivo's glasshouse, a good one, clear, with a wide mouth, and a collar of leather that Abloh had made to his drawing, that fitted the mouth of the jar on one side and his own wrist on the other, close, sealed with tallow. He put his left hand into the jar. He tied the collar round his wrist and sealed it. And he let the mana go out of his hand into the jar, told nothing, and kept letting it, and did not let it thin, and did not let it go anywhere, because there was nowhere in the jar for it to go.

It filled the jar. He felt it fill. He felt it press, the way air presses in a bladder that is blown too full. And he went on letting it, and pressing, and then, because it was the only thing he could think of that was like cold for mana, he told it, very hard, all at once, to be still.

It became liquid.

He felt it happen. The press in the jar let go, all at once, and something ran down the inside of the glass round his hand like water down a window, and pooled in the bottom of the jar round his fingers.

He lit the candle with his other hand and looked.

Pale blue. The colour of a winter sky an hour after sunset. A little pool of it in the bottom of the jar, round his fingers, a finger's depth, perhaps two ounces. It did not move like water. It moved more slowly, and it caught the candle's light and held it, so that it seemed to glow faintly from inside, and his fingers in it felt cool, and very slightly tingling, the way a hand tingles when the blood is coming back into it.

And he was empty.

He had not noticed it while he was doing it. He noticed it now, sitting at his table with his hand in a jar, all at once: hollow, cold, light, the way he had felt after the first nights in the delve in the first winter, the way he had felt at the bottom of the cursed gallery with the hood of good air gone thin. Two ounces of pale blue in a jar had taken out of him most of what he had.

He sat and looked at it.

Then, because he wanted to see it better, he untied the collar, and pulled his hand out of the jar.

The moment his hand came out, the moment the seal broke, it was gone.

Not spilled. Gone. The little pale blue pool in the bottom of the jar went up, all of it, at once, into the air, the way water goes up off a hot iron, and he felt it go, a cool breath across his face and his hands, like the air before rain, and then nothing. The jar was empty. Dry. Not a drop.

He sat there with his wet hand and the empty jar and the candle and laughed, very quietly, so the board man would not hear.



He took the next jar to Armand.

He made it the next night, sealed this time, his hand out of it before he made it liquid, with a long glass tube through the collar instead of his wrist, that Ivo had drawn for him, so that he could push the mana down the tube into the jar and then seal the tube in the candle flame, and keep it. It took him most of the night and left him so drained that he slept until noon. But he had it. A jar the size of a fist, sealed, with an inch of pale blue in the bottom, glowing very faintly in the dark of his coat pocket.

He put it on the board across Armand's knees.

Armand looked at it for some time without touching it.

"You've been building things without me again," he said.

"I wanted to surprise you."

"You did." Armand did not look up from the jar. "I'm surprised. I'm surprised and I'm offended, and I'm eighty, and I'll be offended for as long as I like." He was quiet. "How long did it take you?"

"Two nights."

"Two nights." Armand looked at the pale blue in the jar. He looked at it the way he had looked at the watch stones on the bench in the room, a long time, a teacher looking at a thing a student has done that he had not taught him. Then something at the corner of his mouth moved, and he put out his hand and touched the glass, very lightly, with one finger. "Well," he said. "It's very pretty."

"You've seen it before."

"I've seen it before," said Armand. He took his finger away. "Every crystal theorist in the tower has seen it before. It's in the tower's book. It's a curiosity. Students make it, in their third year, to show off, and then never make it again." He folded his hands on the board. "Shall I tell you why?"

"Yes."

Armand told him. Six reasons. He counted them off on his fingers in the lecture-hall voice, the way he had counted off the eleven tests of the grey stone in his notebook, and Emeric sat on the stool and listened to every one.

It cost more to make than it held. A mage spent more of himself pressing it into a jar than the jar would give back. His own reservoir refilled itself for nothing in a day, so bottling it was like bottling water beside a well. A mana stone did the same thing better: it held a charge as long as you liked, and could be told what to do with it, and did not vanish when you opened it. Liquid mana could not be told anything; it was not a spell and no spell could be cast from it, it was fuel with no engine. It would not stay. Break the seal and it was gone, as Emeric had seen. No soldier could carry it, no caravan could ship it, no battle would ever be won with it, because the first dropped flask was the last. And sixth.

"Drinking it kills people," said Armand. "A man eats food with mana in it and he stops, because he's full. That's the body's brake. This has no brake. It's all mana and nothing else, and a man who drinks it takes all of it at once, faster than any rite." He looked at the jar. "I knew a student who did. Third year. To see. He got the tracery on his arms in an hour, the silver, ring over ring, and then on his chest, and his neck, and then he died, badly, in the tower's infirmary, with four masters standing round him who could do nothing." He was quiet. "It's in the tower's book too. With his name."

Emeric sat on the stool.

"All six," he said. "None of them holds."

Armand looked at him.

"Not if you're not the source," said Emeric. "Every one of your reasons is about a mage making it out of himself and spending himself to do it. If it came from somewhere else. Somewhere there was more of it than any man could ever spend. Then it doesn't matter what it costs to make. It doesn't matter that a stone does it better, because it's not instead of a stone, it's what you fill the stones with." He looked at the jar. "If there were somewhere you could just take it from."

Armand did not say anything for a long while.

Then he said: "The climbs."

He told it once. Emeric never heard him tell it again.

When Armand was a young man, before the tower, before anything, a low apprentice to a tower master, the tower had sent mages up. Up into the sky. Because there was a thing every mage who flew knew and nobody could explain: that the higher you went, the thicker the mana felt round you. Denser. Richer. As if the sky were full of it, the way the sea is full of salt. And the tower had wanted to know how far up it went, and how thick it got.

So they climbed. In pairs. One mage flying, with gravity under him and air pushing, and the other on his back, riding, with a sack of charged stones. When the flier ran low, he drained a stone into himself by touch and kept going. When the stones were gone, they changed places. Up and up. Two miles. Three. The air went thin and cold, colder than any winter, and a man's breath went short and his head went light, and some of them saw things that were not there and laughed for no reason. Keeping warm cost. Holding height cost. Every breath up there cost. And the mana got thicker the higher they went, so thick that a man could feel it on his skin like fog.

"And they couldn't use it," said Armand. "That was the joke. The thickest mana anybody had ever felt, all round them, and they couldn't take it. Their own reservoirs filled from it, a little, but they were spending faster than they filled, holding up two men in thin air. And the stones." He shook his head. "They took empty stones up to fill. The stones wouldn't take it. Not up there. A stone up there would take a twentieth of a charge in the time it took a man to fall." He looked at the jar. "So they came down. Every time. Exhausted, half frozen, half mad from the thin air, and empty-handed. And told the tower there was more mana in the sky than in all the stones in the kingdom, and they couldn't bring any of it home."

"What happened?"

"The tower was working on it," said Armand. "My master was. He made the last climb himself. He had ideas." He was quiet. "And then the Church closed it. A letter came down to the tower. Wasted funds. Wasted research. Wasted men, who could be doing better things for the realm than freezing in the sky. And the tower stopped." He looked at the fire. "I thought it was strange, at the time. I was nobody. An apprentice. I thought it was strange, and I didn't say so, and I forgot about it for fifty years." He looked back at the jar. "Until just now."

Emeric looked at the jar too.

A jar of something that would not stay, that could not be carried, that was worthless if a man made it out of himself. And somewhere above Kaldo, two miles up, three, more, a sky so full of it that men had felt it on their skin like fog, and could not bring any home.

A jar would bring it home. If the jar were sealed. If somebody up there could press it in.

"Don't," said Armand.

Emeric looked up.

"Whatever you're thinking," said Armand. "Not this week. Not this year. You've a gravity mine full of bad air and a chair for a girl and four baronies and two women who have just told the whole table what to do with you." He picked up the jar off his board and held it out. "Take your pretty jar. Go to bed." He held it a moment longer before Emeric took it. "And then, when you've slept, come back and tell me what you were thinking. All of it. Before you do any of it. I'd like to be in it this time."
