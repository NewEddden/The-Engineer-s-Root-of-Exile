Chapter 114 - Seeded Glass

The glassblower's name was Jory Talland, and the first thing he did in Kaldo was tell Osric to stop whistling.

He said it from the back of Saban's cart, as it came under the South Gate in the second week of April, before anybody had told him where he was or who anybody was or what he was for. He was sitting on a crate with his knees up and a bag in his lap, a long thin man of fifty or so with a stoop that had been made by bending over a pipe for forty years and one eye milky and white from the day, he said later, that a gather had burst in his face when he was twelve. Osric was on the gate. Osric was whistling, as he whistled every afternoon on the gate when nothing was happening, a tune from the capital that had been popular eight years ago and that he had never once got the middle of right.

"Stop that," said Jory Talland.

Osric stopped, more from surprise than anything.

"Thank you," said Jory. He looked round the gate yard with his good eye. "You don't whistle near glass. Everybody knows that. It cracks in the pot."

"There isn't any glass," said Osric.

"There will be," said Jory, darkly, "and it'll remember."

Saban got down off the box and came round the cart, and caught Emeric's eye across the yard, and gave him the very small shrug of a man delivering exactly what he had been asked for and declining responsibility for any of it.

"Glassblower," he said. "From the north coast. Thirty years at the glasshouse on the strait, the one that does the crown's windows, till the master's son came into it and put his own cousin on the pipe." He looked at Jory, who was looking at the gate tower's lamp on its post with professional disapproval. "He didn't want to come. He said the south's soft and the water's wrong and there's nobody here knows the difference between a crown and a cylinder." He took his hat off. "I told him the wage. He came."

"I heard that," said Jory, from the crate.

"I meant you to."

Jory climbed down. He did it slowly, the way a man does who has been three days on a cart, and stood in the gate yard with his bag held against his chest in both arms, and looked at Emeric for the first time. He looked with the good eye, close, the way he looked at the lamp, and Emeric felt himself being judged for something, and could not tell what.

"You're the prince," said Jory.

"Yes."

"Gold eyes." Jory looked at them a little longer. "That's a colour you don't get in glass. Not without gold. You put gold in the pot, a coin's worth to a whole melt, and it comes out red. Ruby. Not gold." He seemed to find this an interesting failure of the world. "You'd think it'd come out gold. It never does."

"I didn't know that."

"Nobody does," said Jory Talland, with great satisfaction, and went to find out where they were going to keep him.



The glasshouse was the old tannery.

It stood at the bottom of the lower ward against the south-east wall, where it had stood empty since the plague, a long low stone building with a yard in front and a chimney at one end, and it had stunk so badly for forty years that nobody had wanted to live within three doors of it, and the stink had gone into the stone and stayed there after the tanner died. Ferra had valued it, in the winter, at less than nothing, since the barony would have had to pay a man to pull it down. Vantic had looked at the figure and then at the building and then at Ferra and written nothing at all in her book, which Ferra said was the House's way of agreeing.

Bram had built the furnace in the long room over March, under Jory's letters, which Jory had written from the north coast before he ever came, three of them, long and cross and covered with drawings, telling them everything they were doing wrong. It was a round brick furnace as high as a man's chest, fed with coke from underneath, with six arched openings round its sides, and in each opening, on a ledge inside, sat a pot: a deep crucible of fireclay as big as a bucket, which Jory had made himself in the first week because he said nobody else's pots could be trusted, and he had been right, because two of Bram's had cracked in the first firing.

In the pots went the batch.

Sand, white, from a bar on the river below the marsh that Jory had walked a day to find and come back from wet to the waist and pleased. Ash: not any ash, the soap works' potash, Abloh's, out of the same leaching tubs that had made Kaldo's soap for three years, boiled down to a grey cake. Lime from the kiln. Jory mixed them on a stone floor with a wooden shovel, by eye, in proportions he would not write down and would not say, and when Emeric asked he said it was in his hands and his hands did not talk, and when Emeric asked again he said it was a third of this and a little less of that and as much of the other as the sand wanted, and that the sand would tell you.

It took a day and a night in the pots before it was glass. Then Jory would put his pipe in.

The pipe was an iron tube as long as a man was tall, with a mouthpiece at one end, and Jory dipped the other end into the pot through the opening and turned it, slowly, and drew it out with a gather on the end: a glowing ball of glass like honey, orange, dripping, the heat of it coming off in a wave that made the air shiver. He turned it. He turned it all the time, never stopping, so that it did not sag, and blew into the pipe, short, and the gather swelled. He swung it. He stood on a box over a pit in the floor and swung the pipe down into the pit like a bell ringer, and the glass on the end of it stretched under its own weight into a long sausage, and he blew, and swung, and blew, and turned, and it became a cylinder, a long bubble of glass as wide as a man's thigh and as long as his arm, glowing.

Then he cut the ends off it, and split it down one side with a hot iron, and put it in the flattening oven, where it lay on a smooth stone and softened and unrolled itself slowly into a flat sheet, and a boy with a wooden block smoothed it down.

A pane. Kaldo's first window.

The first one came out of the oven on the eighteenth of April, and everyone in the glasshouse crowded round to look at it, and it was full of bubbles.

Small ones, hundreds of them, like the bubbles in a glass of new spirit, all through it, so that when Jory held it up to the window the light came through it broken and speckled. Seed, Jory called it. Seeded glass. He looked at it with his good eye and swore at it in the dialect of the north coast, steadily, for some time.

The second one cracked. It came out of the flattening oven whole, and lay on the bench cooling, and an hour later, with nobody touching it, there was a sharp small sound like a stick breaking, and a crack ran across it from one corner to the other.

The third cracked. The fourth was seeded and cracked.

Out of the first forty panes, thirty-one were seeded, and twenty-six cracked, and some were both. Nine were clear and whole. Jory held each of the nine up to the window in turn with his good eye shut and his bad one open, which he said was the only way to see a fault in glass, because the good eye forgave things, and he put down seven of them, and kept two.

"Those two," he said. "Those two are glass. The rest is a lesson."

It was Jory who solved the cracking. Emeric had known what it was the first time he heard the sound: the glass cooled too fast, the outside going hard while the inside was still soft, so that when the inside shrank it pulled against the outside until something gave. Strain. He had known the cure, too. You cooled it slowly, in a second oven, a long tunnel that was hot at one end and cool at the other, and you drew the panes through it over a day. A lehr. He had said so, and drawn it, and Jory had looked at his drawing for a long while.

"That's a lehr," Jory said.

"Yes."

"I know it's a lehr. I've had a lehr for thirty years." Jory gave him the drawing back. "Your lehr's fine. Your lehr's not the trouble. Your trouble's the furnace door." He went and stood by the flattening oven, beside the door to the yard, which was propped open with a brick because the room was hot. "Every time a boy goes out to the privy he opens that, and the wind off the river comes in and goes straight across the flattening stone. Cold. A breath of it. That's your crack." He kicked the brick away and the door swung shut. "Shut the door. Put the lehr where you like. Shut the door."

They shut the door. They built the lehr. In the next forty there were nine cracked.

The seed took longer. The seed was the batch: too wet, or the sand too coarse, or the pot not hot enough for long enough to let the bubbles rise out of the melt. Jory fought it for a week with his shovel and his hands that did not talk, and got the count down by half, and then half again, and by the end of April one pane in three that came out of the lehr was clear enough to see a face through without the face looking as though it had been rained on.

The first clear panes went into the ward. Imani had asked for them in March, and she was Health, and that was how it went.



She had not asked for a lens.

She had said it, once, at the ward, in the winter of the second year, at the end of a long night with a child who had a fever that would not break, standing at her desk with the lamp and the register: that she would give a year of her life to see what was in the water. Not what it did. She knew what it did. What it was. She had said it the way people said they would give a year of their lives for a thing, meaning nothing by it, and then she had gone to bed.

He had remembered it. He had remembered it on the floor of Maren's kitchen turning a tin of cream, and in the room with Imani at her table looking at a body she could open as often as she liked, and at the ward in February with her face a hand's breadth from his. He had a great many things he could not give her. He had decided, walking back from the ward that night with the lamp stone's light still in his eyes, that this was not going to be one of them.

The mundane way was grinding. A lens was a disc of glass shaped by rubbing it, for hours, against a curved dish of iron with a paste of sand and water between them, finer and finer, until its face was curved to match and polished clear. Jory could learn it. Jory would want to; Jory would sneer at it and then do nothing else for a month. It was the right way, and the way it would be done in Kaldo for a hundred years, and he would teach it.

He did not do it that way.

He did not want a lens that was good enough. He wanted one that was right. And there was a lens he knew of that nobody ground at all.



He made it in his room, behind the shutters, on the night of the twenty-fourth, with one of Jory's two good panes broken into strips on the table in front of him, and a candle, and a basin of water, and Ward's cloak over the shutters so that no glyph light would show to the yard.

A bead. That was all it was. A tiny ball of glass no bigger than a mustard seed, perfectly round, perfectly clear. On his other world a cloth merchant in a flat wet country had made them, hundreds of years before anybody thought of a microscope with two lenses in a brass tube, and looked through them at a drop of rain water, and seen it full of things swimming. A single bead of glass, held very close to the eye, with the thing to be looked at held very close to the bead. Nothing else. The rounder the bead, the clearer it saw. The smaller, the more it made great.

The merchant had made his by melting the end of a glass thread in a flame and letting the drop hang. Most came out lopsided, pulled long by their own weight. A few came out round. He had made hundreds to get the few.

Emeric did not need hundreds.

He drew a thread off the end of a glass strip in the candle, thin as a hair, the way Jory drew canes, and held the tip of it in his fingers over the table. He put his fire to the very end. Not the candle; his own, shaped small, a bead of heat no bigger than a pea, white, and the tip of the thread went soft and gathered itself into a drop, glowing.

And began to sag.

He took the weight off it.

It was the smallest gravity he had ever done. Smaller than the coin, years ago. A drop of molten glass weighing less than a grain of wheat, and he took its weight off it, all of it, held it at nothing, so that it hung on the end of its thread in the heat with nothing pulling it down. And it did what anything liquid does when nothing pulls it. It pulled itself together. It rounded. He watched it go from a tear to a ball in the white heat, perfect, the way a drop of water rounds in a pan of hot fat, the way rain does falling.

Fire on it to keep it molten. Gravity off it to keep it round. Two at once, and he had done two at once in the dark in Kaldo's river with a child in his arms, but not this small. It made his eyes water.

Then the hard part.

It had to cool. Slowly, evenly, all round at once, or it would strain the way Jory's panes had strained and crack, or cloud. He let the fire go out of it a little at a time. And with the third thing, the one he was worst at, the one Linnet had told him in bay three was a crowd and not a wind, he moved the air round it. Not a breath. Less. A slow stir, round and round the bead, so that no side of it was ever colder than the others, turning the air round it the way Jory turned his pipe so that the gather never sagged.

Three things. The first bead clouded. The second cracked off its thread when the air went wrong and fell into the basin with a hiss. The third went lopsided, because his attention slipped on the gravity at the moment the fire went out of it and it sagged half a hair before it set.

He sat back. His head hurt behind both eyes, the way it had the first winter, and his hands were shaking. He looked at the candle, which had gone down a finger's breadth.

He thought about not doing it this way.

Then he thought about her at the desk, with the register open, not writing, and drew another thread.

The seventh came out round.

He knew it before he looked. He felt it set, all through, at once, with no pull in it anywhere, and he let the air go still and the weight come back to it, and it hung on the end of its thread in the candlelight, a ball of glass the size of a mustard seed, so clear that he could see the candle in it upside down.

He broke it off the thread with a pair of Bram's tweezers. He did not breathe on it.

The rest of it he made in the same night, because he could not have slept. A plate of brass, thin, the length of his finger, from an offcut on Bram's bench: he softened it with fire on the table and pressed it flat with his own weight through a smoothing stone, and put a hole in it with a hot needle the exact size of the bead, and seated the bead in the hole, and closed the brass round its edge with a touch of heat, and then made a second plate and riveted the two together with the bead between them. Behind it, on a screw, a pin to hold the thing to be looked at, close, closer than a hair, where the bead could see it; and a second screw, to move the pin nearer or further until it came clear. It was ugly. It looked like a brass hinge with a nail through it. It had taken him till the birds began.

He held it to his eye at the window as the light came up, with a fleck of the dust off the sill on the end of the pin, and turned the screw, and the dust came clear, and it was not dust.



He took it to the ward at noon.

He had not been there since the night of the lamp stone except once, with the table, to say she had won. He went in by the side door. She was at the desk with the register, writing, with the new panes in the window behind her letting in more light than that room had had in a hundred years, so that he could see her clearly for the first time in it: the line of her neck where the hair was tied up hard under the cloth, the ink on her fingers, the tiredness gone from under her eyes.

She looked up. She saw his face, and whatever she saw in it, she put the pen down.

"You haven't slept," she said.

"No."

"What have you done?"

He put it on the desk in front of her. The brass hinge with the nail through it, on a square of clean linen.

Imani looked at it. She did not touch it.

"You said once," Emeric said, "at the end of a night with a fever, that you'd give a year of your life to see what was in the water."

She looked up at him, quickly. Then down at the brass.

"I don't remember saying it."

"I do."

He showed her. He took a cup off her shelf and went out of the side door to the ward's own pump, which drew from a well in the lower ward that Imani had never trusted and still boiled everything from, and brought back a cup of it, and put one drop of the water on the end of the pin with the tip of a straw. He gave her the brass. He showed her how to hold it: close, the bead against her eye, almost touching her lashes, facing the new window. He showed her the screw.

She held it to her eye.

She turned the screw. Nothing, and then nothing, and then he saw her hand stop.

She did not say anything. She sat at her desk with the brass held to her eye and the light from her new window coming through the drop of water on the pin and the bead and into her, and she did not move at all, and he stood beside the desk and watched her not move for so long that the woman knitting by the stove at the end of the ward looked up to see what was the matter.

"They're moving," Imani said.

Her voice was not her voice. It was very quiet.

"Yes."

"They're alive. There are things in it. Swimming." She did not take the brass from her eye. "Some of them are long and some are round and there's one going round and round on itself, like a dog after its tail. Dozens. In one drop." Her other hand had come up and was resting against the edge of the desk, flat, the way it had rested on the door frame in the first summer. "In one drop of my well."

"Yes."

She took the brass away from her eye. She looked at the cup of water on the desk, at the clear harmless water in it. Then she put the brass back to her eye and looked again.

"Get me the tower water," she said. "From the aqueduct. The cistern under the keep."

He went. It was a long way up and down the hill and he ran some of it. When he got back she had looked at the well water twice more and drawn what she saw on the back of a page of the register in charcoal, quickly, small, the long ones and the round ones and the one that turned, and she took the cup of cistern water out of his hand without a word and put a drop on the pin and looked.

She looked a good while.

"Three," she said. "Two of the round ones. One long one." She took the brass down. "In the whole drop. Three."

She sat with it in her lap.

"I boiled everything," she said. "Since before you came. The whole lower ward. I made them carry it from the standpipes when it was nearer to go to the well. I fought for the burial rows to be moved. I stood in the yard and told people the water was making them sick, and they asked me how I knew, and I said because they were sick, and that's no answer, and they knew it was no answer, and they did it anyway because I was the healer." She looked at the brass. "And it was these."

"Not only these," Emeric said. "Most of what's in there won't hurt you. But some of it will. And the ones that will are too small to see even with that."

"Then how do you know?"

He did not answer that. She heard him not answer it, and he saw her hear it, and let it go, the way she had begun to let things go with him: not putting them away now, not with the lid closed. Leaving them out, on the desk, where she could see them.

She turned the brass over in her hand and looked at the bead in it. She held it up to the window. A mustard seed of glass, set in two thin plates of brass, perfectly round.

"Jory didn't make this," she said.

"No."

"Jory can't make this. I've been to the glasshouse. He's spent a week trying to make a window you can see a face through." She looked at the bead a while longer. "Who made it?"

"I did."

"Last night."

"Yes."

She looked at him. He waited for the question. He had stood on the stair to the ward that noon and known it would come, and known he would not answer it, and come anyway, and he stood beside her desk now and waited for her to ask how.

She did not ask how.

She held the brass in her palm, closed her hand round it, and held it against her, the way a person holds a thing they have been given and mean to keep.

"Thank you," Imani said.

She said it simply. She did not look away. And he understood, standing there, that she knew exactly what she had been given, which was not a lens, or not only. It was a thing he could not have made with his hands, made in a night by a man who had not slept, so that she could see the one thing she had wanted to see more than anything; and that he had given it to her instead of the thing she had asked for at the corner of the desk, because it was the nearest to that he could give. She knew it. She had decided, he could see, not to mind that it was not the same.

"I'll want another," she said. "For the girls. And Jory's going to grind me a bigger one, the slow way, so I can show the town." She opened her hand and looked at the brass. "And you'll show him how."

"Yes."

"Then go to bed," said Imani. "You look like the cooper."

He went. At the side door he looked back, as he had in February, and she had already put a fresh drop on the pin from the well cup and was bent over the brass at the window with the light on her face, and the charcoal in her other hand, and she did not look up.
