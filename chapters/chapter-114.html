Chapter 114 - No Levers

The forge took his pattern on the seventeenth night, and in the morning they fitted Ward.

They did it in the pattern store, the way they had done it at the start of the summer, with Ward standing in the middle of the sand with his arms out and Bram and Emeric going round him plate by plate. Osric stood at the next frame with nobody fitting him, because he had got bored of waiting and started on his own greaves. The suits had stood on their frames since the bags came home, and Emeric had let them stand, because he had wanted both off the forge before either went on a man, and had wanted to watch the second go on while the first was still new in his hands.

The breastplate went on, and the backplate with the yoke across it and the rear heart in the yoke, and the lattice of the pack down Ward's spine. The shoulders went on, lions and all. The cape went on at its two catches and hung down his back furled, and Abloh, who had come up from the soap house to see it and had been told to stand by the door, came in from the door and put his hand on the breast pull and took it away again without being asked.

Ward walked the length of the room and back. He knelt and stood. He found both levers without looking, and found the notch at the back of the left one, and put his hand on the breast pull, and on the toggle behind it, and threw the toggle both ways and back to safe.

"How does it sit?" said Emeric.

Ward took his time over it.

"It sits where a man carries it," he said.

That was all. It was what all four of them had known inside ten seconds the day Bram's suits first went on in the yard, years ago, and Ward said it the way he would have reported the depth of a ford. Emeric heard it and did not say anything back, because anything he said back would have been worse than the sentence.

At the next frame Osric had got his breastplate on, and had turned to look at himself in the pane of his own front heart, bending his neck to do it. It was not for the fit. He looked at the fur round his throat and the lions on his shoulders and the black steel with nothing on it anywhere to say it had ever been the Crown's, and his face did something Emeric had not seen it do before, and then Osric saw him looking and said the collar needed brushing.

Ward had seen the fifth frame.

It stood beside Osric's, empty, with the forge's first night of it laid out on a cloth beneath, which was a breastplate. Ward picked it up and weighed it in his hand, and then lifted the same piece on his own chest with his thumb under its edge, and put the thin one down.

"That's thin."

"It's mine."

"It won't stop a sword."

"It's not meant to meet one." Emeric turned the thin breastplate over so that Ward could see the inside of it. "I'm not a knight. Nothing I've done in the delve in ten years has put a pound on me. I can't carry what you carry, and I'd rather be a man who can fly and not fight than a man who can fight and not get up."

Ward considered that, and did not argue with it.

"There's no slots," he said. He had found the place on the breast where his own levers sat, and there was nothing there. "No levers."

"No levers."

Ward looked at him for a long moment. He knew what that meant. He had been the one to say it, in the yard, in the early summer, with the practice blade in his hand.

"You said it had to be you," Emeric said, "because if I went up, every man in the capital who's ever poured into a hull would say I lifted myself, and nobody could say he was wrong. Not Armand. Not me." He put the breastplate down. "You've flown all summer with nothing in you that can lift a cup. The stones lift. Anybody who's watched you knows it now. Nobody can say that about me any more, whatever I'm wearing."

"And the levers?"

"I'll tell it what the levers tell it."

He showed him on the pattern. In the middle of the front heart, where Ward's deciding stone sat alone, there was a second seat, inside the iron, with a narrow neck from it to the deciding stone and a small door at the breast that opened by hand.

"The stone I carry goes in there," he said. "When I'm in it. It comes out when I'm not." He touched the seat. "From the boat at the Deep Channel I could wake the lamps in an empty guardroom, and that was as fine as a reach from a distance ever got. Lamps. With my stone sat against the deciding stone there's no distance at all."

"And it drives the stones."

"No." This was the part he had been most careful with, on the nights he wrote it, and he said it carefully now. "It drives the deciding stone. The same as your hand does. I tell it lighter, heavier, push, turn, the way a lever would, and it does the rest. If I reached past it and drove the stones myself I'd be doing the Branch again. Four stones and two tons, and it cost me the day." He shook his head. "Where the stones are concerned, my thought's a hand on a lever. It isn't a hand on the stone."

Ward nodded slowly. Then he looked down at his own breast, at the front heart's pane and the two brass levers either side of it, and Emeric saw him find it.

"There's a seat in mine," Ward said.

"Wired and capped."

"For what?"

"For yours. The stone you carry. When you can tell a stone a thing from the room." Emeric did not soften the rest. "It isn't free. To put your stone in, the levers come out. They can't both be telling it what to do. And the levers are the part everybody else can see. A man standing next to you can see what you've told it, and put his hand on it, and a hand on a lever beats a thought. When the stone goes in, that goes." He left it there. "It's your call. Not now. When you can."

Ward looked at the capped seat under the pane for some time.

"That'll be a while," he said.

"I know."

"I'll keep them," said Ward, "for the while," and put his hand on the left lever, not to move it, only to know where it was.

Imani wanted heat, and in the first week of the winter there was only one place in Kaldo that had any.

The frost had come down on the island in the night and stayed, white on every roof and in the ruts of the long street, and the lake had a skin at its edges in the mornings that the ferry broke going out. Emberfell was a mountain that smoked. Imani had not been there and did not need to go there to know what a man in black steel would feel like on its quay at noon, and she had said so at the table, and then she had come to the foundry with her canvas roll and a small sand-glass that ran for a quarter of a minute and asked Bram when he was next pouring.

It was Imani's by right, and nobody argued it. She was the barony's Secretary of Health. She had put the belays on the lines when Ward first flew, and she had watched lips in the band, because what a suit did to the man inside it was her department and always had been, and whoever cooked in his plate at Emberfell would be brought to her and not to anybody else.

Ward's left vambrace had been back through the forge between two of Emeric's pieces, and had the gap at the wrist she had asked for.

He stood at the furnace mouth through the morning pour in the full suit, as near as Bram would let him, with the heat coming off the iron in a wall and the light of it red on his breastplate. The loop was shut. The fire stone in his back was off, and the air stones were drawing through the padding and out at the vents, and that was all. At the end of the pour Imani put two fingers to the gap at his wrist and turned her little glass and counted, and wrote the figure in her book.

In the afternoon pour he stood in the same place, and this time the water was running, round through the tubes against him and out to the plate across his back, where the vent air crossed it. At the end of it she took his wrist again and counted again, and wrote the second figure under the first.

Emeric read it upside down. It was a good deal smaller.

Imani said nothing about that. She looked at the two figures for a moment, and then she went back over the second one with her pen and made it larger, and closed the book.

"How was it?" Emeric said to Ward.

"Hot," said Ward. "Then less."

His own suit came off the forge on the twenty-second night, and on the twenty-third they took it to the foundry after the fires were drawn.

It was the same floor and the same sand, raked level in its square with the four posts at its corners, and the same four lines, and Imani's two belays running up through the blocks on the tie-beams. Imani was on her stool by the east wall with the canvas roll across her knees. Bram was at the doors. Everything was as it had been all the late summer, except that Emeric was standing in the middle of the sand, in black steel that fitted him and weighed less than Ward's by a good deal and more than anything he had ever worn in his life, and Ward was standing at the cleat.

Ward had taken the near belay off its cleat and was holding it himself. That had not been in any plan. Emeric looked at him, and Ward looked back, and said, "I'll keep this one," and that was the plan now.

He opened the door at his breast and put the grey stone he carried into its seat, and closed the door on it, and felt the deciding stone come up against it inside the iron like a hand taking a hand.

Then he told it lighter.

It was not like the ship. The ship was a hundred feet of iron, and he felt her go up through the soles of his boots the way a man feels a floor move. This was himself. The weight went out of his feet and his knees and the whole of his back at once, as if he had put down a load he had been carrying so long he had stopped knowing he carried it, and he came up off the sand to the height of his own knees and hung there, swaying, with the lines lifting behind him.

The first thing he noticed was his hands.

They were at his sides, doing nothing. He had watched Ward fly all summer with his hands on two brass levers the whole time, never once off them, and he had not known until now how much he had been watching that. He opened them and closed them. He put one on his sword hilt and took it off again. The suit hung where it was.

"You can draw," Ward said, from the cleat.

"Yes."

"In the air. With both hands on the hilt, if you want them." Ward was looking at his hands too. "A man on levers has to take one off to draw, and then he's flying on one, and the one he's got left is the push or the lift and not both. He picks. Every time." He was quiet for a moment. "You don't pick."

Emeric drew. The blade came out clean, and he held it in his right hand in the air at the height of a man's knees, and nothing about the suit changed at all.

Then he reached for the palm stones, to turn, the way he had written them, and nothing happened.

He reached again. The left palm breathed at once, and turned him a quarter round, and the right palm, around the hilt, did nothing whatever. He had written the rule himself. He had written it in the delve at three in the morning with great care, because a man with a sword in his hand had given his hand to the sword, and he had been pleased with it. He had not expected to be the first man it refused.

He laughed. He could not help it, and it came out of him in the empty foundry loud enough to go up into the tie-beams, and on the stool by the east wall Imani looked up from her roll.

He sheathed the sword and turned on the spot with both hands open, and it was easy.

He did not fly it far. The lines would not have let him, and he did not want to. He flew it finely.

He brought himself down until the sole of his boot hung a hand's breadth over the sand, and held it there, and it held. He turned on the spot, slowly, a full round, and came back to where he had started to within the width of a finger, and stopped dead. He rose to the height of a man and stopped dead there. He went forward a pace and stopped, and back a pace and stopped, and there was no sway in any of it, because there was no lever between the thought and the stone, and no hand between the lever and the thought, and nothing for the suit to wait for.

Ward watched it from the cleat without a word.

At the end of a quarter of an hour Emeric found he was tired.

It was not in his arms or his back. It was behind his eyes, and it was the tiredness of a long evening over a page of figures that would not come out. A lever stays where it is put. A thought does not. Every breath he stayed off the sand he was telling the deciding stone where to hold him, and the moment he stopped telling it he would be somewhere else, and he could not stop telling it. It was attention, and attention was what it cost.

He came down and stood on the sand, and it was very good to stand.

"You fly it finer," Ward said. "I fly it longer."

"Yes."

"Both of those are true for a while yet," said Ward, and put the belay back on its cleat for the next part.

The next part was the one that mattered, and it was the one he had worried about most on the nights he wrote it. Ward's suit had a failsafe in his hand. If Ward let go of the levers, they stayed where they were. If Emeric's orders stopped, there was nothing in the suit to say where they had stopped. So he had written it one more rule. If no order came for a count of twenty, go light, and settle, and keep settling until the ground comes up, and then go heavy.

He went up to the top of the lines, to the tie-beams, with the belays taut above him and the sand eighteen feet down. And then he tried to stop thinking at it.

He found that he could not. He told himself to stop telling it anything, and that was telling it something. He thought about not thinking about the suit, and there was the suit, in the thought. He hung under the tie-beams for a long, stupid half-minute with his face hot inside the helm, discovering that there was no part of a man that would stop on command, and on the stool below him he could see Imani watching him hang there without moving, and the more he thought about that the worse it got.

So he did sums.

He took seventeen and began multiplying it by itself, and by the next number, and by the one after, and carried the figures in his head the way he had carried figures in a carriage on the road south, and somewhere in the third of them the deciding stone reached twenty and heard nothing.

The suit went light under him. Not all at once: the weight came back into him slowly, a little at a time, as if somebody were lowering him on a rope, and he went down past the belay blocks and past the height of two men and the height of one, turning a little as he went, and the air stones breathed at the sand below him and raised a slow ring of it that spread out from under him across the raked floor. His boots touched. The heels went heavy. He was standing.

He was still in the middle of a sum. He found that he could not remember which.

"Again," said Imani.

They went down the river on the twenty-fifth day, all of them who had gone the time before, and Imani, and Osric in his suit under a cloak for the first time, with the hood up against the cold.

The dummy went first, because Emeric had said the next one would be a bag in a man's suit and he had meant it. It was Osric's suit, stuffed with sand and straw to the weight of a man, and dressed properly, cape and chute and helm, with its lift lever lashed forward at Light and its push lever lashed at nothing. Orrin seated the count stone behind its breastplate. It was the same small grey stone from the bags, and Emeric had written one more rule into it the night before: when the toggle is thrown, count two, and cut the deciding stone's feed. Then count thirty, and give it back.

"It's wearing my collar," Osric said, watching them carry it aft.

"It's wearing your suit."

"The collar's the part I mind."

They took her out a dozen miles past the bar, and up, and this time higher than the bags had gone. Fletcher let the lead down over the side again while she hung there, and paid it out until it touched, and read a hundred and eighty fathoms off the marks. Then the dummy went over the rail of the open deck aft.

It fell like a stone for two, and for three more while the bellows emptied. Then the cape cracked open and caught it, and it went out from under her in a long lurching slide over the grey water, and Abloh's line in the pack threw the chute out below it, clean, and it came down under the chute, very slowly now. At thirty it was still well up, three or four masts off the sea, and Emeric saw the dead hang of it change. The deciding stone had come back awake, and read the lashed lever, and told the suit Light, and the weight went out of the thing all at once, and it came the rest of the way down slower than a leaf and settled onto the sea and lay there on the swell, face down, in plate, and did not sink.

The steamboat went to it. Osric said nothing for the whole of the time it took to haul his suit out of the sea, which was the longest Emeric had ever known him say nothing.

Then it was Ward.

Nobody had asked him. Nobody would have. He had come down the river knowing he was the first man, and he had never at any point said so, and at the table in the long room when Emeric had put the drop in the plan with no name against it, Ward had taken the pen out of Ferra's hand and written his own in the gap and given it back to her.

Abloh packed his chute on the deck, twice, the second time because the first had been done in front of Ward and he wanted to do it again where nobody was watching him. Orrin seated the count stone. Two dark, thirty awake, the same as the dummy. Abloh had worked the thirty out from the bags, and worked it on the high side on purpose, and the dummy had woken higher than he reckoned, which was the side he had wanted to be wrong on. A man who woke too high only had further to float. A man who woke too low had woken too late.

"When do I pull?" Ward said to him.

"When the steamboat's the size of your thumbnail at arm's length," said Abloh. "Not before. The cape first. Glide till then."

Ward nodded, once.

Emeric was not at the rail for it. He was forward, under the roof, with his hands on the levers, because Ward was the only other man who could fly her, and Ward was standing on the open deck aft in his helm with the cape furled down his back. Fletcher was at the helm beside him. The iron shell over them shut the medium out, the way it had been built to, and for once in his life Emeric could not see what was happening to the stones. He could only look out of a port, the same as anybody.

He saw Ward go past it.

It was very fast. A black shape dropping past the glass like a sack from a loft, the toggle arming itself as his boots left the rail, straight down, no cape, no wing, no anything, for the count of two and the three counts more that were the bellows, which Emeric had chosen, and which he felt now, for the first time, as three counts of watching a man fall in iron with nothing to stop him. Then the cape went out.

It went out from wrist to heel, all at once, the battens snapping straight, and Ward was not falling any more. He was going away from the ship on a long, flat line over the grey water, a black wing against the grey, not lurching as the dummy had, because he was a man and not a sack, and knew what to do with his arms. He went a long way. Emeric had not known a man could go that far with nothing but cloth. Then, far out and well below, a pale shape went out under the black one, and opened, and Ward was hanging under his chute with his cape still spread above him, turning slowly, coming down toward the sea.

Emeric counted. He had counted all the way. At thirty the black shape under the chute changed. It was hard to see from so far, a hundred feet off the water by his guess, but he saw the legs come up, and the long slow drop of it ease, and Ward came down onto the sea a quarter of a mile off her quarter as lightly as the dummy had, and lay on the water in plate.

He brought her down. Fletcher said nothing at all the whole way, and when she was on the sea again and they could open the ports, the steamboat was already there, and Bram was leaning out over its side with a boathook, and Imani was in the bow with her canvas roll.

They hauled Ward into the steamboat between them, streaming. He sat down on the thwart, and took his helm off, and sat there with it in his lap and the sea running off him onto the boards.

"If it hadn't woken," Ward said, to nobody in particular, "I'd be on the bottom."

He said it the way he said everything, and nobody in the steamboat said anything back, because it was true and they had all known it was true since the table. A man in plate with every stone in him dead was not a man in the water. He was iron in the water. The cape and the chute would bring him down as softly as anybody could ask, onto a sea that would take him straight to the bottom the moment he touched it.

Imani heard it. Emeric saw her hear it, from the rail. She did not say anything. She took a pencil out of her roll and wrote two words on the inside of its flap, plate sinks, and put the pencil back.

Then she looked at Ward, sitting in the bottom of her boat with the winter sea running out of his collar and the fire stone in his back already coming up warm, and she said the thing she had said in the foundry.

"Again."

"No," said Ward.

Declan's suit came off the forge on the twenty-sixth night.

His measure had been taken before Osric's, in the summer, and Osric's suit had come first all the same, because Osric was going. Ward had made that call and nobody had questioned it, least of all Declan, who questioned most things on the yard and nothing that came from Ward.

They fitted him in the morning. He stood in the middle of the sand with his arms out like a man being fitted for his wedding, as Osric had once said, and this time he looked at himself in everything: in the pane of his own front heart, in the smoked glass of the forge's port, in the bright blade of Bram's shears on the bench. He walked the length of the room and back three times without being asked. He tried both levers, and the notch, and the breast pull, and the toggle, and the toggle again. He put on the helm and took it off and put it on again to see how the wings looked from the side. He said that it sat better than Ward's. He said that his lions were the best of the lions. He was, for about the length of a quarter-hour glass, unbearable, and everybody in the pattern store let him be.

Then he stopped, in the middle of turning to look at the back of the cape, and was quiet.

He had remembered. Emeric watched him remember. It was the same look as in the yard, the head coming up a little and going down again, and he stood still with the helm under his arm for a moment. Then he said, in his ordinary voice, that it would do, and began taking it off.

Osric had been leaning on the fifth frame while all of this went on. Emeric's suit hung on it, thin, black, with no slots on its breast and a small iron door over the place where a stone went.

"Will it answer to anybody else?" Osric said. "Yours."

"No."

"Not if I stood in it and thought very hard?"

"It answers to the stone at the breast. You'd need a stone to put there." Emeric said it plainly, because it was only true. "You'd need to be able to hold one."

Osric could not. He was not one of the six, and had never asked to be, and had never once in all the years since the table said a word about it. He looked at the small iron door for a moment. Then he looked at his own suit on its frame beside it, with its two brass levers either side of the heart, sitting where a man's hands fell.

"Then I'll keep my levers," Osric said. "At least I know what they're thinking."
