Chapter 22 - A Barony Without a Baron

There was a man on the Western Gate who Emeric did not recognize.

Older than the boys they usually put there, bored in the honest way, and entirely correct about everything. He looked at the carriage, looked at the escort, worked out what he was looking at, and stood aside without making a performance of it.

Nobody rioted. That had been the thing Emeric had spent six days preparing arguments for, and it did not happen.

Nobody cheered either.

The market was quieter than it had been in weeks. The shop on the south row was open and had two people in it. The north well was boiling, and had a woman at it who did not look up. The ravine below the south wall had fresh earth in it, three weeks of it, and a new line of stakes going further down the slope than the last time he had seen it.

What had changed was how they watched him. It was not fear. Fear he could have worked with, because fear has a shape and can be argued down. This was the flat, patient attention of four hundred people waiting to find out what they now lived under, and there is nothing in that to answer, because nobody has asked you anything.

Bram put it plainest, that evening, in the yard, with a barrow between them.

"They believed you about the water. That is not the same as knowing what you are." He set the barrow down. "Wexler was a bastard and he was theirs for thirty years. You have been here four months and you have had one man killed."

Emeric started to say that he had not.

He stopped, because on the accounting Kaldo was doing, that was not the relevant distinction.

Hale was standing in the entry hall when he came up the hill, and had clearly been standing in a variety of places like it for four days.

He had run the household books, the staff, the repairs and the correspondence for eleven years. He was the only man in the building who knew where anything was. He was also, as of a dispatch that had arrived ahead of them, the steward of a man executed for treason, and he had the look of somebody who had packed a bag and left it somewhere he could reach it quickly.

Emeric asked him what he thought should be done about the drawbridge chain.

Hale did not answer for a full four seconds, and the reason was not that he had no opinion. It was that in eleven years nobody had ever asked him for one and he did not have the mechanism ready.

Emeric kept him. Not out of mercy, though he let the man believe that for a while because it cost nothing. He kept him because Hale was the only living person who knew which of the castle's arrangements had been real and which had been handholds Wexler had built into a wall he expected to climb, and turning him out of the gate handed that knowledge to nobody at all.

The rest of it arrived as comedy and then stopped being funny somewhere around the second afternoon.

Who signed for the mill lease. He did. Who authorized the ferry repair. He did. Who settled a boundary dispute on the west farms between two men whose grandfathers had started it, who set the tithe schedule with the parish, who told forty-odd men in the lower ward whether to keep drilling and why, who decided what happened to a widow whose husband had died owing the barony four gold and whose barn was the only thing she had that was worth four gold.

He had spent four months solving this town's problems as a visitor with a good idea and no obligation to any of them. He was now the reason the problems formed a queue at all.

It landed properly on a Thursday, over a roof.

A woman came up the hill about a roof. Not a plague. Not a conspiracy. Not a murder. A roof on the north side of the lower ward that had been bad for two winters and was now going to be very bad for one more, and a landlord in the forge district who said it was not his and a parish that said it was not theirs.

Emeric listened to the whole thing twice and then discovered that there was nobody standing behind him to hand it to.

He sorted it out badly and slowly. It took an hour and a half, involved two people who should not have been in the room, and produced an arrangement that he could already see was going to be wrong in about a year. Then he went and sat down and wrote a new list, and this one was not a diagnosis. The last one had been a list of everything wrong with a place. This one was a list of things that were now his job.

He asked Hale for a map of the barony's borders and got an accounts map, which was borders drawn by who owed what, and was useless in every way except the one that mattered.

Then he asked who was on the other side of them, and Hale turned out to have been waiting eleven years for somebody to ask him something he knew.

Milford, directly west, one day's ride, sharing a land border along Kaldo's western flank past the farms. Plains and open pasture. Cattle, grain-fed beef, wool and leather, traded for Kaldo's fish and grain back when Kaldo had been sending any. No mine, no great wealth, no debt either. A whole barony quietly solvent under a Baron Paxton, who, in Hale's first genuinely useful sentence of the afternoon, was not a fool, and had been perfectly content to sell Kaldo beef for eleven years without once asking aloud why Kaldo had stopped buying much of it.

Grayrock, upriver in the hill country, at the headwaters. Roughly a day out. Mining. Ore, cut stone, and every crystal vein in the region worth putting a tool to. The current off those hills ran fast and steep, so ore came down on the water for almost nothing and nothing at all went back up it.

Emeric stopped him there and made him say the last part again.

A man who thought he was describing geography had just told him that there was a barony upstream whose entire economy ran in one direction. Down. Into here.

Trade without treaty, Hale said, when pressed. Wexler had let every political tie in the region lapse and had kept exactly one contract alive, which was the ore, because ore was money and the rest was courtesy.

Wayford, on the road north to the capital, at about the midpoint, two to three days from either end. A junction town living on a waystation economy, because the land gave no other way through without adding a week to the journey, so every road worth using funneled into it whether anybody liked Wayford or not.

Emeric sketched it that night on the back of the accounts map, badly, and saw the whole thing in one pass.

Kaldo sat where the rivers joined and ran on south to the sea. Grayrock's water came down into it. Wayford's road came down into it. Everything out of two directions arrived here and went past, and had been going past for eleven years on a dead port, which meant that a town which had been starving had spent a decade watching its own trade sail by on its own water.

The barony was not at the end of the map. It was the last stop before the sea with the door shut.

That was the moment the port stopped being a sentimental idea about an old shipwright with nothing to do and became the largest single fact in the barony.

He wrote it on the list and did nothing about it, because the farm ate first.

He rode west with Rylan on the Monday and understood the farms in about an hour, which was the part that frightened him.

Fields worked flat on a two-field rotation. The same grain in the same ground, year after year, with the fallow half doing nothing but resting and not being fed. Yields that everybody within ten miles had agreed were simply what this soil did now, in the way that people agree about weather. And a lake sitting above the entire west shore, thirty feet above it, with every single drop that reached a field getting there in a bucket carried by a person.

He had the fix inside the hour. He had most of it inside twenty minutes. That was not the frightening part.

The frightening part was that four months ago he would have gone straight back and announced it, and he had since learned, at a cost of one season and one nineteen year old, that having the fix was the easy part and the cheap part and the part that was worth almost nothing on its own.

So he announced nothing. He asked Rylan who the farmers were that other farmers watched, and got three names, and put them in the book.

The mines were two days out and modest. A barony working with a small hamlet at the head-frames, spoil heaps visible from a mile off, a foreman who came down and reported honestly because nobody had ever given him a reason to do otherwise. Enough ore for the foundry Bram was getting and not a great deal beyond it. Worked shallow, by men who stopped when the seam turned down, because there was no way to follow it.

The rock was hard. The tools were iron. The method was a man with a hammer. There was no powder in this kingdom, in any form, anywhere, and Emeric knew exactly what that meant and did not write it down.

At present consumption the diggings supported one foundry for a few years and nothing larger. There was almost certainly more down there. Nobody in Kaldo could reach it.

So every plan he had that involved iron now had a clock attached to it, and the only two things inside a day's travel that solved it were Grayrock and the sea.

He noted both. Then he noted that he did not care for how quickly the same answer kept arriving from different directions, and that he was a man in the fourth month of holding land who had now had that thought twice in a week, and he was entirely aware of what kind of man has it, and he closed the book.

The garrison was fifty-eight names on a roll in the lower ward, drilling out of habit because nobody had told them to stop and nobody had told them why they should keep going.

Sixteen of those names had been struck through in a magistrate's evidence nine days earlier, which made the real number something closer to forty-two, and Emeric did not know which forty-two, because the roll was a lie and had been for years.

Ward's read on it was one sentence long and he did not soften it, and Emeric had already heard it in a yard in the capital.

And neither of them said, that week or for a long while after, the thing they were both carrying, which was that one of those forty-two might have taken a walk out to the mill road on an evening in the autumn.

The foundry argument took two days and was, by a distance, the best time Emeric had in that week.

The site was across the eastern branch, on the mainland bank, where the current ran hardest. Below the town. Below both wells. Close enough to walk to in a quarter of an hour and far enough that the smoke and the noise were not in anybody's street.

The build was brick over stone and timber, two storeys, a water wheel off the race, and framed for three furnaces.

Bram objected to the third. Then to the second.

He had been the only smith in this barony for twenty years and had never once needed two of anything. One fire, one hammer, one man. He said it without heat, the way he said most things, and it was not stubbornness. It was twenty years of evidence.

Ferra objected harder and with numbers, which was worse. A third again on brick. A third again on wheel gearing, because the gearing had to be cut for the load the building might one day carry rather than the load it would have. Somewhere around half a season of soap revenue, spent on capacity that would sit cold and be swept out once a month by a man being paid to sweep it.

Emeric did not argue that they would need three. He was careful about that, because he could feel how much he wanted to sound like a man who knew the future and had decided several months ago that this was a voice he was not going to use in this barony.

He argued the other thing. The day they needed a second furnace, adding it would mean tearing out a wall. Tearing out a wall meant stopping the first furnace. Stopping the first furnace meant losing a season, at exactly the moment when the work had finally become worth having, which is the only moment anybody ever needs a second furnace.

"A device you install before you need it costs money," he said. "A device you install after you need it costs money, and time, and everything that was running while you stopped."

He put it in terms Ferra could price. The third bay now, against eleven weeks of a working foundry standing idle in two years' time.

She ran it. It took her most of an evening. She came back and said that the arithmetic held, that she did not like it, and that she was conceding the arithmetic and explicitly not conceding the judgment, and that she wanted that written in her book in those words. Emeric said that she could write whatever she wanted in her book. She said she was aware of that.

Bram conceded differently.

He walked the marked ground on the second afternoon, on his own, with the pegs already in. He stood for a while in the rectangle where the third bay would go, hands behind him, and looked at it the way a man looks at a room in a house he has not bought yet.

"I suppose I had better find somebody to teach," he said.

That was the actual reason for the third furnace and neither of them said it. It was not for Bram. It was for two apprentices who did not exist yet.

Imani came out to look, because she went where the town went, and stood on the bank with her arms folded and asked one question.

"Where does it go?"

"Where does what go?"

"Whatever comes out of it."

Emeric opened his mouth and nothing came out of it.

He had sited a foundry by transport. Water for the wheel, road for the ore, bank firm enough for a building. He had spent an entire season teaching this town, house by house, that everything goes somewhere and that the somewhere is usually downhill and usually into somebody's water, and he had walked a site four times without once asking the question he had made four hundred people learn.

He got away with it. The site was already below the town and below both wells, because the current ran hardest there and he had wanted the wheel.

He said out loud, in front of Imani and Ferra, that it had been luck and not design. Ferra wrote that down too, and he saw her do it, and let her.

That night he put a standing rule in his own notebook. Nothing gets sited in this barony until somebody has asked where it goes.

The last argument was the one Bram did not understand and took the longest to stop returning to.

The wheel drove the bellows and the hammer. The crystals gave the heat, and would give it evenly, and would hold a number the way no charcoal hearth in the world could hold one. And the whole building was built so that it would run on charcoal and a hand bellows if every stone in it went dead in the same hour.

"You keep drawing the worse version alongside the good one," Bram said. "It is twice the work."

"The good version belongs to whoever controls the stones." Emeric did not look up from the sheet. "I would rather Kaldo own something slow than rent something fast."

Bram thought about that for a while and then said that he supposed the boy had been in one or two rooms after all, which was as close to agreement as he got that week.

Ferra's assignment came on the Friday and she thought it was a joke for the first thirty seconds.

Every ledger. Every tithe roll. Every parish record, work document, lease, apprenticeship paper and militia list in the barony. Not for money. For people.

Who had ever been paid as a carpenter. Who had worked the diggings before the war. Who had cut stone, laid brick, driven a cart, kept bees, handled a team, built a boat. Who was currently doing something far beneath what they knew how to do, because the thing they knew how to do had stopped existing here eleven years ago and they had gone and found whatever was left.

And whether there was one single person in this barony who could cut a rune.

She pointed out that no such list had ever been compiled in any barony she had heard of. That records were not organized that way and had never been organized that way. That a tithe roll told you what a household owed and not what a household could do. That it would take months.

Then she said yes before he had finished offering, because she had already worked out that she could do the whole thing inside, on sheets, and was three steps into designing the columns.

Fletcher and Abloh were going to be on that list. Emeric knew both names and did not say either of them out loud, because he wanted them to come off the record rather than off his memory, and because a list you have already decided the answer to is not a list.

He did not go into the shop on the south row.

He had sent money from the capital through Imani, without a note, which Sela had taken because she had two mouths less to feed than she had a year ago and none of the pride that would have made refusing it feel like anything at all. She had not closed the shop. She had not stopped running the north well. She had not spoken to him since the counter.

He did not send another purse and he did not walk past the window twice. He ran the barony well enough that the reason she had to hate him stayed the one she already had, which was the only thing available to him and was not anything.

The list went into the back of Armand's notebook that night, written out properly for the first time.

Farms. Foundry. Garrison. Port. Petram. Armand, who had not said no. A burial ground half moved. A roof on the north side of the lower ward. A woman at the north well who would not look at him.

And at the bottom, where he had been keeping it for six days without putting it into words:

Wexler paid for the road. Wexler did not order Tam. Those are not the same hand.

He looked at it for a while.

Then he closed the book without writing the next line.
