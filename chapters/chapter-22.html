Chapter 22 - A Barony Without a Baron

The drawbridge was down, and it was hanging on rope.

He saw it from the far end of the moat bridge, because it was the kind of thing he saw first. Two tarred hawsers ran up into the gatehouse where a chain should have run, and a third had been lashed in beside them by somebody who did not trust the first two. Ferra's pages had said the chain was paid for twice. Nobody had written down what was hung there instead, and here it was, holding.

The man on the South Gate was new. He was older than the one in April, grey in the beard, and he did it correctly. He came out, looked at the carriage, looked at the cart, and asked for the paper before he asked for anybody's name.

Petram was the crown's until somebody signed for him, and the escort's sergeant had ridden six days to see it done. Emeric signed on the cart's tailboard, under the gate arch. The sergeant folded the paper into his coat and had his men turned for the north road before the ink was dry, and Declan took Petram up the hill on foot, irons on, the guard's cloak still over them.

That was the whole ceremony.

Nobody rioted and nobody cheered. He had built four arguments on the road, and nobody in the street wanted any of them.

The shop was open, with two customers inside, one of them counting coppers out of a palm. At the north well the fire was lit under the big kettle, and the woman tending it did not look up as the carriage passed, and went on not looking up until it had gone by. Beyond the south-east wall, where the ground fell away to the hollow, a long strip of new earth lay darker than the grass round it, three weeks of it, and further down a line of stakes marked where the next three weeks would go.

People stood in doorways. They did not come out of them. They watched him the way you watch weather coming in over water, neither afraid of it nor glad of it, waiting to see what it would do to the roof.

Ward had said they would want to watch. Emeric did not look round at Ward.

Bram was waiting at the corner below the forge district with his apron still on. He had come for his daughter. He handed her down out of the carriage, took her board without asking and tucked it under his arm, and then looked up at Emeric on his horse for long enough that Emeric reined in.

"They're not angry," Bram said. "Don't think it."

"What are they?"

"Watching." Bram shifted the board. "Wexler was a bastard and he was theirs for thirty years. You have been here four months and you have had one man killed."

Emeric opened his mouth to say that he had never killed anybody.

He shut it again. Bram had not said he killed anyone. He had said had, and had was exact.

"Yes," he said.

Bram nodded once, as at a bar that had come out of the quench right, and turned for home. Ferra went with him. She had her hand up at her hair, where the road had put a week of dust into the red, and she was already telling her father what paper cost in Wayford.



Hale had a bag packed.

It stood just inside the door of his office: leather, the size a man carries when he is not sure how far he is going, buckled and tied, with a cloak folded over the top. Hale was at the table behind it with the daybook open, writing, in the same coat he had worn every day Emeric had known him.

He stood. "Your Highness."

He had carried a speech up the stair with him, the one he had been building since the capital about who held Kaldo now, and with the bag on the floor between them he could see that it was the wrong one. Anything he said here about who held Kaldo would go into the room as an order. The man in front of him had taken orders in this room for twenty years and had packed a bag against the next one.

"The drawbridge," Emeric said. "It's on rope."

"It is, Your Highness."

"What should it be on?"

Hale did not answer.

He did not look at the candle, which was where he looked when he had bad news. He looked at Emeric, which he almost never did, and the pause went on long enough that Ward, in the doorway, shifted his weight. Emeric watched a man work out that he had been asked a question, and what kind, and that there was nobody else in the room it could be for.

"Iron," Hale said. "Not rope." And then, as though it had been standing behind those two words a long while: "It was always meant to be iron."

"Then it'll be iron."

Hale looked down at the bag. He came round the table, picked it up by the strap, and carried it back to the corner behind his chair, where there was a pale square on the boards the size of its base. He set it there, and sat, and turned a page in the daybook.

Twenty years in the house and eleven of them as steward, and as far as Emeric could tell nobody in all that time had asked him what anything should be. They had asked him what it cost, and whether it was done.

He kept Hale because Hale knew which of Wexler's arrangements had been real. Half the barony's business had never come in by the front door, and only one living man knew which doors it had used. That was the reason he gave Ward on the stair, and it was true.

He did not move into the baron's rooms. He kept the one he had been given in April, and that night he lay in it with his boots off and his mother's stone in his hand, and it was what it had been all summer: a room in a house he had not bought yet.

Then the barony arrived, and did not stop.

A mill lease that had run out in the summer, while the man who would have renewed it rode north to be tried. A boundary on the west farms that two families had been moving back and forth by night for a generation, and now wanted settled, by him, since there was a him. The tithe schedule. The garrison's pay, which fell due whether or not anybody had decided whom the garrison answered to. A widow who owed the barony four Silver Rand and had nothing to pay it with but a barn, who had brought the deed to the barn wrapped in a cloth and set it on his table.

He did not take the barn. He did not know yet what he would do instead, and he told her so. She looked at him as if it was the first honest thing anyone in the keep had said to her in years, and no use to her at all.

A woman from the lower ward came about her roof. Half of it had come down in the spring. The landlord said the roof was the parish's, because the parish had put it on after the war. The parish said a roof belonged to the house it was on. They had been saying so since April, and it had rained in her back room all summer.

It took him ninety minutes. At the end of them the landlord would find the timber, the parish the men and the woman the beer, and the arrangement held only because none of the three wanted to tell a prince no in front of the other two. He gave it a year. He could see where: the landlord would be short of timber in the spring, and the parish would decide its men had been lent, not given.

By the third evening there was a new list in the back of Armand's notebook, under the old one. It had no heading, because he did not have a word yet for things that were now his job, and it was already the longer of the two.

On the third day after they came home, Hale brought him the dispatch.

It had come down the south road by crown rider under the judiciary's seal, and it was the only thing the capital sent him. It said nothing about the barony and nothing about him. It had one line on it.

Sentence carried out.

He read it twice. Then he gave it back to Hale, who entered it in the daybook under the date, in the same hand he used for lamp oil. Hale was now the steward of a man executed for treason. He did not appear to think it needed saying.

Ward had read it over his shoulder, one of the few liberties he took.

"That is done," Ward said.

"The road's done." Emeric looked at the window. "Wexler paid for the road. Wexler did not order Tam."

Ward said nothing.

"Those are not the same hand."

"No," said Ward, and that was the whole conversation, and neither of them needed to have it twice.

Hale's figures came up the same afternoon, in Silver, in his small upright hand: the barony's coin, all of it, counted twice. Emeric turned it into Gold in his head and it came to less than half of what was left in his own pouch. Everything else Kaldo was worth was grain in the keep's stores, the mill, and land nobody could sell to anyone. What the holder of Kaldo could draw from it rose with what Kaldo produced, and Kaldo produced very nearly nothing.

He had four guards on his purse at two Copper a day each. He could put them on the barony's payroll and save the pouch, and then every copper they drew would sit in Hale's daybook beside a garrison nobody had sorted yet, and the first thing that went wrong would read as bad government instead of bad luck.

He kept paying them himself.



Hale gave him the neighbours on the fourth evening, standing, from memory, the way another man might name his children.

Milford, a day west, under Baron Paxton: cattle, beef, wool and leather, and a man who paid his bills. Milford had gone on selling Kaldo its beef straight through the plague and never once written to ask why the orders kept shrinking. Grayrock, about a day upriver, in the hills the big river came down out of: ore, cut stone, a few crystal veins. The current brought Grayrock's ore down to Kaldo cheap, and nothing went back up against that water at a price anyone would pay. Wayford, at the junction on the south road, halfway to the capital, which lived on the traffic going through it and on nothing else.

"And Wexler?"

"He let them go, Your Highness." Hale looked at the candle. "All but the ore."

Emeric drew it afterward on the back of a spoiled ledger sheet, with the bad pen. Kaldo sat where the river ran on to the sea. Ore came down from Grayrock, beef across from Milford, everything else by the south road. Trade had been passing from two directions toward the coast since the war, and going by the dock quarter on its way, where three moorings in four stood empty and the piers were going soft. A town at the last stop before the sea, with its door shut.

The port was the biggest fact in the barony. He looked at it on the page and put it aside, because the farm ate first, and nobody opened a port in an autumn.

The mines were two days out, Hale said, in the barony's own hills, and shut. Shallow workings, cut with hammers and iron bars, abandoned wherever a seam turned down and the water came in. Emeric asked how deep was deep. Hale did not know. Nobody had gone below where a man with a hammer could reach.

There was no powder anywhere in the kingdom. He had known that since the capital and never once followed it to the end. He followed it now, to a seam that turned down and a man with a hammer standing at the bottom of it, and did not write it down.

What he wrote, before he had decided to, was that the ore in those hills might carry one foundry for a few years, and that after that only Grayrock or the sea would solve iron. He looked at the line. He had arrived at that answer before, by other roads, and every time it came more easily. Take Grayrock. Open the sea.

He closed the book.

Ward brought the garrison roll up the same night. He had been carrying it since the capital and had evidently decided it had kept long enough.

Fifty-eight names. Ferra's sixteen were already struck through, the men who drew pay and existed nowhere else, which left forty-two. They went down them one at a time by a single candle, Ward saying what he knew of each and Emeric listening, and most of what Ward knew was a face and a post and a habit.

Tam's name was on the second page.

It was still there because nobody had told the roll he was dead. Emeric dipped the pen and struck it through himself, one line, and wrote the date beside it, and the count at the foot of the page went to forty-one.

Neither of them said the other thing: that somewhere in those forty-one there might be a man who had walked out along the mill road on a summer evening and come back alone. Ward looked at the roll and Emeric looked at the roll, and the candle burned down about the width of a finger, and then Ward took the roll back and went to bed.



The old works stood across the east bridge on the far bank, below the dock quarter, where the river began its long bend south.

Somebody had built it to last and then stopped needing it. Brick over a stone footing, timber above, two storeys, with a wheel on the river side that still turned when the water let it, which at the end of summer was not often. The wheel's lower paddles stood in mud. Inside, the ground floor ran back in three long bays, each with a stone footing for a furnace at its head. The first two had carried furnaces once; their footings were black. The third footing was clean, with old surveyor's pegs still standing round it, grey and split, where whoever built the place had marked out a hearth he meant to come back for.

Bram walked the length of it twice with his hands behind his back.

"Three," he said.

"Three bays."

"I've been the only smith in this barony twenty years." He stopped at the head of the second bay and glared at its footing. "I've never once needed two of anything."

"You've never had anything to need them for."

Bram made a noise that did not commit him to anything.

Ferra had come with her board and a stub of chalk, because Emeric had asked her to price it and she did not price what she had not seen. She had walked it once, more slowly than her father, and was now sitting on a fallen beam in the light from the river door, adding. Ward stayed in the door.

"Fitting the first bay is the forge moved across the river," she said, without looking up. "Fitting all three is about half a season of soap."

"Half a season."

"Of what the yard took this summer, before the winter takes it back off us." She tapped the chalk on the board. "For a bay nobody is going to stand in."

He did not argue that anybody would. That was the trouble. He did not know who would stand in the third bay, or when, and anything he said about it would be a guess, and she would write it down as one.

So he argued the other thing. Fitting a bay while the works stood empty cost what it cost. Fitting it after the first two were running meant shutting them: drawing the fires, moving the men, breaking open the floor of the one building in Kaldo that would be making anything, and paying every man in it to stand about while that was done. He did the sum aloud for her, weeks of a shut works against the price of stone now. He got it wrong in the middle. She corrected it without lifting her eyes from the board, and the corrected sum still came out his way.

She looked at it.

Then she wrote in the corner of her book where she kept lines she meant to come back to, and turned it so he could read.

Arithmetic: agreed. Judgment: not.

"That's fair," he said.

"I know it's fair."

When Emeric looked round for Bram he was standing in the third bay, inside the old pegs, at about the place a man would stand to feed a furnace that did not exist. He looked at the clean footing at his feet, then at the two black ones beside it, then at the river door, like a man counting something that was not there.

"I suppose," he said, "I had better find somebody to teach."

Nobody answered that. Ferra wrote something else, small, under the first line.

They went over how it would run. Crystals from the chest in the hearth mounts, for the heat. The wheel, when the river came back up, for the bellows and a hammer. And under all of it the old way, charcoal and hand bellows, so that on the day the stones went flat the works slowed down and did not stop.

"It'll be slower without the stones," Bram said.

"Yes."

"A good deal slower."

"I would rather Kaldo own something slow than rent something fast."

Bram found nothing in it to object to, which from Bram was close to applause.

Imani came over the east bridge late in the afternoon with her bag on her shoulder and her sleeves still rolled to the elbow from whatever had kept her in the dock quarter since morning. She saw them through the river door and changed course without breaking stride, the way she went toward anything that might be about to need her.

Ward let her past. She stood just inside and looked down the three bays, and then out at the river, low and brown under the wheel, running on south past the works.

"Where does it go?"

He looked at her.

"All of it." She tipped her head at the footings. "Whatever you burn. Whatever you wash off. Whatever comes out of the bottom of the thing. Where does it go?"

He opened his mouth, and there was nothing ready to come out of it.

He had chosen a works the size of a barn on a river without once asking what it would put into the river. He had the hearth heats in his head and not one line about what came out the other end. The site was below every well in Kaldo, and below the dock quarter's water too, and from here the river ran on to nothing but the salt runs and the sea. That was luck. He had picked it because it had a wheel.

"I never asked," he said. "It's downstream of everything. That's luck, not sense."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth, and she put it away again at once, the way she put away a tool she had finished with. Her forearms were red to the elbow from somebody's hot water, and she had not rolled her sleeves down.

"I know," she said. "I wanted to hear you say it."

He laughed. It got out of him before he could stop it, too loud for an empty building, because she had done it again, put her finger on the one thing he had not looked at, and he could not remember being so glad to be caught at anything.

She watched him laugh with deep suspicion, as though he had paid her for a delivery in advance.

Her name had been read out three times in a room in the capital she had never stood in, and she did not know it. He was not going to be the one to tell her on a riverbank. He put that away as well.

"Nothing gets sited," he said, "until somebody has asked where it goes."

Ferra was already writing it. She did not put it in the corner with the other line. She turned to a clean page of her working book and put it at the top, by itself, where it could be found again, and underlined it once.

Imani watched her do it, and nodded to her, and Ferra nodded back without looking up, and that was the whole of what passed between them. Then Imani hitched her bag higher on her shoulder and went back over the bridge toward the town.



On the walk back across the bridge he gave Ferra the next job.

He wanted to know who was in Kaldo. Not how many: who. Every carpenter, every man who had dug before the war, every mason and carter and beekeeper and boatbuilder. Everyone working beneath the trade they had been trained to, and what the trade had been. And whether there was anybody, anywhere in the barony, who could cut a rune.

"From what?"

"Every record there is."

"There aren't any records. There's the tax roll, which is wrong, and Doran's book, which is the dead, and the tithe map, which is thirty years old." She shifted the board to her other arm. "And whatever people will tell me at their doors. Which won't be what they'd tell you."

"No."

"It's a month. More."

"I know."

She walked half the length of the bridge without a word, and he could see her starting it: which records first, which streets, what she would ask at a door and in what order. Somewhere about the middle of the bridge she said yes, before he had finished telling her why he wanted it, and kept walking.

He did not mention Abloh, or Fletcher, or anyone else he already knew could do something. He wanted to see who she found.

At the bridge foot she turned off toward the forge district, and he and Ward went up through the lower ward toward the keep. The straight way ran past Sela's shop. He took the next lane over, and then the one after that, and came out at the canal ring a good deal further round than he needed to, and Ward walked beside him the whole way and did not say one word about the route.
