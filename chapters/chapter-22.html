Chapter 22 - Shear

The idea was Ward's, which Emeric thought about a great deal afterward.

"You've a wall you've never seen fought," Ward said. "You've walked it. You've had it built. You've stood on it in the rain and told me where you'd put a man. You've never once watched anything come up that approach at you, and neither has anyone else in this barony under fifty."

"Corliss has."

"Corliss has, at Petram's camp, on the wrong side of a ditch, and he'd tell you it was nothing like a wall." Ward set the plan of the circuit down. "Which is my point. You've drawn the ground and I've drawn the ground and we've both drawn it as men who know how it looks. I want it drawn by somebody who knows how it goes."

So Emeric asked Corliss, and he did it properly, which he was afterward glad of in a way that gave him no comfort at all.

He asked him in daylight, awake, in the office with the door open and Ferra at the table. He explained what it was, as plainly as he could and without making it sound better than it was. He explained that the cost fell on the opener and not the carried. He explained that it was geometry and that it had no physics in it, and that it would agree with whoever held it, and that this was a hazard and not a convenience. He read him the three rules off the sheet. He told him he could say no, and that saying no cost him nothing, and that if he said yes he could come out at any time by asking to.

Corliss listened to the whole thing with his arms folded and his face like a closed door, which was how he had listened to everything Emeric had said to him for eleven months.

"Why me," he said.

"Because you're the only man in the garrison who's been on the receiving end of an assault and lived, and because I've drawn that approach nine times and every one of them is a drawing by a man who has never been frightened on it."

"And because I'll say no and you want to be able to say you asked."

"That's genuinely not why, and I've no way to prove it to you, and I'd be suspicious too." Emeric put the sheet down. "You can say no. Nothing changes. You keep the file, you keep the pay, and I'll go and ask Ward's second and get a worse answer."

Corliss looked at the sheet for a long time.

"I'll do it," he said. "Not for you."

"I never assumed otherwise."

"There's forty of us on that wall and thirty-eight have never seen a spear come at them and they think it'll be like the drill." He unfolded his arms. "If there's a room where I can show you what it's actually like so you stop putting men where you'd put them, I'll go in the room."

They did it that evening in the long room with Ward outside the door, because Ward would not come in and had said so, and because somebody had to be able to say afterward how long it had been.

Emeric opened it and put up the ground.

He built the wall first, the whole circuit, the way it actually stood, patched and closed and running further than forty men could hold. He built the gate towers and the ford and the hundred paces of graded approach with no cover on it, and he built the rise where the road came over, and the two river arms anchoring the ends.

It came up fast and clean and he was, he admitted later, showing off slightly.

"That's it," Corliss said.

"That's it."

"That's it exactly." He walked to the parapet and put his hands on it, and then looked at his hands on it, and then, in a completely different voice, said, "That's the stone. That's the actual stone, that's the chip in it where the mason's boy dropped the wedge."

"You've stood there every third night for a year. You know that stone better than I know my own hands."

Corliss stood on Kaldo's wall in a room with no floor in it and looked out over the approach.

"Put them on it," he said.

"Put who on it?"

"Them. Whoever's coming. Put them on the rise and bring them down the approach and let me stand here and watch it, because that's what you asked for and that's the only way I can tell you anything you don't already know."

Emeric hesitated. He remembered hesitating. It was, in the way these things go, the only part of the evening he could reconstruct with confidence afterward, and he had gone over it perhaps two hundred times.

Because there was a rule about that. Not one of the three on the sheet. A fourth one, unwritten, which he had held privately since Bram had burned his hand on a cold stone: the place gave you what you believed, and therefore you did not build people in it. Shapes, yes. Stone and water and iron and slope, yes, because a shape held still was the whole value of the thing. But he had never put a person in it, not once in sixteen years, not his mother, not his brothers, not anyone, and the reason he had never done it was that a shape you believed in was a shape and a person you believed in was something else.

He had, he realised, never written that rule down, because he had never had to say it to anyone.

"I can put the shapes up," he said. "Men-shaped. Not men. There's nothing in them, they'll do what I expect them to do, and what I expect is worthless, which is the entire reason you're here."

"Then put up what you expect and I'll tell you where you're wrong."

Which was, Emeric thought at the time and still thought afterward, exactly the right procedure.

He put them on the rise. Four hundred of them, because four hundred was the number in his head, in loose order, coming down the graded approach with no cover on it toward a closed wall.

And Corliss, standing on the parapet, believed it.

Emeric felt it happen. That was the part he could not explain to Imani afterward and could not explain to Ward at all: he felt the shapes get heavier. He was holding them, all four hundred, the way he held anything in there, approximately and then corrected, and something else took hold of the same weight from the other side and pulled it into focus.

Corliss knew what men coming at a wall looked like. He knew the sound. He knew the specific way a line does not stay a line. He knew, with forty years of body behind it, what it felt like to be a man behind stone with something arriving, and every single one of those things was true in a place whose only law was that it gave you what you knew.

The approach filled up.

"They'll not come down the middle," Corliss said, and they stopped coming down the middle, and Emeric had not moved them.

"Corliss."

"They'll come at the joint where the new stone meets the old, because that's the only place on this whole circuit where the line steps, and a man running at a wall goes where the wall looks broken even if it isn't."

They went for the joint. Hundreds of them, all at once, in exactly the way Emeric had never once drawn and would never have thought of, and the noise of it was extraordinary, and it was Corliss's noise, out of Corliss's memory, and Emeric was carrying every ounce of it.

"Corliss, I'm putting you out."

"Wait. Wait. There's a thing about the second rank, they'll not." He turned to point at it and did not finish the sentence, because the thing that had come over the parapet in front of him was one Emeric had never made, and Corliss put his left arm up the way a man does who has done it before, and the arm was where it needed to be, and it did not matter.

Emeric let go of everything at once.

The long room came back. He went down onto one knee and put his hand out and got the table leg. Somewhere above and to the left of him Corliss made a sound that was not a word.

Then the smell.

Ward was through the door before Emeric had got his eyes to focus, which meant he had been standing with his hand on it, and he took two steps and stopped, and Emeric heard him stop, and in eleven months he had never once heard Ward stop.

Corliss was on the floor with his left forearm laid open from the wrist to below the elbow.

Not broken. Not bruised. Opened, along the outside of the arm, in a single clean line, the way a limb opens when something with an edge on it comes down on the place a man has put it, and there was nothing in the long room with an edge on it, and nobody else had touched him, and there had been two people in the room and one of them had been on his knees by the table.

"Imani," Ward said, into the corridor, at a volume Emeric had never heard him use. "Now. Rylan, run."

Emeric got there on his hands and knees, which was the only means of travel available, and got his coat under the arm and both hands on it above the elbow because that was the only thing he knew how to do, and Corliss looked at him with a face full of nothing but honest confusion and said, "What did that?"

"I don't know."

"There's nothing in the room."

"I know. I know there isn't."

"Then what did that," Corliss said again, not angry, which was the worst of it, and then Imani was there and Emeric was moved out of the way by a woman half his size who did not ask permission.

She worked for a long time. It was, by the standards of what it looked like, an extremely good wound: clean-edged, no dirt in it, nothing crushed, the vessel that mattered missed by the width of a finger. She said so twice while she was working, in the flat voice she used when she wanted people to stop making noise.

"This is a blade wound," she said, eventually, without looking up. "I have seen forty of these. This is a blade wound made by a blade, on a clean edge, with the arm braced. And there is no blade."

Nobody answered.

"I'm going to be told," Imani said. "Not because I'm owed it. Because I'm going to be dressing this arm for six weeks and there are eleven ways to treat a wound and the right one depends entirely on what made it, and I have just picked one out of a hat in front of you."

So Emeric told her, sitting on the floor of the long room with his back against the table leg, because standing was again not available and because he had run out of any argument for waiting.

He told her all of it. What it was. That it agreed with whoever held it. That he had asked, in daylight, awake, with the rules read out, and that Corliss had said yes and had said why, and that the why had been about thirty-eight men who thought it would be like the drill.

And then he told her the part that mattered, which he had worked out on the floor while she was working on the arm, and which he was fairly sure was correct.

"I built the ground," he said. "I built the wall and the slope and the stone, and all of that was mine, and none of it could hurt anybody because I don't know anything about being hurt on a wall. Then I put four hundred shapes on the approach, and they were mine too, and they were rubbish, and they were rubbish because I've never seen it." He looked at his own hands. "And he had. He has forty years of knowing exactly what that looks like and exactly what it feels like, and in there, knowing a thing is the same as it being true. He believed it harder than I built it. So it stopped being my shape and started being his memory, and I was still carrying it, and I did not let go fast enough."

"Say the last part again," Imani said.

"I did not let go fast enough."

"No. Before that."

Emeric was quiet for a moment.

"He was telling me something I needed," he said. "He was four words from telling me something about the second rank that I have wanted for nine months and cannot get anywhere else, and I had my hand on it, and I could feel it going wrong, and I waited."

Ward said nothing at all. That was somehow the loudest thing in the room.

Corliss said, from the table, in a perfectly level voice, "Two seconds."

"Corliss."

"I felt you wait. I don't know how I know that and I know it." He was grey and his arm was wrapped and he was entirely lucid, which Imani had said was the good news and which did not feel like news of any kind. "You'd have got me out. I know you'd have got me out. You waited because I was about to say something useful."

"Yes."

"Right," Corliss said, and closed his eyes, and did not say anything else.

Ward walked Emeric out into the corridor and shut the door behind them and stood with his back to it.

"Say the day," he said.

"What?"

"You agreed to a thing in the long room a month ago. You get eleven days on the good news and none at all on the bad. So say the day."

"Today. It's today."

"Aye. And?"

Emeric stood in the corridor and understood that he was about to either keep the agreement or find out what he actually was.

"There's one more," he said. "It's a month old and it isn't about tonight and I've been sitting on it since the second week."

Ward waited.

"The first night, after Bram was out and before I woke up, I went down further than I've ever been. To find the floor of it." He made himself say it in the plain order. "It got easier the deeper I went. Everything I've ever done gets harder the further in you go and this got easier, and there was a moment where I understood that I could keep going and that going would feel extremely good and that there was no reason at all to stop. And I stopped. And I have not told anybody, including Ferra, including Bram, and I wrote down a reason for not telling anybody and the reason was that it wasn't a number."

"How long ago?"

"Thirty-four days."

"And you sat in my long room a month ago and agreed to tell me on the day."

"Yes."

Ward looked at him for a long moment, and what was on his face was not anger, and Emeric would have very much preferred anger.

"That's the one that frightens me," Ward said. "Not the arm. The arm's a thing that happened in a room nobody understands and I'd not hang a man for it." He straightened off the door. "You agreed to a rule in front of me and broke it inside a fortnight by doing nothing at all, which is how every man I've ever watched go bad started, and you're the one who wrote the rule, and you're the one who's just told me, and I have no idea what to do with either of those facts."

"Neither have I."

"No." Ward opened the door back onto the room where Imani was working. "Write it in the book. Tonight. All of it, the arm and the thirty-four days, in Ferra's hand and not yours, and let her put the date on it."

Emeric wrote a fourth rule that night, and Ferra wrote the rest of it, and the fourth rule was five words long and he did not think it was sufficient and wrote it anyway.

Nothing alive goes in there.

Under it, in his own list, under the space, in the place where he kept the things he could not put in a book anybody else read:

Every rule was followed. I asked, awake, in daylight, with the cost read out, and he consented, and he consented for a good reason, and a man has a hole in his arm. The rules were not the safeguard. I was the safeguard, and I waited two seconds for a piece of information.

Corliss kept the arm and the use of it. It took eight weeks and Imani was extremely rude to him about the exercises and he did them.

He never once told anyone in the garrison what had happened. Emeric found that out by degrees over the following month, from the elaborate stories that went round instead, none of which involved the long room, and he understood eventually that Corliss had not done it to protect him.

He had done it because thirty-eight men who thought it would be like the drill would have to stand on that wall regardless, and a story about a prince's magic opening a man's arm was not a story you wanted in a garrison six weeks before a muster.

What he did say, the one time he said anything, was at the door of the barracks with his arm still strapped, on his way back onto the roster.

"The joint," he said. "Where the new stone meets the old. Put your best four there and don't let anyone talk you out of it."

"I'd worked that out. From in there."

"I know you did." Corliss went in. "That's what it cost."
