Chapter 158 - Boarded and Burned

They got her off the rocks with two harbour boats and a line, in the last of the light, and towed her in stern first to the fish quay because it was nearest, and she sat down against it with a groan of timber and lay there, down by the head, with the water in her forepeak and the smell coming off her.

Emeric smelled it from the terrace before he reached the quay. Burnt sulphur. He knew it from the spoil at Grayrock and the iron-salt heaps and the corner of the foundry yard in the thaw, the sharp choking reek of it that got into the back of the throat and stayed. It came off her in a haze. It came up out of her hold where the hatch covers had burned through, and off the scorched planking of her deck, and off her one standing sail, which was brown with it.

She was the Gannet. Saban's hire. He had hired her in the first spring off a Kaldo-side coast owner with three boats and a bad knee, and put her on the lane with a mixed crew, half the owner's men and half from the lower water, because the lower water had lightermen who wanted the sea and Saban wanted men on the lane he could vouch for.

There were eleven of them aboard her when she left the works quay laden, four days ago.

There were five alive.

The harbour-mistress's men brought them up one at a time. Two could walk. Two were carried. One was carried and did not wake. Imani was not here. There was a physician of Rowena's, a lean brisk woman from the upper town, and she took them into the fish market's shed and cleared the tables and sent for water and lamps, and did not ask anybody's permission.

Then they brought up the dead.

There were six. They laid them on the quay in a row, on sailcloth, in the lamplight, because there was nowhere else to lay them, with their faces covered. And one more, apart from the others, a little way off by the bollards, on the bare stones, not covered, because nobody on the quay knew who he was.

Emeric walked down the row.

He knew three of them.

He had not expected to. He had thought, walking down from the terrace, that they would be the owner's men, coast men, strangers. He lifted the first sailcloth because the harbour-mistress asked him whether he could say who they were, and under it was Bede Harrow, who had rowed a lighter at the lower water for as long as there had been a lower water, and who had taken Fletcher's hull up the river on the day it was bought, on a tow, singing. And under the third was a young man called Tollin, whose mother kept the eel traps below the quay, and who had asked Saban for the lane because he wanted to see a mountain that burned. And under the sixth was Moss, old Moss, who had been a lighterman forty years and had never been further than the marsh mouth in his life until the spring, and had come back from his first run with a lump of sulphur in his pocket to show his grandchildren.

He covered them again. He did it carefully, the way he had seen Doran do it.

"Harrow," he said to the harbour-mistress. "Bede Harrow. Tollin Sedge. Moss." He did not know Moss's other name. He found that he had never known it. "The others I don't know. They'll be the owner's."

The harbour-mistress wrote them down.

Saban had the story from the two who could walk before the physician had finished with them. He brought it up to the quay where Emeric was standing, and told it quietly, with his back to the row.

Two days out from the inlet's mouth, laden, making east along the coast in a fair wind in the forenoon. A boat came out from the shore. Long and low, with oars and a lugsail, fast, twenty men in her or more. No colours. Nothing painted on her. The men in her dressed like anybody on any coast. The Gannet's master had thought they were fishermen until they were near enough to see the hooks and the bows.

They came over the side. The Gannet's men had knives and a boathook and two old crossbows the owner kept aboard, and they fought, and they lost, and it did not take long. The raiders did not want the boat. They did not want the men. They did not ask for anything and they did not take anything that anybody saw. They opened the hatches and threw the sulphur over the side, sack after sack, as fast as twenty men could do it, and when they had thrown what they could they poured something on the rest and fired it, and went back over the side into their own boat, and rowed away east along the coast with their sail up.

The survivors got the fire out. It took them half a day and most of their water, and two of them went down into the hold to do it and came up burned, and one of them did not come up. By then the Gannet was leaking at the bow where the hold fire had opened her seams, and her mainmast was gone, and they were closer to Emberfell than to anywhere, and they turned her round.

"They didn't want the boat," said Emeric.

"No."

"They didn't want the cargo."

"They wanted it at the bottom of the sea." Saban looked out at the harbour. "It's not piracy. Pirates sell sulphur. It's somebody who doesn't want sulphur coming up this coast to Kaldo."

The dead man by the bollards was one of theirs.

Kerrow's second went through his clothes, because somebody had to, and Emeric stood by while he did it. He was a raider the Gannet's men had killed on her deck with the boathook, and the others had not taken him back into their boat, which said something about them too. Thirty or so, strong, weathered. Plain brown clothes, patched. No ring, no chain, no mark on his skin but old scars. A knife. A purse on a thong round his neck under his shirt.

The purse had four coins in it.

Three were Rands. Copper, small, worn. The kind any man on any coast in the south had in his purse.

The fourth was silver. It was a little larger than a Silver Rand and a good deal thinner, and it was not a Rand. It had no king's head on it. On one face it had a tower, or a gate, with something over it that might have been a crown or a flame, rubbed nearly smooth, and on the other a ring of letters in a script Emeric did not recognise round a mark he did not know. It was not Emberfell's. He had handled Emberfell's coin all week, and it had the mountain on it.

He held it for a moment in the lamplight.

Saban put out his hand.

Emeric gave it to him. Saban looked at it for a long time, turning it, holding it close to the lamp, and his face did not change at all, which on Saban was a kind of change. Then he put it in his own purse, and did up the string, and put the purse inside his coat.

"You know it?" said Emeric.

"No," said Saban. "Not yet."

He knew what he could do, and he stood on the quay in the dark with the six covered shapes in front of him and knew it exactly.

The ship was at Kaldo, in the lake shed, with her racks full. Ward could fly her. Osric could fly her. He could fly her from here by thought if she were here, and she was not. But the relay ended at the Kaldo marsh and there was no key box in Emberfell, and a man on the coaster's deck could not send for her. So it was not that. It was the other thing. It was that if he had had her here tonight, in the band above this harbour, he could have been over the coast east of the inlet's mouth by first light, with the sense out and the day clear, and found a long low boat with twenty men and a lugsail before noon. And put her down on it.

And then everybody in two realms would know what had hunted them.

Twenty men in a boat would see a hull a hundred feet long come down out of a clear sky onto them. Some of them would live, because some always did. And they would go home to whoever had paid them in thin silver with a tower on it, and tell them. And every coast from here to Solreth would know by the autumn that the prince of Kaldo had a ship that flew and came down out of the sky on men, and so would the Crown, and so would the Circle, and so would everybody Armand was writing to.

The one thing he could not show was the one thing that would have helped most.

He did not send for her. He could not have sent for her if he had wanted to. He stood on the quay and found that he wanted to, very much, and that the wanting was not about finding the boat.

It started while he was standing there.

He felt it start. That was new. In the cellar it had come up out of him like water out of a spring, all at once, before he knew it was there. This time he felt the first of it, the way a man feels the first shiver of a fever: a pressure at the back of his skull, low, and a heat, and then the sense going out of him without his asking, out over the quay, over the six shapes under their sailcloth and the one on the bare stones by the bollards, and into the harbour, and it was not listening. It was pushing.

The lamps on the quay dipped.

Not all of them. The ones nearest. Their flames lay down sideways in their glass as if a door had opened, and there was no wind, and they stood back up. Round the fish quay, in the black water of the basin between the Gannet's hull and the stones, the surface shivered. It shivered all over at once, in fine rings, the way water in a cup shivers when a cart goes past, and there was no cart and no wind and the harbour beyond the basin was flat as oil.

Two of Rowena's knights were standing at the end of the row with torches. One of them put his hand up to his jaw. The other one stepped back from the covered dead, one step, and made the sign against the Dark, and said something to the first in a low voice. Emeric heard it. Something's wrong with them. The dead. Can you feel it.

He could feel it. He could feel all of it: the ambient mana of the quay and the basin and the six still bodies and the one on the stones gathering in toward him the way water gathers toward a drain, and the pressure in his skull going up, and a hot wet trickle starting at his upper lip.

He did what he had done on the cellar stair after Ward had put his hand on him. He did it himself this time, because there was nobody's hand. He took hold of the sense, the way he had taken hold of the burning beam in the tannery loft, and he made it light. He made it small. He pulled it back in, off the basin, off the dead, off the quay, a yard at a time, the way a man hauls in a line hand over hand against a fish.

It came.

It came back much faster than it had in the cellar. The lamps stood up straight and stayed straight. The water in the basin went still. The knight with his hand at his jaw lowered it and looked at it, puzzled, and looked at the dead again.

Emeric wiped his lip with the back of his hand. It came away dark. Not much. A little.

That frightened him more than the rest of it. In the cellar it had taken Ward's hand and a quarter of an hour and his sense had been dark for an hour after. Here it had taken him no more than twenty breaths, alone, and the sense had not gone dark. It was still there. He could feel it lying in him, quiet, obedient, as if nothing had happened.

It was getting easier to stop.

He did not know if that meant he was learning to hold it, or if it meant it was getting easier to start.

Rowena was at the end of the quay.

He did not know how long she had been there. She had come down from the hall when the bell rang, in her blue coat, and she had been standing with the harbour-mistress by the harbour house door, a long way off, fifty yards or more, talking to the physician. She was not talking now. She was standing still with her hands at her sides, looking at him.

Her knights had felt something and blamed the dead. Rowena was not looking at the dead.

She had stood on this same quay in the autumn when he came in on the ship, and he had put the sense out to get the lay of the town, and it had reached her at the foot of the slope and she had felt it pass over her and stepped back from it. Didn't know you'd gotten so skilled, Emeric. She had not said anything else about it then. She had said something about it three days later, in the yard, after the rematch, and he had not answered her properly.

She looked at him across fifty yards of lamplit quay for as long as it took the harbour-mistress to say her name twice. Then she turned and answered the harbour-mistress, and did not look at him again that night.

She asked him at her table.

It was the worst possible moment and he knew it was when she chose it. It was the next evening, at the long table in the room off the hall with the windows on the mountain, with the whole of her table there by office and the whole of his party across from it: Ward, Saban, Rylan, Nevin. The dead had been buried that morning in the sailors' ground above the harbour, the six together, the owner's men and the lower-water men side by side, and the raider on his own in the corner by the wall. Emeric had stood at the graves with Ward and Saban. Rowena had stood at the graves with her knight-captain and said nothing.

And now he was asking her for ships.

He had laid it out plainly. The lane was cut. Not the boat, the lane. A boat could be lost to a storm, and that was the sea, and every coast knew it. This had not been a storm. Somebody had paid twenty men to take a laden sulphur coaster two days out from her harbour and put her cargo at the bottom of the sea and her crew with it, and they would do it again, because it had worked. The Emberfell was iron and could not be burned and had a steam engine that could outrun a lugsail. But the Emberfell was one hull, and the lane was two boats a month, and Kaldo had no warship and no sailors who had ever fought at sea and could not build either before the autumn. Emberfell had both.

"I'm asking for an escort," he said. "Your ships, your waters, as far as your waters go. From the works quay to wherever your writ stops along the coast. And then mine."

"Your writ stops at your marsh," said Rowena.

"Then from your waters to my marsh."

Rowena did not answer that. She sat at the head of her table with her glass in front of her and her hand flat on the cloth beside it, the way she had put her hand on the pile of books in the autumn, and she looked at him.

"The ground in the yard," she said.

Nobody at the table moved.

"On the fourth morning. When we fought the second time. I slipped on ground I'd walked every morning of my life, and you were there before I'd finished slipping." She said it quite evenly. "That was you too. Wasn't it."

It was the question she had filed in the yard, after the rematch, with her sword still in her hand. He had seen her file it. He had known she would take it out again. He had not known it would be here, with her Treasurer on her right and her knight-captain at the end of the table and Ward beside him.

He gave her the answer he gave about everything he did.

"I read ground," he said. "I always have. I read stone the way Bram reads iron, and I can feel where it's soft and where it's hard, and where it's going to give. I've done it since I was a boy with a carving knife." All of that was true. "In the yard I read where you were going to put your foot. I didn't make it soft. I knew it was."

That was nearly true.

"And the quay," said Rowena. "Last night."

"I don't know what the quay was."

He said it because it was the most honest thing he had, and because she would have heard anything else.

"That's new," he said. "It's happened once before. It's not the ground and it's not the reading. I don't know what it is. I know when it starts, now, and I can stop it. I don't know why it starts." He looked at her across the table. "I'd tell you if I knew."

Rowena looked at him for a long time.

She took it. He watched her take it, the way she had taken his book of cracked furnaces and his well standard, and put it somewhere and keep it. She did not believe it was all of it. She did not need it to be.

Then she leaned back in her chair.

"I'll tell you something you didn't ask for," she said.

The table was very quiet.

"I've felt a great many mages work," said Rowena. "In the war and after it. In the capital, at your father's court, for longer than you're old enough to remember. I've fought beside Circle men and I've fought against hired ones. I know what it feels like when a strong man draws hard, or throws, or loses his temper and throws without meaning to. It's loud. You feel it in your teeth and you know where it's coming from." She turned her glass a quarter turn. "I've never felt anything like what I felt on that quay last night. It didn't come from you. It came to you. It pulled. The whole quay leaned toward you, and my men felt it and thought the dead were walking, and I didn't think that, because I was watching your face."

She looked at him directly.

"If a Circle mage ever feels that," said Rowena, "you're finished. Not in trouble. Finished. There's no court in Randillia that will hear what it is before it decides what to call it, and whatever they call it, they'll call it with a torch in their hand." She said it without any heat at all. "And I won't be able to help you. I'm your father's wife and I'm the duchess of a coast he doesn't rule, and neither of those is worth anything to a man the Church has named. I want you to know that now. Not after."

Nobody at the table spoke. Ward, beside Emeric, did not move, but Emeric felt him go very still, the stillness of a man on a wall who has seen something on the road.

"I know," said Emeric.

"You didn't," said Rowena. "You do now." She picked up her glass. "Ships."

She turned to her knight-captain.

"Two of the grey sloops," she said. "Tomorrow. They take the Emberfell down the inlet and along the coast to the Kaldo marsh, and they stay a day, and they come home. After that, every sulphur boat that leaves the works quay for Kaldo goes with one of them as far as the marsh mouth, and waits for the next one coming back." She looked at her Treasurer. "Kaldo's sulphur goes before anybody else's at the works quay. First loaded, first away, every month. If there isn't enough for everyone, it's the others who wait."

The Treasurer opened his mouth, looked at her face, and wrote it down.

"And the cost of the sloops," said Rowena, "is paid in your iron. Plate. For my boats. When you have it to spare." She looked back at Emeric. "I'll not have it paid in Rands. I've seen what you can make. I'd rather have what you make."

"Done," said Emeric.

"Done," said Rowena.

Saban, at the end of the table, had his hand inside his coat, where the purse was. He had kept it there most of the evening. He did not take it out. Rowena's Treasurer asked him across the table whether there was anything from the boat that ought to be shown to the harbour-mistress, for her records, and Saban said pleasantly that there was not, and asked the Treasurer whether he had had his two figures back from Kaldo's Chancellor yet, and the Treasurer went red and the conversation went elsewhere.

The Emberfell went down the inlet on the morning ebb with two grey sloops of Rowena's ahead of her and one astern, and the lime in her hold, and her master at the wheel.

Nevin stayed. He stood on the quay with Tavy, both of them with ink on their hands, and did not wave. Rowena did not come down. She had said her goodbyes at the table the night before, with Saban's figures and the harbour-mistress's and a sealed copy of her law for Kaldo in a wooden case, and she had not said Emmy this time, and he had not expected her to.

He stood at the rail as the harbour went away behind them.

He stood there as they went down the inlet between the black hills, and past the cove where they had anchored on the way up, and past the fishing villages, and the men in their skiffs who stood up to watch the black boat go by with Emberfell's grey sloops round her. He stood there all afternoon.

He had his hands on the rail. He kept them there. He kept them flat on the rail, on the painted iron, where he could see them and feel them, and every so often he looked down at them to see that they were only hands, and that the water alongside was only water, running past under the bow.

Ward stood a little behind him and to his left, where he always stood, and did not say anything the whole afternoon. Once, toward evening, he came up to the rail beside him and stood there too, with his own hands on it, a foot from Emeric's. He did not look at Emeric. He looked at the sea.

They stood there like that until the sun went down.
