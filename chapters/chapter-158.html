Chapter 158 - The Milford Line

Paskin opened the first cutting on the Milford line on the twenty-seventh of May, in a morning, with three of Armand's students and a crowd of two hundred people watching from the bank, and afterward he sat down on a sleeper and would not speak to anybody until noon.

The line went west. Out of the yard below the works where the Grayrock line came in, across the river on the old stone bridge, which Bram had looked at for a week and pronounced good for anything short of the end of the world, and then west, thirty miles, along the cart road to Milford's gate, more or less. More or less was the trouble. A cart road goes round a hill. A rail goes through it, or over it on a bank, because a train cannot climb what a cart can and cannot turn as tight; and between Kaldo's bridge and Milford's gate Linus's survey had found four hills that the cart road went round and the rails could not, and nine low places it went down into and up out of that the rails would have to cross on banks, and one long ridge of grey rock above the river at the halfway mark, the bench, that the road climbed over on a shelf a cart's width wide and the rails would have to go through.

The first hill was two miles out. It was a low round hill of red clay with sheep on it, and the line had to go through its shoulder, a cutting a hundred yards long and as deep at its middle as a house is tall.

Paskin stood at one end of where the cutting would be, and the three students stood along its line, a hundred yards apart from each other and from him, at the other end and in between; they were all earth, two boys and a girl from the farms who had been reading the ground since they could walk. They put their hands down. Paskin had told them what to do the night before, in the long room, with a lump of clay on the table and his thumb: not dig, not lift, not push. Lean. Lean all four together, at the same time, on the same line, and the clay would go where it was leaned.

Four amber glyphs came up off four pairs of hands.

The hill opened.

It did not split. Nothing so sudden. The turf along the line of the cutting sagged, first, as if a long weight had been laid on it; then it went down, slowly, into a trench, and the trench went down, and its sides stood up steep and red and smooth on either hand as the clay between them moved, all of it, the whole long wedge of the hill's shoulder, flowing away from the line to either side like water parting round a boat's bow, and heaping itself up beyond the cutting's lips in two long mounds as neat as a mole's. It took the time it takes to eat a slow breakfast. The crowd on the bank did not make a sound for the whole of it. When it was done there was a cutting through the shoulder of the hill, a hundred yards long and as deep as a house, with its floor level from end to end within a hand, Declan said afterward, who had run his board along it, and its sides at the slope Paskin had said they would stand at, and two long red banks of clay on either side of it with surprised sheep on them.

The students sat down where they stood. The girl was crying, and laughing, and wiping her face with clay on her hands.

Paskin did not sit down for some while. He walked the length of the cutting, slowly, along its floor, looking at its walls and putting his hand on them here and there, the way Fletcher put his hand on a hull. Then he sat on a sleeper at the far end with his back to everybody and his pipe unlit in his hand.

Emeric went to sit by him at noon. Paskin let him.

"Nine days," said Paskin, without looking round. "That's what it'd have taken the levy. Picks and barrows. Nine days for that, if it didn't rain." He turned the pipe in his fingers. "We did it in an hour and a bit. Four of us." He was quiet. "I'll not lift a spade till Thursday, and the girl won't till Sunday. That's the price. And there's three more hills."

"Then the levy does the next one."

"The levy does the next one," Paskin agreed. "And I stand and watch them and don't touch it, and they find out what an hour of mine is worth in days of theirs. That's the rule." He put the pipe in his mouth. "It's a good rule. I'd not have stood for it in the tower."



The levy did the next one. Fennick's crews did the banks.

Fennick did not blast banks; there was nothing to blast. But he had the spoil wagons, because a powder crew's whole work is moving the rock it has broken, and he had the little rails Odo had laid him from the quarry to the tip, and the wagons that ran on them, and the men who could make a wagon run full and come back empty all day without a horse; and he put all of it on the Milford line. Every cutting the levy dug out by hand, the spoil went into Fennick's wagons on a light track laid along the cutting's floor, and down to the next low place, and was tipped there, and spread, and rammed, a layer at a time, and a bank grew across the low place out of the hill that had been in the way. A cut-and-fill, Emeric told the gangs, which was the name. The gangs called it shovelling, which was also the name.

It was slow. It was a month slower than the mages. Every man on the line knew it, because the first hill had been done in an hour and the second took nine days, and the nine days were raining days.

It was also the way that would still work when the mages were somewhere else. Emeric did not say so. He did not need to. On the morning of the ninth day, when the second cutting was through and the levy stood on its floor covered in red clay from boot to hat, Paskin walked the length of it with his hand on its walls, as he had walked his own, and at the far end he stopped and looked back up it.

"Good," said Paskin, to the levy, and went away.

The levy talked about that for a week.



The gauges were made on the third of June, at night, in the cellar, because of a wheel.

The wheel came down from Grayrock on the line on the first, in a wagon, with a letter from Bryda. Grayrock's wagon shop had made a new set of wheels for the ore wagons over the spring, twenty pairs, cast at the Grayrock foundry and turned on the Grayrock lathe, good wheels, Bryda wrote, the best that shop had turned out, and every one of them ran on Grayrock's own line like a clock. And the first wagon that went onto the Kaldo line on them had come off at the curve below the brickfield. And the second. Bryda would like to know why.

Bram had the wheel in the works yard on its axle, standing upright in a frame, and was walking round it, when the apprentice came out of the shop with the new gauge.

It was Kerran's lad. The one who had used the brine tub for the small work all winter because it was quicker, and quenched the slide-bar bracket that broke; he had been very quiet in the works since the bracket, and very careful, and Bram had put him in charge of the tally room's new gauges, which everybody understood was either a kindness or a punishment and nobody, including the lad, could tell which. The gauges were not the forge's yet. They were the shop's own, cut by Bram's men the week before from Odo's stick and a wheel off the Kaldo engine: a sheet of steel with a notch cut in its edge the exact shape a wheel's rim and flange should be, so that you could hold it up against a wheel and see daylight where the wheel was wrong.

The lad held it up against Grayrock's wheel.

He held it there a long time. He turned it, and held it at another place on the rim, and another, all the way round. Then he lowered it and looked at the wheel, and then at Bram, and his face had gone a dull red.

"It's perfect," he said.

Bram stopped walking.

"It's perfect," said Kerran's lad. "Every place I put it. The same all the way round, not a hair out. I've never seen a wheel turned that true." He held the gauge up again, so Bram could see. The daylight in the notch was not a hair. It was a finger's width, on the flange side. "It's just the wrong wheel."

Bram looked through the notch. Then he looked at the flange of Grayrock's wheel, and at its depth, and its angle, and went to the shop and came back with Odo's stick, the gauge stick from the winding house wall, which had come down to Kaldo for the gauges and was due back at Grayrock on Thursday, and laid it on the rail.

"Grayrock's drawing," he said. "Which drawing did they turn it to?"

Nobody knew. It took Bryda two days, by wire, to find out. Grayrock's wagon shop had a drawing of a wheel on its wall. It had had it on its wall for a year. It was the drawing Odo had made of his first wagon's wheel, the summer before last, before the wagon came off the curve twice and he moved the lip from the outside of the wheel to the inside; and when Odo had moved the lip, he had made a new drawing, and put it on the winding house wall beside his stick, and nobody had thought to take the old one off the wagon shop's.

So Grayrock's wagon shop had turned twenty pairs of wheels, as true as any wheels in the south, to a drawing that was a year out of date.

Bram stood in the works yard with the wheel and the stick and the gauge and the lad, and looked at them all for some while.

"The gauge," said Bram, "caught it." He took it out of the lad's hand and turned it over. "Not the drawing. Not the man. Not me. That," he held it up, "a sheet of steel with a notch in it, in the hand of a boy who'd been told to put it on everything." He handed it back. "Put it on everything."

The lad went red again, a different red.

That night the forge made the masters. A wheel profile gauge, true to Odo's stick and the Kaldo engine's wheels, that every other wheel gauge in four baronies would be cut from and tried against. A back-to-back gauge, a bar with two faces on it exactly as far apart as the backs of a pair of wheels should be on their axle, so that no pair could leave any shop set a hair too wide or too narrow and climb the rail on a curve. And axle-fit gauges, a ring and a plug, for the place where the axle went into the wheel's hub, so that a wheel made at Grayrock would go onto an axle made at Kaldo, and a wheel made at Milford, when there was anything at Milford to make one, would go onto either.

They went into the tally room under the glass with the thread masters. The shop gauges were cut from them that week, three sets, and one set went up the line to Grayrock with Odo's stick and the new drawing in a tube and a letter from Ferra that said only that the wagon shop's wall was to have one drawing of a wheel on it and not two. And one set went to the Milford line's railhead, in a box, and Kerran's lad went with it.



The railhead camp moved west with the line, a mile or two a week, and by the middle of June it was a long untidy village of tents and wagon sheets and cook fires strung out along the new bank, with a smithy on a flat wagon and a pay table on another and Maren's second cousin selling bread and onions off a third.

Emeric slept there two nights in seven. He had not been asked to, and Ferra had pointed out at the long table that the holder of four baronies sleeping in a tent on a railway bank was a thing the capital would hear about and not understand. He slept there anyway. He ate at the gangs' fires, which they did not like at first and then stopped noticing, and listened to them complain about the clay, and the rain, and the food, and Fennick, and each other, which was what gangs talked about on every bank that had ever been built, on any world.

The earth student found him at the fire on his second night out, the girl who had cried in the first cutting. She was seventeen, a farmer's daughter from the west side, with clay ground into the creases of her knuckles that would not come out, and she sat down across the fire from him and looked at him for some while before she said anything.

"Master Paskin says you asked him to teach you," she said. "Earth. On the Grayrock grade."

"I did."

"You're fire."

"Everybody keeps telling me."

She frowned at the fire. "It costs you three times."

"It does. More, when I do it badly. Which is most of the time." He picked up a stick and poked the fire with it. "Paskin says I lean on it like a man trying to open a door by falling on it."

The girl laughed, before she could stop herself, and then looked embarrassed. "He says that to me," she said. "He's said it to me every day for a year." She was quiet. Then, carefully, as though it were a thing she had been told not to ask and was going to ask anyway: "Why? If it costs you three times. You've got Master Paskin. You've got us. You could have us do anything you wanted with earth."

He thought about how to answer that honestly.

"Because I don't know what it can do," he said. "And I won't know unless I've done it with my own hands. Badly. I can't ask you for a thing if I don't know whether it's a thing earth does." He put the stick in the fire. "Bram taught me that. Not about earth. About iron."

She thought about it, with her chin on her clay-creased knuckles.

"Show me," she said.

So he showed her, there by the fire, on the trodden ground beside it, with half the gang watching sidelong over their bowls: his hand flat on the earth, the lean, the small amber glyph, wobbling. The ridge came up under his palm the length of his forearm, with its steep face toward him, faster than it had on the Grayrock grade and smaller than it had needed to be, the glyph no bigger than a ring. He had been doing it every night in the stable yard, on the one patch of beaten earth by the midden where the cobbles had never been laid.

The girl looked at it with her head on one side, the way Paskin looked at a cutting.

"That's not bad," she said. "For fire." She put her own hand down beside his, flat, and leaned, and a ridge came up beside his under her palm, twice as long and twice as high and with its face as straight as a brick, and no glyph that he could see at all until it had already closed. She took her hand away. "But you're still falling on the door."

Two nights later he went out alone along the line past the last of the camp's fires, to the first grey outcrop of the bench where it came down to the cart road, and put his hand on the rock, and leaned.

Nothing happened. Nothing at all. It was like leaning on the side of the keep. The glyph came up off his hand and wobbled and closed and the cost of it went into him behind the eyes, and the rock under his palm did not move by so much as a grain, and he stood there in the dark with his hand on it until his head hurt, to be sure, and then took his hand off. Paskin had said so. He had wanted to know it with his own hand.

He walked back to the camp with the ache closing on the back of his skull and thought, the whole way, about Fennick.



The bench was at the halfway mark, and it was rock, and the mages could not do it.

That had been known from the survey. Linus had walked it, and Paskin had walked it after him with his hand on the ground, and come back and said so plainly at the long table: earth was earth, and clay and soil and gravel went where they were leaned, but the bench was a ridge of grey stone a mile long running down to the river, hard and old and in layers like the leaves of a book, and stone like that did not lean. You could shift it. A tower mage could shift it, a little, slowly, at a cost that would put him in his bed for a week. Four of them from Armand's room could not shift it at all.

So the bench was Fennick's.

He was there in the second week of June with his crews and his powder wagon and Ward's rules, and Ward, because Ward did not let Fennick near a powder wagon without being near it himself. He was laughing, which he always was. He had walked the bench three times, the way Linus walked a line, and sat on it for the whole of an afternoon with his back to the river looking at the face of the rock, and come back into camp with his eyes shining and his singed hair standing up and two fingers less than a man ought to have on his left hand, and said that it was the most beautiful piece of rock he had ever seen and he would have it open in three weeks.

But not the way the drawing said.

The drawing said what every drawing of a rock cutting said, and what Paskin's cuttings in the clay had been: two walls, steep and smooth, standing up on either side of the line. Fennick looked at it and laughed so long that the clerk who had brought it out to him took it back.

"Look at it," he said to Emeric, the next morning, at the face. He put his three-fingered hand flat on the grey stone. "Look how it lies. In leaves. Like a book on its side. All tipping down that way, toward the river." He traced a line in the rock with a finger, a thin dark seam running along the face at a slant. "Every one of those is a crack waiting to happen. You blast a smooth wall through that, straight up, the way the drawing has it, and you've cut every leaf in the book through at its foot, and there's nothing holding them up but habit. And the first wet winter the habit goes, and the leaves come down, all of them, one after another, onto your nice new rails." He took his hand off the rock. "Slab. Every winter. For ever."

"So what do you do?"

"Steps." Fennick grinned. "I blast it along the leaves, not across them. Small. Lots. A shot here and a shot there, timed, so each one breaks its own leaf and no more. And I leave the wall in steps, a ledge every man's height, back and back and back, so that every leaf has a ledge under it to sit on, and the ones that want to come down come down onto the ledge and not the line. Ugly." He was delighted. "It'll look like a staircase for a giant. And it'll stand till your grandchildren are old."

Emeric looked at the face. He had read about it. Benching, on rock faces, in a slim grey book about quarries he had read once in the other life on a train and never thought of since. He had not thought of it here. He had looked at the drawing of the cutting, smooth-walled, and approved it, and gone on to the next thing.

"Do it your way," he said.

"I was going to," said Fennick, and went away laughing to fetch his fuses.

It took him three weeks to the day, as he had said. He worked it the way he had worked the gravity drive at Grayrock the spring before, many small charges in long patterns with their fuses cut to different lengths, so that they went off one after another down a row like a man drumming his fingers on a table; except that at the bench he drilled every hole along a leaf, not across, and every shot lifted its leaf off the one under it and dropped it, broken small, onto the ledge below, for the wagons. The face went back in steps. Twelve of them. It looked, the gangs said, like a staircase for a giant, and they began to call it that, and the name stuck, and was on Linus's maps afterward in his small neat hand.

A mile upstream on the same ridge, where the cart road climbed over the bench on its shelf, there was a place where the road had been widened, years ago, before the plague, when Milford still had money to spend. The barony had paid a tower mage to do it, a single tower mage in a single day, and he had shaved the face of the rock smooth and straight up beside the road as neat as a wall in a church.

It rained on the twenty-sixth of June. On the twenty-seventh, a carter on the road found the shelf blocked by a slab of grey stone as long as his cart, lying across it in pieces, fallen in the night off the smooth face the tower mage had shaved.

Fennick went up to look at it. He came back very quiet, for him, and stood at the foot of his giant's staircase for some while looking up at the twelve ledges in the rain with water running off every one of them.

"Book," he said. "Leaves." He sounded, for once, not delighted at all, only certain. "I told you."
