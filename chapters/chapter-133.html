Chapter 133 - Floor Crust

The river was high with the thaw, brown and fast and full of the winter's rubbish, and the steamboat took the whole of a day to push up it to Grayrock against the current, with her boiler working and a man in the bow with a pole for the branches.

Emeric spent most of the day at the rail. Ward spent it beside him. Once, at the long bend below the ford, where the rail's embankment came down to the river and ran beside it for a mile on its raw new cutting, with the first lengths already spiked down and shining and the rest of it bare ground marked with stakes, Ward said that it looked further along than it had in the autumn, and Emeric said it was, by about a mile, and Ward said nothing else until they tied up.

Bryda was on the landing.

She was always on the landing. He had never once come up to Grayrock without finding her standing at the foot of the landing stage in her dark widow's coat, with her arms folded and the tally clerk behind her with his book, as though she had known the hour, and possibly she had, because the relay post above the tally house could see the river for three miles.

"You're a week early," she said.

"The river's high."

"The river's always high in the spring. You're a week early." She looked at Ward, and nodded to him, and looked back. "You'll want the ore first."

He did want the ore first. It was what he had come up for, as far as anybody at the table knew, and he did it properly, the whole of the first afternoon, in the tally house with her books and Hask's. The shallow veins were failing, as he had known since the survey that they would; the deep ground under the crosscut was giving good iron now, better than the shallow had ever given, and the carts were coming up out of the Low Drift loaded to the rails. The coal was the new thing. The paper works wanted it, and the converter wanted it, and the coke oven by the mill yard wall burned it faster than anybody had supposed a small oven could burn anything, and Bryda had put two more men on the coal seam on the strength of one letter from Ferra, without being asked twice.

"Ferra says you'll want three times this by the autumn," Bryda said, with her finger on the column.

"Ferra's right."

"Ferra's always right. That's why I put the men on." She closed the book. "What else?"

He had been going to work round to it. He found, with her looking at him, that there was no point.

"Dirt," he said.

She waited.

"The old stables. The pony stalls up by the first adit, that Hask showed me, that were bricked up with the floors in them. I want the floors."

"The floors," said Bryda.

"The top of them. A hand deep. The crust. As much of it as your men can get into casks without taking the walls down, and on the steamboat down to Kaldo."

Bryda looked at him for a moment with no expression at all. She had buried a husband who had kept a box of secrets under a floor in his own house, and she had found the box, and read it, and brought it to Emeric in her own hands, and she had been learning ever since, one quarter at a time, what it was to deal with a man who did not tell you why.

"How many casks?" she said.

"As many as there are."

She turned to the tally clerk. "Get Hask. Tell him four men and a pick and the empty flour casks from the store." She turned back. "They'll break the brick in the morning. The air's good. Hask says it's the best air on the hill."

"I know."

"I didn't ask how you knew," said Bryda, and went to find her coat.

They broke the first wall at first light.

Emeric went up with them, because he wanted to see it and because he did not want anybody to break a wall on his say-so and then go in without him. It was dry inside. Hask had been right about that, and about the air: the sense went ahead of Emeric into the dark, down the short gallery, and found nothing wrong in it, only still old air that had not moved for a long time and a faint sharp smell that caught at the back of the throat. The lamp burned tall and steady. The stalls were cut into the rock on both sides, low, with the iron rings still in the walls where the halters had been made fast, and a manger of split stone in each.

The floor was a hand deep in something that was no longer dung. It had been dung once, forty years of it, trodden down and left. It was dirt now, grey-brown and fine and packed hard, and in the lamplight along the foot of the walls and in the corners of the stalls there was a crust on it, pale and faintly glittering, like frost that had not melted.

He knelt and put his fingers to it. It came away in flakes, and he touched one flake to his tongue, which made Hask's youngest man look away, and it was cold and bitter and salt, and not quite like salt.

"That," he said. "The top of it. Where it's white."

Hask's men filled casks all day. They filled them from the three stalls and from the gallery floor between, scraping the crust off with the flat of a shovel and the loose dirt under it after, a hand deep, and knocking the lids on, and rolling them down the adit to the tally house yard. Bryda came up at midday to count them, and counted eleven, and at the end of the day there were twenty-six, and she counted those too.

She did not ask what they were for. She stood in the yard by the stacked casks in the evening with the tally clerk and his book, and watched Hask's men chalk a number on each lid, and looked at Emeric once, over the casks, and did not ask.

He found the other thing in the old spoil, while he was waiting for the last of the casks.

The spoil by the first adit was the oldest on the hill, a long grassed-over bank of broken rock thrown out of the workings when Grayrock was still new, and nobody had looked at it in two generations because there was nothing in it that was iron. He walked along the foot of it in the evening with his hands in his coat, not looking for anything.

There was a stone at his feet that was the wrong weight.

He knew it before he picked it up. It was a lump of grey stuff the size of an egg, dull on the outside where the weather had been at it, and when he picked it up it pulled his hand down, much heavier than any stone that size ought to be. He turned it over. One corner of it had been broken off at some point, and the broken face was bright, a hard bluish-silver shine, and it had broken square. Not in a curve, the way rock breaks. In steps. Little cubes, stacked, every face flat and every corner true, as though somebody had cut it.

There was more of it along the bank, when he looked. Not much. A lump here and there among the ordinary broken rock, thrown out with it forty years ago because it was not iron and nobody had a use for anything that was not.

And among it, in the same spoil, in streaks and crusts on the broken rock, there was a black grit, sooty, that came off on his fingers. It was the same grit Colm had shown him the pane with. It came down to Kaldo on the carts with the coal spoil, and Colm had a sack of it for nothing off the steamboat.

He weighed the grey lump in his palm for some time. Then he put it in his coat pocket, where it pulled the coat down on that side, and went back down to the tally house yard.

"The spoil up by the first adit," he said to Bryda. "There's a heavy grey stone in it. Breaks square. If your men turn up any more of it, anywhere, don't throw it away. Keep it for me."

"How much?"

"All of it."

"It's not iron," said Hask, who had been listening.

"No," said Emeric. "It isn't."

Bryda wrote it down.

He did not say why. Not to Bryda, not to Hask, and not on the steamboat going home, with the twenty-six casks roped down on her deck under a sail and Ward sitting on the nearest of them.

He thought it, though, the whole way down the river.

He thought of the first winter, and the doctrine he had written cold in the notebook the week he understood it: that anything in Kaldo which depended on the medium could be switched off by any man with one crystal and a grudge, standing in the right yard, burning it. He had built every good thing in Kaldo on the other side of that rule since, with the mundane underneath and the stones on top, so that when the stones went out the town went on. He had kept to it. He had made his armour of steel first and crystal second, and the pump would work by hand, and the wall stood without the board.

And he had made three dead zones himself, the winter before the war, at the lane. Three stones, built to be drawn to nothing on purpose, and inside each of them, for as long as it lasted, nothing worked: no enhancement, no working, no charge held in any crystal, his own or anybody else's, because a dead zone did not ask whose stone it was.

Everything that made Kaldo more than a town now ran on stones. The ship. The suits. The printer. The board. The relay. The jars in the forge. Every one of them had a backup, and every backup was slower and weaker, because that was what a backup was.

The first enemy who thought to do what he had done at the lane, on Kaldo's own ground, would switch off everything Kaldo had that ran on stone, and he would do it with one crystal and the nerve to overload it. And powder did not care. Powder did not know what the medium was doing. Powder was sulphur and charcoal and the salt that grew on the floors of old stables, and a spark, and it would go off in the heart of a dead zone as readily as anywhere else in the world.

He did not like where that went. He followed it to the end anyway, because not following it would not stop anybody else from following it, and then he put it away.

Ward, on the cask, was watching the bank go by.

Most of the work was off the page of anybody's book but his.

He did it himself, in the foundry yard, in the evenings, with one of Bram's men who did not ask questions and a row of tubs. The crust went into water, and the water was run through wood ash, and boiled down, and cooled, and what grew in the bottom of the pan was skimmed off and washed and done again, and again, until what came out was clean. He did not write the steps into anything but the notebook. He did not say them aloud. He did it slowly, over nine evenings, and it went wrong twice, once badly enough that the whole of one tub's work went on the midden.

What came out of twenty-six casks of stable floor was a few pounds of white crystals, long and thin, like needles, in a stone jar with a lid.

A few pounds. Twenty-six casks.

On the tenth evening he took a pinch of the white out of the jar, and a pinch of sulphur off the heap, and a pinch of charcoal off the pile by the furnace, and ground them together very lightly in a dish with the back of a spoon, and set the dish on the flags in the middle of the yard, well away from everything, and touched a long taper to it.

It went.

Not a bang. There was not enough of it for a bang. It went with a flat hard sound like a hand slapping a table, and a flash, and a gout of thick white smoke that rolled up out of the dish and hung in the lamplight over the yard and stank of struck matches and something worse.

Ward flinched.

Emeric had never seen him do it. He had seen Ward stand still under a charge, and on the deck of a ship in the band with nothing below it but the sea, and never once seen the man's body do anything that Ward had not told it to. It did now. It was very small. A shift of the weight, back, and the hand going to where the sword was.

Then it was over, and Ward stood in the yard with the smoke thinning round him, and looked at the black scorch on the dish, and then at Emeric, for a long time.

Emeric looked back.

"That's years from being anything," he said.

Ward did not say anything. He went on looking at him until the smoke had gone, and then he looked at the dish again, and then he went and opened the yard gate to let the rest of the smell out, and stood by it.

He had not forgotten the band.

He had stood on her deck on the night they took her over Kaldo, and watched Ward at the levers with his lips gone pale and his breath coming short, as though he had run the keep stair, and he had felt the same thing in himself, the ache behind the eyes and the weight in the arms, and written it in the notebook that night under a line he had done nothing about since. Every flight went up into the band first to fill her cells, because he had made that the rule, and every flight paid for it in the same coin. It was not enough to kill anybody at the height she went. It was enough to make a man slow at a lever, and wrong about a sum, and sure of himself while he was wrong. He had watched it in all of them. He had felt it in himself, which was worse, because the first thing it took was the part of a man that noticed.

The suits were worse again. A man in the black plate carried a small fire stone at the small of his back to keep the cold out of him, and it failed off, never on, because Emeric had built it to. In a dead zone it would fail off with everything else, and the man would be high, and cold, and short of breath, all at once, at the moment he most needed his head.

The stones would not help. He had said as much at Grayrock, over the dead gallery, with two men already carried out of it: a stone could push air and pull it and it could not make it any better than it was. Up in the band there was nothing wrong with the air except that there was less of it. He could seal a room one day and push air into it until it was thick enough to live in, and one day he would. A suit could not be sealed like a room, and a man could not breathe against a push.

The breath had to come from something that did not care what the medium was doing. It was the same sentence he had thought on the river about the powder, turned round the other way, and he noticed that, and did not much like what it said about which half of it he had got to first.

In the world he had come from they made it out of a salt. They pressed the salt into a stub with iron dust and sealed it in a tin, and hung the tin over a man's seat against the day the air failed, and when it was lit it gave off breath the way a coal gives off heat, steadily, until it was spent. He had sat under one a hundred times and never once thought about what was in it.

Nobody here made the salt. To make it he needed three things, and when he counted them over he found, somewhat to his surprise, that Kaldo had all three. Common salt, which there was no end of. The black grit, which Colm had a sack of for nothing and which came down on every coal cart. And the acid.

He had written the acid off a fortnight before, standing over the iron trays on the spoil, because a chamber for it wanted lead and Kaldo had none. But a chamber was for barrels. A jar's worth could be driven out of the green crystals themselves by fire, in a closed vessel, and caught. It was slow and wasteful, and it was the way it had been done for centuries before anybody thought to line a room with lead, and all the vessel had to be was something the acid would not eat.

Colm blew him a flask with a round belly and a long neck bent down, and a second to catch what came over, and asked what they were for.

"Boiling something that eats pots," Emeric said.

Colm went away to decide whether that was a compliment to his glass.

The first flask cracked in the fire on the second evening, and the charge went on the midden with it. The second he bedded in sand and brought up slowly, and at the end of the night there was a finger's depth of something in the catching flask that was heavy and clear and oily, and smoked very faintly on the air, and turned a scrap of rag black where he touched it.

The next part he did on a still evening in the far corner of the yard, with what wind there was at his back and a wet cloth tied over his mouth. He sent Bram's man home first. He sent Ward to the gate, well upwind, and Ward went, because he had been told to in the voice that meant it.

Even so a thread of it got round the cloth. It came off the flask the colour of a bruise going yellow, low and heavy, and crept along the flags instead of rising, and when it reached him it was like breathing a bleached sheet with a knife folded in it. He coughed for an hour. Ward did not come over. He had been told not to, and he stood by the gate with his jaw set and did not come over, and Emeric could see from across the yard what that cost him.

He knew what else men had done with that air, in the world he had come from, in a war that had a name. He did not follow that one to the end. He had followed enough things to the end for one spring.

What came out of the soap house lye when the air had gone through it, and it had cooled, was a second salt, flat and pearly, that did not look like much. He washed it and grew it again the way he had grown the needles out of the stable crust, and again, and at the end of the month there was a stone jar of it about the size of the other one.

He did not put it in the dry store. He carried it out of the foundry yard altogether, to the old smoke house under the lake wall that nobody had used since the fish went to the new racks, and set it on a shelf there by itself, and put a lock on the door. Ward walked across with him and watched him do it.

"This one," Emeric said, "and that one." He nodded back toward the dry store, where the sulphur and the jar of needles were. "Never in a room together. Not a pinch of either. Not on the same cart or the same bench or in the same man's pocket. Put them together and they'll go off if you look at them hard."

Ward looked at the lock. "Then why make it?"

"Because on its own it doesn't."

He pressed the candles in Nevin's shop, because Nevin cut anything small that had to be exactly one shape and would not ask a question he could not measure. The salt went in with iron filings off Jorrin's floor and a little of Colm's glass ground to flour to bind it, rammed into a tin the length of his finger, with a striker in the cap. He made nineteen. It was all the jar would give.

He lit the first on the flags of the yard, at night, with Ward and one lamp.

It did not flame. It hissed, faintly, and the tin went hot, hotter than a hand could stay on, and out of the vent in the cap came a thin clear breath that looked like nothing at all. He blew out a taper so that it only glowed, and held the glowing end in the stream, and it burst into flame in his hand, tall and white, as though it had been waiting its whole life to be asked.

Ward looked at the taper, and then at the tin, and then at Emeric, the way he had looked at him over the scorched dish, and it was not the same look.

"What is it?"

"Breath."

Ward was quiet for a moment. "For up there."

"Up there. And anywhere the stones go out."

"How long?"

"A quarter glass for a man sitting still. Less for a man working." Emeric watched the taper burn down. "Eighteen left."

"That's not many."

"It's three a suit. It's enough to get six men down."

He drew the fitting that night, at the bench, with the tin cooling beside him. A rack of three on the hip belt, clear of the heart at the chest and the pack at the back. A mask of oiled leather over the mouth and nose, with a flap to let the spent breath out, which Nevin would cut. And the hose from the rack to the mask did not run straight: it went first through the warm channels in the padding, one turn round the small of the back, so that the breath gave its heat to the man on the way to his mouth and reached it cool enough to take. A man who struck one in a dead zone, with his fire stone gone cold behind him, would get the breath and the warmth both, from one stroke. It was the neatest thing he had drawn all spring, and it was a thing that would only matter once everything else had gone wrong.

He thought, too, of Grayrock, and the gallery behind its canvas wall, and of what a man with one of these could do in that air. He did not write that down. A candle in a miner's hand was a reason to walk into bad air, and a man who walked in because he had a candle would one day walk in with a spent one. The canvas stayed.

He put the eighteen in the smoke house, on the shelf beside the jar, and locked the door, and gave Ward the second key without being asked for it.

Back at Kaldo there was no stable floor forty years deep, and there never would be. So he began one.

He had them built in the lee of the old barn behind the foundry, where nobody would have to walk past them and the prevailing wind would take the smell away over the lake. Long low heaps, each the length of a cart and as high as a man's waist, of dung and earth and old straw, with wood ash and rubble mixed in, laid on a floor of beaten clay so nothing soaked away, under thatched roofs on posts to keep the rain off. They were to be turned every week and kept damp and not wet. And then they were to be left.

When the heaps were laid, and before the first turning, he went down the row with a shovel and threw a shovelful of the Grayrock crust into each heap, the loose dirt from the bottom of the last three casks that he had kept back from the refining, and turned it in.

The man with the shovel beside him looked at it and did not ask.

The dung for them had to come from somewhere, and there was not enough in the foundry's own stable. So the handcart came back.

It was the same handcart. It had gone round the island two summers before, with a boy pushing it and a scale on the front, buying rags by the pound for the trial. It went round again now, with a different boy and the same scale, buying the sweepings of every stable and byre and pig run on the island by the pound, and the sweepings of the privies behind the inn, and paying for them in Copper, a Copper for a full basket.

The town noticed. The town could hardly fail to. The rag cart had smelled of rags. This one did not, and it came round every week, and there was a queue of children behind it every time with baskets, and a smell behind the children. People asked what it was for. The boy did not know. The man at the foundry did not know. Ferra's clerks entered the Copper against a line that said beds, behind the barn and nothing else, and when anybody asked Ferra she said it was for the beds, which was true.

Nobody would say what the beds were for. By the third week nobody asked.

Bram came out to look at them once, the day they were finished. He stood at the end of the row with his arms folded and looked down the length of the thatched roofs, and at the heaps under them, and sniffed, and said that the charcoal was the furnace's.

"It is," said Emeric.

"You'll not take it off the furnace. There's never enough for the furnace. There's been never enough since the first winter."

"I won't take it off the furnace."

Bram nodded, and went back to the mill.

Emeric took a sack of it off the furnace pile that evening anyway, a small one, and carried it down to the foundry yard himself, and put it in the dry store beside the stone jar with the lid.

It rained the night the beds were finished.

It was the first real rain of the spring, soft and steady and warm, and it came down on the thatch of the long roofs behind the barn and ran off the eaves in lines and soaked into the clay outside the drip, and did not touch the heaps. He went down in the dark to see that it did not. It did not. The heaps lay in their rows under the thatch in the dry, dark and quiet, smelling of the byre and the midden and of nothing else yet, and the rain came down all round them.

In the keep, at her table, under the lamp, Ferra had the column open. It had the beds in it, and the thatch, and the posts, and the carting, and the boy, and a Copper a basket.

She had asked him, that afternoon, when the beds would give.

"Three years," he had said. "If they give at all."

She had not asked what they would give. She wrote it in at the foot of the column, in the same hand as the rest, against the line that said beds, behind the barn, in the space where a date went.

Three years.
