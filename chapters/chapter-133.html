Chapter 133 - Scar Tissue

The sheet had started in the third summer as a column.

Imani's column. She had kept it in her ward's book that summer, in the margin beside the still crew's names, because she had noticed that the men who handled the recharged fire stones at the still cut themselves as often as anybody else and healed faster than anybody else, and she would not name a cause until she could prove one. She had refused to name it at the table, in front of everybody, with the column open in front of her. Then she had given the column to Armand, because it was about stones, and Armand had given it to his students, because he was old and they were not, and they had begun, very quietly, to read people.

Not with a stone. With a hand. The way Armand had read every child in Sela's room for the faculty, in the spring: two fingers on the inside of the wrist and a dull lamp stone in the other fist, and the reader's sense let out thin into the person, just far enough to feel how much was there. Capacity. How much mana a body carried and could carry. The tower had read capacity that way for four hundred years, once, at birth or in childhood, by eye and by feel, and written it down, and never read it again, because capacity was set at birth, and everybody knew it.

Linnet and Paskin and Dorren had read every stone handler in Kaldo by the second winter. The still crew. The foundry's stock men, who carried the charged stones from the charging room to the furnace. The board's men, who handled the wall's stones when they came out for cutting. The ship crew, who had been running the black hull at night with her air stones under their hands since April. And for comparison, the same number of people who had never touched a charged stone in their lives: Maren's kitchen girls, the mill hands, the farm crews.

They had read them all three times, on three days, and written it down. And then they had read them again, a year on, the same way, the same people. Paskin had ruled it into a sheet, a great sheet the size of a tabletop, every name down the side and every reading across, because Paskin had been a quarry man for twenty years and liked to see a thing all at once.

Armand brought it up to Emeric's room on the sixteenth of December.

He did not carry it. He was not carrying anything yet; he was walking with Linnet's arm and his stick, a week out of his bed after the gravity stone, grey still and short of breath on the stairs. Paskin carried it, rolled, and spread it on the table under the window, and weighted the corners with Emeric's books, and stood back. Linnet put Armand in the chair by the fire, and stood beside it with her arms folded, daring anyone to say he should not have come.

"Read the line," said Armand. "The one Paskin's put his thumb on."

Paskin had put his thumb on a name. Mott.

Emeric knew him. A big slow quiet man of thirty from the dock quarter, who had come to the still in the second summer when the still was new and had worked it ever since, at the fire end, where the recharged fire stones went into their iron basket under the copper every morning and came out every evening, by hand, because the tongs were clumsy and Mott was not. Bare-handed. For two years.

The first reading, a year ago. A figure. A little above the kitchen girls, a little below the board's men, where a strong healthy commoner ought to be.

The second reading, a year on. A figure.

Emeric read it twice.

"That's inside knight range," he said.

"It's inside knight range," said Armand, from his chair. "Low in it. A green knight, a year after the rite. But in it." He was quiet. "Paskin read him four times to be sure. Linnet read him. I read him, yesterday, from this chair, with Linnet holding his wrist out to me like a dog's paw, because I couldn't believe Paskin." He looked at the fire. "It's in it."

"Has he got the lines?"

"No," said Linnet. "We looked. I made him take his shirt off in the cold for it. Arms, chest, neck, back." She shook her head. "Clean. Not a mark. Skin like a baby's."

Emeric stood at the table and looked at the name and the two figures.

A man with a knight's capacity and no knight's lines. A man who had never been anywhere near a stone room in the capital, or four mages, or a floor, and had gone, in a year, from where a strong commoner ought to be to where a knight was after the rite. By handling charged stones. Bare-handed. Every day. For two years.

"Tell me the rite," he said. "All of it. The way the tower teaches it."



Armand told him.

He told it from the chair by the fire, with his hands folded on his stick, in the lecture-hall voice, the one that made a room go quiet because it expected to be listened to. He told it the way he had told it, Emeric understood, to rooms of young mages in the tower for forty years, every word in its place, worn smooth.

The rite was the tower's oldest working. Eight mages, in two shifts of four, because no four could hold it alone. A stone room under the Circle with a floor. Nineteen young men on the floor, or twelve, or twenty, however many the army sent, lying still. And the mages forcing mana into them. Not pouring. Forcing. Pure, uninstructed, from outside, through the skin, into the body, far more of it and far faster than any body was made to take. Nine hours. Some nights fourteen.

"Every body has its pathways," said Armand. "The ways the mana goes through it. Fine. Narrow. Set at birth, the tower says, the way the width of a man's veins is set at birth. A mage is born with wide ones. A commoner with narrow." He did not look at Emeric. "The rite drives mana through narrow pathways until they widen. Or tear. That's the whole of it. If they widen, the man gets up off the floor with a knight's capacity, and the silver lines on his skin where the pathways have come up to the surface, ring over ring. If they tear,"

He stopped.

"Nobody counts the dead," said Armand. "That's the other thing the tower teaches. You don't count. You read the ones who get up and you write them down and you send them to the army. The others are carried out at dawn by the bishop's men, and you don't ask where." He looked at the fire. "I didn't ask. In my rotation. I didn't count." He paused. "I've counted since."

Emeric stood at the table.

He had stood against the wall of that room in the winter after his first summons, with a ladle in his hand, and watched it. He had seen the men get up off the floor on their hands, the way a man gets up out of a river. He had seen the lines on their arms, raised and silver, ring over ring, as if someone had drawn them on with a pen dipped in metal.

He had seen it before, on his other world. Not lines. But the same thing. The way skin heals over a wound that has been opened too fast and too wide to close clean. The way it heals raised, and shining, and harder than the skin round it, and never quite the same.

"It's scar tissue," he said.

The room was quiet.

"The lines," Emeric said. "The tracery. It isn't the mark of the capacity. It's the mark of the damage. The pathways are forced wider than they can stretch in a night, so they tear and heal and tear and heal, nine hours of it, and they come up to the skin the way a scar comes up, ring over ring, and the man who survives it has the right total and a body that's been torn to get it." He put his finger on the sheet, on Mott's name. "And this is what it looks like when the same thing happens slowly. The same total. A year. Two. At a rate the body can take. No tearing. No scar." He looked up. "No lines. And nobody on the floor."

Nobody said anything. Linnet, by the chair, had unfolded her arms.

Armand did not move.

He sat in his chair by the fire with his hands on his stick and his face perfectly still, and Emeric watched him, and saw what it cost him. Saw him take it in: the floor, the rotation, the young man of nineteen whose name he had never been told and whose face he had carried for twenty years. And the others. All the others. Every rite. Four hundred years of floors. Nine in ten, carried out at dawn.

And then saw him put it down.

"No," said Armand.

He said it quietly. He said it in the voice the tower used, the one that had answered every question for four hundred years the same way.

"Capacity is set at birth," said Armand. "Read once, by eye and by feel. It does not change. Every reader in the tower knows it. I have read ten thousand people in my life and none of them ever changed." He looked at the sheet on the table. "Mott is one man. One. A big healthy man who was read once a year ago by a quarry mage on a cold morning, and read low, and read again on a warm one, and read right. That is a reading. It is not a result."

"You read him yourself. Yesterday."

"I read him once." Armand's hands had tightened on the stick. "One man, once, is a story. It is not a result. If I stood up in the tower and said that the rite was unnecessary, on one man from a still in a southern barony, they would put me in a room with a window and send me broth." He looked at Emeric. "And they would be right to."

Emeric looked at him.

He understood. He had understood the moment Armand said no. If Armand accepted it, if Armand looked at Mott's name on that sheet and said yes, then every man on every floor in Armand's rotation had died for nothing. Had died because the tower had never thought to read a man twice. And Armand had stood in that room and watched them die, and taken the tutoring post afterward because it was a warm room and a quiet one, and counted it ever since with the things he had been afraid of and called obedience.

Armand could not say yes. Not on one man. Not yet. Perhaps not ever.

"Then prove me wrong," said Emeric.

Armand looked up.

"Read them all," said Emeric. "Not the stone handlers. Everybody. Every person in the four baronies who'll let you. Three readings on three days, so no cold morning makes the difference. And again next year. And the year after. Every year. Against the same number of people who never touch a stone, matched, man for man, age for age." He put his hand flat on Paskin's sheet. "You hold the sheets. Not me. You decide what they say. You can refuse to show me any of it, ever, and I'll put that in writing, in Ferra's book, at the table." He held Armand's eyes. "And if Mott is one man, the sheets will say so. And you can stand up at the table and tell me I was wrong, and I'll write it down."

Armand looked at him across the room for a long while.

"Five years," he said at last. "To prove anything. Seven, to prove it so the tower couldn't argue." His voice was quite flat. "I'm eighty."

"Then you'd better start tomorrow."

Linnet made a sound that she turned into a cough.

Armand looked at Emeric a little longer. Then something moved at the corner of his mouth, very slightly, and did not get any further.

"You were always like this," he said. "At seven. You'd answer the question I asked and then hand me a harder one." He put both hands on his stick and got up, slowly, with Linnet's hand under his elbow. "I'll start tomorrow. I'll prove you wrong." He looked at the sheet on the table. "And Paskin will rule it, because his hand's steadier than mine." He turned to the door. Then he stopped, and turned back, and looked not at Emeric's face but at something a little to one side of it, the way he had looked by the fire in July, listening for a draught in a house.

"Everybody," said Armand. "Every person in the four baronies who'll let me." He was quiet. "Will you?"

Emeric did not answer at once.

"Not yet," he said.

Armand looked at him a moment longer.

"No," he agreed. "Not yet." And he went out on Linnet's arm, slowly, with his stick, and Paskin rolled up the sheet and followed him.



The first sheet of the study came back on the twenty-first, five days later, because Armand had started the next morning and Paskin and Linnet and Dorren had read a hundred and forty people in four days, in the ward and the school and the exchange and the kitchen, and in the market, at a table, which the market had found extremely entertaining.

Paskin brought it up himself, without Armand. He spread it on the table and put his thumb on two columns: the upper town and the lower.

The upper town read higher.

Not much. A shade. Not knight range, not anything like it. A hair above the lower town, man for man, woman for woman, child for child, all the way down the sheet. As if every person who lived above the inner ward's arch had gained the same small thing in the same small time.

"We don't know why," said Paskin. "Master Armand says it's the cold morning in the lower ward and the warm one in the upper, and we'll know when we read them again." He did not sound as if he believed it. He took his thumb off the sheet. "Upper town's had the tower water since May. Lower town hasn't."

Emeric looked at the two columns.

The tower water. Filtered, and boiled. In a copper pan. Over eight recharged fire stones, charged by Orrin every week, lying in the grate an inch under the copper, hot, every day, since May.

It tastes like the air before a storm, Bram had said, at the tap, at the top of the high street, and asked for more.

He did not say anything to Paskin. He thought it, and kept it, and let Paskin roll the sheet up and take it back down to Armand, who would call it a cold morning, and be right to, until he could not.



The same afternoon Ferra brought up Bryda's charter for his seal: steward of Grayrock, in her own name, answerable to the holder and to no one else, at the wage Ferra had set and a little over, because Ferra said a woman who had held a barony two years by herself should not be paid like a man who had held it one. He sealed it. It went up the river on Somebody Came's next run with Ilsa's first ledger coming back down on the same boat.



He told Rylan that evening.

In person. In Rylan's own house, in the lower ward, because it was Rylan's to hear and Emeric did not want him to hear it from the guardroom or from Armand's students or from a sheet on a table. Nessa let him in, and looked at his face, and took Finn and Wren out to her sister's, the one on the west road she did speak to now, without being asked.

Rylan was at the table, with his boots off and his feet on the hearth, mending a strap. He looked up and saw Emeric's face, and put the strap down.

Emeric told him. All of it. It took about four minutes. He did not soften any of it, because Rylan would have known if he had and would not have forgiven him for it. Mott. The two readings. No lines. The tracery: what it was. What it meant. That the rite took a man's pathways and tore them, all night, to make in one night what a body could make on its own in two years, if somebody gave it two years and a stone. That nobody in the tower had ever read a man twice. That Armand was going to spend the rest of his life trying to prove it was not true.

Rylan did not say anything while he told it. He sat with his feet on the hearth and his hands on his knees and looked at the fire, and when Emeric said the word scar he looked down at his own forearm, at the silver going up into his sleeve, and looked back at the fire.

When Emeric had finished, Rylan sat for a long while.

Then he got up, and went to the table, and sat down on the edge of it, with his boots off, the way he sat on the edge of the bench in the guardroom, and looked at Emeric.

"Would it have worked twelve years ago?" he said. "On the men in that room with me?"

Emeric did not look away.

"I don't know."

Rylan looked at him for a while longer. Then he nodded, slowly.

"That's the right answer," he said.

He got up off the table, and took his coat off the peg, and put it on, in his stockinged feet, and went out of his own front door into the lane, without his boots, and shut the door behind him, and Emeric heard his feet go away down the lane on the frozen cobbles toward the river, and did not follow him.

He sat at Rylan's table until Nessa came back with the children, an hour later. She looked at him, and at the empty chair, and at the boots by the hearth, and did not ask.

"He'll be at the water gate," she said. "On the stair. He goes there." She took Wren's coat off her. "He'll come home when he's cold."



He drew the chair that night. Not the room's drawing. A real one, at his table, on paper, by candle, with the fist-sized stone from the bottom of the cursed gallery on the table beside the paper, pressing up, gently, against nothing.

He drew it carefully. He had thought about it all the way down the river and every night since. Tess's chair. Not the wheeled one she had now, that Bram pushed and she pushed and the guard pushed. One that moved by itself. That lifted. That would take her up the keep stair and down the river stair and over the cobbles of the lower ward without anybody's hands on it but hers. Gravity stone under the seat, to take her weight off the ground. Air at the corners, to move her. A stick under her right hand to tell it where.

And he drew a governor on everything.

A top speed, no faster than a man walking. Lift capped at a hand's width off the ground, never more. The turn limited, so that it could never spin. A parachute, for the stairs, locked out unless he unlocked it. And a key. A small brass key that started it and stopped it, that only he would hold, so that if anything went wrong, if anything at all went wrong, he could stop it.

He drew it all. It took him until the candle was half gone. It was a good drawing. It was careful and clear and every part of it was safe. It was exactly what he would have built for a stranger.

He looked at it until the candle guttered.

Then he looked at it again, the way he would have looked at a drawing Tess had brought him, and saw what it was.

Every safeguard on that paper was against the chair. Against the stone, and the air, and the speed, and the height. There was not one against her. She did not need one against her. She had drawn the water-tube boiler. She had found the tuyere. She had sat in the dynamo house in the war with the cut-outs and kept the lamps on while men died outside the door, and she had nearly died herself, and she had come back to the school in this chair and taught current to children. And he had drawn her a chair that would only go as fast as he said, and as high as he said, and stop when he said, with a key in his pocket.

He had drawn her a chair he could hold.

He tore the drawing in half. Then in half again. He put the pieces in the grate and watched them catch, and burn, and curl, and go black.

Then he took a fresh sheet, and drew it again. The same stone. The same air. The same stick. And no governor at all.
