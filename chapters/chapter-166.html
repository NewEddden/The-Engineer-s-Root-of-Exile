Chapter 166 - Two Hearts

Ward's complaint about the first suit had been one word, and he had said it once, in April, hanging in the shed a man's height over the sand with his body rigid in the harness and his heels slowly rising past his head.

"Backwards," he had said.

That was the whole of it. The first suit had one heart, in the back, and a heart was heavy: the housing, the iron, the cells in their glass, the stone; and a man with a heavy thing strapped to his back and the plate round him lifted from the shoulders and hips and heels went over backwards, slowly, the way a man goes over who leans back on a chair, unless he fought it with the stick every breath he was in the air. Ward had fought it. Ward had flown the first suit for six months, every week, and fought it every breath, and said nothing more about it after that one word, because it was the suit there was.

The second suit had two hearts.



The forge made them in October, a piece at a night, in the cellar under the keep, on Bram's new rails.

Only Emeric shaped. That had been the rule since the first night the forge was lit and it would be the rule as long as it stood: whatever went into the chamber went in by his hand and came out by his word, and nobody else in Kaldo would ever stand at the stone door and tell the forge what to make. Bram did the rest. Bram made the casings for the hearts and the harness and the buckles and the straps, by hand, in the works, to the masters, and brought them down the narrow stair in the dark in a sack, and laid them on the bench by the forge's door, and went back up.

Two hearts, then. One at the breast, under the breastplate, and one at the back, and each of them a housing of iron with nine flask cells in it in their glass liners, enough to fly the whole suit alone for about a quarter of an hour at full push and a good deal longer at less. Either heart could fly it. Both together could fly it for twice as long; and a man whose back heart died in the air did not go over backwards, because he had another heart on his chest to balance it and carry him down. Ward would not hang head down over a sand pile again in his life.

The rest was the chair, as the first suit had been, and a year's flying on top of it. The thumb stick in the left gauntlet's grip, push to go and twist to turn, with the little counter-burst at the end of every twist that stopped a man spinning; Tess's, from her chair. The lift lever on the belt in its notched quadrant, a notch a stone. The heater in the back housing, shielded, that failed off and never on. Imani's cooling, all three tiers of it, the air and the water tube and the wet felt, which had kept Osric's pulse down in the foundry in August when he had stood in the suit at the furnace door for half an hour to prove that it would. The cape-wing over all of it, furled, on Abloh's battens, with the bellows watchdog under it that threw it open three counts after a heart died. Seamless blacked steel, every plate one piece. Winged helms, because Sorrel had said a man flying ought to look as if he meant it, and Bram had said that wings on a helmet were vanity, and made them anyway. A lion's head on each shoulder plate, because Osric had asked, and once Osric had asked, Declan had to have one too. Every pane of glass in every helm and every node dark violet, like the panes in the K Discovery's glass house, to cut the glare off water and snow.

And the first suit's stones went into the second suit's hearts. All of them. Its nodes and its air stones and its one heart's nine cells, out of the old casings one by one in the forge's chamber and into the new, so that nothing that had flown once hung on a peg in the shed afterward doing nothing. That had been Emeric's own rule, made at the table in the spring after the Grayrock commission's three stones sat in a bag in his drawer for a year: nothing hangs unused.



He coded the nodes on the twentieth, at night, in the chamber, with Tess at the dial.

It was the part nobody saw. The forge made the steel and the glass and the casings, and Bram made the straps, and Tess fitted them; and the stones that went into the nodes, the small gravity stones under their violet glass at the shoulders and hips and heels and the small of the back, went in raw. A raw stone pushed, if you charged it, straight up or straight down along its own axis, as hard as it could, and did nothing else. It did not know it was in a suit. It did not know there was a stick, or a heart, or a man. Somebody had to tell it.

That was him. Only him. Nobody else in Kaldo could go into a stone from inside and write in it; nobody else in the kingdom, as far as he knew. He sat on the stool by the forge's stone door with the first node's stone in his left hand and his mother's grey stone in his right, and went into the room.

Tess had asked him to say it aloud.

She had asked in the summer, the first time she sat in the cellar while he cut a stone for the chair's spares: not what the room looked like, which she could never see, but what he was doing in it. Because she was the one who would have to fix it, she said, when it went wrong in the air with a knight in it, and he was not there. So he said it aloud now, sitting on the stool with his eyes shut and the stone in his hand, slowly, the way a man reads a recipe to somebody else in the kitchen.

"The node," he said. "Left heel. It has one job."

In the room the stone opened in front of him the way they all did: like a small locked box with one shelf in it, and the shelf empty. He did not have a better way to say it, even to himself. In the other life Marcus had spent his working years telling machines what to be with drawings; and the trial had put the rest into him whole, a trade he had never studied, the writing of instructions for machines that had no more idea what they were for than this stone did and did nothing but follow them. It had fitted into the place in him where the drawing board had been as if it had always been there. The stone's shelf was not a page. It did not take words. But it took a job, if the job was put on it whole and plain and in the right order, and it would do that job, exactly, every time, until somebody came and took the job off the shelf again.

"It listens to one thing," he said. "The rod from the back heart. The rod tells it how much. Nothing else it can feel tells it anything."

He put that on the shelf. One thing to listen to. A stone could feel a great deal, if it was let: its own field, a hand on it, the warmth of a man, the next stone along. He did not let it. A stone that listened to more than one thing was a stone that could be told two things at once, and he did not want to find out in the air what it did then.

"It pushes up," he said. "Along its axis. As hard as the rod says, and no harder. Never more than the weight of the plate it's on and a fifth again." That went on the shelf. "If the rod goes quiet, it stops. It doesn't hold. It doesn't guess. It stops, and the plate's weight comes back on the harness, and the watchdog does the rest." That went on the shelf, and he put it first, under the push, so that it was the thing the stone would reach for first if anything went wrong. He always wrote that first now. He had since the oven shed.

"That's all," he said, and came out, and opened his eyes, and handed Tess the stone.

She tried it on the bench. Every node, before it went into a suit, went on her bench with its own small cell and a rod and a dial, and she moved the rod and watched the dial, and moved it again, and wrote the numbers in her data book. The left heel node pushed up when the rod said up, as hard as the rod said. It stopped when she cut the rod. She wrote it down.

He did two hundred and ten of them. Five suits' nodes, forty to a suit, and ten spares. It took him three nights, each to three in the morning, and he said every one of them aloud, the same, with only the place and the limit changing, and Tess wrote down every one; and at the end of it his head ached the way it ached after a long night in the room, which it had been, and he went up the narrow stair to bed.



The suit rolled on the twenty-fourth.

Ward was in it. Ward was in all of them first; that had been settled without anybody saying it, the way it had been settled with the first. He stood on the sand in the shed in the new black plate with his helm off under his arm and the test rope clipped to the ring at his back, the rope going up over the block on the gantry to four men at the winch, and Tess in her chair at the edge of the sand with the data book and a hand on the cord that would kill both hearts at once if she pulled it.

"Helm," said Tess.

Ward put the helm on. The violet glass of its face turned to the shed doors.

"Lift," said Tess. "One notch."

Ward's right hand went to the lever at his belt and moved it. One notch. The nodes woke. Emeric felt them wake, in his sense, all round the suit, forty small bright points of stone, the plate's weight going off the harness, the suit lightening round the man inside it.

And the suit rolled.

Not much. Not fast. A slow, smooth, horrible lean to the left, all of it at once, plate and man, going over on its left side on the sand as if somebody had put a hand under its right hip and lifted; and Ward, inside it, flung out his right arm the way a man does who is falling off a horse. The men at the winch took the rope up hard. Tess's hand closed on the cord.

"Don't," said Emeric.

She did not. She looked at him.

He was looking at the suit. With his sense, not his eyes. Forty nodes. Thirty-nine of them pushing up, as hard as their rods told them, soft and even, as they should. And one, low on the left, at the heel, pushing down. As hard as its rod told it. The wrong way.

"Off," he said. "Lift off."

Ward's hand moved the lever back. The nodes went quiet. The suit came down onto the sand on its left side with a clank, and lay there, with the rope taking Ward's weight off it, and Ward inside it saying something into his helm that the helm did not let out.

"Left heel," said Emeric.

Tess looked at her data book. "Left heel tried on the bench. Pushed up. As the rod said." She ran her finger down the page. "It's here. It's right."

"It's right on the bench," said Emeric. "On the bench it was lying on its side."

He saw it as he said it. On Tess's bench every node lay flat in a cradle, on its side, with its axis along the bench; and up, on the bench, was a direction along the bench, toward Tess's end, because that was how he had always laid them and how she had always tested them, and the dial on her bench read push toward her end as up. In a heel, a node stood on end. And whether it stood on end with its up toward the knee or toward the sole depended on which way round it had been put into the cradle on the bench when he cut it, and which way round Bram's man had put it into the heel. One of the forty had been put in the cradle the other way round. He had cut it to push up along its axis, correctly. Its up was the heel's down.

It took a quarter of an hour. Bram's man had the heel off the suit in five minutes. Emeric took the node's stone into the room on the stool by the shed door, with Ward still lying in the sand watching him through the violet glass, and took the job off its shelf and put it back with one word changed, and came out; and on Tess's bench this time she stood the node on end in a clamp, the way it would stand in a heel, before she tried it. It pushed toward the knee. She marked the stone's top with a scratch of chalk. She went down the data book and marked a column she had not had before, headed which end up, and spent the next two hours standing every one of the other thirty-nine nodes on end in the clamp and marking it.

The suit went up again at five. It went up level. Ward hung in it a man's height over the sand, quite still, upright, with his arms at his sides and the two hearts humming, and turned his head inside the helm to look at the shed, and then down at Emeric.

"Better," said Ward.

Emeric found that he was grinning, and could not stop, and did not try. He had found it in a quarter of an hour. One wrong word on one shelf in forty, and he had felt it in the suit with his sense before Tess's dial knew anything was wrong, and found it, and fixed it, and the suit hung level. In the other life Marcus had sometimes spent three days on a thing like that, a machine doing exactly what it had been told by somebody who had told it wrong, and found it at four in the morning, and sat back in his chair and felt exactly this.

Tess was looking at him. Then at the data book, at the new column.

"You're pleased," she said.

"I'm not."

"You are. You've got the face Bram gets when he finds a crack before it goes." She wrote something. "I'm going to put a clamp on every bench that tests a node. And a scratch on every stone. Top end." She did not look up. "So you don't have to be pleased again."



Tess fitted the knights in the last week of October.

Ward, then Declan, then Osric, then Emeric last, because Tess said a man who had drawn a suit always thought it fitted him, and he could wait. She did it the way she had done the first suit: each man standing, then walking, then kneeling, with the harness on and off and on again and the buckles moved and the straps moved; the stick's spring set by feel, with the man's thumb on it and hers over his; and every man flown a man's height over the sand on the rope, and then two, and then up to the gantry, and across the shed and back, with Tess calling the hearts' readings off her dials and writing them in the book.

Five suits stood on the racks in the shed when she had finished. Ward's, with its lions. Declan's, with its lions. Osric's, with lions slightly larger than Declan's, which Osric had asked Bram for privately and Bram had made without comment. Emeric's, plain, with no lions, because he had not asked.

And the fifth. Rylan's. Built to the measurements Tess had taken off him in April for the first suit, with its lions and its winged helm and its violet panes, standing on its rack at the end of the row with its hearts empty and its cape furled.

Rylan had not asked for it. He had come to the shed on the day the suits stood finished, with the others, and looked at the five of them on their racks, and looked at his own a little longer than the others, and gone back to the line room without saying anything. Tess had looked after him, and then at Emeric.

"It stays," Emeric said. "Charged and fitted. Till he asks."

"And if he doesn't?"

"Then it stays."



The springs were the last thing, and the least to look at, and the thing Bram cared about most.

Every suit had springs in it that had to work once and never fail. The watchdog's springs, that threw the cape-wing open three counts after a heart died. The chute's release. The latch that held the cells in the hearts. And Bram, coiling them in the works by hand on a rod in the vice, the way every spring in four baronies had ever been coiled, could not tell one spring from the next by looking, or by feel, or by anything but trying it; and he could not try a spring that had to work once without using up its once.

So the forge made him a set of mandrels, the rods to coil springs on, each one true to its size; and a set of reference springs, one for each kind, coiled in the chamber from wire run to recipe, each one compressing a known distance under a known weight, every time, as true as the forge made anything. Bram coiled every watchdog spring on the mandrels and put it in a frame beside the reference spring under the same weight, and if it squeezed the same distance, it went into a suit, and if it did not, it went in the scrap.

He did the boiler safety valves' springs the same way, all of them, every boiler in four baronies, because a safety valve was a spring that had to work once too, and the oven shed had had one. And the forge's own fail-closed latch on the stone door, which Emeric had made himself in the first winter and never tried.

It was a fifth weaker than its reference. Bram held it up in the tally room's lamplight between his thick fingers and looked at Emeric over it, and did not say anything; and coiled him a new one on the mandrel; and fitted it himself, that night, in the dark, at the bottom of the narrow stair.



He tried the other thing with Ward on the last night of October, in the shed, after the others had gone.

Ward was in his suit on the sand, with the rope on and the hearts humming and the helm under his arm; Emeric had asked him to stay. He had been thinking about it for a fortnight, since he had stood at the end of the rack and looked at the five suits and thought about the night in the yard with the width of a hair, and Ward's word coming down it.

"A line," he said. "From here to the gantry. Up, over the slip, along the top of the hull, round the propeller arm, and down to the doors. I want you to fly it."

"I know the shed," said Ward.

"I know you know the shed. I don't want you to fly the shed. I want you to fly the line." He put his hand in his coat, on the grey stone. "I'm going to put it where you can see it."

Ward looked at him. Then he put the helm on.

Emeric went into the room. Not all the way; the width of a hair, the way he had in the yard, with his sense out across the shed to Ward's note, slow and even, a wall, standing on the sand in the black plate a dozen paces off. The door gave. He felt the stillness go through Ward's note a breath before, the room going quiet. And in the room, on a shelf he had made for it that afternoon, he had a drawing: the shed, as he saw it from where Ward stood, the slip and the gantry and the long brown back of the hull and the propeller arm folded at her stern, and on it, in a line of thin bright red like a thread pulled through a picture, the way he wanted Ward to go.

He took the drawing off the shelf and held it up to the hair-width, the way a man holds a page up to a window.

Ward did not move. Then his helm turned, slowly, inside its violet glass, from the doors to the gantry, and up, and along the hull, and round, following something.

"There's a line," said Ward. His voice was very flat. "Red. In the air." A pause. "Over the shed. I can see the shed through it."

"Fly it."

Ward flew it.

He went up off the sand on the lever, a notch at a time, up the red line to the gantry, and over the slip, and along the long top of the hull with his heels a yard above her brown planking, exactly where the line ran, and round the folded propeller arm in a slow turn on the twisted stick with the counter-burst coming at the end of it, and down, along the line, to the doors, and onto the sand a yard from where he had left it. He did not look at the shed. He looked at the line.

It cost. Emeric could feel it cost, behind the eyes, more than words through the door had cost, a steady pull, as if he were holding the page up to the window with his arm out straight; and when Ward's boots touched the sand he let the drawing go and the door shut and he sat down on the bench by the wall and put his head in his hands.

Ward took the helm off. He stood on the sand and looked at the empty air over the shed where the line had been.

"Gone," he said.

"Yes."

Ward was quiet for some while.

"At night," he said. "Over the marsh. Over the river. With no lamp, and a line in front of me where you want me to go." He looked at Emeric on the bench. "How far?"

"I don't know yet. The shed."

"The shed," said Ward, and put the helm on the rack, and began to take the suit off a plate at a time, as Tess had taught them, in order. "Then further," he said, with his back to Emeric. "Before we go anywhere I'm going to be glad of it."
