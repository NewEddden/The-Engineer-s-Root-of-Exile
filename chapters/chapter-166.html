Chapter 166 - Winter Accounts

Ferra closed the year on the shortest day, as she always did, at the long-room table after the others had gone to bed, with the fire built up and one lamp and the book.

It was a tradition nobody had asked her to keep and nobody had ever told her to stop. The barony's year ran from midwinter to midwinter because that was when Emeric had come, and she had closed his first year on the shortest night of it at her father's kitchen table with a candle, because the figures would not wait, and she had closed every year since the same way. Not the quarter; the quarter had its own day. The year. Everything that had happened, in the book, in order, and then a line drawn under it, and then whatever was true at the bottom.

Hobb was asleep in the chair by the door with his coat over his knees. She had told him to go to bed. He had said the lanes were full and he would walk her down, and then he had fallen asleep, and she had let him.

She began at the top.

The count first. It was Ilsa's count now, and it was the first count in two years that Ferra believed.

Ilsa had walked the barony every week since the autumn, every lane on the island and every farm on the lake road and every hamlet at the lower water, with a book and a pencil and Dace's old list, and asked at every door who slept there, and written it down, and come back the next month and asked again. She had not taken anybody's word for anything. She had found three families on the island living under the names of families who had gone back to Milford in the summer, and a man on the east bank who had given four different names at the gate in four months, and she had found him a fifth, which was his own. Ferra had read every page of it.

The barony of Kaldo, by I. Renner's walking count, at midwinter: five thousand three hundred and eighty, not counting the camp.

She wrote it, and looked at it. A year ago she had said near five thousand and could not say nearer. Now she could say nearer, and it was higher than she had guessed.

The camp on the flat and the foundry bank, counted apart: four thousand seven hundred and sixty, of whom one thousand nine hundred and forty are men on the quarry's tally. From four baronies. Moving.

She wrote Moving on purpose. It was the only honest word for the camp. Ilsa counted it every month and every month a tenth of it was new and a tenth was gone, and the number in the middle stayed near the same, like a river.

Then the work.

The wall's footing: closed on the full circuit, two miles and three furlongs by the Tarrants' chain, on the rock, faced two courses, in the last week of autumn, four days before the first frost. She remembered the day. She had not been there. Emeric had been there, and the mages, and two thousand men, and the last gap at the east side by the marsh had been closed at dusk by a woman from the north called Rook, alone, because the others were spent and she had asked to, and the men on the flat had stopped work and watched her do it. Emeric had told the table about it afterwards. He had not told it the way he told most things. He had told it as if it had been the best thing he had seen all year, and Ferra thought it probably had been.

The first quarter's drains and water mains: laid, under ground, on the north-east quarter, to the Tarrants' falls, and the ground made good over them. Nothing yet built on them. She had been down to look at that. It was the strangest sight on the flat: a great square of rough pasture with nothing on it but the stakes of streets that were not there yet, and under it, if you knew, pipe. Clay pipe and stone culvert, laid in the summer and covered in the autumn, running under the grass to places where there would be houses in two years, carrying nothing.

The settling beds and the farm below the lower water: drawn, costed at eleven Platinum and four Gold, begun. The first tank cut. The water rate at a Copper the household the month for three years, subscribed at the winter count by three thousand one hundred and six households. It was not every household. It was more than she had thought it would be. She had a list of who had not, and she did not intend to chase all of them, and she knew which ones she did.

The lower-water wells: shut, four. New wells dug across the stream, three, to the standard, sited by the Secretary of Health and walked by her to the ground above. Imani had walked it three times. She had walked it with a chain the third time, herself, with Tamsin holding the far end.

At the lower water, two keels on the slips. Hull two, and the coaster on the second slip. Hull two framed to the turn of the bilge. She did not write anything else about hull two in that line. It had its own line, further down.

There was one more line for the lower water, and she wrote it in pencil, because nothing in it had a price. The ridge foot beside the yard, where the ridge went down into the salt: walked by the Tarrants with Fletcher, chained, drawn. A wall round the yard. A dock cut into the rock, gated to the lower water, roofed over. Not costed. Not begun. The drawing was Bram's. He had made it on the back of a boiler tally the week he hauled the Emberfell's boiler down to the lower water on two carts lashed together, and had said, putting it on her table, that he was not doing that again for anything bigger, and Fletcher had drawn the wall round it the same night. She had not asked what the roof was for. She knew what the roof was for.

The rail works: reopened after the boiler, the new rule in force. Four hundred lengths of steel rail rolled, the first ever rolled in the south. Two hundred and sixty laid on the Grayrock line before the frost, from the fourth mile. She had worked the new figure for the line off Bram's tallies and Bryda's and the frost and the harvest bounty and the rest. To the hill: the autumn after next.

It had been six summers two years ago. It was a summer and a half now. She wrote the figure plain. She did not write what it had cost to move it. That was in the lines above: Rudd's, in the spring, and the two burned men, and the eleven dead at the lower water, which were not the rail's but were the year's.

Then the stone.

She turned to the gravity page and sat with it for a long time before she wrote.

The page had three figures on it, as it had had since the summer. Stone raised at Grayrock at the spring raising and the autumn raising, in pounds, weighed at the pithead by Ilsa in the spring and by Bryda in the autumn. Stone sold out of the barony under the covenant, in pounds, the Crown's first purchase taken on every pound of it and paid, the Crown's share against it. And the third: Kept, in the barony. And beside the third, Set, in hull two.

She did her own arithmetic on it. She did it the way she did it every quarter, with a pencil on scrap, from the bottom, and then from the top, and got the same answer both ways.

Raised, less sold, was kept. Kept was in hull two's racks, set in pairs in the chiselled slots on her keel, a long cart-load of it under a cloth on a night in the autumn with Bram and Emeric and Fletcher and nobody else on the lower water awake. Every pound of it in the book. Every pound of it lawful. The covenant gave the Crown the right of first purchase on stone sold out of the barony, and said nothing about stone the barony did not sell, because nobody who wrote the covenant had imagined a barony that raised a stone worth two Platinum the pound and did not sell it.

She wrote under the three figures: Reconciled. And the date.

Then she took up the letter that had come by the Crown's rider in the first week of winter, and read it again, though she knew it.

It was from Fenner. Oswin Fenner, the Crown's assessor, the narrow grey man in the chancery coat who had come to Kaldo three years ago and gone up to Grayrock and watched Hask draw the sealed shaft in the dust with a stick, and written a date in a black book, and gone away. It was a courteous letter. He wrote that he was instructed by the chancery to attend the spring raising at Grayrock in his own person, as the covenant allowed, to witness the weighing of the stone and the taking of the Crown's first purchase, and that he would present himself at the Western Gate on his way, and would ask for nothing but water for the horses and the loan of a table.

He had never come to a raising before. The covenant allowed it. It had never needed it. The Crown's share had come to the capital every half year in a locked chest with Ilsa's weigh-sheet sealed on top of it and never once been questioned.

Ferra put the letter down on the gravity page.

She wrote in the margin, beside the three figures, in pencil, small: the difference. Raised, less sold. The number of pounds in hull two's racks. She did not write hull two beside it. She wrote the number and nothing else.

Then she looked at it.

Fenner would stand at the pithead in the spring and watch Ilsa weigh the spring's stone. He would write the figure in his black book. He had, somewhere in that book, the figure from the chancery for every half year's share since the covenant, which was the figure for stone sold. He had, somewhere in the chancery, or in his head, which was the same thing with Fenner, the Grayrock survey and what it said the ground held. And he was a narrow grey man who had been in the chancery's service for thirty years and wrote dates in a black book and did not ask questions until it was useful to.

He would not need to see her book. He would be able to work it out for himself.

The strongbox.

She did not enter the strongbox in the book. She never had. The strongbox had its own line in the book, strongbox, keep, contents per the box, and the box was the single-lock box her father had made in the winter before the Wayford net, with the key on the cord round her neck, and the itemisation was in the box.

She took the key off the cord and opened the box and wrote.

In the strongbox, at midwinter: the second of the Queen Amara's two grey stones, the chipped one, bound to nothing, as received. The first, the root, reshaped in the forge in the autumn of last year to a bead, bound to the holder, holding the names of fifteen knots now in a crate in the keep's cellar, and nothing else. Neither in use. Neither valued.

She read it back. It was all true. It did not say that the chipped one was the only unbound stone of its kind that anybody in Kaldo knew of, in the world. It did not say that Armand had stood by the forge and watched the root grow its lattice back over every new face and said nobody made these, or nobody made them the way we were told, and that she had written that down in the box too, at the time, and never read it since.

She closed that page and turned to the next.

Armand's readings came in the last week of every year. He brought them to her himself, on paper, in his own hand, which was worse every winter. Six years of readings on the etching floor and the finished-stock foundry men and the ship's crew and the six at the table, taken every winter with his instruments in the keep's east room, one person at a time, with the door shut. He had shown her the first year's. He had shown her every year's since, because she was the only person at the table who would look at a column of figures and see the column and not the people in it.

He had brought this year's two days ago. He had put them on the table without a word and sat down, and she had read them while he sat, and when she came to the line that mattered she had stopped reading, and he had seen her stop.

The Secretary of Works, it said. And the figure for this winter, beside the figure for every winter before.

Her father's figure had not moved in six years. Not once. Not a hair. A smith of no capacity to speak of, a man who had never been able to see a thing that was not there, read every winter in the east room by the grey robe and found to be what he had been the winter before. Every year, the same.

It had moved.

It was not much. It was the smallest move in the column that was not nothing. Armand had read him twice to be sure, a week apart, and written both. It was a little under a year since the bands were issued. A little under a year of a charged stone on his wrist every day, and a room opened out of a boiler, alone, at night, and the figure beside his name was not what it had been.

Armand had said, while she read it, "It's the band."

"You don't know that."

"No," Armand had said. "I don't. I know what the column says." He had looked at his hands. "I know what the other column says, too. The one in my box. It's the same shape."

She did not enter it in the book. She entered it in the box, under the stones.

The Secretary of the Arcane's winter readings. The Secretary of Works: moved, a little, measurably, read twice. The first reading in six years that is not the same as the one before. And the date.

She sat with the pen in her hand after she wrote it and looked at the fire for a while. She did not think about what it meant. She had got very good, over twelve years, at writing a thing down and not thinking about it until it was time.

Saban's winter report had come in the day before, as it always did. He wrote it himself and gave it to her sealed and she read it and entered what the book needed and put the rest in the box, and he never asked which was which.

Most of it was the lane. The Emberfell had made four runs since the summer under Rowena's escort and nothing had come out of the coast at her. The hired coasters had made eleven. Rowena's Treasurer had written twice, once about tonnage and once to ask for his two figures back. The coin was in Saban's purse still and he had nothing new on it and said so.

And one paragraph at the end, which Ferra read twice.

Lisle. Lord Corwen Lisle's heir, his only son, was married at the autumn quarter to a daughter of the house of Sallow, which is Aldane's by marriage twice over and sits at his table. Lisle did not go to the wedding. It was in the north. The son did.

Lisle's land runs across the Wayford road at its northern bridge, where the road crosses the stream a mile south of the town, and has done since the Staple's charter. His house holds a charter of the same age to take toll at that bridge, on everything that crosses it, carts and beasts and men. It has not been used in twenty years. Nobody in Wayford remembers it being used. I found it in Sethe's third box, in a copy, in a bundle of old road papers that Ferra's clerks entered as road, old, no action, and nobody has read since. It is lawful. It has never been revoked.

Every cart between Kaldo and Wayford crosses that bridge. So does the relay line.

That was all. Saban had not written what he thought it meant. He never did, in the report.

Ferra sat with it.

There was nobody listening in Wayford. There had not been since the autumn before last, when the net came down under the fifth condition, all fifteen knots in three nights in the dark of the moon, and the office closed, and the root put in the strongbox. She had signed that. She had been glad to sign it. And Lisle had never touched the ink. He had sent his gift back unopened by a servant in brown on the first day, with thanks and no reason, and it had sat on the back-room shelf in the Ambry house until the net came down. Not a page of Lisle's hand had ever gone through the root. If every knot were still on every chimney in Wayford tonight, it would have heard nothing from Lisle's house, because Lisle's house wrote in somebody else's ink, or did not write at all.

She entered it. In the book, because it was a fact about a road. Lisle: heir married into the house of Sallow, at Aldane's table. Lisle holds an unused charter to toll the Wayford road at its northern bridge. And in the box, under the stones and the readings, Saban's last line, copied out in her own hand. Every cart between Kaldo and Wayford crosses that bridge. So does the relay line.

Vantic had come for the winter quarter day a week ago, as she always did. As she had come every quarter day for seven years, in the House's carriage, with her small book and her clerk and her courier, and sat at the long-room table and copied what the House needed from Ferra's book.

Ferra had watched her do it.

She had not meant to watch. She had never watched before. She had always sat across from Vantic on the quarter day with her own work, and let Vantic read, and answered what Vantic asked, and talked to her about clauses and figures and the price of lamp oil, and it had been the easiest hour of any quarter. This time she had sat across from her and watched Vantic's pen.

Vantic had read the Grayrock page. She had read the gravity lines, the three figures, the Kept, in the barony, and the Set, in hull two. Ferra had watched her read them. Her eyes had gone down the page and stopped at the third figure for about as long as it takes to draw a breath.

Then she had copied the first figure, stone raised, into her own book, in her small neat hand, and the second figure, stone sold and the Crown's share, because the House took its account from the sold stone and needed it. And she had not copied the third.

She had turned the page and gone on to the paper works.

Between the Grayrock page and the paper works a loose sheet had slid half out of the back of Vantic's book, folded in four, and Vantic had pushed it back with one finger without looking at it. Ferra had seen it before. It had been at the back of that book on every quarter day for three years or more, behind the House's pages, and she had never once seen it come out. It came out this time far enough for her to read the first fold upside down, which she could do as easily as the right way up.

It was a table, ruled in Vantic's small hand. Down the left side, names: Kaldo first, and under it eight or nine that Ferra did not know, a mill and a salt works and a bridge toll and two baronies, the kind of names that stand in a lender's book for the thing a loan was made against. Across the top, years. In every square, two small figures one above the other, and in some squares a third in pencil that had been rubbed out and written again.

The House did not ask for it. Ferra knew exactly what the House asked for. Nobody had asked for it at all.

Ferra had not said anything. Vantic had not looked up. They had talked, afterward, about the price of lamp oil, and a clause in the second draw, and Rowena's Treasurer, whom Vantic had met once at Emberfell and thought a fool. And then Vantic had gone, in the House's carriage, and her courier had gone the other way, west, by the coast road past Milford, and Saban's man had lost him there again.

She came back to the gravity page at the end.

The fire had gone down. Hobb was still asleep by the door. She had closed every other page of the year and drawn her line under it, and the book was nearly done, and she turned back to the gravity page because she had known all evening that she would.

The three figures. Reconciled. The date.

And in the margin, in pencil, small, the difference.

It was not a large number, as the barony's numbers went now. It was smaller than the loan. It was smaller than the beds. It was smaller than the number of people in the camp. It was a number of pounds of grey stone, sitting in pairs in the chiselled slots on a keel on a slip at the lower water, where the yard men thought they were building a coaster.

It was lawful. Every pound of it. She had made sure it was lawful. She had not made sure it was comfortable, and she had said so at the table in the summer, and she had meant it.

In the spring a narrow grey man with a black book would stand at the pithead at Grayrock and watch Ilsa weigh the stone, and write the figure down. And then he would go home to the chancery and sit at his table and take out the figure for the stone sold, which he already had, because the Crown had been paid it every half year in a locked chest. And he would subtract.

He would not need her book. He would not need anybody to tell him. He would be able to work it out for himself, at his own table, with a pencil, in about the time it had taken her.

She looked at the number in the margin for a long time.

Then she did not rub it out. She left it where it was, in pencil, beside the three figures, and drew the year's line under the page, in ink, straight, with the rule. And under the line she wrote the date, and closed the book, and sat with her hand flat on the cover of it while the fire went down, before she woke Hobb to walk her home.
