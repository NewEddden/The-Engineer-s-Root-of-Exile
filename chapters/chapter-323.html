Chapter 323 - Over the Capital

The bank began to grow under them at a quarter to four on the first of December, a little before the sun went.

The Band began a little above two miles over the capital, as it began everywhere anybody had climbed to it; and the fleet hung in the bottom of it, in the thin cold air where the stones drank and the tanks filled, with the seeder's cloud spreading under their keels along the Band's foot like a floor being laid. Two miles down, under the floor, was the city.

From the K Amara's tower Emeric could see the seeder working if he leaned out past the helm and looked aft: the hopper on her stern with its feed screw turning in its throat, and the thin white breath coming off it and going out behind her and down, spreading as it went. On the stern rail beside it, in their Band suits with their hands out over the rail as if they were warming them at a fire, stood the wind watch, three of Armand's students, holding the air still round the bank so that the night wind would not tear it to rags and carry it off east. An hour on and an hour off. Armand had said they were seventeen and would want to do two, and somebody was to stop them.

Through the holes in the cloud was the capital, going to dusk. The river had gone the colour of pewter where it bent round the west and the back of the city, and the roofs inside the outer wall were one dark field with the lamps coming on in it street by street, in the order the lamplighters walked them. The hill stood up out of the middle with the last of the sun on its top: the castle on the east shoulder, pale, a lit window in it; and the summit, and the cathedral, the one dark thing, and at the tip of its spire, small as a pin's head from two miles up, a point of gold that caught the sun after everything under it had lost it, and held it, and let it go.

Then a drift of the seeder's cloud came over the hole and the city was gone.

He had his sense down through the bank anyway, as if through wet wool: a hundred and twenty thousand notes coming up muffled and smeared, too many to sort, and round them in their ring the cold still points he had felt from a cart's tail ten days ago, the chests asleep under the Venn's, each with its small grey sleeping note beside it. He could not count them through the blur. He did not need to. He had them on the sand table.

He knew where everything was. That was the thing about standing at Band height over a city with the sun going. He had the rods in the Hod's hold, finned and nosed, and Ward could put one a stride from a white cross from four miles up. He could have every tower in the ring down by midnight and every chest with it, and the chancery, and the room over the council stair with whoever was in it; and in the morning there would be no government in the capital at all, and the war would be over before the bread was baked. It was very easy to see from up here. It would take an evening.

If only he were evil.

He let himself think it to the end, because it was true; and at the end of it was the other thing, which was also true. If he were evil he would not have to do any of this. Not the forty thousand sheets. Not the bells. Not the count in the morning, and the waiting after it, with a ship hanging over the close in daylight for as long as it took one old man in a cell to be carried out. An evil man would not need to be believed. He would only need to be obeyed.

"You've got the face," said Ward, beside him.

"Which one?"

"The one you had at the quarry." Ward was looking down at the place in the cloud where the city had been. "Don't."

"I'm not."

"I know you're not. I'm saying don't anyway. It's cheaper than finding out."



The fleet lay over the city in the order it had flown in, a little loosened. The K Discovery a mile north at the point, small and black against the paler sky, with Fletcher at her helm and the king aboard her in his plain brown coat; escort one off her port quarter and escort two off her starboard, each a mile out. The K Amara on the centreline, with the K Endeavor and hull four a length off either beam, and Imani in the K Endeavor's hold with her racks and her beds. Escort three at the tail, a mile astern of the K Amara. The Hod astern at the bank's south edge with the rods. Eight at Band height; and hull two two miles down, under the floor, with the leaflets. Nine. He counted them the way Ferra counted a column, top to bottom and back up, and they were all there.

Below the tower, on the forward gun deck, the turret crews stood at their stations in their boots and harness with nothing to do yet but stand. Ruan was there, square and quiet, with his hand on a breech; and three paces from him a thin note that kept shifting its weight, which was Tolliver. Emeric felt for his clip, as he had got into the way of doing since the shed. It was on the line.

The radio in the cupboard under the tower hissed, and it was Sorrel.

"Balloon," she said. "Up. I'm on six hundred feet of rope and he says I can have another four if I stop shouting at him." "I've Ivo's glass. I can see the whole of the south wall from here. I can see a man on the South Gate scratching himself. I can't see a thing above me but cloud, so I'll take your word you're up there."

The Hod had put her down in a turnip field four miles south of the gate at two that afternoon, with the bag and the basket and the burner, a winch on a cart and six of the builders' yard men to crank it, two of Ward's regulars to sit on the hedge with rifles, and a radio set in a crate. It had been her idea, said at the long table in the middle of a discussion about kites, without putting her hand up: that a kite with a stone in it was a stone, and a stone dropped dead the minute anybody threw a dead field at it, and the fleet's eyes over a city full of dead fields wanted to be a thing with no stone in it anywhere. Hers had a fire and a bag and a rope. You could throw a dead field at it all night, she had said, and it would hang there and look at you.

"Sorrel," said Emeric into the mouthpiece. "You're hull two's."

"I'm everybody's," said Sorrel. "I'll start with hull two."



Garrick had worn the key to Ward's lock on a string round his neck since Kaldo, and had touched it through his shirt so often in three days that the string had rubbed a red line on his skin; and at five past four, at the head of the hold ladder, he took it off and gave it to his hold mate.

"Open it."

The door swung back on the racks, and there were the bales, two hundred of them, stacked to the deckhead with DO NOT OPEN BEFORE THE DAY on every one in Hobb's red; and the smell of Hobb's ink came out into the hold so strongly that the shore company along the far bulkhead with their rifles between their knees all turned their heads at once, like cattle at a gate.

Hull two was a thousand feet over the turnip fields by then, coming up on the south wall in the last of the light with her propeller turning slow and her lamps dark. Linus's sheet was pinned on the board by Garrick's hand, wet at the corners from the cloud they had come down through: the passes drawn ward by ward in thin red ink, with the height for each and the wind for each and a little arrow showing which way the sheets would drift. Fly it as drawn, Linus had said on the quay, and every sheet comes down within a street of where it's meant to; fly it otherwise and half go in the river and half in the turnips, and I'll know which you did by the turnips.

The chute was in her belly amidships, where the shore teams' drop doors were, a square mouth with a canvas hood over it and a fan in the hood's throat that blew down. The hold crew slit the bales over it and fed them in two hundred at a time, and the fan threw them out of her bottom in a long white smoke that came apart in the air and fell.

They went in over the South Gate at five hundred feet. Garrick saw a man on the gate's tower look up and catch a sheet out of the air, and stand there with it in his hand.

He went across the lower town in lines, as Linus had drawn them, east and then west and then east again, a street further north on each pass, like a man ploughing; and behind him the city stopped under the falling paper a street at a time. A lamplighter on his ladder with his pole up. A carter standing on his seat with a sheet in both hands. And the children, out of every door there was, running up and down the lanes after the sheets with their arms up, shrieking, as if it were snow.

"Tanners' gate tower," said Sorrel, in his horn. "West of you, on the wall, the one with the flag. They're swinging their Venn's. Both of them. They're loading something that isn't a ball." A pause. "It's grey."

Garrick turned her east. She had never been a quick ship and she went round on her stones like a barge on a tide, the hold crew sliding on their boots and swearing, the white smoke of the sheets bending out behind her in an arc. On the wall the tower's two upright bombards went off together without a sound, a jerk of their frames and nothing more, and two dark specks went up into the dusk where she had been going to be.

Then they broke. A dead field has no sound. But a quarter of a mile behind her a piece of the dusk went wrong, and under it every lamp in three streets went out at once, in a ring, black and round and perfectly clear against the lit streets round it. Then the lamps in it came back one by one as the dead air closed.

"Missed," said Sorrel. "Feed your sheets."

They did the lower town and the old gate wards, and Sorrel called the old gate tower, and the dead field opened behind her over the cloth market and closed. Over the chancery the hold mate fed a whole bale straight down onto its roof on purpose, and Garrick let him. Then they came up the hill.

Linus had drawn the hill last because it was the worst. The ground came up under her as she went north, five hundred feet over the lower town becoming three hundred over the second ring and less over the crown, the towers of the crown wall standing up into the dusk on her starboard bow like teeth. Ahead was the summit: the close's wall, and in it, square in front of her, the gatehouse with its two towers, and on them, small and upright against the last of the sky, the four dark frames of the Venn's.

"Last bales," said Garrick. "Crown wards and the close. Then we climb."

"Gatehouse," said Sorrel. "Garrick, the gatehouse, they're..."

It broke under her keel.

Every stone in hull two stopped. He felt it in the helm first, the lift going out of the levers like a rope gone slack; then in his stomach, as the deck dropped away under his feet and every soul in the hold came up off the plates on their boots and lines with their arms out. The hold lamps went dark. The fan ran down with a whine. The sheets under her stopped falling away and began falling with her, a cloud of them round the hull, white, turning over and over, close enough to touch. Under the helm the steady hummed on its battery, the ball in its glass level, telling him which way was up while he fell toward it.

One. The roofs came up.

Two. A little air stone kept the latch's bellows full, and the full bellows held the striker cocked; in a dead zone the bellows went slack, and that took three counts, and there was nothing anybody could do about the three counts but count them.

Three.

Something went amidships with a crack like a bottle broken in a cellar. The spike through the disc; the tank behind it letting go through its horn into the gap between the iron skin and the matrix ring, and boiling off there all at once into the dead air, into the stones. Not the whole ship. The ring, and the room round it. Her lamps stayed out and her fan stayed dead, and her lift came back into his hands like a dog that had run off and come back, and he put it all on at once, every station, and she caught.

She caught at a hundred and fifty feet over the crown ward's roofs, close enough that he could read the maker's stamp on a chimney pot going under the starboard apron. Every lamp in the lanes below her was out, in a ring whose edge ran across the roofs a stone's throw short of the close's wall; and beyond the edge the lamps on the gatehouse burned. The gatehouse was level with him, forty paces off the starboard bow.

There was a man on the leads of its near tower. Not a bombard crewman: a thin man in grey, bareheaded, in his shirt and breeches, as if he had been asleep on a tick beside a chest a minute ago and had come up a stair with nothing but the thing in his hand. Garrick saw him put his arm out straight and his thumb into the thing, and it went white.

The lightning came across the forty paces in no time at all and went in at the hold's side hatch, which stood open for the fan's air.

A blow through the deck, and a stink, and the shore company shouting; and then from the hold a thin high whistling note, like a kettle a long way off.

"The racks," the hold mate shouted up the ladder. "It's gone into the flask racks. One's cracked. Inside. The inside case is cracked." A pause, with the whistling going on under it. "The sleeve's holding. It's frosting. It's holding."

Double cases of steel round every flask of liquid mana the fleet carried, an inner and an outer, because Bram's rule was that the case must be stronger than what it held; and the outer one plated inside with Tess's shard dust, so that whatever got through the steel slowed over the last inch. The whistling was the sound of it holding.

"Nobody touch it," said Garrick. "Let it frost."

"Nobody's going near it."

The man on the leads was looking at the stone in his hand. It had gone dark. Once, badly, at forty paces, Armand had said at the table. He looked up at hull two going past him, a black wall of steel with a cloud of paper round it, and Garrick saw his face clearly in the last of the light: a man woken to do the one thing he had been kept for, who had done it, and did not know what happened now.

Behind him the Venn's came round. The crews heaved the frames about on their pivots and stood them up straight, and the first went off with that jerk and no sound, and a ball went up past her bow close enough that Garrick heard it, a deep short hum like a bee in a jar. He put her nose up. She came up slowly, heavy, on what the inducer had given her and no more. The second bombard went.

The ball took her on the starboard quarter. A ringing slam that shook her end to end; the helm house's glass coming in on his left side in a spray; something going overboard with a long tearing scream of metal. Then she was past, climbing, with the cathedral's dark bulk going by under her keel and the whole summit turning slowly away below her like a plate on a table.

"Where?"

"Stern quarter, starboard." A deckhand's voice from aft, high. "It's had ten yards of rail off and the edge of the apron. There's a hole in the plate the size of a door, above the line." A pause. "Nobody was on it, Garrick."

He took her up on the matrix through a thousand feet, out of the towers' reach. The inducer's dial read empty; there would be no second catch tonight. The fan had come back with the hold lamps, and the hold crew were feeding the last bales out over the north wards without being told, because the sheet said north wards.

"Hull two," said Garrick into the mouthpiece. "Up. Clipped on the starboard quarter by a ball off the close's gatehouse; rail and apron edge gone, plate holed above the line. One flask cracked in its sleeve by a breaker's stone; sleeve holding. Inducer fired and spent. Nobody hurt." He looked at his left hand on the helm. There was glass in the back of it, a great many small pieces, and blood running between the knuckles, and he had not felt any of it. "Nobody much. Last bales going out. Coming up."

"Come up slow," said Emeric's voice, under the clerk's. "Into the K Endeavor's lee, and stay there."

She would want the Cradle after this, Garrick thought: floated up onto the old races' pier on South Sentinel and the whole quarter taken off her and done again.

"Garrick." The hold mate had come up the ladder and stood in the hatch to his waist, looking past him, aft and down. "Look at the close."

Something was coming up out of it. Out of the walled summit, from behind the long roof of the cardinal's house: long and pale, lifting level and without a sound, with a row of small lit windows along its side like a street seen from a hill, and a violet glow at its front. And behind it, another.



"There's something coming up out of the close," said Sorrel. Her voice had changed. "White. Two. Four. Straight up off the top of the hill. They're lit." A pause. "They're ships, Emeric."

He had already gone down after them, and got wet wool and a smear, and on the summit a thing he could not read: a lot, and moving. He came back up and waited, because whatever it was would have to come through the floor to reach him.

They came up through it a mile off the starboard bow. White out of white: the cloud's top swelling and breaking open, and something long and pale rising out of the break with the cloud pouring off its back. A hull the length of a river barge, oak, whitewashed, with a pointed prow and tall ribbed fins standing up off its back like a church's buttresses; a gilt rail catching the last light from the west; a row of lit windows. In its prow, under glass, a cluster of great stones glowing violet, openly, as a man might put his purse on a table. On its back a dome of coloured glass lit from inside, like a rose window seen from the nave. At its fin tips, small lamps.

Then another beside it. Then two more.

He went straight to the place where the man should be, as he had at the crossing. There was nobody at the seat. There were men aboard, a good many; a knot of them close together in the stern, all tired, doing something he could not make out; there were mages, several. And the stones were working. The lift. Something in the prow that felt like the K Amara's own shell. Something amidships cold and full that he knew for liquid mana sealed in glass. All of it working steadily, the way the K Amara's worked under his feet, and nobody touching any of it. He had read every stone of that summit from inside the nave on the twenty-first, and there had been nothing in the close but stone and people. They had been brought in since, then, by night.

"They're held," he said.

Ward did not say anything.

Off to the north, past the bank's edge, over the dark country where the north road ran out toward Brask, more were lifting. Out there there was no blur, and they came into his sense clean and hard as they rose out of a walled place among the fields off the road: seven, small, like the four. And north-east, out of the low cloud that had lain along the river all afternoon, which nobody on any Kaldo ship had looked at twice because it was weather, five more. Four big ones, longer than the K Endeavor, with heavy dark frames on their decks whose shape he knew. And one very big one.

It came up last, as if it had all the time there was. Nearly as long as the K Amara and a great deal taller, white and gilded, with fins in rows like flying buttresses and a dome of coloured glass on its back as big as a chapel, burning blue and gold and red; in its prow the violet stones, each as tall as a man; and on its prow's tip and its spires, small blue lights, like the windows of a lantern.

"Sixteen," said Ward.

Four out of the close. Seven off the north road. Four big and one very big out of the river. He had doubled the escorts' shells in November because of a walled yard at Ostrel that took oak in by the cartload and let nothing out, and he had thought, when he thought of it at all, of three. Of five.

They did not stop at the bank.

They came up through two miles, where nothing in the kingdom had ever flown but Kaldo, and kept coming. Into the Band. He felt their stones wake in it and drink, as his own did; and they came on, level, unhurried, on every side of the bank, until they hung in the dusk at Kaldo's height and above it, in a ring a mile off and two, with their coloured domes burning. The lamps on their fin tips began to talk, long and short, ship to ship across the sky.

Emeric found the big one's note then, as it came level a mile and a half off the port bow: not the hull's but a man's, high in the stern under the dome, with others round him. Tired, pale, patient, in no hurry about anything. He had felt it once before, across a table in Kaldo's hall over a cup of Maren's tea, the spring a Visitor from the close came down the south road with a little book and a pencil and asked how high the old climbs had gone.

"Orme," he said. "Brother Orme. Year seven. He's on the big one."

"He asked about the marsh light," said Ward slowly. "And the climbs." He was quiet a breath. "He wasn't asking. He was checking."



Emeric gave the orders he had written on the slate in July under a line, against something that flies, and had never thought he would give. The K Discovery back: she was at the point, unarmed, with nothing between her and the four from the close but a mile of air and escort two, and his father was aboard her. "Fletcher. Astern of us and above. Now." Escorts one and two to close on the K Amara's bows, half a mile. The K Endeavor and hull four to close on her beams. The Hod to keep the rods out of everybody's way.

The clerk read it into the mouthpiece in Ferra's groups, a number and a number, faster than Emeric could have said it in words, and the answers came back up the horn one after another. K Discovery: coming. Escort one: closing. Escort two: closing.

Then the big hulls opened on the K Amara.

Two of the four big ones, a mile off her port bow and a little above. The dark frames on their decks swung, and he saw what he had known they were: the crown's gravity bombards, mounted on a ship. A jerk and no sound; and four iron balls the size of a man's head came across the mile at her, curving down as they came, because a bombard only pushes for a few paces in its trough and after that a ball goes like any thrown thing. Two fell short into the bank and made four small holes in it that closed. Two did not.

The shell took them a yard off her port side. He felt it as a thump in his chest and in the deck and in the stone in her prow, twice: the shell gripping the air round each ball and holding it to the world, so that it stopped dead a yard from the black plate as if it had hit a wall nobody could see, and dropped turning into the white. And the stone paid. A ball of iron coming across a mile cost the shell a great deal more to stop than a bolt or a rifle's ball, and he felt the charge go out of it for each of them, a bite, the way a man feels a tooth go.

"Two," he said to the tally clerk at the back of the tower, who had a slate and a chalk and nothing to do all night but write down what the K Amara spent. "Bombard shot, port. Stopped."

She could not answer sideways without her pin, and the pin cost a third of the stone's day for four rounds. He set it. He went down into the keel and found the stern facet, and she stopped as she had stopped over the sea in October, as if somebody had driven a nail through her stern into the sky; the thump went out from her and the keel spine groaned. Ruan's after turret came round. Emeric felt him lay it, three long barrels coming down onto the nearer of the two big hulls, and his hand go flat on the breech.

The big hull moved. It did not turn. It went sideways, a quarter of a mile across the sky, level, without swinging its bow, the way a man steps aside in a doorway; and stopped there dead and hung. Ruan's barrels were laid on empty air.

Kaldo's ships turned in a long slow curve, like a cart in a yard. The white hull had stepped, and not lost a foot doing it.

Two hundred years, he thought. Or four hundred. Of men doing nothing else.

"Ruan. Hold. Don't chase it."

And then escort two.



The four from the close had not come for the K Amara. Two had gone straight up, a thousand feet, in the time it took the K Discovery to begin coming about; the other two had gone down under the bank's edge and nearly out of his sense in the blur. And the K Discovery had come round wide and slow, Fletcher flying her as hard as she would go, with nothing between her and the four of them but escort two.

Escort two did not close on the K Amara's bow. She turned across the K Discovery's wake, between the Discovery and the place where the four had gone, and slowed, and hung there. Her captain was a man called Selby, forty, off the north quay coal lighters, who had built her rivet by rivet in Fletcher's yard before he flew her; and he said nothing on the radio about why. The K Discovery went by under escort two's keel toward the K Amara's stern, and was clear, and escort two was where she had been.

The two from above came down on her first, out of the Band, nose down and steep with their lamps out. Emeric said her name into the mouthpiece and heard the clerk say it, and escort two's port racks went: twelve of Fennick's rockets in a long rippling roar that lit her whole black length orange, going up in a fan at the nearer hull and bursting on its shield, a smaller shield than the K Amara's, wrapped close round its prow and its stones. Twelve bites, small. It held. It came on through the smoke without slowing, and turned.

It turned inside her. Escort two was coming round on her propeller to bring her forward turret to bear, slowly, wide; and the white hull went round her the way a swallow goes round a cow, close and tight, under her bow and up the other side, and hung two hundred feet off her starboard rail, still.

And the two from under the bank came up.

They came up out of the white close under her keel, so close the cloud came up with them and wrapped her belly. Somebody aboard her saw them; Emeric felt a note jump. Her keel battery went, six short barrels straight down into the white together, a flash that lit the bank from inside like a lamp in fog and a slam that reached the K Amara's tower a breath later. Six shells into the shield of the hull below. It held.

And escort two went up, as escort one had gone up over the quarry: six barrels down, and the whole hull jumping off its place in the sky by the height of a house, level, like a lid off a pot. Her stone-keeper would have been at her levers to take the jump and let her down. She did not get the chance. The jump put escort two up into the two hulls above her, which had been waiting there for exactly that, and their launchers went.

The grapples came across on thin lines, silvery in the dusk, iron hooks the size of a man's two hands on their ends, thrown faster than anything a man could throw because a gravity stone threw them. One into her rail amidships. One into the roof of her forward turret. One through the plate under her after apron where the hull curved in.

Three, before her stone-keeper had her pinned.

He had never felt draining done to a ship. It was like watching leeches go onto a horse. Each hook was a drain stone on iron, and the iron bit steel, and the steel ran to her stones; and the mana came out of them through her own frames and up the lines into the white hulls, slowly and then not slowly. Her pin stone went first, half seated when the hooks bit; it emptied like a cup knocked over, and her stern came free of the sky. Her forward turret had come round onto the hull off her starboard rail and fired once. He felt the round stop a yard short of the white oak, and the thump of it. Then the turret slid off its mark as she swung, and the second round did not go.

"Hook-cutters," said Selby's voice in the horn, quite calm, to his own deck; his set was open. "Amidships. Cut that line."

Then the mages came off the white hulls, four of them in white, robes going round their legs in the wind; off the rails, and they did not fall. They hovered as the kingdom's mages had always flown, one hand down holding a glyph under them and the other out, drawing, a ring and a hook and a line lit bright in the air on the end of each drawing hand for anybody to see.

Escort two's twenty knights went up off her aprons to meet them in pairs, as Ward had drilled them over the island all autumn. The first pair reached the first mage as his glyph came to its last line, and a charged blade went through the lit ring in the air the way a knife goes through a cobweb; it came apart in sparks, and the lightning it had been going to throw went nowhere. The mage drew another. The second knight cut that one. The mage looked at the two of them in their black plate and went back to his ship.

Every one of them was drawing. Emeric noted it far down, under everything else.

A second mage got his off from fifty yards at her forward gun deck, where the crews were hacking at the line on the turret roof. A knight had put himself in front of the deck. The bolt went into his plate and down his earthing strap and he dropped twenty feet and caught himself, with his left arm burned where the strap had not been quite enough. He came back up.

They cut the amidships line; Emeric felt it go like a thread snapping. The hull above let the cut end trail and fired another, and it bit into the plate by her bow. Three again.

"Ruan. The one above her to port. Can you lay it?"

"I can lay it." Ruan's voice, flat. "I can't fire it. She's under it."

She was. The white hull had put itself above escort two and a little behind her, so that anything the K Amara threw at it from where she hung would go through escort two's deck on the way. Somebody aboard it knew what the K Amara's guns could not do.

"Hold," he said.

Escort two's lift began to go: the bow stations thinning first, then amidships, and she began to settle nose first, the white hulls paying out their lines to let her down and keep hold of her at once. Selby was not fighting it. He could not. He was flying her down on what was still awake, keeping her level so that her crew stayed on her deck.

"Escort two," he said, to the K Amara. "Pin's gone. Lift's going, forward first. Three hooks in her and I can't get them out. I'm coming down." A pause. "I'll want somewhere to put a hundred people."

Then her inducer fired. It was built for a dead zone, not a drain; but the drain had emptied the little stone that kept her latch's bellows full, through the glass tube from the very tank it guarded, and the bellows had gone slack, and the latch did not know a dead field from a leech and did not care. The tank went through its horn into her stones all at once, and every station woke along her as if hot water had been poured into a cold bath, and for a breath she steadied.

And the hooks drank that too.

It bought her a slow fall. He felt her lift come back and go out again, slower, the inducer's mana going into her stones and up the lines nearly as fast as it went in; and she came down out of the Band toward the bank at a walking pace, level, with her knights round her in the air and her crew on her deck clipped to their lines.

"Hull four," said Emeric.

Hull four had already seen it. She came off the K Amara's starboard beam before the clerk had finished the group and went down across the mile on a long slant, and came in two hundred feet under escort two, matching her fall. She was a carrier. She had a deck like a market square.

"Abandon," said Selby, loudly, on his own deck. "On hull four. Go."

They went off escort two's rail in sticks, as Ward had drilled them into the bay all autumn, static lines clipped to the jackline; the lines pulled the chutes open one after another, white in the dusk, and they came down two hundred feet onto hull four's deck in a long untidy rain. Most of them hit it. Some hit it hard. Some went wide in the wind.

Ward had sent the K Amara's after apron. Emeric had not heard him do it. He felt them go, twenty of her hundred, flat out and low across the mile toward the chutes; and among them three notes close together, as they always were. Arthur. Mira. Bren.

A chute went wrong: a man whose canopy came out of its bag and did not open, a long white streamer over his head, and the man under it falling fast past hull four's deck edge with his arms going round. Mira came across under hull four's keel at a speed that turned Emeric's stomach and took him by the back of the harness with one hand, as Ruan had once taken Tolliver; the streamer wrapped round them both and they fell forty feet together in a tangle of silk before her nodes took the weight. Then she was climbing, slowly, heavily, with a man on her arm and his chute round her helm like a veil, and she set him down on hull four's apron, and he sat down on the plates and was sick.

Bren took two the wind had carried out toward the city, one in each hand. Arthur went down into the white after a woman drifting under her reserve toward the city, and was out of Emeric's sense for a long count, and came up with her.

Escort two's knights came last, with two from the gun deck who had to be carried. Her stone-keeper did not come. She would not leave her levers.

"She's taking her east," said Selby, from the empty tower. "She's four stations awake aft. She says if we're coming down, we're not coming down on the city."

Emeric had not thought of it. He had been counting chutes.

He felt the stone-keeper do it: four stations at the stern on what the hooks had left, and the held stones in their gimbals pushing sideways at half their lift; and escort two began to slide east, nose first, out from under the bank toward the outer wall and the dark where the river came round the back of the city. The white hulls let her. Their lines went with her. They were still drinking.

"Count," said Emeric.

The clerk read hull four's answer off the key. "Ninety-six on her deck. Two with our knights, coming in. Two still on escort two."

"Get them off," said Ward.

Two of escort two's own knights went in over her rail. Selby argued with one of them for exactly as long as it took the knight to clip a lanyard onto his harness. The stone-keeper did not argue. At the outer wall she lifted her hands off her levers and let them go, and the knights came up off the tower with the two of them hanging underneath; and escort two went on east with nobody aboard her, over the suburbs, out over the river.

"A hundred," said the clerk. "Hull four says a hundred. All."

The white hulls let go of her over the water. All three hooks came out of her at once, as if there were nothing left in her worth drinking, and the lines went whipping back up into the dark; and escort two, empty and unlit, with her stern stations dying one by one, came down through the last thousand feet of dusk, level, almost gently, onto the river east of the city below the bend.

He did not see it. Sorrel did.

"She's in the river," said Sorrel, after a while. "Below the last bridge. She went in flat. She's sitting on the bottom with her deck under and her tower out, and she's dark." A pause. "There's people coming out of the houses on the bank with lamps to look."

The tally clerk had stopped writing. He looked at his slate, and then wrote, slowly, under the two balls of bombard shot: Escort two. Then he looked up, not knowing what else to put.

"Crew, a hundred," said Emeric. "Off. Alive."

The clerk wrote it.

Imani's voice came up the horn from the K Endeavor, not in cipher; she never used it on the set when it was about a body. "Hull four's sending me nine. Two ankles. A wrist. A knight's arm, burned to the elbow; he says it's nothing, and it isn't. A woman with glass in her face from her own helm house, because Garrick's not the only one. And Garrick, whether he likes it or not." A pause. "Nobody's going to die of anything tonight that I've been told about. Tell me if that changes."

"I'll tell you."

"Tell me first," said Imani, and the horn went quiet.



It had gone dark while he was counting chutes.

The last of the light was out of the west, and over the bank the Band's thin air had gone from grey to blue to black, with more stars in it than anybody ever saw from the ground; and under the stars, on every side, the sixteen. Twelve hung where they had been, in a broken ring round the bank, a mile off and two. The four that had taken escort two came back up out of the east and took their places in it as if they had been out on an errand. Their lamps talked, long and short, from the big hull's stern to the four big ones and from them to the small; and Emeric could have drawn every one of them on the sand table by its lamps alone.

The fleet hung in the middle of them. Eight. The K Amara pinned, with two bites out of her shell stone. The K Endeavor and hull four off her beams, hull four's deck full of people sitting on the plates in their harness. Hull two in the K Endeavor's lee with her rail gone and her inducer spent. Escort one off the K Amara's port bow, alone, where there had been two. Escort three at the tail, back from the north. The K Discovery astern and above, with his father aboard. The Hod at the edge with the rods. All of them dark. Kaldo flew dark at night and always had; there was not a lit window among them but the glow of a dial behind glass here and there, and from a mile off they were eight black shapes against a black sky.

The Church knew where they were. Its ships could step across the sky while his were coming about, and he had no answer to grapples but distance; whenever they chose to close, they would close. He gave the order for distance anyway, because it was the order there was: a mile between hulls. The fleet began to open on its propellers, wide and slow, eight black shapes drawing apart like a hand opening.

The Church's lamps followed them. Long and short. Ship to ship.

"They have to talk," said Ward.

He said it once, quietly, looking at the ring of lamps.

"Every order the big one gives, it gives with a lamp. Every ship that hears it says so with a lamp. They can't move together without lighting up to do it." He was quiet. "We don't light anything. We never have."

Emeric looked down at the bank under the K Amara's keel: the floor the seeder had been laying since a quarter to four, white and wet and freezing along the Band's foot two miles over the city, a mile and more across, holding still because three tired seventeen-year-olds on the stern rail were holding it still. The stars stopped at its edge. Inside it there was nothing to see at all.

A lamp in a fog was a glow, and a hull that stepped sideways in a fog stepped into nothing it could see. Kaldo did not need to see. It had the radio and Ferra's groups, and it had him; and a ship riding the bank's edge, keel in the white and deck in the clear, could read every white hull by its stones and call them down the radio.

Which ship, and who aboard her, he would settle in a minute.

"Feather the propellers," he said. "Every hull. Held stones only, once we're in. Nobody stirs the cloud."

The clerk read it.

"Seeder to full. Tell the wind watch I want one more hour out of them, and then they can sleep till Lantern Night."

The clerk read it.

Then Emeric took the mouthpiece out of the clerk's hand, which he had not done once since the yard, and the clerk let him; and he said it himself, in his own voice, so that every set in the fleet heard it at once: the K Discovery and his father in his plain coat, and Selby among his people on hull four's deck, and Garrick on the K Endeavor's table with Imani picking glass out of his hand, and Sorrel on the end of her rope in the dark over a turnip field, and Ruan and Tolliver on the forward gun deck, clipped on.

"Lights out. We fight in the dark."



He let the pin go, and the K Amara came free of the sky and swung a little, gently, under her matrix; then her stones eased under his feet and she began to go down. The helmsman feathered her propeller, and the slow beat of its blades behind the tower slowed and stopped, and there was no sound aboard her but the steady humming in its box and the seeder's feed screw turning on the stern. She went down on her held stones alone, level, a foot at a time and then a yard, toward the white.

All round her in the dark the others went down with her: escort one on her port bow and escort three astern of her; the K Endeavor and hull four and hull two in a slow cluster to starboard; the Hod at the edge; and the K Discovery astern, coming down after her, with the king's note in her small and steady and awake.

Above them the ring of Church lamps went on talking.

The bank came up round the K Amara's keel. It came up her black sides, past her aprons, where the knights stood in their suits and dimmed their hearts one by one; past the forward gun deck, where Ruan stood with his hand flat on a breech and Tolliver beside him with his clip on the line; past the turrets. It came up the tower, and over the rail, and beaded on the iron and on the backs of Emeric's hands, very cold. The stars went out above it one by one and then all at once; and the last thing he saw before the white closed was the big hull's dome a mile and a half off, burning blue and gold and red like a church window with the sun behind it, and a lamp at its fin tip flashing long and short and long to somebody who could no longer read it.

Then there was nothing but white.
