Chapter 162 - The Dairy Page

The Seers' Table sent Pell to Milford because she was the only one of the twelve who could milk a cow.

That was the reason they gave, at any rate, in the long room, in a deputation of four to Emeric's office, with the boy from the dock quarter at its head and ink on his chin as usual. Maren had asked them a question two years ago at the kitchen door: why milk soured faster in summer than in winter, and faster in one dairy than another, when it came out of the same cow the same way. They had drawn milk under the glass every week since, fresh and old and sour, and they had found things in it, small things, rods and beads that moved, more of them every day the milk stood and many more on a warm day than a cold one; and they had drawn every one and dated it and labelled it, and they had got as far as they could get with milk that came up to the castle kitchen in a churn from a farm whose cows they had never seen.

"We want to see where it starts," said the boy from the dock quarter. "In the cow. And the bucket. And the dairy." He looked at the others. "Milford's got the best dairy in four baronies. Everybody says. Paxton's cheese."

"And Pell can milk," said one of the others.

Pell could milk. She was fifteen, a farm girl from the west side who had come to Sela's room late, at twelve, and caught up in a year, and she had milked her father's two cows every morning of her life before school; and she stood at the back of the deputation with her hands behind her and her face red and said nothing, because the others had decided all of this without asking her and she had found out on the stair.

"You'd go?" Emeric asked her.

She looked at him. Then she nodded, once, hard.

"I'll be at Milford the week after next," he said. "For the steadings. You can come up on the line with me."



The dairy was not Paxton's. That was the first thing Pell found out, and she wrote it down.

Paxton's cheese, which everybody in four baronies said was the best, did not come from the steward's house, or from any farm of the steward's. It came from a woman called Hester Mull, who had a stone dairy and a cheese cellar dug into the foot of the bench at the end of the bay, and twenty-two cows, and four girls who milked them, and who had sold every cheese she made to the barons of Milford for thirty years at a price the barons had set, and had let them call it theirs.

"He calls it his," said Hester, "because he's the one that sells it. I let him." She was in her forties, broad and red-armed and grey at the temples, with a man's leather apron over her skirt and her sleeves rolled to the shoulder, and she looked at Emeric in her dairy door with no curiosity at all, the way a woman looks at a man who has come to read the meter. "I make it. He sells it. We both know which of us is which." She looked past him at Pell, standing behind him with a satchel of paper and a box with the microscope in it. "What's that?"

"A microscope," said Pell. "For looking at your milk."

Hester looked at her a moment longer.

"Well," she said, "it's nothing to be ashamed of," and stood back out of the door.



Pell stayed four days. Emeric was at the steadings in the bay for three of them, with Paxton and the masons and the line's survey party, and came back to the dairy in the evenings, and each evening Pell had filled more paper than the evening before.

She had milked. She had milked every morning with Hester's girls at five, in the byre, and drawn what she found in the first spurt from every cow and the last, and in the bucket before it was used and after, and on the girls' hands, and in the strainer cloth, and in the cooling trough in the dairy where the milk stood in shallow pans in the stream water that ran through it; and in the cellar, where the cheeses sat on oak shelves in the dark, and the walls ran with wet, and the air smelled of Milford's best cheese, which was to say very strongly indeed. She drew everything. She labelled it, and dated it, and said where she had got it, in the square careful hand Sela taught.

And she talked. That was the thing the Seers' Table had from Ferra, without knowing it; Ferra's census, the year the whole barony was counted, house by house, when every child in Sela's room had gone round with a clerk and a slate and learned to ask every person in a house every question on the census sheet, whether they wanted to answer or not. Every one of the twelve asked everybody everything. Pell asked Hester's girls how long they had milked, and where, and for whom, and whether their hands hurt, and what they did for chapped skin, and what their mothers had done, and whether the cows were ever sick, and with what, and whether the girls caught what the cows had. She wrote down every answer. Hester's girls, who had never been asked anything by anybody but their mothers and the priest, told her all of it, and then a good deal more.

On the third evening she showed him the page.

It was not a drawing. It was what the Seers' Table called the column: a page at the back of every notebook where they wrote down what people said, in their own words, as near as could be got. Pell had written the girls' answers down it, one under another, with their names and ages. And three-quarters of the way down the page she had written a line, and then drawn a box round it in pencil, because she did not know what else to do with it.

The cows get blisters on their teats some summers. Little ones, that weep and crust. The girls catch them on their hands from the milking. Not bad. A sore or two on the fingers, a few days poorly, then it's gone. Every one of Hester's girls had had it, some summer or other. And every one of them, and every woman in the bay who had milked as a girl, had been asked by Pell about the bad year, the speckled year, nine years ago, when the pox came down the river and took a fifth of the bay and marked half of the rest; and not one woman who had had the cow blisters as a girl had caught the pox. Not one. Their brothers had. Their mothers had. They had nursed them. Not them.

"It's odd," said Pell. "Isn't it?" She looked at the box she had drawn. "I don't know what it means. I wrote it because it was odd." She looked up at him. "Mistress Mull said it. Not like it was anything. When I asked her about the pox. She said, oh, my girls never get the pox, they've all had the cow sores. Like you'd say the hens never lay in November." She put her finger on the box. "Then I asked all of them. And then the women at the steading. And it was true."

Emeric looked at the box.

He knew what it was. He had known it in the other life, the way every schoolchild in that life knew it, from a page in a history book with a drawing on it of a country doctor and a milkmaid and a cow: a doctor called Jenner, two hundred years before Marcus was born, who had heard a milkmaid say exactly what Hester Mull had said to Pell, and had believed her, and had taken matter from a cowpox blister on a milkmaid's hand and scratched it into a boy's arm, and then, weeks later, the smallpox; and the boy had not caught it. And out of that, in the end, the thing itself, the speckled death, gone from the whole of that world. Every child in a whole world, scratched on the arm.

He knew how to do it. He knew it the way the trial had put medicine into him, whole, with judgment: the matter from the blister, the lancet, the scratch, the fever after, the scar. He could have stood up in Hester Mull's dairy that evening and told Pell what she had found, and gone back to Kaldo and begun it.

He did not.

He sat at Hester's table with the page in front of him and the reasons came up one after another, and he looked at each of them the way he would have looked at a column of Ferra's, to see if it held. There was no pox in the south now. There had not been since the speckled year. Imani had never seen what lived in a wound or a well under the glass until two years ago, and she was only now, slowly, at her own pace, by her own trials, coming to believe that the small things moving in the Seers' Table's drawings were what made people sick; a cure she had not proved herself was a cure she would not stand behind, and nor should she. And the Church. He thought of the man in the grey coat at the receiver's table, and the clerk's thin face at the coach window looking back up the hill. A prince who scratched pus from a cow's teat into Kaldo's children's arms, on the word of a dead man in a world nobody had ever heard of, and called it medicine, was a prince the Church would have a word for, and the word would be the end of everything.

Every one of those reasons held. He looked at them all and they all held, and he sat at the table and did not like any of them, and did not like himself much either, and knew that a man who had seen a fifth of the bay die nine years ago would not have cared about any of them at all.

"Keep going," he said to Pell.

She looked at him.

"Keep asking," he said. "Every dairy you can get to. Every woman who milked as a girl. Whether she had the cow sores, and when, and whether she had the pox, and when. Write it all down. Every one." He gave her back the page. "And draw the blisters. On the cows and on the hands. Under the glass, if you can get any of the matter on a slide without anybody minding."

"Why?"

"Because it's odd," said Emeric. "And odd is where everything starts."

Pell took the page back and looked at the box she had drawn round the line. Then she put it in her satchel, carefully, at the front, where she would find it.



The fuzz was in the cellar, in pots.

Hester showed it to them on the last morning, because Pell had asked her about the cows' sore feet, and Hester had said they got no sore feet worth the name in her byre, and Pell had asked why, and Hester had looked at her for some while and then taken a lamp and gone down the cellar steps.

On a shelf at the back of the cellar, away from the cheeses, there was a row of small brown crocks with cloths tied over their tops. Hester took the cloth off one and held the lamp over it.

It was bread. Old bread, broken up, damp, in the bottom of the crock; and on the bread, thick and soft as velvet, a mould. Blue-green. The colour of the sea on a grey day, with a white rim round the edge of every patch where it was still growing, and a smell like a wet cellar and a damp book.

"When a cow cuts a foot on the stones," said Hester, "or tears a teat, or a girl cuts her hand on the strainer, I take a bit of that and lay it on the cut, and bind it. And it doesn't go bad." She put the cloth back. "Every other dairy in the bay, a cut goes bad and swells and stinks, and half the time the cow's lost or the girl's lost the finger. Not in mine." She looked at Emeric over the lamp, with the same incurious face. "My mother did it. Her mother. The priest says it's witchcraft. The women in the bay say I'm a witch. They still come and ask me for a bit of it when their husband's leg goes black." She shrugged. "And I give it them, and I tell them it's bread, because it is."

Emeric looked at the crocks.

That one, too. He had known that one longer, and better. A man called Fleming, in a laboratory, with a dish of something he had meant to throw away, and a ring round a blue-green spot where nothing else would grow. And all the rest of it after, which had taken that world twenty years and a war and great vats of broth to get out of a mould into a bottle; and none of which Kaldo could do, not this year, not for years. But a mould laid on a wound, crude, raw, the way Hester's mother had done it, would do some of it. Some. Sometimes. Enough that a dairywoman in a cellar had noticed.

"May she take some?" he said, and nodded at Pell.

Hester looked at Pell with the satchel and the box.

"Take what you like," she said. "It grows back."



Imani would not believe it.

She said so in the hospital's cutting room on the evening Pell came back, with the crock on the table between them and its cloth off and the blue-green velvet sitting in it under the lamp, and Pell's drawings of it beside it, and Emeric in the doorway. She had listened to Pell all the way through without interrupting, which she did not do for many people, and looked at every drawing, and at the crock, close, with her hands behind her back so as not to touch it.

"No," said Imani.

Pell looked at her.

"Not no, it doesn't work." Imani straightened. "No, I don't believe it. Those are different." She looked at the crock. "A woman in Milford says it works. Her mother said so. The women in the bay come for it when a leg goes black, and some of the legs don't go on going black, and they remember those, and they don't remember the ones that do." She turned to Pell. "That's not a reason. That's a story. Every remedy in the world has a story. Half the ones the priest's wife sells at the church door have a better one than this."

"Then how do you know?" said Pell. She said it hotly. Then she went red, at having said it to Imani, and did not take it back.

Imani looked at her. And something in Imani's face changed, Emeric saw from the door, the way it had changed six years ago in a lane in the low town when he had credited her map in front of the whole of Kaldo and she had not expected him to.

"You try it," she said. "Properly. Two lots of the same thing, alike in every way but the one. One with the mould. One without. And you count." She pulled a slate toward her and wrote on it with a stub of chalk, in her plain square hand: two columns, and a line between them. "In the first year, before you were in Sela's room, a man came to Kaldo and told me the wells were killing people. I didn't believe him either. I'd been the barony's healer for three years, and I'd watched them die, and I knew why they died, God's will and bad air and the low streets' sin, like everybody knew." She tapped the slate. "So I took two lanes. Alike. One drank from the well, one didn't. And I counted." She put the chalk down. "That's how I know. That's how anybody knows anything worth knowing. Not because somebody's mother said."

Pell looked at the slate.

"Not on people," said Imani. "Not yet. Not on anybody's leg. On the glass first, and on bread, and on broth, and on the things you've drawn growing in milk. Put the mould on a dish of broth that's going bad, and another dish going bad without it, and see which goes worse. Twenty dishes. A hundred." She looked at the crock. "If it does something, the glass will show it. If the glass shows it, I'll believe it. If I believe it, I'll try it on a cut. And if it works on a cut, I'll stand up in front of the priest and the bishop and the whole of the Church of the Light and say so, and they can call me what they like." She folded her arms. "But I'll not say it on a story."

Pell looked at her a while longer. Then she picked up the slate, with Imani's two columns on it, and put it under her arm with the crock.

"Twenty dishes," she said.

"A hundred," said Imani. "Twenty's a story too."

Pell went out past Emeric in the doorway without looking at him, with the crock held in front of her in both hands like a girl carrying a lamp, and he heard her go down the hospital stair fast, two at a time.

Imani looked at the doorway where she had gone.

"You knew," she said, without turning round. "About the mould. You knew before she did."

He did not answer at once.

"I know there's a story about it," he said at last. "In a book. A long way off. That's all I know." It was the truth, as far as it went. He had told her once, at the table, that he did not know how to say the rest. "And I know you won't believe it on a story."

Imani turned round and looked at him.

"No," she said. "I won't." She was quiet. "So you'll say nothing. And let a fifteen-year-old with a crock of bread and a slate find it out the slow way, with a hundred dishes."

"Yes."

"Because if she finds it, it's hers. And mine. And nobody can say it came out of a prince's head that he won't tell them about." She was not asking. She was laying it out, the way Ferra laid out a column. "And because if you're wrong, you'd rather she found that out than you told everybody it was true and some child died of believing you."

"Yes."

She looked at him a while longer, with the lamp between them.

"Keep going," she said. "That's what you told her?"

"That's what I told her."

"Good," said Imani. "So do I." And she turned back to the table and began to put the drawings away.



The milk was answered by the end of the month, the question Maren had asked at the kitchen door two years ago.

Pell answered it, with the rest of the twelve. The rods and the beads that the Seers' Table had been drawing in milk for two years were in Hester's milk too, in the first spurt from every cow and on the girls' hands and in the strainer cloth, few at first, and in the pans in the cooling trough they were few for longer, and in the pans in the warm byre they were many by noon. They grew. The warmer the milk, the faster. The colder, the slower. Hester's milk lasted longer than any in the bay because Hester's dairy had a stream running through it, cold off the bench, and Hester's girls scalded the buckets and the strainer every morning in water off the copper, as Hester's mother had made them, and nobody else in the bay did.

Pell wrote it up in a page, with drawings, and the Seers' Table took it to Maren in the castle kitchen in a deputation of all twelve.

Maren read it standing at her table with flour to the elbows. She read it twice.

"So it's cold," she said. "And clean."

"Cold and clean," said Pell. "And the colder the longer."

Maren looked at the page. Then she looked at the twelve of them crowded into her kitchen door.

"Two years," she said. "I asked you two years ago." She put the page down on the table, carefully, out of the flour. "My mother said cold and clean. Her mother said it." She looked at Pell. "Nobody ever told me why."

She gave them each a honey ring off the cooling rack, which was the highest honour Maren's kitchen had to give; and then she went out to the cold store under the north wall, that afternoon, and stood in it with her arms folded and looked at its shelves until the cold got into her arms, and came back and asked Emeric at supper whether there was any way of making a room colder than the cold store, in the summer, without ice.

"There might be," he said.

Maren looked at him over the stew pot with her eyes narrowed.

"You always say that," she said, "and then there is."



The dairy page went into the Seers' Table's book in September, bound with the rest of Pell's four days at Milford: the drawings, the column, and the line with the box round it.

The cow sores. The girls who never caught the pox.

Nobody at the Seers' Table did anything with it. It was odd, and they had written it down because it was odd, and there was nothing to do with it that any of them could see. The book went on the shelf in the long room with the others, between the volume on barm and the volume on the canal ring's water, and stayed there.

Emeric looked at it on the shelf sometimes, going past.
