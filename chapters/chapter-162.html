Chapter 162 - The Heap

Ferra had two figures on the page in front of her, one on the left and one on the right, and they were both correct, and they could not both be paid.

On the left was the heap.

It was the barony's muck. All of it. Every byre and stable and pigsty and privy and hen house on the island and in the farms round the lake and down the river road, every midden in every yard, and the ash out of every hearth, and the old straw off every floor. She had had it counted. It had taken Dace eleven days and a cart and a boy, and Dace had come back smelling of it and put the figure on her table without a word and gone to wash, and she had not said anything to him about the smell because the figure was very good.

On the right was what wanted it.

The fields wanted it. They always had. Every field in the barony had been dunged since there had been fields, and the farms took every load the byres made and would have taken more, and in the good years they grew enough and in the bad years they did not. That was the first claim, and it was the oldest one in the book, and Hollis sat across the table from her with his one good hand flat on his own sheet of paper and read it off to her in his slow careful voice as if she did not know it.

And the beds wanted it.

The beds were behind the old barn beyond the foundry, the long thatched heaps on beaten clay that the prince had seeded the spring before last with a shovel of Grayrock crust each and told nobody why. She had entered them as beds, behind the barn, and under that Three years, because that was what he had told her, and she had not asked him what they were. She knew what they were now. Everybody at the table knew. The beds were where the white salt grew, slowly, out of dung and urine and straw and ash turned over weekly in the dark, and the white salt was the one thing between Kaldo and the powder that there was no other way to get.

They had been growing for a year and a half on what the handcart bought at a Copper a basket. That had been enough for small beds. It was not enough now. The prince wanted the beds tripled this autumn, and he wanted them fed.

"Four thousand one hundred loads," said Hollis. "Near enough. That's what the barony makes in a year, with the new people. And the fields took four thousand last year, and wanted five."

"The beds want nine hundred," said Ferra.

"I know what the beds want. You told me." Hollis did not look up. "Nine hundred loads off the fields is nine hundred loads of dung not on them. I've worked it at what a load does on an acre of wheat, the way my father worked it and his, and it's a poor way but it's the only way we've got." He turned his sheet round. "That's two hundred and twenty acres not dunged. On the lake farms that's a fifth of the bread corn. It won't fail. It'll come in thin. A thin year's no famine. But you'll be buying in grain from somewhere by the spring, with a camp of two thousand men on the flat to feed and their families in the lean-tos."

"I know."

"I've written it down," said Hollis. "So nobody says nobody said."

It was what he said at every table now. He had said it about the harvest bounty in the spring. Ferra did not mind it. It was the most useful sentence anybody at the table said, and she had begun, privately, to write it at the foot of her own sheets.

Paxton had written in the second week of autumn.

He had written himself, in his own hand, not his factor's, which he did perhaps twice a year. Milford had cattle. Milford had more cattle than any barony in the south, and Milford killed them every autumn before the grass went, and Milford had heaps of bone behind its slaughter yards that nobody had any use for but the dogs. It was known, Paxton wrote, that bone crushed and spread did the land good, and was nearly as good as dung on corn ground. Milford would sell Kaldo's farms crushed bone. By the ton. Carted to the Kaldo road.

At seven Copper the hundredweight.

Ferra read it twice. Then she worked it, because it was her work to, and it came out at a little under three times what the bone was worth, carted, anywhere in the south.

She took it to the prince at the long-room table with everybody there, and read the figure out, and read the price, and said nothing about the difference.

"He's making a point," said Saban.

"He is." The prince looked at the letter in Ferra's hand. "He's been making it since the notice. The debt clause. Every pound of that price over what the bone's worth is a line he's writing against it."

"He could just write the line," said Ward.

"He's Paxton," said Saban. "He'd rather sell it."

"It's good bone," said Hollis. "I've seen it. His father sold it to mine."

The prince was quiet for a moment. He sat with his hands flat on the table and looked at Ferra's two figures, the left and the right, which she had copied out fair for him that morning on one sheet, and she watched him read them, and watched him know what he was going to say before he said it, and knew what it was.

"The beds get the heap," he said. "All nine hundred. The fields get Paxton's bone, at his price, as much of it as we can pay for, and whatever else Hollis can find." He looked at Hollis. "It won't be enough."

"No," said Hollis.

"Then it won't be enough. I'm not going to pretend it will." The prince looked back at the sheet. "The beds are two years from giving anything. If we starve them now they're four. I'd rather buy grain in the spring than wait two more years for the one thing that can't be bought."

Hollis did not argue. He folded his sheet in four and put it in his coat.

"The fields'll want to hear it from you," he said. "Not from me."

"They'll hear it from me."

Ferra wrote it. The heap: to the beds, nine hundred loads, by order at table. The fields: Milford bone at Paxton's price, seven Copper the hundredweight, to a ceiling of, and she wrote a figure she did not like, and looked at it, and left it. Then she wrote the date.

She could not keep the census.

She had known it since the winter, since the six hundred and twelve names at the gate and the number she could not tell nearer than five thousand. She had said it at the table then. It had got worse since, not better.

It had got worse because of the flat. The rail works had taken its men after harvest, as the prince had said, and they had come in from all four baronies with the harvest bounty in their pockets, a hundred and forty of them. And the flat had taken men all summer, and was taking more now that the frost was near and the footing had to close: two thousand men on it, near enough, by the tally-sticks at the quarry. And every one of those men had come with somebody. A wife. A mother. Four children. A brother who had heard there was work and come to look. They lived in the lean-tos along the foundry bank, and in the camp that had grown up on the thorn ground at the north edge of the flat, where the prince had put in drains and a pump before he let anybody pitch a tent, and some of them lived on the island in rooms that let now at two Silver the month.

She had three clerks. Hobb, who wrote the best copy of her hand in Kaldo and was needed for everything. Wenna, who was seventeen and came top in every sum and was needed for everything else. Dace, who was slower than either and never wrong, and who had spent eleven days in the autumn counting dung.

Three clerks could keep a town of two thousand. They had. They could not keep a town of five thousand and a camp of five thousand more that moved every week, and she did not have a fourth, and every morning she did not have one the book fell a day further behind the ground.

She was sitting with the census book open at the page with no heading on the evening Ilsa came up the stair.

Ilsa had come down from Grayrock on the steamboat that afternoon with the month's tallies. She did that now, most months, since her mother had given her the tally house and Ferra had begun sending the month's queries back up with her instead of by the post. She was in her twenties now, Bryda's daughter in every way that counted, square-shouldered and plain-spoken and with a face that did not give anything away until it had decided to. She had been on Kaldo's books since the day of Grayrock's settlement, when she was fifteen and had stood beside her mother and asked the prince what would happen to her, and she had never once sent the Chancellor anything that did not add up.

She put the Grayrock packet on the table. Then she did not go.

"I'll have the census," she said.

Ferra looked up.

"The new people," said Ilsa. "The camp, and the foundry bank, and the island. I've watched your clerks at the gate. They take a name and a place and look up and there's another cart." She did not sit down. "It wants a room of its own, and a book of its own, and somebody who does nothing else. Walking. Every lane, every lean-to, every tent on the flat, once a month, with a list of who was there last month and a pencil. Not a clerk at a gate. Somebody who goes and looks."

"And that's you."

"That's me." Ilsa said it without any particular weight. "I've kept the tally house two years. I know how to count things that move. Ore carts move more than people. And I know what a man looks like when he's giving a name that isn't his." She looked at the census book, open on the table. "My mother can keep the tally house. She kept it before I did."

Ferra looked at her for a long time.

She knew what Ilsa was asking for, and she knew what Ilsa was not asking for, and she knew that Ilsa knew both. Ilsa was not asking to be Ferra's second. There was no such post. There had never been such a post, because there had never been anybody Ferra would give the map to, and Ilsa had not asked to see the map. Ilsa had asked to do a piece of work that nobody was doing, and do it properly.

It was hands. It was very good hands.

"There's a room over the old weigh house," said Ferra. "It's damp. It'll want a fire."

"I'll light one."

"You'll report to me. Everything. Every month." Ferra closed the census book on its ribbon and pushed it across the table. "This is the harvest census and the gate book and the page with no heading. They're all wrong. You'll find out how wrong. You'll write it in a new book, and I'll read it, and when there's something in it I don't like I'll ask you about it, and you'll know the answer."

"I will."

"And you don't get a key," said Ferra.

Ilsa did not ask to what. She looked at the book in front of her, and then at Ferra, and nodded once, the way her mother nodded, and picked up the book and put it under her arm.

"To nothing," said Ferra. "Not the strongbox, not the box, not the fund, not the keep's books. You get a room and a fire and a book and the right to walk into any tent in the barony and ask who's in it. That's all."

"That's all I asked for," said Ilsa.

It was. That was what Ferra liked about her. She watched her go down the stair with the census book under her arm, and heard her stop at the foot of it and ask the girl on the door where the weigh house was, and go out.

Then she sat and looked at the place on the table where the book had been, and found that she felt lighter by exactly one book, and no lighter than that at all.

The House's man had come in the late summer, as Vantic had said he would.

He had come up the Kaldo road in a hired carriage with two clerks and a chain of his own, a grey quiet man called Aurel who had been the House's surveyor of pledges for twenty years and had walked every piece of ground the House had ever lent against in the south. He had walked the flat. He had walked the whole of the footing line with his own chain, the whole circle, in four days, in the heat, and checked it against the Tarrants' book, and found it good to within a hand. He had walked the quarry and the kilns and the rail works and the mill yard, and looked at the converter blowing for a quarter of an hour without speaking, and asked to see the rolled plate, and been shown it, and put his hand on it.

He had not been shown the lake shed. He had not asked.

He had gone home. And now, in the autumn, the House lent.

Vantic drew the instrument.

She drew it at the long-room table over three days, with Ferra across from her and the two of them alone at the table's end for most of it, apart from Hobb at the door, and Saban coming and going, and the prince at the head for the parts that needed him. It was the largest instrument Ferra had ever read. It was the largest Vantic had ever drawn, and she said so, once, in passing, in the voice she used for weather.

It lent against the works and the flat.

The first draw was a hundred and twenty Platinum, against the rail works and the mill yard and the converter and the plate, and the survey and the quarry and the kilns and the footing, at one part in fifteen a year, drawn by the season as the work went on and not before, with the House's man to walk the work at each draw and see that it was there. It could not be drawn against the island. It could not be drawn against the soap or the paper or the lane. It could not be drawn against anything that was not being built.

"That's the House protecting itself," said Ferra.

"That's the House protecting you," said Vantic. "If the city fails, the House takes a quarry and some trenches. It doesn't take the island. It doesn't take the soap. You'll still be here."

"And if the city doesn't fail?"

"Then the House owns a good deal of paper against the best-built town in the south," said Vantic, "and is very pleased with itself, and lends you the second draw at one part in eighteen." She turned a page. "They've done it before. Not often. Not for anybody I've drawn for."

They went through it clause by clause. Ferra had expected that, and had cleared three days for it, and it took all three, and she enjoyed every hour of it more than she had enjoyed anything since the spring.

It was how they talked. It was the only way they had ever really talked, in seven years, since the first loan: across a table, over a clause, with a pen. Vantic drafted a line and Ferra read it and found the place where it would bend if somebody leaned on it, and said so, and Vantic looked at it and either agreed and redrafted it or explained why it would not bend, and was usually right. And sometimes Ferra drafted a line and Vantic found the bend in hers. They did not raise their voices. They did not praise each other. On the second afternoon Vantic found a place in Ferra's schedule of draws where a season's slip would put the whole of the next draw a year late, and pointed at it with the end of her pen, and Ferra looked at it for a long time and then laughed out loud, which she did not often do at a table, and Vantic very nearly smiled.

And under every clause there was the thing Vantic would not say.

Ferra had known it was there for seven years. She did not know what it was. She had never asked. But it sat under everything Vantic drew, the way a stone sits under a field and the plough goes round it without anybody deciding to: a care in the drafting that was not for the House and not for Kaldo. Some clauses were drawn tighter than the House needed. Some were drawn looser than the House would like. There was a pattern in which, and Ferra had seen the pattern for years and could not read it, because she did not know what it was a pattern of. Something far off, with reach. Something Vantic was drawing round.

On the third day, near the end, Vantic stopped turning pages.

She had Ferra's book open in front of her, the barony's book, at the page for Grayrock. She had the right to read it. She had had the right since the first loan, and read it every quarter day, and copied what the House needed and nothing else. She had her finger on a line.

It was the gravity line.

Stone raised at Grayrock at the spring raising, in pounds. Stone sold out of the barony under the covenant, in pounds, with the Crown's share against it. And a third figure under those two, which had been there since midsummer. Kept, in the barony. And beside it, in Ferra's hand, Set, in hull two.

The first figure and the second did not agree. They did not agree by some hundreds of pounds. The third explained the difference exactly.

Vantic looked at it for a while.

"This is kept," she said. It was not quite a question.

"Kept in the barony," said Ferra. "Raised at Grayrock, in Kaldo's barony, and not sold out of it. The covenant binds stone sold. It doesn't bind stone kept. It never has."

"And hull two."

"Is a hull," said Ferra. "On Fletcher's slip."

Vantic looked at the line a moment longer. Then she looked at Ferra, directly, across the table, for perhaps as long as it takes to draw a breath.

She did not write anything down.

She had her pen in her hand. She had written down everything she needed from Ferra's book every quarter day for seven years, in her own small neat hand, in her own book, and taken it back to the House. She sat now with her pen over her own page and did not write. Then she turned the barony's book back to the page they had been working from, and went on with the clause about the season's slip, as if she had never stopped.

Ferra did not say anything either. She noticed, and entered it in the place she entered things like that, which was nowhere but her own head.

Two other things went into the book that autumn in one line each.

The first was a machine in the works yard, beside the new cloth shed the prince had put up for the camp's women. It was a frame of wood as tall as a man, with a big wheel on one side and a row of eight spindles on the other, and a carriage that ran along a rail, and a girl could turn the wheel with one hand and draw the carriage back with the other and spin eight threads at once where her mother had spun one. Bram's carpenter made it from the prince's drawing in a week, out of wood and string and some bent wire. The women called it the jenny, after the carpenter's wife, who had been the first to make it work. Ferra entered it as Spinning frame, eight spindles, for the camp cloth, at a cost of eleven Silver, and did not write what she thought, which was that eight women in the cloth shed now did the work of sixty-four, and she did not know yet what the other fifty-six would do.

The second was a keel.

Fletcher laid it on the second slip at the lower water, beside hull two, in the last week of the autumn. It was a coaster's keel, the same as the Emberfell's, long and straight, of the converter's bar. His yard men had watched him lay hull two's keel in the summer and been told it was a second coaster, and now they watched him lay this one and were told it was a third. They believed it. There was no reason for them not to. There were two keels on two slips side by side at the lower water, and the yard men were building two coasters.

Ferra entered it as Keel laid, coaster, second slip. It was true. It was the only one of the two lines about the lower water that autumn that was entirely true, and she noticed that too.

She sat at the long-room table on the last evening of the autumn after everybody had gone.

Not everybody. Hobb was at the door with his coat on, waiting to walk her down, because it was dark and the lanes were full. And the prince was at the far end of the table, at the head, with the survey sheets for the first quarter's mains spread out in front of him under a lamp, working, not looking up. He had been there when she sat down. He was still there. At some point in the last hour he had got up and gone out and come back with a cup of something hot from the kitchen, and put it down by her elbow without saying anything, and gone back to his sheets. She had not looked up either. The cup was still there, half drunk.

She had the book open at the heap.

The page for the autumn's allocation. The barony's muck, in loads, by source, in Dace's count. The beds, nine hundred loads, by order at table. The fields, the rest. Paxton's bone at seven Copper the hundredweight, to the ceiling she had not liked. Hollis's two hundred and twenty acres. Hollis's sentence, which she had written at the foot of the sheet in her own hand, because it was true: So nobody says nobody said.

She read it through once more.

Then she dipped her pen and wrote one more line under the allocation, in the same hand as everything else, at the same speed, the way she would have written the price of a barrel of iron salt.

Bread, less.

She looked at it. She did not cross it out. She sanded it and closed the book, and picked up the cup, which was cold, and drank the rest of it anyway, and looked down the table at the prince, who had not looked up, and who she thought had known exactly what she was writing.
