Chapter 222 - The Long Room

The records went first, because Ferra said so, and because they were the only thing in the keep that could not be made again.

They went in the second week of the spring, in closed carts, under Osric and six of the company, forty loads of them over five days: the rolls and the ledgers and the day books, the census before Ilsa's and the tallies before the census, the war record, the Staple's books, every letter the barony had written and every letter it had kept, the whole of Kaldo on paper from the week the prince came to the week before, packed in crates lined with oilcloth and roped and sealed with Ferra's seal on every knot. They went over the Left Bridge and out along the road across the flat and in at the east gate, and up the avenue to the palace, and round to the back of it, to the stone rooms.

The stone rooms had been waiting for them for three years. They lay in the palace's great stone platform, under its floors, forty vaults in two rows off a long passage, every one of them stone to the floor, stone to the ceiling, stone shelves in stone bays and an iron door on each, with not a stick of timber in any of them anywhere, not a lintel, not a peg, not a shelf. The prince had drawn them that way on the first sheet of the palace, before there was a palace, and had told the table why in one sentence: a book is only as safe as the room it's kept in, and a room with no wood in it cannot burn. Ferra had not needed telling twice. She walked every vault herself before a single crate went in, with a lamp stone in her hand, and put her palm flat on the stone of every shelf, and in two of them she found damp, and the crates for those two went back on the carts and waited a week while Hewitt's men found where the water was coming from.

Then the counting room, Ferra's own, which was the room at the end of the east range on the ground floor with windows on two sides and a stove that did not smoke, and her table under the south window and Hobb's and Dace's and Wenna's in a row down the wall. Then the courts, which were two rooms on the west side with benches and a railed floor and a table for the clerk. Then the treasury, which was a room inside a room, the inner one with an iron door and no window and a strongbox Bram had made that three men could not lift. Then the printing office, which did not go into the palace at all, but into a long shed built against its north range, where the presses could thump all day and nobody under the drum would hear them.

Ilsa's census had been there since the autumn, in its room on the east range with her name painted on the door. She stood in her doorway every morning of the move with her arms folded and watched the rest of the barony come to join her, and said to Ferra, on the third day, that it was very like watching a family come home one at a time to a house where the youngest had been living on her own.

The table went last.

There had been a question about it, for most of the winter. The palace had a room for the long-room table, the great round room at its heart under the drum, which the drawing had always called the long room because the prince had called it that the day he drew it, although it was round. There were other tables that would go in it. Weekes had had one made, in the joiners' shop on the flat, of new oak, long and fine, with the city's plan inlaid in its top in holly and walnut, as a gift, and had brought it to the prince on a cart in the winter with some pride.

The prince had thanked him, and meant it, and had it put in the courts.

Because nobody at the long-room table would sit at a different one. Nobody said so. It was not the kind of thing that got said. But when it was put to the table, in the winter, whether the old one should go out to the palace or stay in the keep for Armand, there had been a silence of a particular kind, the kind that means a room has already decided and is waiting for somebody to say it. Bram said it. He said it was a good table and he had mended its left leg twice and he was not starting again with one he had not mended. Imani said she had done more of her real work at that table than at any table in the ward, and she would like to be at it when she was old. Ferra did not say anything. She looked at the place in front of her own chair, where the wood was worn smooth and pale in a patch the size of an open book, from years of an open book lying on it, and did not say anything, and that was the end of the question.

So the table went out whole, on a dray, on the last day of the move.

It would not go down the keep stair. Bram had known that, and had measured it, and it had to go out of the long room's window. They took the window out, frame and all, and slung the table from a gantry on the wall walk outside, and let it down forty feet into the keep yard on ropes, with Bram at the gantry and eight men on the ropes and the whole of the keep's household standing in the yard below with their faces turned up, and the prince among them, not breathing much. It came down slowly, turning a little on the ropes, black against the sky, a long old table with a mended leg and no top on it but its own worn wood, and when it touched the cobbles somebody in the yard let out a breath that was nearly a cheer.

Then they lifted it onto the dray, and roped it, and the ox team took it out of the keep gate and down the hill, and across the island, and over the Left Bridge.

The island came out to watch it go. Not many; there were not many left to come. But Tam Brewer came up from the water steps, and old Mistress Aggett on his arm, and Holm the baker in his apron, and the widow Eames from Dock Street, and a dozen others, and stood along the market street under the gas lamps in the grey spring morning and watched a table go past on a dray. Nobody said anything. The dray went down to the bridge, and across it, and out onto the road, and the island stood and watched it go until it was a small dark shape on the road across the flat, and then went home.

It took most of the day to get it to the palace. It went in at the east gate and up the avenue with half the first quarter walking beside it, and round to the palace's south porch, and in through the porch's great doors, which were wide enough for it, because the prince had drawn them to be, and across the hall, and into the round room at the heart of the palace under the drum.

They set it down in the middle of the floor, and stood back, and looked up.

The drum went up round them in a ring of dressed stone, fifty feet across and forty feet high, pierced with tall windows all the way round at the top, and above the windows, where the dome would one day go, there was nothing. There was a ring of stone against the sky, and the sky, white and grey and moving, and a crow going across it. The light came straight down onto the table from above, as it would come into a well. The round room had never had a roof and would not have one for a year. Weekes had tarred canvas ready to rig over it in the rain. Today it was not raining, and the old table stood in the middle of the stone floor under the open sky with its mended leg and its worn places, and looked very small, and exactly right.

"Well," said Bram.

It was what Fletcher had said, at the head of the dock, in the autumn. Emeric noticed that. He did not say so.

He went back to the keep that night, alone, on a horse, because he had one more night in it and he meant to spend it there.

His own rooms were already empty. The trunk had gone, and the books, and the bed; there was a camp bed in the second room under the window where the trunk had been, with a blanket on it, and nothing else. He did not go into them. He went up the stair to the long room with a lamp stone in his hand, and pushed the door open, and stood in the doorway.

It was bigger without the table.

That was the first thing. He had thought of it for years as a room the size of a table, because it had always been full of the table, and the chairs round it, and the people in the chairs, and the papers on it and the maps pinned on the floor round it and the benches stacked against the wall. Now it was bare boards from wall to wall, and the hearth at one end with the fire out, and the window at the other end with its frame back in and a new pane in it where the old one had cracked coming out, and the lamp stone's light in his hand lying on the floor in a pale circle and not reaching the corners.

He walked into the middle of it, where the table had been. There were marks on the boards: four pale squares where the legs had stood for years, and a long dark band round them where the chairs had gone back and forth. One of the four squares was not square. It was a wider pale patch, a hand broader than the others, where Bram had put a block under the left leg the second time he mended it and the block had been there ever since.

He stood in the middle of the four marks for a while.

Most of it had happened here. Not all of it. The wells and the pit and the foundry and the ship and the island had happened in other places, a long way from this room, in the dark and the rain and the cold. But most of what was decided had been decided here, at that table, with Ferra's book open in front of her and Ward in his chair by the door. The water. The war. The loan, and its terms, and the day Vantic came up the stair with her strongbox. The rule that nothing on the flat went up above ground until what went under it was laid. Every Monday of Ilsa's figures. The night he told them about the band. The night he told them about the delve, one at a time, with his hand on their wrists. Imani telling him no, about bodies, again and again, and being right. Ferra writing it down.

He did not make a speech to it. There was nobody to make one to, and it would not have been true. He only stood in the pale squares with the lamp and let the room be a room, for a while, before it stopped being theirs.

Then he walked round it once, along the walls, the way he walked a thing he was about to hand over.

On the wall by the door, at the height of a man's shoulder, there was a torch bracket.

It was iron, black, a plain ring on an arm, with a stain of old soot on the stone above it. It was empty. He had seen it on his first day in the keep. He remembered seeing it: the entry hall sized for a household that no longer existed, and half the torch brackets in the building empty, and a corridor where there had plainly been pictures once and now there were only the brackets. He had walked into this room behind Hale on his first evening, when it was a cold dark hall with a table in it and nothing else, and seen that bracket by the door, empty, and thought something about it that he no longer remembered, something about payrolls and what an empty bracket told you about a house.

Nobody had ever put a torch in it. There had been no need. Within a year the room had been lit by lamp stones, and then by more of them, and then by the glass lamps from the works, and nobody had ever needed a torch in the long room again. So the bracket had stayed on the wall by the door, empty, for all of it. Every meeting, every argument, every Monday. He must have walked past it ten thousand times. He had not looked at it once since the first evening.

He put his hand on it. It was cold, and a little loose in its seat, and there was soot on his fingers when he took them away.

He thought about taking it down. It would come out of the wall with a twist; the mortar round its pin had gone to dust years ago. He could take it out to the palace and put it on the wall of the round room, by the door, and nobody would know why, and it would be the kind of thing a man does.

He left it where it was. It was Armand's wall now, and the bracket had been empty on it before Emeric came and would be empty on it after, and there was nothing he could carry four miles in his hand that would be worth more than leaving it. He wiped the soot off his fingers on his coat, and went out, and shut the door, and went down the stair to the camp bed, and slept, badly, for the last time in the keep.

Armand came up the hill in the morning with his students behind him, forty-one of them, in a straggle, carrying their own books.

He stopped in the keep yard and looked up at the keep for a long time. It was a good plain square building of grey stone with a tower at one corner, and it had been built for a barony twice the size of Kaldo, and it had been the barony's heart since before there was a barony. Now it was his. The hall, where the long room was, would be his teaching room, with his bench under its windows, where the light was best in the mornings. The rooms off the hall would be his students' workrooms. The prince's old rooms would be his own. And the students would lodge in the empty houses of the upper streets, on the list with Armand's name at the top of it, the houses under the keep that Ilsa had sealed street by street all through the summer and autumn and swept and left, and that would now, in the order the prince had drawn, be unsealed again and aired and lived in by boys and girls from all four baronies who could lift earth and hold water and call a spark out of a stone.

"There's one thing," Emeric said, at his shoulder.

"I know," said Armand.

"The forge stays at the works. Across the water. And the stones I use." He did not say which stones. "And the other work. The work I do that you've seen and your students haven't. That goes to the palace with me. There'll be nothing in this building you couldn't show the Circle tomorrow if they came down the road and asked to see everything."

Armand did not look at him. He looked at the keep.

"I know," he said again. "I'd have asked for it that way if you hadn't." He was quiet for a moment. "A mage who lives beside a thing he mustn't see can always be asked what he saw. A mage who lives four miles from it can only be asked what he's been told." He put his hand on the gatepost. "And I've been told very little. I've been very careful to be."

He went in. His students went in after him, one by one, through the keep door, looking up at the hall's roof and round at its walls and at each other, forty-one of them, with their books, and one of them, a Wayford girl of about fifteen, stopped at the door of the long room and looked at the bare boards and the four pale squares on the floor and asked Armand what had been there.

"A table," said Armand, and went past her to the windows to see how the light fell.

The founding stone stayed where it was.

It stood on the flat inside the new city, on its plot off the north ring, where Armand had laid it in the autumn the wall closed, with the words he had cut into it himself and the forty names under them. There was a building round it now. It was not the building the drawing had shown; that one had been a tower, and the tower had gone to the island, to the keep. This was smaller. A long low house of three rooms in the Walker's grey, with Hewitt's pale quoins at its corners and a yard behind it walled for practice, and the founding stone set into the front wall beside the door where anybody going in would pass it.

It was a school for children. For the ones Tessaly and Paskin found each year among the families on the flat and in the four baronies, who could do the thing without being taught: the girl who warmed her hands on a cold stone until it was warm, the boy whose well-bucket never spilled, the one Rafe had been once, sweeping an etching floor at eleven and lighting stones in his fist by accident. They would come to the long low house off the north ring and be taught their letters in the morning and their first workings in the afternoon, and be fed at midday, and go home at night to their families in the new city. And when they were old enough, and had passed up, they would cross the Left Bridge to the keep on the island, to Armand, and the tower would take them.

Two schools, one road between them. For now. On the drawing of the island that the prince had shown the table in the winter, the long low house was not on the flat at all. It was on the island too, on a street by the water, a lower school next door to the upper one. But that was the drawing, and the drawing was twenty years. For now it was here, off the north ring, where the families were, and where the children could walk to it.

Tessaly took two rooms at the back of it. She did not ask anybody. She moved her books and her bed into them in the week of the move, and when Ferra asked her about it she said that somebody had to be in the building at night with a yard full of children who could set things on fire, and that she had taught Rafe for a year and could teach twenty, and that it was two streets from the palace, which was convenient, and she did not say for what.

Zale's letter came in the third week of the spring, by the Emberfell boat, in a packet from Rowena.

It was the first since the island. It had been written, as far as Emeric could tell from the state of the paper, somewhere very wet, and a long time ago, and it had come a long way in a great many hands before it reached the queen of Emberfell, who had sent it on under her own seal with a note of her own that said only: He is well. He was well when he wrote it, which is all anybody can ever say of Zale. R.

Zale's letter was longer. Not much.

Brother. I have found the town. The one he sailed from, our friend with the book. It took me a winter to find it and it is not on any chart you own and I doubt it is on any chart anybody owns, and it rains here the way the sea does it, sideways and from underneath. They remember him. Not his name, or not a name I can write in your letters, but there is an old woman who keeps the quay who says a man came down to the harbour with a book like mine when she was a girl and asked for a boat east and was given one and never came back, and that her mother told her so. That is all I have yet. It is a great deal more than I had.

I am not coming home yet. I think you knew that. There is a thing I want to see here first, a stone the old woman says is in the hills above the town with writing on it, and it will take me the summer to get there and back, and I will not get another chance. When I have seen it I will come. Keep the book safe. Keep the girl with the pencil safe; I think she is the only one of you who writes anything down properly. Z.

Emeric read it twice and gave it to Ferra, who read it once and put it in the box, in the place where she kept the book's papers, beside her ruled sheets of Zale's readings. She did not say anything about the last line. She did not need to. She wrote the date it came, and by Emberfell, and then, after a moment, under it, not coming home yet, as a line in the book, because that was what it was.

His own rooms in the palace were on the first floor of the east range, looking inward.

He had drawn them that way. Every other room in the palace looked outward, over the rings and the city and the wall and the flat. His looked in, across a narrow gallery, through a tall window in the drum's wall, into the round room where the table stood. From his desk, if he sat back, he could see the table below him in the middle of the stone floor, and above it the drum going up, and above the drum the sky. He sat there on his first evening in them, with the window open and the spring coming in, and watched the light go off the top of the drum's stone and the sky go blue and then darker blue over the ring, and the first stars come out in it, one and then another, directly over the long-room table.

Then he took a sheet of paper, and drew.

He drew triangles. Not many kinds; three, and then, when he had looked at them for a while, a fourth. Flat triangles with their edges measured in Bram's master foot, each one a little different from the next in the length of one side, so that a great many of them, laid edge to edge, would bend round into a shape. He did not draw the shape. He drew the triangles, each on its own, with its sides marked and a note beside it in his own hand: glass, clear, Orrin's new batch, cut to template, twenty of this. Forty of this. Sixty. And at the foot of the sheet, the edges ground true.

He sent it down to the glassworks in the morning, by Hobb, with a note to Orrin that said: To this sheet exactly. No questions yet. E.

Orrin sent back one line by the same boy. How many in all?

Emeric looked up through his window, into the round room, at the open ring of the drum and the sky over it, and did the sum.

He did not answer that day.

Armand hung no sign on the keep. He did not paint a name over the door or cut one into the lintel or have one put on the gate. He said that a place was what people called it, and that if they called it the wrong thing no sign would stop them, and if they called it the right thing no sign was needed.

The first student to come across the Left Bridge on the first morning, a Grayrock boy of sixteen with a satchel and no idea where he was going, stopped a man at the Western Gate and asked him the way to the mages' school.

"The tower?" said the man, who was Pollard, on the gate. "Up the hill. You can't miss it."

So it was the tower. By the end of the week everybody on the island called it that, the dozen households that were left and the forty-one students and the watch on the gate. By the end of the month everybody in the new city did too. Armand heard it the first time from a baker's boy on the market street, and stopped, and looked back up the hill at the square grey keep with its one corner tower that had been the heart of the barony for longer than there had been a barony, and said nothing, and went on down to the bridge.
