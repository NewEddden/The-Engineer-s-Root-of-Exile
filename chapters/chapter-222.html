Chapter 222 - The Licence

The crown's inspector came with the licence, on the second of April, on the ship's public run from Wayford, with a leather case and a box of seals and a cold.

He was a young man from the chancery, thin and polite and very pale, who had never been further south than Wayford and had been sick over the rail the whole way, and he stood on the slip below the bridge and looked at the works across the river with its chimneys and the wall with its lamps and the town going up the hill in its white-plated lanes, and blew his nose, and said, to nobody in particular, "I was told it was a village."

"It was," said Ferra. "Have you eaten?"

He had not. She took him up the hill and fed him in the counting room and read the licence while he ate, every line, with her pencil, and then read it again. It was three sheets on the chancery's heavy paper with the Lieutenant's seal: the house of Kaldo might make and keep powder for its mines and its works, by weight, so much a year and no more, the figure written out in words and in numbers both; every pound of it to be entered in a book kept for the purpose, and the book open to the crown's inspector at any hour; and the powder that the house of Kaldo held for arms, of whatever kind, to be sealed in its armoury under the inspector's seal against the day the crown should call for it.

"Of whatever kind," said Ferra.

"Yes, Chancellor." The inspector looked up from his soup. "Cartridges. Loose powder. The, I'm told, eggs." He said the last word very carefully, as a man handles a thing he has heard about and does not believe in. "Everything with powder in it, Chancellor, that is meant to go into a man rather than into a rock."

"Then we'll start this afternoon," said Ferra, and wrote the figure from the licence at the head of a new book, ruled, and turned it round so that he could see she had copied it right.



It took three days. The cartridges came out of the armoury's racks a box at a time and were counted on a trestle in the yard, box by box, by Declan and the inspector with Ferra writing, and went back in, into two long iron chests that Bram had made for them in a week with hasps a man could not lift with one hand. Fennick's last thirty eggs went into a third chest by themselves, on a bed of sawdust, and Fennick stood by with his hands behind his back while the inspector counted them and did not breathe until the lid came down. The inspector put his seal on every hasp in red wax with the crown on it. Ferra put hers under his, in black, with the barony's arms.

"Now it takes two of us to open it," she said.

"The crown's seal is sufficient, Chancellor."

"For the crown," said Ferra. "Mine's for us."

On the third afternoon the inspector asked, very politely, if he might see the iron tubes.

Ward took him to the quarry. Emeric went too, because Bram and Ottoline had been in the armourers' shop at the works for a week with the door shut, and the thing they had been doing behind it was in a canvas case on Bram's shoulder.

It was a rifle from the racks: one of the three hundred, with the levy's scratches on its stock and a notch filed in the butt by somebody in the second company to count something. Under the breech, where the action closed, there was a new thing: a short steel housing the size of a man's thumb, screwed fast, with a hinged lid, and in it, when Bram opened the lid, a small clear stone the colour of water, cut long, sitting in a cradle of brass.

"Air," said Bram. He did not like it. He had not liked anything about it all week, and had made it anyway, and made it well, which was how Bram expressed most of his opinions. "One of Armand's hedge mages charges it. It pushes the ball down the barrel instead of the powder pushing it. No cartridge. You put the ball in the breech, bare, and you shut it, and you pull." He pointed at a small lever on the side of the housing with a scarred thumb. "That way, air. That way, it's a rifle like any other, and takes a cartridge, and doesn't care whether there's a stone anywhere in the county."

The inspector looked at it as if it might do something.

"It uses no powder?"

"None," said Ottoline. She had her magnifying glass up and was looking through it, not at the rifle, but at the inspector. "Not a grain. Your licence is for powder."

The inspector opened his mouth. Then he shut it again, and opened his case, and took out the licence, and read it through standing in the quarry, all three sheets, while they waited. Emeric watched him look for the word that was not there.

"It doesn't say," said the inspector at last.

"No," said Saban, who had come up on the cart behind them and was sitting on a block of stone eating an apple. "It doesn't."

Ward fired it.

He fired it from the quarry floor at a board set up against the far face, two hundred paces off, with a white ring painted on it; lay down on a sack with the rifle at his shoulder, put a bare ball into the breech from the bag at his belt, closed it, set the lever, and was still for a breath.

There was no bang.

There was a sound like a door being shut hard in another house, a flat cough with no echo, and nothing else: no flash, no smoke, no smell. Across the quarry, two hundred paces off, the board jumped on its prop and a piece of it went spinning off into the rubble.

The inspector had flinched anyway. Then he looked round, and up, and at the rifle, as though the bang had been mislaid somewhere and he expected to find it.

"Did it go?" said Rylan, from the cart.

Ward stood up and opened the breech. He took the stone out of its cradle on his palm, and looked at it, and it was a little less clear than it had been, as a lamp stone is at the end of a night.

"It went," said Ward. He looked across the quarry at the board. "Low. A hand at two hundred." He gave the stone to Bram. "Less push than powder. Close, it will not matter. Far, it will." He looked at the inspector, flatly, without any unkindness. "In a dead field it will do nothing at all, and the lever goes to powder, and the powder is under your seal. So it is a peace gun, sir. That is all it is."

The inspector wrote that down. He wrote it slowly, and then underlined something, and then looked up at the quarry face where the board had split.

"I shall have to tell the chancery," he said.

"Tell them," said Saban, and finished his apple. "Tell them it doesn't say. They'll find a word for it and put it in the next licence, in a year or so." He threw the core into the rubble. "They capped the men and licensed the powder. Nobody ever had to licence air."



They held Lantern Night on the sixth of April, a month late, because Imani would not have the whole town on the river wall breathing on one another until forty days had gone by without a new case anywhere in the four baronies, and the fortieth day was the thirty-first of March.

Nobody argued. The town had been folding lanterns since February anyway, and putting them on the shelf, and folding more.

"It's April," said Ferra, on the wall at dusk, with her column open on the parapet. She had kept the river's winds in it for seven years now. "I've March. I've never needed April." She ran her finger down the page. "The barge pilots say the wind turns down the valley at dusk in April one evening in two, if the day's been fine. I've entered it." She shut the book. "As a guess."

The day had been fine.

They wrote the names first, as they always did. Every lantern had one, at least; the town did it at trestles along the inside of the river wall with pots of lamp-black ink and brushes from the school, and the children wrote for the ones who could not, which was fewer every year. Emeric walked the trestles slowly with Ward, as he did every year, not to read the names, and read them anyway.

Garth Hallam, in a woman's hand that pressed too hard, on a lantern Wenna held in both hands at the end of the river street's trestle, with three other women round her.

Abner Tuck, from Milford, sent down by the post in an old woman's shaking capitals on a slip of paper, and copied onto a lantern by Sela's eldest grandchild, carefully, letter by letter, with her tongue between her teeth.

Dicky Marsh, on a lantern Arthur held, which he had written himself.

The nine from the stream ground, one to a lantern, the third company's carried by the third company. Jory Dace, on the dock quarter's. Long Tom, from Milford, who had been seventeen, and the camp's last. And the four baronies' own dead from the speckled winter, and the nineteen of the camp, more lanterns than any year since the first, so many that the trestles ran out of ink and a boy was sent running to the school for more.

At the hospital's trestle Imani wrote one name on her own lantern and did not show it to anybody. He knew it anyway. He had been there.

Ferra wrote two. He saw her do it, at the counting room's trestle, standing, in her small upright hand, on one lantern between them: Nye. Barnaby. She did not look up while she did it. When she had finished she capped the pot, and looked at the lantern, and then at him across the trestle.

"Four died at Harrow," she said.

He took a brush from the pot and a lantern from the stack, a small one, and wrote on it, under the lamp, in letters as small as he could make them, one name. Ferra watched him do it. She read it upside down. She did not say anything at all, and after a breath she put her hand flat on the trestle beside his, not touching it, and left it there while the ink dried.

Harlow.

They lit them from the wall lamps, as the town always did, a taper to the arc and drawn out burning; and at the turn of dusk, as Ferra had said one evening in two, the wind came round in the valley and ran down it toward the marsh and the sea, and the town let them go. They went up off the river wall in a long slow drift, carried down the valley, glowing, turning, rising, more of them than anybody could count; and the wall went quiet, as it did every year, all along its length; and then, as it did every year, somebody on the dock quarter's side began to sing, badly, and was joined.

Ferra opened her column on the parapet and wrote the wind, and the hour, and drew a line under her guess.

"Held," she said.



Bram brought the plate to the counting room on the ninth.

He put it on Ferra's table without a word, in a twist of oiled paper, and stood back with his arms folded: a disc of steel the size of a large coin, ground bright, with a hole through the middle of it so fine it was hard to see against the window, bell-mouthed on one side and true all the way through. Ferra unwrapped it. She held it up to the light. Then she took the glass that Ottoline had left on the shelf, and looked through it, and through the hole, at the window, for a long breath.

"Who?"

"The Grayrock girl," said Bram. "The one at the former. Evenings, after the benches. She got the recipe out of the book in January and started on a punch the week after, and she's spoiled nine." He considered. "Ten. She threw one in the river." He looked at the plate. "Pulled a hundred yards of the fine wire through it this morning. Clean."

Ferra put the plate down. She opened the forge book, back, to January, to the night of the needle run, where she had written his words that night about the open works cutting one the slow way by spring, and under them her own: By summer.

She looked at it.

Then she took her pen and wrote under her own line, in the same small upright hand, without hurrying.

9 April. Correction. By spring. The open works, the Grayrock girl at the former, ten spoiled. F.

She did not strike her own line through. It stood there above the correction, By summer, in her own ink, where anybody could see it. She blotted the correction and turned the book round for him to read, and looked at him, perfectly straight, perfectly grave, daring him.

He did not say anything. It took a great deal of effort.

"You can say it," said Ferra.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were going to for about a quarter of an hour, and then at dinner, and then in bed." She closed the book. "Say it now and it's done."

"Spring," said Emeric.

"Spring," Ferra agreed, and put the book under her arm. "The ninth of April is spring by three weeks. Don't push it." She turned to Bram. "Find out her name. She's going on the book."



He went down to the cellar that night with Ferra and the book for the kiln's masters.

They were nothing much to look at, when they were done, and they had taken him three hours: the plaster mould masters for a closet pan and its trap, a water-sealed bend that kept a drain's air in the drain, and the trap's siphon, which kept the water in the bend; and the die for the stoneware pipe, a heavy ring of steel with a core held in it on three thin legs, so that clay forced through it by the press came out a pipe, round and true, by the yard. Ferra wrote the stones against each line before he had finished saying what it was, and at the foot she wrote the total and ruled it, and under it, in the same hand: Recipe to the open works this week. Rule seven.

"Cheap," she said, which she had said in January. It was still the closest she came to approving a run.

By the end of the month the pipe-and-pan kiln stood by the brickfield on the far bank below the works, two round downdraught kilns of firebrick with a press shed between them; the clay came from the brickfield's own pit, and the firebrick from Grayrock's fireclay, which Bryda had been selling to the foundry for years at a price nobody in Kaldo had ever looked at twice and now everybody did. The first pipe came out of the die on the twenty-eighth, a yard of it at a time, brown and wet, and Tess had it cut and stood up on end in the shed to dry, in a row like a regiment.



The town's water began to taste of something on the fourteenth.

Emeric had told them it would. He had stood at the long table in March, home from the capital, and said that the water from the aqueduct would have a thing put in it at the aqueduct head, a little of it, and that the thing would kill what the boiling in the towers killed, all the way down the pipes to the last tap, and that it would taste. He had not said, because he could not, that it would taste like a pool he had swum in once in another life with a great many other children on a hot day; he had said it would taste a little like the bleachers' yard smelled.

It was a cell on the mill dynamo: a tank of strong salt water from the coast works with carbon plates in it from the lamp shop and the current from the mill run through it, so that the salt gave up the gas at one plate, and the gas went down a stone pipe into the water at the aqueduct head in a measured trickle that Linus had worked out with Imani over a week of jars. The towers went on boiling beside it. Imani would not let them stop until she had a year of the other.

Ferra kept the complaints. They came to the counting room at a rate of about thirty a day for the first week, and she wrote every one down in a column, by lane and number: tastes of the bleach yard, tastes of a new Copper, the cat won't drink it, my husband says it's poison, my husband says it's the prince's piss, which she wrote down exactly as it was said to her, and initialled.

Imani kept the fevers. Every morning a runner from each of the town's wards brought her the night's sick, and she wrote them on a sheet on the dispensary wall in a column of her own, and in the second week, when the complaints began to drop because people had stopped noticing, she came up the hill to the counting room and put her sheet down on Ferra's table beside Ferra's column and did not say anything.

Ferra looked from one to the other.

"Yours is going down faster," she said.

"Mine's the one that matters," said Imani. "Yours is the one they'll remember."



Down across the river, in the works' engine house, Bram was losing an argument about steam.

He was losing it to himself, which was the only kind of argument Bram ever really lost. The engine house had been built for the rolling mill on a full run, which happened one day in four; the rest of the time the boilers made steam nobody used, and in December some of it had gone under the bridge to Imani's ward, and in February more of it to the hospital, and now Emeric wanted the rest of it for a third engine on the long bed at the north end of the house, of the works' own pattern but bigger than anything the works had built, and on the end of its shaft a dynamo bigger than anything Tess had ever wound, to light the east half of the town.

"And when the mill wants its steam?" said Bram.

"The mill has it. The engine runs on what the mill doesn't want."

"Which the ward wants."

"The ward wants a little. There's plenty."

"There's plenty now," said Bram darkly. "There won't be plenty when there's three of you wanting it." He looked at the foundations going in on the north bed, the bolts set into the stone in their wooden boxes. "I've been building that house for six years to have spare steam. Six years. I'd got used to having it." He took his spectacles off. "Tess has the winding room ready. She says she wants the copper by the end of the month." He put the spectacles back on. "The copper. From Grayrock. Bryda says Fennick's taken his powder book down the hill and her crews won't stand near him. I don't know what she means by that and I don't want to." He turned away. "Go and bother somebody else. I've a sword to make."



The ship came on the twentieth, a little after noon, from the north.

Emeric felt her coming before anybody saw her. He was on the keep leads, because he had been up there every afternoon for three days, and at a mile out, over the north road, low, he felt a small hard brightness in his sense that was a lift stone held, and around it a man's note, thin and grey and running down like water out of a cracked jar; and then she came over the ridge into sight, slow, small, dark against the April sky, settling toward the town in a long series of small corrections, each one of them the whole of a man's attention.

She was smaller than he had remembered the capital's hulls. No longer than a market barge; a shallow wooden boat with a rail round it and a stubby mast for a lamp, and a little cabin aft with a glass skylight, and in the middle of her, in a well in the deck, a seat facing forward with a plate of brass in front of it, and a man in the seat with both hands on the plate.

She came down on the field below the north gate, where Ward had had a ring of men waiting since dawn with ropes. The hull touched and bumped and stopped, and the old man in the seat took his hands off the plate, and the stone under the deck went out of his sense like a candle pinched; and the man sat there.

He was seventy and looked a hundred. He had flown her from the capital in fifteen days, at half her pace and half days, because he was seventy, with a landing every night, at farms and posting houses and once in a churchyard, and he had poured himself into the lift every day from dawn to the middle of the afternoon, and he sat in the casting seat now with his hands in his lap and his face grey to the lips, and did not try to get up.

Ward's men lifted him out. Imani, who had come down from the hospital without being sent for, took his wrist on the grass, and counted, and looked up at Emeric, and did not say anything, and did not have to.

He slept for two days in the hospital. On the third he ate, and asked to see the hull's new owner, and when Emeric came he looked at him from the pillow with pale tired eyes, and took his time about it.

"She's a good boat," he said. "Forty years I flew her for the old lord. She'll do anything you ask, if you've the arms for it." He looked at Emeric's hands. "You'll not have a caster for her. Nobody has any more. You'll put her in a shed."

"Something like that."

"Pity." The old man shut his eyes. "Pity. She liked to be up."

He went home to his daughter's on the coast on the steamboat on the twenty-fourth, with his fee in his coat and a bottle of Maren's broth in a basket, and Emeric watched the boat out of sight from the bridge and then went across the river to the shed by the lake, where Bram and Fletcher had already had the old hull's deck up.



Under the casting seat, in a box of brass-bound oak below the deck, the old lift stones sat in a row of four cradles under a glass lid, so that a caster at the seat could look down through a window in the deck and see his stones while he poured into them. The glass was the first thing to go. Bram took it out and put a plate of iron in its place, riveted, painted the same dull brass colour so that from above it looked like the old lid gone dark with age; and under it, in the old cradles, Emeric put four stones of his own.

They were not the old stones. He had coded them in the room over three nights: lift that held whatever height it was given until it was told otherwise, the same as the ship's, and a lever under the casting plate, notched, that told it, with a second lever beside it that let the air stone at the stern push her on. A man at the casting seat with his hands flat on the brass plate and his fingers on the levers beneath its lip would look, from the deck or from the ground, exactly like a man pouring.

"And the man?" said Ward.

That was Edrin.



Armand brought him up to the study on the evening of the twenty-sixth: a tall stooped old man with white hair cut very short and a long face like a horse's, one of the fifteen students who had come south on Armand's letters years ago, who had never asked for anything and was never late for anything and did most of what the hedge mages did and a little more, quietly, on his own. Nobody in Kaldo called him anything but Old Edrin. He had been Armand's pupil in the tower fifty years before, a boy of twenty with a gift for air, and before he went up into the tower he had crewed lift hulls in the capital for three years, as a lad, to pay his fees, which was a thing very few people in Kaldo knew and Armand did.

"He'll do," said Armand. "I'll vouch for him. I've known him longer than I've known anyone alive." He sat down in Emeric's second chair and folded his hands on his stick. "Tell him."

So Emeric told him.

Not all of it. Not the ship, or the room, or the delve; only the hull in the shed and the stones under the iron and the levers under the plate, and what they would do, and that the man at the seat would not need to pour anything at all, and would have to look as though he did, from the capital to Kaldo and back, in front of every mage in the city.

Old Edrin listened to it with his hands on his knees and his long face perfectly still. When Emeric had finished he was quiet for a while.

"It flies itself," he said.

"Yes."

"Without a caster."

"Yes."

"Then what am I for?"

"To be seen not letting it," said Emeric.

Old Edrin looked at him. Then, slowly, he began to laugh: a long dry wheezing laugh that went on longer than seemed possible for a man his age and ended in a cough that Armand thumped him on the back for.

"Fifty years," he said, when he could. "Fifty years ago I sat in a seat like that from dawn to dusk for a lord who never once looked at me, and was put to bed by his grooms at night like a sack of meal, and thought it an honour, because they told me so." He wiped his eyes. "And now I'm to sit in one and do nothing, and look as if I'm dying." He looked at Armand. "You said he was interesting."

"I said he was worth the journey," said Armand. "I'll grant you interesting."

"And if the stones fail?" said Ward, from the door.

Old Edrin turned his head. "Then I pour, Captain. For real." He held up one long thin hand, and a little light came off the back of it, faint and pale and steady, and went out. "I can hold her up for an hour, perhaps two, on what's left of me. Long enough to put her down somewhere soft." He lowered the hand. "I'd not want to do it twice."

"There is a thing you should know," said Ward. "So that you know what you are agreeing to." He did not come in from the door. "A man who knows how she flies is one more man who could be taken, and held, and have what he knows taken out of him, by anybody who knows how. It has been done. I have seen it done." He was quiet. "You would be one more door."

"I'm seventy-one," said Old Edrin. "I've not been interesting to anybody since I was twenty." He considered. "I find I don't mind being a door." He looked at Emeric. "Where do I sign?"

He signed the oath in Ferra's book in the counting room the next morning, in a long thin old-fashioned hand, and Armand put the band on his wrist himself, and Old Edrin turned his wrist over and looked at it, and said it was the first jewellery he had ever owned.

He went up in the hull that afternoon from the lake, with Emeric on the deck beside him and Bram and Fletcher on the shore, and sat in the casting seat with his hands on the brass plate and his fingers on the levers, and lifted her off the water to a hundred feet, and held her there.

"You're breathing," said Emeric.

"Am I?"

"A caster breathes hard. From the chest. You've seen them."

"I've been one." Old Edrin closed his eyes and began to breathe from the chest, slowly and heavily, with a little catch at the top of every breath, and let his face go grey, which Emeric would not have believed a man could do on purpose, and his shoulders sank, and a fine sweat came out along his hairline. After a minute he opened one eye. "Like that?"

"Like that."

"I've had fifty years to study it from the inside," said Old Edrin, and closed the eye again.



"Will they feel the stones?" Ward asked that night, on the keep leads.

It was the question Emeric had been asking himself for a fortnight, and he had an answer for it, which was not the same as being sure.

"A mage who's just standing there feels a charged stone at a few paces," he said. "Close. Like warmth off a hand. Through iron he hardly feels it at all; iron comes back to him dead, the way wood does, and a stone behind iron is behind something dead. To feel further than that he has to go out after it. Send his sense out on purpose, thin, the way Armand taught me." He looked down at the dark lake, where the hull lay in its shed. "That costs him. It hurts. Not many of them are trained to it, and the ones who are don't spend it on a hull with a caster sitting at the seat pouring his heart out in plain sight. Why would they? They know how hulls fly."

"You felt hers from a mile off."

"I felt the stone the old man was holding, from a mile off, through glass, with him pouring into it." Emeric was quiet. "I'm not a mage who's just standing there. You know that."

"I know that," said Ward. "So nobody climbs aboard."

"Nobody climbs aboard."

Ward nodded once, and went down.



The clothes were Ferra's argument, and she won it the way she won most things, by having done the arithmetic before anybody else had noticed there was a sum.

"Not silver," she said, at the long table, with Abloh standing behind her chair with a bolt of black silk under one arm and the expression of a man who had been sent for from the sail loft and told nothing on the way. "You've worn charcoal and silver since the capital. Silver's Emberfell's now. Rowena put you in it at her table and every trader on the south coast has the picture." She laid a strip of black silk on the table, and on it a strip of gold thread. "Black and gold. Gold's the duchy. It's nobody's yet. If you wear it first, it's yours."

"It's a great deal of gold."

"It's a great deal of duchy." She turned to Abloh. "Roses down the skirts of the coat. Gold, worked close. Clusters at the waist of the waistcoat. And vine between them, so it doesn't look like a garden."

"That what you want?" said Abloh, to Emeric, not to her.

"Apparently."

Abloh grunted. He had cut the cape for every flying suit Kaldo owned and packed every chute aboard the ship, and let down the hem of Ferra's one good grey dress for a bride in a single afternoon, calling it a crime against good wool the whole time; and he had never in his life, so far as Emeric knew, cut a coat for a prince. He looked at the gold thread on the silk with his head on one side, as he looked at a seam he had not been told about.

"Why roses?" said Emeric.

Ferra looked at him as if he had asked why a column was ruled.

"Because they're good embroidery," she said. "They take the gold well and they read from across a hall. A tree doesn't read. A hammer looks like a smith's sign." She put her hand flat on the silk. "And I'm having them on mine."

Nobody at the table said anything for a breath. Imani, at the far end, with her papers, looked up.

"Yours," said Emeric.

"Black," said Ferra. "High at the neck. Gold down it, and gold at the sleeves, and roses down the skirt. To match." She did not look away from him. "The capital's never seen us in one room. They'll read whatever we wear. They'll read it if I come in grey with my book under my arm; they'll say the Chancellor came as a clerk, because she's his clerk. I'd sooner they read what I wrote." She took her hand off the silk. "So I'm writing it." She turned her head down the table. "And Imani's."

"No," said Imani, without looking up.

"We'll talk about it."

"We won't."

Abloh looked from one end of the table to the other, as a man looks who has been in a sail loft with two masters arguing over the same canvas and has learned to wait them out. "I'll measure for two," he said. "Cutting's later."



He measured Emeric in the sail loft over the boat shed the next morning, in silence, with a knotted cord and a stick of chalk, as he did everything.

"Arms out."

Emeric put his arms out. Abloh went round him with the cord, across the back from shoulder point to shoulder point, and round the chest, and down the arm, and chalked each figure on the loft wall; and stopped, and measured the back again, and looked at the wall.

"Something wrong?"

"No." Abloh wound the cord round his hand. "You've a back on you now." He looked at the figures a while longer. "Last coat that fitted you was cut for a man who leaned on things."

Emeric thought of the coats he had brought south nine years ago, cut for a prince who drank at noon and leaned on doorframes, which had stopped closing across the shoulders some time in his third year here; he had never once wondered why.

"Ward," he said.

"Ward and the hammer," said Abloh, and wrote one more figure on the wall. "And that iron collar all winter. I'll cut it to stand off the neck." The chalk slipped out of his fingers and rolled away across the boards toward the middle of the loft, and kept going, and stopped against a trestle. Abloh went and got it. "Floor's grown a slope," he said, and put the chalk in his pocket. "She says roses. I'll do roses. Don't ask me what a rose is for. I've never sewn one."



She showed him the pattern that night in the counting room, after the lamps, on a sheet of Abloh's tracing paper laid over the black silk: a single rose drawn in gold pencil, open, with its leaves, at the size it would be on the skirt of a dress.

"It's the same rose as yours," she said. "Exactly. He's drawn it once and he's pricking it through for both."

He was standing behind her chair. He bent down to look at it over her shoulder, and she did not move out of his way, and his cheek was beside hers.

"It's a good rose," he said.

"It's a very good rose. I chose it out of twelve." She turned her head a little. "Fourteen. I counted wrong." She was very close. "Don't tell anybody I counted wrong."

He kissed the side of her neck under her ear, where the pinned-up hair left it bare.

"I'm doing the Grayrock returns," said Ferra, without moving.

"You've done them."

"I'm doing them again." She did not pick up her pen. He kissed her neck again, lower, and felt her breath go out of her, slowly, all of it, and her hand come up blindly over her shoulder and find the back of his head and hold it there. "I'm doing them," she said, "again, because Bryda's figures are wrong, because Bryda's figures are always wrong in April, and somebody has to." Her fingers tightened in his hair. "Work first."

"I know."

"Fun later." She turned her head the rest of the way and kissed him on the mouth, hard and short, with her hand still in his hair, and let go. "Much later. Go away. You're on my rose."

He looked down. He had put his hand flat on the tracing paper to lean on, and the gold pencil had come off on his palm.

Ferra took his wrist and turned his hand over and looked at the rose printed backward across it, smudged and gold, and for a breath something in her face that he had seen only twice before came up into it and stayed there.

"Now you're read," she said, and let go of his wrist, and picked up her pen.



Bram made the sword in the second week of May, at night, in his own forge, because he would not make it in the works with forty men watching.

Emeric went down on the third night and sat on the sacks by the door where Bram let him sit, and watched. It was Kaldo steel, the long bar of it folded and drawn and folded again on the anvil under Bram's hammer in the red light, and when the blade was drawn and ground and hardened and tempered Bram set it aside and made the guard, which took him two nights alone: a cross of steel, and on it, wreathed round both arms and the grip's root, roses, some of them gold, beaten thin and laid on, and some left black, the steel's own colour, so that the gold ones stood out from the dark ones like lamps in a lane.

"Ferra's roses," said Bram, filing a petal. "I'd have put a hammer."

"She said a hammer looks like a smith's sign."

"It is a smith's sign. I'm the smith." He blew the filings off. "She's right. It'd look like you'd stolen it off my door."

He did the other thing on the last night, after the sword was done and in its scabbard on the bench: a single scale of steel the size of a man's palm, curved to a chest, so thin that it flexed when he held it at the ends between finger and thumb and sprang back true when he let go. He had hardened it four times. He tried it on the anvil with a punch and a hammer, and the punch skidded off it and scored the anvil, and Bram looked at the score in his anvil for a long breath with deep personal offence and then put the scale down.

"Between the silks," he said. "Over the heart. Abloh's to sew it in a pocket of its own, so it lies flat and doesn't show and doesn't shift." He looked at Emeric over his spectacles. "You'll not take it out because it's hot."

"No."

"You'll not take it out because the court's too warm, or you're dancing, or she says you look stiff."

"No."

"Good." Bram wrapped it in a piece of leather. "A sword's for show. That's for the day the show goes wrong." He gave it to Emeric, and turned back to the bench. "Don't make me make another."



Imani would not go.

He asked her in the dispensary, in April, with the chlorine sheets on the wall behind her, and she said no before he had finished the question and went on folding bandages.

"Somebody has to be in Kaldo who isn't a duke," she said. "The pairs are still going out to the hill hamlets, and Grayrock's rows want doing again in the autumn, and the hospital's got the tail of the winter in it still." She folded a bandage in three and then in three again. "And I've no interest in watching a king hand you a sword. I've watched you hold a bucket of lime. That's the one I'd go and see."

"Ferra wants you there."

"Ferra wants a great many things. She's got a column for them." She put the bandage on the stack. "They don't need to see me. I don't want to stand in a hall in a dress being one of yours in front of a court that'll count. I'll be one of yours here, where nobody counts but Ferra, and she's on my side." She looked at him. "Don't argue."

He did not argue. Ferra did.

She came into the dispensary at a quarter to ten that night with her book under her arm and her shawl over her head, and shut the door, and did not sit down; and Emeric, who had brought the fever sheet up because the runner was ill, stood by the window with it and understood that he was not going to be asked to leave, or asked anything else.

"If I go up that hall alone in his roses," said Ferra, "they'll write me down as the one."

Imani did not stop folding.

"Every letter that leaves the city that week," said Ferra. "Every house in the north and every merchant on the coast road. The Chancellor of Kaldo, in his colours, on his arm, matched to the thread. First." She put the book down on the dispensary table between the bandages and the jars, and opened it at the page from Emberfell with the two initials at the foot of it, and turned it round so that it faced Imani. "Term one. I wrote it. I'll not break it in front of two hundred people because you'd rather fold bandages."

"I said I don't want to stand in a hall in a dress being one of his in front of a court that'll count."

"Then let them count to two."

Imani's hands stopped.

She looked at the book, and at the term in Ferra's small square hand, and at her own initial under it; and then up at Ferra, for a long breath, with her chin up, as the two of them had looked at each other across the counting room table in January; and then at Emeric, by the window.

"Did you put her up to this?"

"No."

"He didn't," said Ferra. "He's frightened of you."

"Good," said Imani. She looked at the bandage in her hands, half folded, and finished folding it, in three and then in three again, and laid it on the stack exactly square with the others. "No roses down the skirt. I'm not a garden."

"One," said Ferra. She put two fingers on her own breast, high, on the left, over the heart. "Here."

Imani looked at the place. Something went over her face that Emeric could not read from the window.

"Where I'd put my fingers," she said.

"Yes."

"And no lace. And sleeves I can push up. And I'm not walking behind you up any hall, I'm not walking behind anybody."

"Nobody's walking behind anybody," said Ferra, and picked up her book, and wrote something in it, standing, with the book against her chest. "That's the point." She shut it. "Abloh's at seven. He's slow with the cord. Wear something you can stand in for an hour."

She went out. The door shut. Imani stood at the dispensary table for a while with her hands flat on it, looking at the place where the book had been.

"I've just been bought," she said, "with my own arithmetic."



On the night before they left, the twenty-second of May, she sent for him.

Not by a runner. By Ferra, which was a thing she had never done before; Ferra came into the study at ten with her book and said that Imani was in the hospital and wanted him up the hill before he went to bed, and that he was not to take a lamp because there was a moon, and then went out again without saying anything else, and Emeric saw her mouth move at the corner as she shut the door.

Imani was in the dispensary, alone, with the lamp turned low and the window open on the garden, where the isolation ward's long roof lay dark at the bottom of it. She was sitting on the edge of the dispensary table in her ward dress with her sleeves rolled and her hair half down, and she had a jar in her hands, a small squat stoneware jar with a cork in it and a paper tied over the cork.

"Maren's," she said, and held it out. "For your collar."

He took it. It was the ointment Maren made for the ward's bedsores from goose fat and comfrey and something she would not tell anybody, and it smelled of the kitchen.

"Ferra showed me the drawing," said Imani. "The coat. The shirt under it, with the lace standing up at the throat." She made a small shape with her hand at her own throat. "Starched lace, all day, on a neck that's been in a plate gorget for a winter. You'll be raw by noon." She looked at the jar. "Thin. Under the collar, at the back and the sides, in the morning, before you dress. And again at night. Don't put it on your face or you'll shine in front of the king."

"I'll shine anyway. There's a great deal of gold."

"There's a great deal of gold," Imani agreed. She slid down off the table, and stood in front of him, close, and took the jar back out of his hand and put it in the pocket of his coat herself, and patted the pocket flat. Then she left her hand there, on his chest, over the pocket.

"Ferra and I have talked," she said.

"About what?"

"About after." Her hand did not move. "We've got it settled. Both of us. Between us. It's written, the bit that's Ferra's to write, and the bit that's mine to say I'm saying now." She looked up at him in the low lamplight. "That's all you get. You'll find out the rest when we're home."

"That isn't fair."

"No," said Imani. "It's term four. You don't get to make it easy on yourself." Her mouth moved. "We do."

He laughed, and she kissed him while he was laughing, so that it went into her mouth; slowly, level with him, with her hand flat over the jar in his pocket and her other hand coming up to the back of his neck, under his collar, exactly where the ruffle would be, as if she were checking the place. He held her by the waist, and then not only by the waist, and felt her pulse go under his thumb at her wrist where her hand had come round to his jaw, and she felt him feel it, and pulled back an inch.

"Don't count it," she said.

"You always count mine."

"I'm the healer." She kissed him again, shorter. "Go to bed. We've a ship in the morning and Old Edrin to keep from laughing at the casting seat." She stepped back, and turned him round by the shoulders toward the door, and pushed him, gently, in the middle of the back. "And use the jar. I'll be too busy being looked at to do it for you."

He went down the hill in the moonlight without a lamp, with the jar in his pocket, and did not look back, because he knew she would be standing in the dispensary door to see whether he did.
